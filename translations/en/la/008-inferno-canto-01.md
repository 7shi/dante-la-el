# Summary of Canto 1

In the first canto of the *Inferno*, which begins: *In the middle of the journey of our life*, the author Dante asserts that in the middle of the journey of his life, that is, in the thirty-fifth year of his age, he found himself in a dark wood, which figures the vicious life; and he asserts that he does not know how he entered such a wood: walking through it for a long time, he at length came to a fair hill, which figures the state of penance among the Theologians, and among the Philosophers figures the power of synderesis, which murmurs against evil deeds. And the sides, or shoulders and parts, of that hill were already illuminated by the sun's rays. And wishing to ascend that hill, he encountered three beasts blocking his path on every side: the first of which was a *leontia* [she-leopard] covered with a spotted coat; which beast, or *leontia*, figures lust, which greatly hinders men in youth from living purely or honorably; which at last he got past. The second was a lion with head held high; which lion figures pride, which greatly hinders men in adult age; the lion too he got past. The third beast was a lean, voracious she-wolf, which can never be sated; and she figures avarice, which tightly binds men in old age. This she-wolf harassed the author so much that he could scarcely resist; nay, he was already beginning to fall back and return to the wretched valley of vices. While he was in such distress, a shade appeared to him, to whom he cried out for help; which shade declared himself to be the shade of Virgil; and this he made known by many tokens: first, because he was a Mantuan; second, because he was born under Julius, namely Cæsar; third, because he lived under good Augustus, namely the Emperor Octavian; fourth, because he had sung, that is, composed a book, concerning Æneas, son of Anchises, who came from Troy after proud Ilium—that is, the most lofty and wealthy palace of King Priam—had been burned. He said to the author: This beast, namely the she-wolf, on whose account you are afraid—that is, you cry out and beg for help—has a most wicked nature, which is never satisfied with food, but after feeding has greater hunger than before; and she is already wedded to many animals, and in the future will be espoused to more and many, until the Greyhound [*veltrus*] shall come, that is, that noble dog, which figures a lord to come, whether pope, or emperor, or duke. Here the author prophesies concerning this duke to come, and follows the opinion of Albumasar in the book *On the Conjunctions of the Planets*. This Greyhound is the foe of wolves: that is, that good lord will not be fed on gold or pewter; rather his food will be wisdom, love, and virtue. This Greyhound will slay the she-wolf and drive her back to Hell, whence envy first drew her forth. And he says, adding to the author: To escape and flee so great an evil, you must hold another course; and if you wish to follow me, I will deliver you and lead you through the kingdom of the sorrowful, where you will see the ancient sorrowing spirits, never hoping to be freed from such torments. Afterward I will lead you through the kingdom of those who suffer in fire and yet rejoice in those very torments, because they hope to depart thence and go to the blessed people. To which blessed people if you should afterward wish to go, a soul worthier than I will guide you; because I cannot enter thither, for the reason that I was rebellious to the law and faith of that Emperor who reigns there; therefore He does not will that I go thither. The first kingdom is Hell; the second is Purgatory; the third is eternal life, Paradise. Then the author said to Virgil: I conjure you by that God whom you knew not, that you lead me as you have said, and that I may see the things of which you have spoken, and that I may escape these perils and other evils, and that I may see the gate of Saint Peter. Then Virgil began the journey, and Dante himself humbly followed him.

# Canto 1

1 In the middle of the journey of our life\
&emsp;I found myself in a dark wood,\
&emsp;Whose straight way was astray.

EXPOSITION OF THE BOOK. — Approaching the exposition of the book, it must first be known that the book by its primary division is divided into a proem and a treatise. The treatise begins at the start of the third canto of this first book, namely the *Inferno*, which begins in the vernacular:

Per me si va ne la città dolente;

and literally begins:

Through me one goes into the city of the sorrowing.

Furthermore, the first part is divided into two. In the first of these are set forth certain dispositions of the author himself. In the second is placed the invocation. The second part begins there, in the vernacular:

Lo giorno se ne andava

at the beginning of the second canto; literally:

The day was already declining.

Furthermore, the first part, which is the first canto, which begins in the vernacular:

Nel mezo del camin de nostra vita;

literally:

In the middle of the journey of our life,

is divided into four principal parts. In the first the author sets forth his vision. In the second he shows how, coming to a certain hill which he wished to ascend, three beasts came against him to block his path, or ascent: there, in the vernacular:

Ed eccho quasi al comenzare;

literally:

And behold, almost at the beginning of the ascent.

In the third part he shows how, while he was in such peril, an unknown figure appeared to him, who lent him aid: there, in the vernacular:

Mentre che io ruinava in basso loco;

literally:

While I was thus falling into the lowest place.

In the fourth part it is shown how Virgil counseled him, explaining many things to him: there, in the vernacular:

A te convene tenere altro viagio;

literally:

Another course you must hold.

To understand the first part, it must be noted that the author considered three kinds of wayfarers: namely, those who sin and are obstinate in their sins, those who do penance, and those who act virtuously. Concerning these he made three books in this *Comedy*: of the first he made the first book, namely *Inferno*; of the second he made the second book, namely *Purgatorio*; of the third he made the third, namely *Paradiso*.

Furthermore, it must be noted that the author, in the year 1300, in the month of March, on Good Friday, had such a vision in dreams; and that was the Jubilee year. The vision was this: it seemed to him that in the middle of the days of our life—that is, human life—he was in a wood, and that the straight way was as it were lost, so that men had strayed from the right path.

The middle of the journey of our life seems to be the span of thirty years, according to the saying of the Philosopher in the *Politics*, who asserts the age of man to be sixty years. Some say that the middle of life is the span of thirty-three years, at which age Christ died and rose again, at which age we shall all rise again, as Saint Paul says; and so say several Doctors of Sacred Scripture. Others, however, say, as the psalmist Prophet says in the eighty-ninth Psalm: *The days of our years in them are seventy years; but if in strength, eighty years; and what is more than these is labor and sorrow*. Which opinion the author holds, and thus thirty-five years are the middle of our journey; etc.

2 Alas, how hard a thing it is to tell what it was like,\
&emsp;This savage wood, both harsh and dense,\
&emsp;Which in thought renews my fear.

3 So bitter is it that death is scarcely more bitter.\
&emsp;Yet, to treat of the good that I found in it,\
&emsp;I will speak of the other things that I perceived therein.

4 I could not well say how I entered it;\
&emsp;So full of sleep was I at that moment,\
&emsp;That I forsook the true way.

5 But after I came to the foot of a hill,\
&emsp;Where that valley ended\
&emsp;Which had pierced my heart with fear,

Note that the wood is the vicious worldly state; since, just as in a wood there are many kinds of barren trees, so is the vicious state, in which men are barren in good works. A wood is also an uncultivated place, a lair of wild beasts, and suited for ambushes; so sinners are without the cultivation of good works, deceivers and liers-in-wait against the good; etc. This wood is called dark, because sins blind men, and especially those habituated in sins, who sometimes lose the judgment of right reason. These have lost the straight way of the virtues. *Ay*: an adverb of one grieving or marveling. *How*: that is, it would be overly difficult, and it is a hard thing, to describe the things that were in this wood. Virgil says in the sixth book of the *Æneid*: *Not if I had a hundred tongues and as many mouths could I tell*; etc. Here the author says that the way of vices is bitter.

Objection: nay, it is sweet; for pride is sweet, as the Philosopher says that it is the highest delight to hold sway. Vengeance, which proceeds from wrath, seems sweet. Homer says that to be avenged is sweeter than dripping honey. The same of the acts of Venus.

It must be said that vices seem sweet at the beginning; yet their end is most bitter and most harsh. The contrary happens with the virtues, especially when one considers the punishments due to sins and the rewards of the virtues.

He says that it is so bitter that death is scarcely more bitter than it. He seems to say that death is more bitter than the vicious way. Vices are the death of the soul, which is worse than the death of the body. Answer, that from the death of vices there is a return through penance; whereas from the death of the body there is no return under the present law.

He tells the reason why he wishes to tell of such a wood, or vicious life: by speaking of the good things that he found in it. What are those good things? He says that it is divine justice and also human justice, the frequent conversion of sinners, the casting off of sins, and turning to the virtues.

*Nor do I know*. *Io non saperia ben redicere*: here he makes an antipophora.

Someone might say to him: Why were you so mad as to enter such a wood, that is, a vicious life? He answers that he was drowsy at that moment. But what sleep was this? The Platonists say—positing that our souls are *dæmones*, either a *Calodæmon*, that is, a good spirit full of knowledge, or a *Cacodæmon*, that is, an evil spirit full of knowledge, coming into our bodies—that when they come, they forget everything and suffer oblivion and lethargy. Which opinion is worthless.

Others would say that this sleep is the ignorance in which our soul is first made and created, since at the start of its being it is like a *tabula rasa*, in which nothing is written. So hold Aristotle and Averrhoës. And others would say that that sleep is original sin, in which men are conceived, as David says in the fiftieth Psalm: *Behold, in iniquities I was conceived, and in sins my mother conceived me*. Yet how does man, growing older and becoming possessed of reason, who beforehand in infancy was pure after baptism, how does he begin to sin, to follow sensuality and not reason, and thus enter the way of vices and not the way of virtues? This difficulty will be clearly deduced and treated in the sixteenth canto of *Purgatorio*.

*Ma poi che io fui a piè*. *But after I came to a hill*: he shows how he came to a mountain, or hill, after he had wandered through the wood. Which mountain signifies the way of the virtues, which is narrow, leading to life, as is had in the Gospel; just as the valley signifies the way of vices, which is broad, leading to death. Virgil says in the sixth book of the *Æneid*: *Easy is the descent to Avernus*, etc., *but to retrace one's steps to the upper air, this is the task*.

The mountain, up which it is difficult to climb, is penance, and figures the way of the virtues; just as the valley figures the descent toward the center. This hill ended that valley, because between virtue and vice there is no middle ground.

6 I looked on high, and saw its shoulders\
&emsp;Already clothed with the rays of the planet\
&emsp;Which leads everyone straight along every path.

7 Then the fear was somewhat quieted,\
&emsp;Which in the depth of my heart had endured\
&emsp;During the night that I passed with such anguish.

8 And even as he who with spent breath,\
&emsp;Having come forth from the sea unto the shore,\
&emsp;Turns to the perilous water, and gazes;

9 So my mind, which still was fleeing,\
&emsp;Turned back to look upon the pass\
&emsp;That never left a single person alive.

10 After my weary body had been rested,\
&emsp;I resumed my way along the desert ridge,\
&emsp;So that the firm foot was always the lower.

11 And behold, almost at the beginning of the slope,\
&emsp;A leopard light and very swift,\
&emsp;Which was covered with a spotted coat.

He says that his heart had been pierced, because many fears come upon sinners: namely, of God's judgment, of shame before men, and the remorse of conscience.

*I looked on high*: hitherto he had looked toward the earth; but now he lifted up his eyes to the mountains. *Shoulders*: that is, the steeps of the mountain, or the upright heights of the mountain, to denote the rigor, arduousness, and uprightness of virtue.

*Of the planet*: that is, of the sun. Morally, understand divine grace; because no virtue can properly and perfectly shine in man without divine grace.

*It guides along every path*: because divine grace directs and rightly leads man in every state and in every degree in which he exists.

*Then fear*: behold the effect of the vision.

*In the depth of the heart*: or rooted in the lake of the heart.

*La notte*. *Through the night*: namely, all the time during which he remained in vices.

*And even as*: the comparison is of a shipwrecked man, who, crossing seas and great rivers, with great toil comes to the bank and shore; then turning around and considering the peril in which he [was], his fear is increased. So it happened to Dante, reflecting and saying to himself: Woe is me, wretch! Behold in what things I have spent my time—part in the love of women, part in factions and partisanship.

*Was fleeing*: because he was thinking of fleeing the vices already committed. *Which pass no living person*: that is, no living wayfarer, except Christ and the Virgin Mary, was without sin and a vicious life; because, except Christ and His Mother, all are at least conceived in original sin.

*After it rested somewhat*: or his weary body rested. The author shows how he began again his journey along the desert mountain. Yet the rear foot was the firmer. By the lower foot understand the love of earthly things; the upper signifies the love of heavenly things. Now the author wishes to say that the love of earthly things drew him toward the valley more strongly than the upper foot—that is, than the love of heavenly things—drew him toward things above.

*And behold, almost at the beginning*. *Et eccho, quasi al comenzare*: this is the second principal part of this canto, in which the author shows how, as he wished to ascend the mountain, there came to meet him a swift and light *leontia* [she-leopard], which was covered with varied color; nor did it withdraw from the author's face, and it hindered his path so much that several times the author wished to turn back.

Here it must be noted that when a man wishes to repent of his sins, there comes the *leontia*, that is, lust, very light and swift.

*Covered with varied colors*: thus lust in varied ways occupies men, especially the young, from the fifteenth year to the thirtieth; afterward comes the lion, holding its head high, with rabid hunger, which the air fears. Behold pride, which most occupies men from the thirtieth year to the fortieth, or even until old age. Then comes a ravenous she-wolf, which is greedy for all things and has made many peoples live wretchedly. This is avarice, which drives man even unto death, and occupies men, especially the old; and as men grow old, avarice grows young in them, whereas the other vices grow old as man grows old; avarice alone grows young. The *leontia* is a female of the leopard species, which is an exceedingly lustful animal. By nature it has a beautiful pelt: so the beauty of women lies on the surface. The *leontia* is light and swift: so is love, which instantly at a single glance captures a man. When the *leontia* seizes a beast, it sucks all its blood: so love blinds the whole man, nay, drives him out of his mind.

12 And it did not withdraw before my face,\
&emsp;Nay, it hindered my journey so much\
&emsp;That I was turned many times to go back.

13 The time was at the beginning of the morning;\
&emsp;And the sun was mounting up with those stars\
&emsp;Which were with it, when divine love

14 First set in motion those fair things;\
&emsp;So that cause to hope well was given me\
&emsp;By the pleasant skin of that wild beast,

15 The hour of the time, and the sweet season:\
&emsp;Yet not so, but that fear was given me\
&emsp;By the sight that appeared to me of a lion.

(1) var. *tollia*.

*From my sight, or face*: by this it is noted that Dante was smitten with love, and several times wished to return to illicit love.

*The time was at the beginning of the morning*. In the vernacular: *Tempo era del principio del mattino*: here he sets forth and marks the time in which he had this vision—namely, when he began to ascend the mountain, which represents penance, the casting off of vices, and the taking up of a virtuous life, and when the wild beast, namely the *leontia*, was hindering his ascent. He means that the disposition of the time helps toward fulfilling his intention, namely to trample down the vice of lust; since the hour was early morning, that is, at dawn, in the twilight of day; and it was springtime: the sun was in Aries.

But why does the author praise this hour so highly? It must be said, as Aristotle holds in the book *On Sleep and Waking*, that sometimes dreams occur in the first watch of the night, or the second, from surfeit and excessive fullness. But dreams that occur after midnight, or near daybreak, once digestion is completed, are commonly true and signify the truth. He wishes to say: My vision in sleep, which I describe here, did not arise from surfeit, because I was already sober; rather it came from contemplation and good forethought, because *dreams follow many cares*, says Solomon in the book of Ecclesiastes.

Moreover, in spring, when the sun is in Aries, all things growing from the earth as it were revive, become green and appear in verdure, bloom, put forth leaves, and recover strength, because of the sun's return to our hemisphere; since from the sign of Libra the sun descends to the other, lower hemisphere until the end of Capricorn; then it returns to us, ascending up to the sign of Cancer in the month of June. In the month of March is the equinox, that is, the equality of night and day, and the sun ascends in Aries. Thus animals too recover strength in spring, and are in a better disposition than in winter; etc.

*The time was at the beginning of the morning*: that is, day was beginning to shine.

*The sun was ascending with those stars*: that is, with Aries, or in Aries. He says in the plural, *with stars*, because the sign of Aries consists of several stars, just like any other sign in the Zodiac; in which sign the sun was created, and [now] stood.

*When God, or divine love*: pointedly he speaks of love, because out of love God made the world and fair creatures, so that He might share His goodness and perfection with creatures.

*Mosse dapprima*. *First set in motion*: that is, made. Here the author speaks not according to the opinion of Aristotle, who posited that the world and motion existed from eternity; rather Dante speaks according to the truth of Sacred Scripture, which sets forth the creation of the world according to that saying: *In the beginning God created heaven and earth* (Gen. ch. 1); and alongside that: *All things were made through Him* (John ch. 1).

[The time] gave me good hope of overcoming the beast of the spotted skin, or *gaietta pelle*, that is, pleasant, or of varied color; that is, of abstaining from lust and from illicit worldly love, which hindered my journey up the mountain, that is, penance. Yet it appears that the contrary ought to be the case: for in springtime strength is fortified in men, natural heat is invigorated, and thus men seem to be provoked to lust more than at another time. Other animals too pair together, male and female; birds build nests; etc. Therefore the Doctors say that in spring the fasts of Lent and abstinences were instituted by the Church to curb the stirring of forces that are renewed at such a time.

Say in reply: That the truth holds regarding the renewal of forces in spring; yet the author's intention aims at this, that if in spring he resolved and determined altogether to flee lust and abandon illicit love and conquer the *leontia*, how much more will he do this at other seasons?

*Ma no[n] sì che paura*. *Yet not so as not to give me fear at the sight of a lion*: he describes the second beast, namely the lion, signifying pride. When lust ceases somewhat in man, pride begins, while a man wishes to trample upon others; and pride greatly hinders the virtuous path. Note that Dante was very proud, as he himself confesses in *Purgatorio*: for he was noble and learned, and pride readily consorts with nobility and knowledge, which puffs up. The roar of the lion terrifies beasts; so pride terrifies subjects.

16 This seemed as though it were coming against me\
&emsp;With head held high and with rabid hunger,\
&emsp;So that it seemed the air feared it:

17 And a she-wolf, that of all cravings\
&emsp;Appeared laden in her leanness,\
&emsp;Which has already forced many peoples to live in misery.

18 She laid upon me so much heaviness\
&emsp;With the fear that issued from her aspect,\
&emsp;That I lost the hope of the height.

19 And as is he who gladly acquires,\
&emsp;And the time comes that makes him lose,\
&emsp;Who in his every thought weeps and grieves;

20 Such did the beast without peace make me,\
&emsp;Which, advancing against me, little by little\
&emsp;Drove me back to the place where the sun is silent.

21 While I was falling downward into the lowest place,\
&emsp;Before my eyes was offered one\
&emsp;Who through long silence seemed hoarse.

22 When I saw this one in the great desert,\
&emsp;Have mercy on me, I cried out to him,\
&emsp;Whoever you be, whether shade or actual man.

*With head held high*: the nature of a proud man is with rabid hunger. The proud man would like to grasp everything, and when he cannot, he rages and becomes as it were rabid.

*It seemed that the air was afraid*: because not only men of lower condition, signified by the earth, but even the wise, signified by the air, fear pride, or the proud.

*And a she-wolf*: behold the third beast, worse than the first two: because, although he had seen the *leontia* and the lion, he still stood his ground; but when the she-wolf came, he almost lost the hope of ascending. The she-wolf is a voracious, rapacious animal; her gluttony is never sated: rightly does she signify avarice.

*Senbiava*: she appeared in her leanness, because she is never filled with food. Avarice has been the cause of sadness to many.

She, namely the she-wolf, laid so much heaviness upon the author that he almost lost all hope of ascending higher; etc.; that is, she posed a hindrance. For when someone wishes to do well, first he is hindered by lust, second by pride, third by avarice, as by the worst; because avarice says to a man: Already you are growing old, you will not be able to earn, you will fall into poverty and wretchedness, and you will be a laughingstock to all. Dante, however, was so magnanimous that he conquered all these things, cast out avarice, and made this noble work.

*And as is*: now he makes a fine comparison of a merchant, who toils long and works hard, and, in order to gain, sells many of his goods, with which he hoped to acquire many others; and when what he hoped for does not turn out for him, he laments, saying: I have fallen into the hands of robbers, I have suffered shipwreck, I have lost all my goods; and thus, hoping and believing I would acquire what I did not have, I lost what I had. Wherefore he is greatly saddened and grieves, because whatever he had planned to accomplish over many years, he sees all lost in a single hour; therefore he grieves exceedingly. Thus Dante, seeing that she-wolf.

*Without peace*: that is, whoever follows that she-wolf, namely avarice, never enjoys peace or quiet. The avaricious man does not rest even by night, as Solomon witnesses in the book of Ecclesiastes. Dante himself was falling back and returning to the valley deprived of the sun—that is, the vicious life, deprived of divine grace.

*While I was thus falling into the lowest place*. *Mentre che io ruinava in basso loco*: this is the third section of this canto, in which it is shown how to Dante as he fell there appeared Virgil, or his shade.

*And there was present a certain one*: namely Virgil, who through long silence was unknown—namely, because it was a long time since he had died, or it was a long time since anyone had studied Virgil's book, or because few used right reason, figured by Virgil. *Fioco*: that is, dim. *While in that desert*: that is, in the matter at hand.

23 He answered me: Not a man, once I was a man,\
&emsp;And my parents were Lombards,\
&emsp;Both Mantuans by native land.

24 I was born under Julius, albeit late,\
&emsp;And I lived at Rome under good Augustus,\
&emsp;In the time of the false and lying gods.

25 I was a poet, and I sang of that just\
&emsp;Son of Anchises, who came from Troy,\
&emsp;After proud Ilium was burned,

26 But you, why do you return to so great a hurt?\
&emsp;Why do you not ascend the delectable mount,\
&emsp;Which is the beginning and cause of all joy?

27 Oh, are you then that Virgil, and that fount\
&emsp;Which pours forth so broad a river of eloquence?\
&emsp;I answered him with bashful brow.

A separated soul has no organs except in the manner that will be treated in *Purgatorio*, and as was touched upon in the sixth preamble. He confesses that he is now not a man, though once he was. And to make himself better known to Dante, as to his people he says that he is from Lombardy, which is in Italy and was formerly called Cisalpine Gaul; as to his native place he says he is from Mantua—yet he was not born in the city, but in a village near the city, named Pietole, as was said in one of the preambles. He also makes himself known by the time in which he was born, namely under Julius Cæsar, Gaius Julius the dictator, who had so great a struggle with the consul Pompey.

*And would that later*: a thing is called late when what is desired is not obtained. Now the text is very difficult; and the author's meaning is that he would have wished to be born later, namely at such a time when he could have been a Christian, so that he might have been saved. In regard to which note that Cæsar, from the time he prevailed against Pompey and held all the Roman offices—on which account it was said, *Cæsar was everything*—Cæsar himself lived only three and a half years. But after him reigned Octavian Cæsar Augustus, and he ruled fifty-seven years; in the forty-second year of whose reign Christ was born. When Octavian died, Christ was fifteen years old. Tiberius succeeded Octavian, in the fifteenth year of whose reign Christ began to preach, and in the eighteenth year of that same Tiberius's reign Christ was crucified. Now, in the last year of Octavian's reign, when Christ was fourteen years old, Virgil died; who, had he lived until the proclamation of Christ's sect, might perhaps have been saved. At that time, while Virgil lived and died, false gods were worshiped. He lived under Augustus, in whose praise he composed the book of the *Æneid*, as was said in the third preamble.

The text says: *Born under Julius, albeit late*: here *late* is taken as "not at a good hour." A man, when he does not have what he wishes, seems to have been too late. And thus it is taken here, that Virgil would have wished to exist in the time of Christ, because he would have been saved. Yet the text does not seem very clear. When he says *late*, it seems the text would stand better if he said *I was born too soon*. Be that as it may, it must be said that *late* is here taken as "not at a good hour"; otherwise the sense would not be sound.

*I was a poet*: he declares himself by his profession, asserting that he was a poet. *And I sang of that just son of Anchises*, that is, of Æneas, concerning whom, as has often been said, he sang and composed the book of the *Æneid*. Yet note that when [he says] Æneas was just, he follows the style of Virgil, who describes him as having been just. And this he did in honor of Octavian, who descended from his blood. For if Virgil had described Æneas as a traitor, as in fact he was, he would not have commended Octavian by his lineage, saying that he descended from a traitor. Anchises was cousin to Priam, king of Troy: Æneas stood in the third degree with Priam, who came from Troy. This was stated in the third preamble.

*But why do you return*? Now Virgil, since he had revealed to Dante who he was, whence he was, and what his work had been, said to Dante: Why are you returning to the valley—that is, to the vicious way—to so great loss and displeasure? Because the way of vices is displeasing and ruinous. Why do you not ascend into the delectable mount? Because the virtues give delight, nay, felicity, and lead to beatitude; therefore they are the beginning and cause of all joy, etc.

*Oh, are you that Virgil*? Now Dante begins to recognize directing reason itself, signified by Virgil. He says that Virgil is the fount of eloquence; for no one made so fine a poetic book in Italy as Virgil, who in his book showed that he could describe as well as any Italian or Latin could do. Virgil poured forth a broad river of eloquence.

28 O honor and light of the other poets,\
&emsp;May the long study and the great love avail me,\
&emsp;Which made me search through your volume.

29 You are my master and my author,\
&emsp;You alone are he from whom I took\
&emsp;The fair style that has done me honor.

30 Behold the beast because of which I turned back:\

*With bashful brow*: as if Dante were saying: You were so excellent that I am ashamed to stand near you, just as a lowly man ought to be ashamed to stand near an excellent man.

*Honor and light of all other poets*: with praise Dante seeks the goodwill of Virgil. *May the long study avail and profit me*: with which I studied your book.

*You are my master and my author*: the literal sense is plain. *You are he from whom I received the fair style*: that is, from your sixth book of the *Æneid*, in which you treated of Hell. Understand implicitly: And you, Virgil, also had it from Homer, who in ancient times described Hell when treating of Ulysses.

*Which has honored me*: because Dante was held in high esteem, since he knew Virgil's books well, from which he afterward made his own work.

*Look upon the beast on whose account I drew back and turned around*: that is, the she-wolf. *Deliver me from her*: behold how Dante entreats Virgil, that is, reason, to deliver him from the beast, that is, from avarice; which avarice persuades a man to steal, to practice usury and lend at interest, to seize others' goods, to sell sacred things and benefices, and to commit simony. Reason persuades the opposite, because reason is famous and wise.

*You must hold another course*. *A te convene altro viaggio tenere*: this is the fourth part of this canto, in which Virgil—that is, reason—now answers Dante: *Another course you*, etc. As if saying: You are returning to the valley, that is, to the vicious way; this path is not good for escaping the beast;

but you must go up the mountain, that is, by the virtuous way, if you desire to be delivered; etc.

*For that beast*: that is, avarice, on whose account—that is, from which—you wish to be delivered, suffers no one to pass along her way. The way of men avails and ought to be virtuous; yet avarice permits no one to pass along such a way, but hinders him; nay, sometimes she slays spiritually, and sometimes is also the cause of temporal death, as is evident in thieves and robbers.

*Whose nature is most wicked*: because she never satisfies her greedy appetite, but after taking food [has] more hunger than before. Behold the properties of the miser and of avarice, since the miser is never satisfied with money. Solomon: *For he who is avaricious and has ten gold pieces straightway desires twenty; then a hundred, then a thousand; if he has two estates, he desires five*; and so of each thing; so that the hunger of avarice, or the thirst of the miser, is never sated.

*Many are the animals to which she*: supply the she-wolf, that is, avarice, *is wedded*, that is, becomes a wife. Here are the fine words of Virgil, that is, of reason. Avaricious men are called animals, that is, brute beasts, because they live thus without reason. To such men the she-wolf becomes a wife, because a wife is not put away or released except in death, since marriage is not dissolved except by death. Thus misers never abandon avarice except when they die and are carried off to Hell. *More there will yet be*: because daily men become avaricious, until the Greyhound [*veltrus*], that is, the dog, shall come, who will make her die with grief.

35 He shall not feed on earth nor on pewter,\
&emsp;But on wisdom, love, and virtue;\
&emsp;And his birth shall be between Feltro and Feltro.

(1) this one. — (2) will be.

*He shall not feed on earth nor pewter*: that is, this dog will eat neither gold, signified by earth—because from earth, altered by the sun's rays, gold is made, and other metals are made, as Aristotle holds in the fourth book of the *Meteorology*, and other philosophers—*nor pewter*: nor will he do other works of avarice, but this dog's food will solely be wisdom, love, and virtues.

*Whose nativity, or birth, is between Feltro and Feltro*: that is, between constellation and constellation. This passage is hard and difficult, to understand which several things must be noted.

First, it must be noted who this dog is that is called *velter*, or *veltrus*.

Although there are many species of dogs—such as those little white Spanish ones, which nobles gladly carry in their hands and keep on beds in their chambers; others are large dogs for hunting and catching boars and wolves; some are those that track quails and partridges and pheasants, and halt and wait for the net to be cast over them, and these in Italy are called *bracchi*. And I do not care here to discuss whether all dogs are of the same species or differ in species; at least they differ in size, quality, and usefulness. One species of dogs is that whose individuals, or specimens, in Florence and Tuscany are called *veltri* [greyhounds]. These especially are enemies of wolves and pursue them more than other dogs, par excellence.

Second, it must be noted concerning Feltro, what the author understands by Feltro. Whence it must be known that on this passage different people speak differently—namely, that this greyhound [*veltrus*] will be born between Feltro and Feltro—because in Italy there are two Feltros. One Feltro is in the region of Friuli, and it is a fair town and distinguished stronghold; the other Feltro is in Romagna, and it is a mountain called Monte Feltro, from which the counts who are lords of Urbino are called Counts of Montefeltro, or Feretro. And thus there is a bishopric of Montefeltro, or Feretro, because upon that mountain is a city called the city of San Leo. Hence those of this opinion say that this greyhound will be born between these two Feltros, namely between the region of Friuli and that part of Romagna. This opinion is ridiculous and vain, in my judgment.

Another opinion is that of certain persons who say that the author intends here to say that this dog, namely the greyhound, will be born of a low and plebeian stock; and therefore they say *between felt and felt*, because, according to those who think so, there is a kind of exceedingly cheap cloth, of which hats or caps are made; which cloth is made without weaving, from the shearings of cloths or from the vilest wool: just as that cloth [felt] is vile, so they say he will be born of a vile, low, and plebeian stock. This opinion is even more ridiculous than the first.

Others, however, say—and it is the third opinion—that *between Feltro and Feltro* is understood, that is, between heaven and heaven, that is, between constellation and constellation. And behold, it must be noted that Dante in this place follows the opinion of Albumasar. Many astrologers also commonly say thus, that diverse constellations exert influence in diverse ways, and are in diverse ways causes of the effects coming forth in these lower regions, beneath the sphere of actives and passives. By constellation I here call conjunctions of the planets; which conjunctions often come about variously with such an aspect toward the stars that are in the firmament. For just as there are diverse stars in heaven that produce diverse things upon earth—thus there are some that produce heat, such as the Dog Star; some produce cold, such as Saturn; some produce goodwill, friendship, and love, such as Venus; etc.—so afterward, when the planets are conjoined, they produce diverse effects, according as the conjunctions are diverse. Hence the ancient philosophers and poets posited the ages of gold, silver, iron, and bronze: the first under Saturn, the second under Jupiter, the third under Mars, the fourth under the Moon; so that one constellation produces benevolent, kindly, gentle men; another produces wicked, wrathful, unruly, drunken men. Now the author wishes to say that just as an evil constellation reigned for a long time—that is, one that exerted an evil influence and produced avaricious, unruly men—so there will come a constellation producing kindly and friendly men; etc. Which constellation will produce a lord, whom he sometimes calls a *dux* [leader], as in the last canto of *Purgatorio*; sometimes he calls him *fortune* and *fruit*, as in the twenty-seventh of *Paradiso*; sometimes the *Greyhound*, as here. And in this regard many poets posit the ages of gold, silver, bronze, and iron, as Ovid in the first book of the *Metamorphoses*; etc.

Here the author wishes to say: I said that the she-wolf, that is, avarice, will be married to many, that is, will become the wife of many—that is, of avaricious men, who love only earthly things, and especially of clerics and prelates of the Church, simoniacs, who sell sacred things—until the greyhound comes, who is exceedingly hostile to wolves and who signifies a good ruler. Whom, as I believe, Dante thinks to be a good Supreme Pontiff, because he will despise temporal dominions and riches; and he will not be simoniacal, nay, he will put all simoniacs to flight; he will hate avarice; he will promote to the cardinalate none but good and virtuous men; and likewise to bishoprics and other dignities and prelacies. Or perhaps it will be an Emperor or a Duke. It matters not. This Supreme Pontiff will slay this she-wolf, that is, avarice, and will pursue her from town to town, that is, from miser to miser, until he has driven her back into Hell.

*Whence envy first drew her forth*: here it must be noted that the author in this place follows Sacred Scripture at the end of the second chapter of the Book of Wisdom, where it is thus written: *But by the envy of the devil, death entered into the world; and they imitate him who are on his side*. The devil, seeing himself cast out of heaven and damned in Hell, and foreseeing that the human race was to be transported into heaven and exalted to the beatitude from which he himself, the devil, had been cast out, moved by envy came into the earthly Paradise, and there tempted our first parents with the sin of gluttony, avarice, and pride, as appears in the second chapter of Genesis; and thus he induced Eve and afterward Adam to sin, to eat of the forbidden tree, etc.; and thus the human race sinned, and was expelled from the earthly Paradise into this wretched world: and behold that, because of the sin of our first parents, death entered into the world; and behold avarice ruling over almost the whole world. This avarice this Greyhound, according to the author's opinion, will drive back to Hell, whence envy first drew her forth.

(1) low-lying in situation.

*He shall pursue her from town to town*, and slay her; etc. Let it be said here that the author speaks in the ordinary manner. For thus a judge pursues a robber; afterward, if he can capture him and captures him, he has him hanged, and thus slays him. Afterward the robber's soul is shut up in Hell. Sometimes the author uses such a manner of speaking here. Now let the literal sense be understood thus. *The beast, on whose account*, etc.: that is, avarice. She is wedded to many animals, that is, to avaricious clerics, pontiffs, cardinals, bishops, emperors, kings, dukes, counts, princes, and others who love avarice; and to more she will yet be wedded, until the Greyhound [*veltrus*] comes—that is, the dog who is the enemy of wolves, and especially of this she-wolf, that is, avarice; that is, that Pope, Supreme Pontiff, or Duke, or Emperor, who will pursue the she-wolf from town—that is, from man—to town, that is, to man, and will slay her with grief; because the avaricious will grieve when they see themselves scattered, or deposed from their stations and dignities.

*Him*—namely the Greyhound—*earth shall not feed*: that is, gold, nor any worldly treasure, nor pewter, nor other riches or vices; rather his food will be wisdom, divine love, and the theological and moral virtues. Whose birth, or nativity, is between Feltro and Feltro, that is, between heaven and heaven, that is, between constellation and constellation. And just as one evil constellation infuses avarice and other crimes into lords and men, so a good constellation will give a good influence and produce a good pontiff, or a wise, loving, virtuous lord who hates all avarice and all vices; and he will depose the simoniacal and vicious; he will pursue them from place to place; until the world becomes good, *and crooked ways shall be made straight and rough ways smooth*, and the Saturnian reign, the golden age, will return: this is the new offspring sent down from heaven—that is, from the heavens and constellations—that is, by God and the clemency of our Lord Jesus Christ.

*He is the salvation of humble Italy, for whom Camilla, Euryalus, and Nisus died of their wounds*: here it must be noted why he calls Italy *humble*. Why does he say *humble*? Some say that here the author speaks ironically, and by antiphrasis, that is, by contraries, because in Italy there is a very proud people—that is, Italians are very proud. Others say that the author, calling Italy humble, says this because Italy is greatly humbled under tyrants, subjugated and trampled underfoot: this second opinion was that of Dante's son, which I do not believe to have been Dante's, although it was the opinion of his son.

Some say *humble Italy*: *humble*, that is, fertile, mild, tangible, abundant; because there is not, as regards all things, a more favored homeland in the world than Italy itself. For thus Virgil calls it in the third book of the *Æneid*, where it is said: *We see humble Italy*. For her died Camilla—a proper name—Euryalus and Nisus, these last two being proper names of men. Note that in the Marittima there is now a city called Piperno, which in ancient times was called Privernum [text: *Primavernum*], which is near the kingdom of Apulia, in which there was a king and there reigned a certain king named Metabus, who because of his cruelties and bad rule was driven out of his kingdom. Fleeing, he took nothing with him except a little daughter of his, still sucking her mother's breasts, whom he carried with him into the woods, not trusting to dwell elsewhere; which daughter he nourished with the milk of wild beasts in the woods: growing up, she began to ride, to use bows and arrows, to hunt, and to catch stags and boars and other wild beasts of the forest. At length she wished to remain a virgin always, even against her father's will. When she was eighteen or twenty years old, she began to wield the lance and gird herself with the sword, and became an armed and daring warrior-maid. She came to the aid of Turnus in the war waged between Turnus and the Trojans; and in battle she was slain by Arruns, a companion of Æneas, as appears in the tenth book of the *Æneid*. Turnus was king of the Rutulians, son of Daunus. He was born in the city of Ardea, which still exists, though small and almost ruined: and it is called Ardea, in the maritime region of Rome, toward the east, distant from Rome perhaps twenty-five Lombard miles. Where it must be noted that at the time when Æneas landed at the mouths of the Tiber, straightway inland, two miles from the sea, he built a wooden fortress after the manner of a stockade, and there left his son Julius Ascanius, born to him of his wife Creusa, daughter of Priam king of Troy, whom he had brought with him across the sea with a great people. Æneas himself, coming with a galley up the Tiber river, landed at a little hill which is now within the walls of Rome and is called Mount Aventine. Upon that hill was a small city in which King Evander reigned, having a son named Pallas, most fair in stature and valiant in arms. While Æneas stayed for two or three days with King Evander, Turnus, son of Daunus and king of Ardea and the Rutulians, besieged the fortress built by Æneas, as was said before. While Julius Ascanius with his companions was thus besieged, he wished to make this known to his father, who was absent thence because he was on Mount Aventine. Those two, Euryalus and Nisus, Trojans, companions of Julius Ascanius, offered to go out by night from that fortress—which was built where Ostia now is, a small city sixteen miles from Rome toward the sea—to announce the siege to Æneas, etc. Going out by night well armed—and they were strong, agile youths—they slew many of the sleeping enemies. At length they too were slain, nor could they go to bring the news to Æneas. Further, it must be noted that at the time when Æneas, coming from Troy, landed at the mouths of the Tiber, nearby was a small city called Laurentum, on account of a laurel, a most beautiful tree, standing in that same city. Here, in this city, there reigned and was king a noble man named Latinus, son of Faunus, grandson of Picus, who had a queen as wife, named Amata, by whom he had begotten only one daughter, named Lavinia, already of marriageable years, that is, fifteen years old; and she was of age to be married. Whom, Lavinia, her mother Queen Amata had promised to give in marriage to King Turnus, king of Ardea; whereas her father—namely Lavinia's—King Latinus wished to give her in marriage to Æneas, as afterward came to pass. On account of which there was a great war and there were many battles between Æneas and his companions on the one side, and the king of the Rutulians and the city of Ardea on the other. Æneas slew Turnus in battle, as appears at the end of the twelfth book of the *Æneid*. Now to Dante's text: behold that for Italy there died of wounds Camilla, Euryalus, Nisus, and Turnus.

*Onde io per lo to meglio*. *Wherefore I for your better good*: in this part Virgil counsels Dante, speaking thus: I think for your good, nay, for your better good I discern—that is, I well know—that you should follow me, and I will be your guide and direct you and lead you forth from here: that is, I will deliver you and conduct you through the eternal place, through Hell, which is an eternal place; that is, in which the damned are tormented eternally.

In which place you will hear desperate cries and without any hope of remedies; and in which place you will see ancient spirits—that is, both diabolical spirits and also the spirits of ancient men who died before the Flood and before Abraham—sorrowing, because they grieve in torments, who always cry out for the second death: that is, because they would wish to die and not to have being. Here the author seems to hold the opinion of Jerome, who says that damned spirits wish not to be—that is, that those who are in eternal torments would wish not to be; the opposite of which, however, blessed Augustine holds and held. It may also be said that the first death is the death of the body; the second death is to be in Hell. Afterward he says to Dante:

*You shall see those who are content in the fire*: and he sets forth the reason why they are content; because they are in Purgatory and hope one day to depart from the torments of Purgatory and go to the place of the blessed, that is, into Paradise. From which it appears that the damned would simply wish not to be, and that those who are in Hell desire the second death; yet they will never die, but will be tormented eternally. Whereas those who are in Purgatory rejoice because of the sure hope they have of departing thence and ascending to eternal life. Behold that Virgil offers himself to Dante to conduct him through Hell and Purgatory up to the blessed people.

*To whom if you thereafter wish to ascend*. *Alle quale poye*: a soul worthier than I must guide you. Virgil, that is, natural reason, could guide Dante through Hell and Purgatory; but into Paradise he cannot; because natural reason cannot of itself and by itself sufficiently teach all things necessary for salvation. Whereas the soul worthier than reason, that is, Sacred Theology, teaches all things necessary for salvation: with her, that is, the soul worthier than I, I will leave you at my departure.

And Virgil assigns the reason why he will leave Dante: because he himself cannot enter into Paradise. And the reason is because Virgil himself was rebellious to the divine law; that is, he did not have the faith necessary for salvation. And therefore the Emperor reigning in Paradise wills not to allow Virgil himself to enter into Paradise, nor wills that he see it. That Emperor commands everywhere and here rules: this is Christ God, who rules all things; in His city, that is, in the empyrean heaven, is His throne; and every elect one to that place is happy.

# The Divine Comedy

The passionate return of scholars to Dante's *Divine Comedy*, and especially the publication or reproduction of the ancient Commentaries with which from the very beginning it was elucidated among us, is for us a happy omen of a not distant return of minds to the integrity of the science and art that created that sublime marvel, whereby Italy had and will always have the primacy among all nations. Such a return cannot be arrested or corrupted by certain deviations brought among us from abroad, which precisely through those publications will necessarily come to an end. The conception of the great Poem could not have been intuited anywhere except in Italy; and those commentaries that follow one another from Dante's son down to Landino, and from Landino to Lombardi, are its true philosophical explanation.

Dante's *Divine Comedy* is the vision in poetic forms of the world to come, which will be the finality of the present world, in a supernatural transformation that there eternalizes the work of God. And therefore of necessity it embraces both the one and the other: the one, as under man's free will it unfolds in space and time; the other, according to the supreme fulfillment that the eternal divine designs will have therein.

The *Divine Comedy*, therefore, is a drama: the drama of humanity created by God, which along the path assigned to it by Him advances toward its future and immortal destinies. The three worlds visited by the Poet in vision are the whole world here below, seen in its naked truth (for thus we do not see it now), seen outside the dusty whirlwind that obscures it here, seen not in its fading leaves, but in the eternal fruit of our actions. There one sees what the life of man is here below, and we learn that it is a sacrifice, in which the corporeal is consumed and the spirit appears in its reality. And in the spirit of each man we discern the two progenitors of our kind, and the first two brothers, and Christ; that is, the fall, the punishment, the resurrection. This, and nothing else, is the essence of Dante's divine Poem; to restrict it, as has been done for a century past, to a representation of one or another of our national vicissitudes, and to a final conception that does not go beyond these limits, is not only to impoverish it, but to turn it into ridicule and annihilate it.

And by this alone does the immense allegory of the Poem appear. First, the drama of the life of humanity is shown to us in Dante, who, having fallen into a low place through his faults, needs, in order to draw back from it, to gaze upon the horrors of Hell; having drawn back from it, he needs to be purified from them through the expiation accomplished in Purgatory; and thence finally he ascends to taste of the resurrection and transformation that awaits us in heaven. His vicissitudes, in short, are those of the entire human race, and the destiny of all created things, and the great mystery of the God who humbles Himself down to man, and expiates human faults and regains the splendor of innate glory.

But what the individual man is, nations likewise are, and humanity entire: and therefore after him, Italy, in the conditions in which she then found herself, was the protagonist of his drama; Italy as she was in those days, as she ought to have been, and as it was to be hoped that, coming to her senses, she might become, so as to show herself worthy of herself and of the high destiny to which she had been ordained by heaven.

After Italy, the protagonist of the great drama was humanity entire, alas from the very beginning the victim of seduction and wicked passions; and at the same time any nation whatever, whenever it happens to go astray. For the coming to their senses of which, the sole remedy is the contemplation of Hell—that is, of the ruins into which, by going astray, they fall headlong, and which without coming to their senses will increase and endure for eternity. Whence the necessity of coming to their senses and of expiating the evil committed, in order to resume the path that leads them to their end.

And therewith the Church herself became a protagonist in the great Dantesque Poem—we mean humanity destined to a supernatural end, for which it must prepare itself upon this earth. Which Church began with our first parents Adam and Eve in the earthly Paradise, and in them first transgressed; transgressed, yet in Christ had even from then, and fully afterward in His descent upon this earth, the expiation that saved her; and in Him she will have it until the end of the ages; after which expiation she will solemnly and fully triumph in heaven.

And therefore it is that Dante, a great Christian philosopher, embraces in his drama all that there has been of good and of evil in all humanity, from the beginning of the world down to himself; assigning to the good and to the evil wrought by man and by nations their own place in the eternal destination, according to the gradations in which the one and the other unfolded in time and space; nor would it be otherwise for the remaining generations that would succeed until the last of days. This is the true conception of the divine Poem, whence derive its greatness, its perennial youth and novelty, its universality, its truth, which can never grow old or fade away. And this conception was seen in it and expounded by all who undertook to comment upon it, having the same faith as Dante. To substitute others for it is a pitiable aberration; it is the annihilation of the immortal Poem.
