# Notes

## On Canto I

1\. In the year 1300 the poet was thirty-five years old, that is, about the middle of seventy, the commonly supposed span of human life. The time of the year and of the day is specified in Canto XXI, 112.

17\. Of the sun.

30\. One who walks on level ground keeps the foot on which he rests lower. We do the opposite in ascending; for we bring higher the foot on which we rest. From this it is inferred that Dante was still on a plain, the ascent toward the hill beginning in the following line.

38\. That is, with the constellation of the Ram, the zodiacal sign of the month of April or of Spring, when, according to an ancient opinion, the motion of the stars began at the Creation. And the day was Good Friday.

45\. The three beasts, probably suggested to the poet by Jeremiah,[^269-1] correspond, according to some interpreters, to the three divisions of sins defined in the eleventh canto, namely the sins of Incontinence, Malice or envy, and Bestiality or pride. Others, interpreting the allegory politically, think that the light, swift, and dappled leopard represents Florence and the continual changes and factions within her; the lion represents France intriguing with the Papal Court for the election of Charles of Valois (de Valois) as Emperor and the establishment of French influence in Italy; and the wolf represents Rome or the party of the Black Guelf (in German *Wolf*, wolf). Some commentators maintain that the leopard morally signifies the allurements of the senses, that is, carnal pleasures; the lion is the emblem of ambition, which in the course of worldly wanderings troubles life with its impetuous desires; and the she-wolf depicts greed and avarice, which the poet loathes above every other passion, as having been the cause of the slanders against him and of his condemnation, and chiefly of the seditious and wretched dissensions of those times.

[^269-1]: “Therefore a lion out of the wood smote them, and a wolf destroyed them even unto their houses, and a leopard watched over their houses.” (Jer. V, 6.)

<!-- p. 270 (290) -->

60\. The poet means not the physical, but the mystical sun, that is, illuminating, virtuous felicity; for with the poet light is a symbol of felicity, and darkness of misery. He calls it silent metaphorically for not shining forth, as if he were saying: «the she-wolf was driving me out from the fair hopes of the ascent up the hill toward the former valley of desolation.» Using a similar metaphor, the poet said elsewhere: ‘in loco d'ogni luce muto’ (V, 28), that is, into a speechless, mute place, instead of devoid, deprived of light. Of a like kind would be the metaphor in Sophocles in his use of the word τυφλός (“blind”) instead of devoid, deprived: “Thou art blind in ears and mind and eyes” (Oed. Tyr. 371), and “in his art he was born blind” (ibid. 389). Perhaps Dante imitated in his use of the word *tacere* (to be silent) Virgil, who said: ‘loca tacentia’ (Aen. VI, 265), which words our Eugenius Voulgaris translated “ἄναυδοι χῶροι” (“voiceless regions”), just as the English poet Milton, imitating Dante, represents the sun as silent, saying:\
“The sun to me is dark,\
And silent as the moon\
When she deserts the night\
Hid in her vacant interlunar cave.” (S. Ag.)

<!-- p. 271 (291) -->

63\. Virgil is represented as long silent and seeming speechless, inasmuch as the study of his poems had been neglected throughout the long period of the Middle Ages.

101\. By the Wolf-hunter the poet means the future saviour of Italy. According to some interpreters, he alludes to Can Grande della Scala, that is, Great Dog of the Scala, who was called among the Italians *Catulus*, that is, whelp of Verona. He was a great leader of the Ghibellines and, by virtue of his name, the natural antagonist of the wolf in Rome. He was also one of Dante's foremost friends in exile.

<!-- p. 272 (292) -->

105\. Namely, between the city of Feltro in the province of Belluno and Mount Feltro (Monte Feltro) in Romagna, called Flaminia among the Latins.

106\. Italy, whose deliverance is foretold here, the poet calls from a political point of view *umile Italia*, humbled Italy, in imitation of Virgil, who, making mention of the view of Lower Italy (Bassa Italia) from the sea, used, though in a physical sense, the same epithet *humilis*, low-lying, flat, saying, . . . . *Humilem videmus Italiam*. (Aen. III, 522.)

122\. Beatrice, the immortalized soul of the daughter of Folco Portinari, the object of Dante's youthful and enduring love. In Dante's poems Beatrice represents divine and eternal philosophy, while Virgil, who poetically narrated the founding of the Roman empire, represents human and political philosophy and literature.

124\. Emperor, God.

## On Canto VI

13\. In this description of Cerberus, Dante in some measure imitates Virgil (Aen. VI, 19 ff.).

52\. The word Ciacco (Ciaco) means “pig” in Italian. It is also a shortened form of James (Iacopo), which was perhaps the Christian name of this Florentine. No such family name has been preserved in Italy; mention is made, however, of a certain Ciacco in one of the stories of the Decameron (IX, 8), as a jesting but insignificant man, greatly given to gluttony and making it his chief business to procure invitations to the banquets of the rich.

64\. In 1300 a feud broke out in Pistoia between two branches of the house of the Cancellieri, one of which was called White from the name of a certain ancestress called Bianca, and the other Black to distinguish it from the first. This feud divided the entire city and spread to other districts as well; but the dissension developed chiefly in Florence, which was then an entirely Guelf city. The ancient house of the Donati, having Corso as its leader, embraced the faction of the Blacks, whereas the Cerchi, a new family recently settled in Florence and sprung from certain mountainous rural districts, were leaders of the Whites, whence the rural faction was named the rustic party (*parte selvaggia*). In February of 1301 the leaders of both factions were banished, Dante being then one of the Priors or Magistrates of the city. But the Whites did not cease working for their return, and in July of the same year they succeeded in banishing the Blacks remaining in Florence. Then Charles of Valois, brother of Philip the Fair (Philippe le Bel) of France, was summoned by Pope Boniface VIII, whose aid the Blacks had previously besought, to undertake the office of peacemaker. He began by making many fair promises to both parties; but after no long time, having led an army into the city, he permitted the Blacks to enter Florence and to plunder and demolish the houses of the Whites, most of whom, including Dante himself, who was then absent as ambassador in Rome, were banished in April of 1302, that is, within three years from the prophecy which the poet placed in the mouth of Ciacco. In the end, the lasting supremacy of the Blacks was established in Florence.

80\. Of Arrigo no mention is made elsewhere. Concerning the others, see X, 32; XVI, 41 and 44; XXVIII, 106.

106\. Namely to the Aristotelian philosophy, to which the poet appeals, alluding perhaps, though not, in my opinion, very happily, to what the Greek philosopher said concerning that which is perfect in virtue and in vice in Book IV, Ch. XVI of the Metaphysics. Note also that Saint Augustine said: “Cum fiet resurrectio carnis, et bonorum gaudium majus erit, et malorum tormenta majora.”

## On Canto VII

1\. None of the interpretations by which many have tried to explain these cries of the Demon is easily acceptable; perhaps simpler is the following: Papai Satan, papai Satan! (exclamatorily) my Alpha! (in Hebrew Aleph), that is, Ruler, by analogy with the first letter of the alphabet. Note that the poet, confusing Pluto (Πλούτων), as other non-Greek writers also do, with Plutus (Πλοῦτος), represents him as presiding over the torments of the avaricious and prodigal, just as Cerberus over those of the gluttons. It is also worthy of note that, as among the Greeks there is a similarity between the names of the two gods Pluto and Plutus, so too among the Latins Dis is the name both of the god of Hell and of the god of wealth.

11\. He alludes to the war in heaven mentioned in the Apocalypse and the victory of Michael over the Dragon.

<!-- p. 280 (300) -->

30\. Why do you hold? (*perchè tieni?*) thus speak the prodigals to the avaricious; why do you cast away? (*perchè burli?*) thus the avaricious to the prodigals. That is, why do you hoard silver, stint money? and why do you scatter silver, squander your substance? Speaking thus, they reproach one another with the cause of their condemnation.

68\. This description of Fortune rivals that of Horace (Odes I, 35).

74\. According to Dante (Par., Canto XXVIII), the Creator gave to the first creatures, that is, to the Angels, the charge of directing the revolutions of the celestial spheres. The orders of these Angels correspond in number to the nine movable spheres of Ptolemaic and Scholastic Astronomy. Fortune is likened here to these angelic Powers; yet her province is human affairs.

106\. A marsh is the Styx called also in Virgil, who says, *Stygiam que paludem* (Aen. VI, 325).

123\. In the preceding circles we saw the punishments of the sins of Lust, Gluttony, and Avarice. In this one are punished those of Anger and Sloth. I have used the word Ἀκηδεία (sloth) for the Italian *Accidia*, from which, in my opinion, is derived the poet's adjective *accidioso* modifying the word *fummo*; wherefore I have translated these two words as “καπνὸν ἀκηδείας” (“smoke of sloth”). Note also that the Italian word *accidia* is hard to render in the other European languages. Perhaps it is worth noting as a curiosity that this Dantean line might be a prophetic condemnation of those who nowadays out of idleness smoke and swallow the smoke of tobacco.

<!-- p. 281 (301) -->

129\. Suffocation in mire was among the ancient Germans one of the principal punishments for cowardice and sloth (Tacit. *Germania*, Ch. 12).

## On Canto IX

23\. Erichtho, a Thessalian sorceress, of whom mention is made in Ovid (Her. XV, 139) and in Lucan, who (Phars. VI, 505 ff.) makes her bring up the shade of a dead man to prophesy to Sextus Pompeius the outcome of the war. That Virgil was sent into Hell by Erichtho is generally regarded as an invention of Dante, since Virgil wrote nothing about a descent of his own into Hell.

44\. Persephone was attended by the Erinyes.

52\. Medusa, one of the Gorgons, whose glances had the power of turning men into stone; she is summoned here to avenge upon Dante the audacity of Theseus, who went down to Hell with Pirithous to carry off Persephone, both of them having been rescued by Hercules when in his twelfth labour he brought Cerberus up to earth, dragging him chained by the neck. To this circumstance the poet alludes when he says below (l. 99) that Cerberus had his neck and chin stripped of hair.

68\. From this line it is inferred that Dante knew the cause of the winds, arising from the tendency toward equilibrium between the heat and cold of the various climes and regions opposed to one another. This contrary struggle of the winds is thus described by Aeschylus:\
“And the blasts of all the winds leap one against another,\
Displaying their counter-blowing strife.” (Prom. 1086-1087.)

<!-- p. 284 (304) -->

99\. See above, note on line 52.

112\. Arles (in Strabo and certain Latin writers *Arelate*, instead of Ptolemy's *Arelatai*), a marshy region beside the Rhone. In it there is an ancient burial ground called the Elysian Field (*Campus Elysius*, now *Eliscamp*), in which there are many gravestones both of pagans and of Christians. Pola, a city in Istria, near the gulf called *Quarnaro* by the poet, now *Quarnero*, anciently that of Flanona.

130\. Here like lies together with like, because each tomb contains followers of the same heresy.

133\. Between the walls and the *martyria*. The word “μαρτύρια” (“martyria”), in the Poet *martiri*, in Latin *martyria*, means here not tortures, as almost all interpreters, both Italian and non-Italian, have supposed, but, in ecclesiastical usage, the tombs and cemeteries of the heretics, in which they buried their prominent men, calling these cemeteries of their co-religionists *martyria* (μαρτύρια). See Canon IX of the Council of Laodicea, and the notes of Zonaras, Balsamon, and Aristenus.

## On Canto XI

8\. One of the Popes of this name (probably Anastasius II) was erroneously believed in the poet's time to have died struck by lightning, as having embraced the heresy of Photinus, Deacon of Thessalonica, who lived in the fourth century and wrote against the divinity of Jesus Christ. The chronicler Martin of Poland (Frère Martin de Pologne), and after him Dante, confused the Pope with the Emperor of the same name.

<!-- p. 287 (307) -->

50\. From the city of Cahors (anciently *Cadurcum*), in Aquitaine (Guyenne), were named the so-called Caursines (*Caursini*), that is, Christian usurers of the thirteenth century, whose rapacious profiteering and extortions were worse than those of any other peoples of that time.

80\. Arist. *Nic. Eth.* VII, 1: “Of things to be avoided in respect of character there are three kinds: malice, incontinence, bestiality.”

90\. The first circle is the Forecourt (Limbo), that is, the dwelling of the virtuous pagans. In circles II, III, IV, and V are punished the Christian deadly sins of carnal Lust, Gluttony, Avarice and its opposite, Prodigality, Anger, and Sloth, referred to the Aristotelian head of Incontinence. In the sixth circle lie the heretics and Epicureans. In the seventh, the violent, divided into three classes—those violent against others, against themselves, and against God—this last class being subdivided into those who offend against God in His own person, in nature, and in art. In the eighth circle, namely Evil-pit (Malebolge), lie the fraudulent, divided into ten classes: 1. seducers of women; 2. flatterers; 3. simoniacs; 4. sorcerers; 5. swindlers; 6. hypocrites; 7. thieves; 8. evil counselors; 9. authors of political divisions; 10. falsifiers, among whom are included alchemists, impostors (those who fraudulently assume false names or wickedly pass under another's name), counterfeiters, and liars. All these are referred to the Aristotelian head of Malice, corresponding to the deadly sin of Envy. The ninth circle contains the traitors, divided into four classes—namely against kinsmen, country, friends, and benefactors—and referred to the Aristotelian head of Bestiality, corresponding to Pride, the seventh and last of the seven deadly sins.

31\. Out of respect for the vast Muslim peoples, many millions of whom live peacefully together with Christian fellow citizens in the Ottoman Empire, I judged it less unseemly, in place of Mahomet—whom the Poet quite improperly subjected to shameful torments in the enclosure of the schismatics—to substitute Arius, the great arch-schismatic, who, being a Christian and a presbyter priest, brought upon the Christian peoples, through his doctrine against the consubstantial divinity of Christ, that ruinous schism which tore the Church apart for many years, though it died out many centuries ago. On the contrary, Mahomet was not the author of a schism, since he was not a Christian; rather, he was the lawgiver of the Arabs, who abolished polytheism among them and introduced the worship of one sole God. Dante might be pardoned as having written his poems in the midst of Christians and at the beginning of the fourteenth century, when religious fanaticism still flourished in Western Europe—not in the present nineteenth century of religious toleration and enlightened civilization. In support of this opinion of mine, I cite here that of the learned Frenchman Littré, who declares in his translation of Dante's *Inferno* as follows: “Dante a soustrait le célèbre Sultan Saladin à toute peine, et l'a dignement placé à côté des sages et des grands hommes de l'antiquité Païenne (Voyez ci-dessus, Chant IV, v 129). Il eût été digne de sa haute intelligence de penser semblablement de Mahomet, et de ne pas nous le faire voir traité comme les vulgaires semeurs de scandale et de schisme. Ce n'est pas un schisme dont Mahomet fut auteur, c'est une religion. Il prêcha l'unité de Dieu au milieu de populations profondément polythéistes, et réussit à les transformer; mais le préjugé qui, dans le moyen âge, régnait en Occident contre Mahom et son prétendu paganisme prévalut, et le grand réformateur religieux de l'Arabie fut précipité dans la neuvième bouge. Il est curieux de remarquer que l'esprit théologique du moyen âge et l'esprit imparfaitement philosophique du XVIIIᵉ siècle furent également injustes envers Mahomet.”

67\. In the original, *Rafel mai amech zabi almi*. These words are without meaning and admit of no explanation, the Poet wishing to indicate the unintelligibility of Nimrod's speech as a result of the confusion of tongues at the daring building of the Tower of Babel, attributed to Nimrod and his followers. Abbate Lanci, for his part, asserts that these words of Nimrod are in the Arabic language and signify, “Exalt my glory in the Abyss, as it shone in the world.” After him, another Abbate, Giuseppe Venturi, maintains that Nimrod's words are in the Syriac language and signify: *Rafel*, O divine power; *mai*, why am I; *amech*, in this deep, or pit; *zabi*, stand back; *almi*, hide thyself. The verse has been rendered unchanged in Greek letters; and the initial alpha of the words *ἀμὲχ* (*amech*) and *ἄλμι* (*almi*) has been doubled to complete the twelve syllables of the verse, just as the Italians pronounce that same *a* as two syllables to complete the eleven syllables of the Italian verse.

## On Canto XXXIV

1\. The words used by the Poet in Latin at the beginning of this canto are the first verse, adapted, of a hymn of the Roman Church sung during Holy Week and on the Feast of the Crucifixion, which runs as follows: “Vexilla Regis prodeunt; Fulget Crucis mysterium.” that is, “The banners of the King advance; the mystery of the Cross shines forth.”

<!-- p. 329 (349) -->

20\. Dis, Lucifer. See note 68 on Canto VIII.

58\. The sinner in the mouth of the front face of Lucifer's head suffers more than the other two, because besides the biting he is also torn by the claws of the Demon, whose blows are far more painful than those of his teeth.

62\. Judas Iscariot the traitor, and Brutus and Cassius the murderers of Julius Caesar, were placed here as having committed the greatest treasons in the religious and political history of the world. It should be noted that the Cassius here in question was, according to the ancient historians, pale, thin, and slender of body; so it may be that Dante confused him with L. Cassius, whom Cicero (Catil. III, 6) calls adipem.

96\. *Ἡμιτριταῖος* (Half-tierce) means that the sun was midway between the first hour—that is, dawn—and the third according to the Catholic liturgy, namely an hour and a half later than the first hour, which is the twelfth hour after sunset, and consequently coincides at the equinox with sunrise, or 6 a.m. Hence it follows that the time indicated by the Poet is half past seven in the morning, reckoned according to the position of the sun as seen from the southern hemisphere, toward which Virgil and Dante, having already passed through the centre of the Earth, were making their way, heading toward the antipodes of Jerusalem.

110\. *Σημεῖον* (The point), that is, the centre of the Earth.

<!-- p. 330 (350) -->

114\. In Jerusalem, the place of the Crucifixion of Christ, supposed according to medieval geography to be the centre of the inhabited portion of the terrestrial globe—an opinion apparently resting upon the words of Ezekiel: “Thus saith the Lord: This is Jerusalem; in the midst of the nations have I set her, and the countries round about her.” (5:5.)

116\. Judecca is represented as a small sphere, being the innermost enclosure in Hell, one half of which is in the northern hemisphere and the other half in the southern.

117\. Judecca, so called from Judas Iscariot, as having betrayed Jesus. It is the last enclosure of the ninth circle.

126\. The mountain of Purgatory, which the Poet supposes to be visible above the sea in the southern hemisphere, and to consist of a portion of the terrestrial globe that lay at the centre before the fall of Lucifer and the hollowing out of Hell, which was contemporaneous with it.

127\. Of Beelzebub, that is, of Lucifer. Speaking from our hemisphere, the Poet says that down below—namely, in the southern hemisphere—there is a chasm (that which leads, namely, to its surface, where the mountain of Purgatory stands), which is as far distant from Lucifer, or Judecca, as the depth of Hell measures. And perhaps the stream mentioned in line 130 is Lethe, in which the souls of the penitent bathe as they ascend toward Purgatory.

139\. The whole duration of the Poet's sojourn in Hell may be divided into three periods. First, from the time of Dante's being found in the Wood until his entrance into Hell, on the evening of Good Friday. Second, from the time of his entrance into Hell until the time of his exit at the centre of the Earth, on the evening of the following day. Third, from the time of his exit from Hell until his arrival upon the surface of the Earth on the opposite side of the globe, on the morning of Easter Sunday.
