If I flame upon you in the heat of love
Beyond the measure that on earth is seen,
So that I overcome the power of your eyes,
Do not marvel; for this proceeds
From perfect sight, which, as it apprehends,
So toward the good apprehended moves its foot.
I see well how already there shines
In your intellect the eternal light,
Which, merely seen, ever kindles love;
And if some other thing seduces your love,
It is nothing but some vestige of that light,
Ill recognized, which there shines through.
You wish to know if with some other service,
For a vow unfulfilled, one can render so much
As to secure the soul against a claim.
Thus Beatrice began this canto;
And, like one who does not break off his speech,
She continued thus the holy discourse:
The greatest gift that God in his bounty
Made in creating, and to his goodness
Most conformed, and the one he most prizes,
Was the freedom of the will,
With which intelligent creatures,
All and only they, were and are endowed.
Now there will appear to you, if from this you reason,
The high worth of the vow, if it is so made
That God consents when you consent;
For, in sealing between God and man the pact,
A sacrifice is made of this treasure,
Such as I say, and is made by its own act.
What then can be rendered in compensation?
If you think to use well what you have offered,
With ill-gotten goods you wish to do good work.
You are by now certain of the greater point;
But because Holy Church in this grants dispensations,
Which seems contrary to the duty I have disclosed to you,
You must still sit a little at table,
Because the tough food you have taken
Still requires help for its digestion.
Open your mind to what I reveal to you,
And fix it there within; for it does not make knowledge,
Without retaining it, to have understood.
Two things are requisite to the essence
Of this sacrifice: one is that
Of which it is made, the other is the covenant.
This latter is never canceled
Unless kept, and it is of it
That so strictly one speaks above:
Therefore it was necessary for the Hebrews
Still to make the offering, even though some offering
Might be exchanged, as you should know.
The other, which is shown to you as the matter,
May indeed be such that one does no wrong
If for other matter it is exchanged.
But let no one shift the burden on his shoulder
At his own discretion, without the turning
Both of the white key and of the yellow;
And deem every exchange foolish
If the thing laid down in the thing taken up,
As four in six, is not contained.
Therefore whatever thing weighs so much
By its worth that it tips every balance
Cannot be paid for with any other outlay.
Let mortals not take the vow as a jest:
Be faithful, and in doing so not perverse,
As Jephthah was in his first offering;
For him it would have been more fitting to say: I did wrong;
Than, keeping it, to do worse: and just as foolish
You may find the great leader of the Greeks,
Whence Iphigenia wept for her fair face,
And made both foolish and wise weep for her,
Who heard tell of such a rite.
Be, Christians, slower to move,
Do not be like a feather in every wind,
And do not believe that every water washes you.
You have the New and the Old Testament,
And the shepherd of the Church who guides you;
Let this suffice you for your salvation.
If evil greed cries something else to you,
Be men, and not mad sheep,
So that the Jew among you may not laugh at you.
Do not do as the lamb that leaves the milk
Of its mother, and, simple and frisky,
Fights with itself for its own pleasure.
Thus Beatrice spoke to me, as I write;
Then she turned, all desire,
Toward that part where the world is most alive.
Her falling silent and changing countenance
Imposed silence on my eager mind,
Which already had a new question before it.
And as an arrow that its mark
Strikes before the string is still,
So we sped into the second realm.
There I saw my lady so glad,
As she entered the light of that heaven,
That the planet grew more radiant from it.
And if the star changed and smiled,
What did I become, who by my very nature
Am changeable in every way!
As in a fishpond that is still and clear
The fish draw near to what comes from outside
In such a way that they deem it their food;
So I saw more than a thousand splendors
Draw toward us; and in each was heard:
Behold one who will increase our loves.
And as each one came to us,
The shade was seen to be full of gladness
In its bright radiance that came forth from it.
Think, reader, if what here begins
Went no further, how you would feel
A painful want of further knowledge;
And for yourself you will see how, from these,
I longed to hear of their estate,
As soon as to my eyes they were manifest.
O you well born, to whom to see the thrones
Of eternal triumph grace grants,
Before your military service is left behind,
With the light that ranges through all heaven
We are kindled; and therefore, if you desire
From us to be enlightened, sate yourself at will.
Thus by one of those pious spirits
I was told; and by Beatrice: Speak, speak
With confidence, and believe them as gods.
I see well how you nestle
In your own light, and that you draw it from your eyes;
For they flash as you smile;
But I do not know who you are, nor why you have,
Worthy soul, the rank of the sphere
That veils itself from mortals with another's rays.
This I said, addressing the light
That first had spoken to me, whereupon it grew
Far more radiant than it was.
Like the sun, which hides its very self
Through excess of light, when the heat has eaten away
The tempering effects of the thick vapors,
Through greater gladness thus was hidden from me
Within its ray the holy figure:
And thus enclosed, enclosed, it answered me
In the manner that the following canto sings.
