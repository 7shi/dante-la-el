After Constantine turned the eagle
Against the course of heaven, which it had followed
Behind the ancient one who took Lavinia,
A hundred and a hundred years and more the bird of God
At the far end of Europe remained,
Near the mountains from which it first came forth;
And beneath the shadow of its sacred feathers
It governed the world there from hand to hand,
And, changing thus, into mine it came.
Caesar I was, and am Justinian,
Who, by the will of the first love that I feel,
From within the laws drew out the excessive and the vain:
And, before I was intent upon the work,
That there was one nature in Christ, no more,
I believed, and with such faith was content;
But the blessed Agapetus, who was
Supreme shepherd, to the pure faith
Directed me with his words.
I believed him, and what was in his faith
I now see clearly, just as you see
That every contradiction is false and true.
As soon as with the Church I moved my feet,
It pleased God through grace to inspire in me
The lofty work, and I gave myself wholly to it;
And to my Belisarius I entrusted the arms,
To whom the right hand of heaven was so joined
That it was a sign that I should rest.
Now here at the first question comes to its point
My answer: but its nature
Compels me to follow it with some addition;
So that you may see with how much reason
Each moves against the most sacred standard,
Both he who claims it for himself and he who opposes it.
See how much valor has made it worthy
Of reverence; and it began from the time
When Pallas died to give it a kingdom.
You know that it made its dwelling in Alba
For three hundred years and more, until the end
When three and three fought for it again.
You know what it did, from the wrong done to the Sabine women
To Lucretia's grief, under seven kings,
Conquering the neighboring peoples round about.
You know what it did, borne by the illustrious
Romans against Brennus, and against Pyrrhus,
And against the other princes and leagues:
Whence Torquatus and Quintius, who from his lock
Unkempt was named, and the Decii and the Fabii
Had the glory that I gladly anoint with myrrh.
It cast down the pride of the Arabs,
Who behind Hannibal crossed
The Alpine crags, O Po, from which you glide.
Beneath it as young men triumphed
Scipio and Pompey, and to that hill
Beneath which you were born it seemed bitter.
Then, near the time when all heaven willed
To bring the world back to its own serene manner,
Caesar by the will of Rome took it up:
And what it did from the Var as far as the Rhine,
The Isère saw and the Saône, and the Seine saw,
And every valley from which the Rhône is filled.
What it did after it came out of Ravenna
And leaped the Rubicon was of such swift flight
That neither tongue nor pen could follow it.
Toward Spain it turned the host,
Then toward Durazzo, and it struck Pharsalia
So that the hot Nile felt the pain of it.
Antandros and the Simois, from which it had set forth,
It saw again, and the place where Hector lies,
And ill for Ptolemy it then shook itself:
Thence it descended like lightning upon Juba;
Then it turned back to your west,
Where it heard the Pompeian trumpet.
And what it did with its next bearer,
Brutus with Cassius barks out in hell,
And Modena and Perugia grieved.
Sad Cleopatra still weeps for it,
Who, fleeing before it, from the serpent
Took death sudden and dark.
With him it sped as far as the Red Shore;
With him it set the world in such peace
That the shrine of Janus was shut.
But what the standard that makes me speak
Had done before, and afterward was to do,
Throughout the mortal realm that lies beneath it,
Becomes in appearance small and dim,
If in the hand of the third Caesar it is viewed
With clear eye and with pure affection;
For the living justice that inspires me
Granted it, in the hand of him of whom I speak,
The glory of exacting vengeance for its wrath.
Now here marvel at what I tell you in turn:
Afterward with Titus it sped to take vengeance
For the vengeance for the ancient sin.
And when the Lombard tooth bit
The holy Church, beneath its wings
Charlemagne, conquering, came to her aid.
By now you can judge of those men
Whom I accused above, and of their faults,
Which are the cause of all your ills.
One to the common standard the yellow lilies
Opposes, and the other claims it for a party,
So that it is hard to see who errs more.
Let the Ghibellines ply, let them ply their craft
Under another standard; for he ill follows that one
Always who parts justice from it;
And let not this new Charles strike it down
With his Guelphs, but let him fear the talons
That from a loftier lion once tore the hide.
Many times already the sons have wept
For the father's fault, and let him not believe
That God will exchange his arms for his lilies.
This little star is furnished
With good spirits who have been active
So that honor and fame might come to them;
And when desires mount in that direction,
Desiring thus, it must indeed be that the rays
Of true love mount upward less brightly.
But in the measuring of our rewards
Against merit lies part of our joy,
Because we see them neither less nor greater.
Thus does living justice sweeten
Affection in us so that it cannot
Ever be twisted toward any iniquity.
Different voices make sweet notes;
So different seats in our life
Yield sweet harmony in these spheres.
And within this very pearl
Shines the light of Romeo, whose
Great and beautiful work was ill received.
But the Provençals who acted against him
Have not laughed; and so he walks ill
Who makes another's well-doing an injury to himself.
Four daughters he had, and each a queen,
Raymond Berengar, and this was done for him by
Romeo, a person humble and a stranger.
And then crooked words moved him
To demand an accounting of this just man,
Who rendered him seven and five for ten.
Then he departed poor and old;
And if the world knew the heart he had
Begging his living morsel by morsel,
Much it praises him, and it would praise him more.
