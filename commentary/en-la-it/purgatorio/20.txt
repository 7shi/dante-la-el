Against a better will, will strives ill;
Hence I, against my pleasure, to please him,
Drew from the water the unsated sponge.
I moved, and my leader moved through the
Clear places, ever along the rock,
As one goes on a wall close to the battlements;
For the people that pour out drop by drop
Through their eyes the evil that possesses all the world,
On the other side come too close to the outer edge.
Accursed be you, ancient she-wolf,
Who more than all the other beasts have prey,
Through your hunger, deep without end!
O heaven, in whose turning it seems to be believed
The conditions here below are changed,
When will he come through whom this beast shall depart?
We went with slow and scanty steps,
And I intent upon the shades that I heard
Piteously weeping and lamenting;
And by chance I heard: Sweet Mary,
Before us, called out thus amid the weeping,
As does a woman who is in labor;
And continue: Poor were you to such a degree
As may be seen by that lodging
Where you laid your holy offspring.
Next I heard: O good Fabricius,
With poverty you desired virtue rather
Than to possess great riches with vice.
These words had pleased me so
That I drew farther on, to have certainty
Of that spirit from whom they seemed to have come.
He spoke also of the generosity
That Nicholas showed the maidens,
To lead their youth to honor.
O soul, who speak so well,
Tell me who you were, I said, and why alone
You renew these worthy praises?
Your word shall not be without reward,
If I return to finish the short journey
Of that life which flies to its end.
And he: And I shall tell, not for comfort
That I expect from there, but because such
Grace shines in you before you are dead.
I was the root of the evil plant
That overshadows all the Christian land
So that good fruit is seldom plucked from it.
But if Douai, Lille, Ghent and Bruges
Could, soon would vengeance be taken for it;
And I ask it of him who judges all.
I was called over there Hugh Capet:
From me were born both the Philips and the Louises,
By whom of late France is ruled.
The son I was of a butcher of Paris.
When the ancient kings had come to an end,
All but one reduced to gray garments,
I found myself holding tight in my hands the rein
Of the government of the kingdom, and with such power
Newly acquired, and so full of friends,
That to the widowed crown raised
Was the head of my son, from whom
Began the consecrated bones of these men.
So long as the great Provençal dowry
Had not taken shame away from my blood,
It was worth little, but at least did no harm.
There began, with force and with falsehood,
Its plunder; and afterward, to make amends,
Ponthieu and Normandy it took, and Gascony.
Charles came into Italy, and, in turn,
Made a victim of Conradin; and then
Thrust Thomas back into heaven, to make amends.
A time I see, not long after today,
That draws another Charles out of France,
To make both himself and his own better known.
Unarmed he comes out of it, alone and with the lance
With which Judas jousted; and that he thrusts
So that he makes Florence's belly burst.
From this not land, but sin and shame
Will he gain, all the heavier for himself
The lighter he reckons such harm.
The other, who once came captive out of a ship,
I see selling his daughter and bargaining over her,
As corsairs do over other men's slave girls.
O avarice, what more can you do to us,
Since you have drawn my blood so to yourself
That it cares not for its own flesh?
And that the evil to come and that already done may be seen,
I see the fleur-de-lis enter Anagni,
And in his vicar Christ taken captive.
I see him once again mocked;
I see the vinegar and the gall renewed,
And him slain between living thieves.
I see the new Pilate so cruel
That this does not sate him, but without decree
He bears into the temple his greedy sails.
O my Lord, when shall I be glad
To see the vengeance which, hidden,
Makes sweet your wrath in your secrecy?
What I was saying of that sole spouse
Of the Holy Spirit, and what made you
Turn toward me for some gloss,
Is appointed for all our prayer as long
As the day lasts; but when night falls,
A contrary strain we take up in its stead.
We recount Pygmalion then,
Whom traitor, robber and slayer of his kin
His desire, greedy for gold, made;
And the misery of avaricious Midas
That followed upon his greedy request,
At which one must always laugh.
Of foolish Achan each of us recalls
How he stole the spoils, so that the wrath
Of Joshua seems here still to bite him.
Then we accuse Sapphira with her husband;
We praise the kicks Heliodorus received;
And in infamy about the whole world goes
Polymnestor, who slew Polydorus.
Last of all the cry goes up here: O Crassus,
Say, you who know, of what savor is gold?
At times one speaks loud and the other low,
According to the feeling that spurs us to speak,
Now at a greater and now at a lesser pace.
Thus in the good that is spoken of here by day,
A little while ago I was not alone; but here close by
No other person was raising his voice.
We had already departed from him,
And were striving to overcome the road
As far as our strength permitted us;
When I felt, like a thing that falls,
The mountain tremble: whereupon a chill seized me
Such as is wont to seize one who goes to his death.
Surely Delos did not shake so hard
Before Latona made her nest upon it
To give birth to the two eyes of heaven.
Then began on all sides the cry,
Such that the master drew toward me,
Saying: Do not fear while I guide you.
Glory in the highest, they all, to God,
Were saying, as far as I understood from those nearby,
From whom the cry could be made out.
We stood motionless and in suspense,
Like the shepherds who first heard that song,
Until the trembling ceased, and I understood it.
Then we took up again our holy journey,
Looking at the shades that lay upon the ground,
Returned already to their accustomed weeping.
No ignorance ever with such strife
Made me desirous of knowing,
If my memory in this does not err,
As I seemed to myself then in my thoughts to feel:
Nor, because of our haste, did I dare to ask,
Nor by myself could I see anything:
Thus I went on, timid and pensive.
