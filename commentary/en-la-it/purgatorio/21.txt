The natural thirst that is never sated,
Except with the water for which the little
Samaritan woman asked as a grace,
Tormented me, and haste spurred me
Along the encumbered way behind my guide,
And I grieved at the just vengeance.
And behold, just as Luke writes for us
That Christ appeared to the two who were on the way,
Already risen out of the sepulchral hollow,
A shade appeared to us, and behind us came,
Watching at its feet the throng that lies there;
Nor did we notice it before it spoke,
Saying: My brothers, God give you peace.
We turned at once, and Virgil
Returned him the sign that befits such a greeting.
Then he began: In the blessed assembly
May the true court place you in peace,
Which confines me in eternal exile.
How! said he, and meanwhile we were going swiftly;
If you are shades whom God does not deem worthy of the heights,
Who has escorted you so far up his stair?
And my teacher: If you look at the marks
That this man bears and that the angel traces,
You will see well that with the good he must reign.
But because she who spins day and night
Had not yet drawn out for him the distaff
That Clotho sets for each and fills,
His soul, which is your sister and mine,
Coming up could not come alone;
Because it does not see as we do.
Therefore I was drawn out of the wide throat
Of Hell, to show him, and I shall show him
Farther, as far as my teaching can lead him.
But tell me, if you know, why such shakings
The mountain gave just now, and why all at once
It seemed to cry out down to its wet feet?
So, in asking, he struck right through the needle's eye
Of my desire, that with hope alone
He then made my thirst less famished.
He began: There is nothing that without
Order is felt by the holy community
Of the mountain, or that is out of the ordinary.
This place is free from every alteration:
What heaven receives from itself into itself
Can occur here, and nothing from another cause:
For neither rain nor hail nor snow
Nor dew nor frost falls higher
Than the little stair of three short steps.
Clouds do not appear, thick or thin,
Nor lightning flash, nor the daughter of Thaumas,
Who over there often changes regions.
Dry vapor does not rise farther
Than the top of the three steps I spoke of,
Where Peter's vicar plants his feet.
Perhaps it trembles lower down, a little or much;
But from wind that hides itself in the earth,
I know not how, up here it never trembled:
It trembles here when some soul feels itself clean,
So that it rises or sets itself in motion
To climb up, and such a cry follows.
Of its uncleanness the will alone gives proof,
Which, wholly free to change its company,
Takes the soul by surprise, and flying delights it.
Before, it wills indeed; but the impulse does not let it,
Which divine justice, against its will,
As once to sin, directs to torment.
And I, who have lain in this pain
Five hundred years and more, only now felt
A free will for a better threshold.
Therefore you felt the earthquake, and pious
Spirits throughout the mountain rendering praise
To that Lord, that he may soon send them up.
Thus he told us; and because one takes pleasure
In drinking in proportion to the greatness of one's thirst,
I could not say how much good he did me.
And the wise guide: Now I see the net
That entangles you here, and how one slips the snare,
Why it trembles here, and in what you rejoice together.
Now may it please you that I know who you were,
And why for so many centuries you have lain
Here, let me grasp from your words.
In the time when good Titus, with the help
Of the supreme King, avenged the wounds
Whence flowed the blood sold by Judas,
By the name that lasts longest and honors most
I was over there, replied that spirit,
Famous enough, but not yet with faith.
So sweet was my singing breath
That, though a Toulousan, it drew me to Rome,
Where I earned the right to adorn my temples with myrtle.
Statius the people over there still call me;
I sang of Thebes, and then of great Achilles,
But I fell on the way with the second burden.
For my ardor the seeds were the sparks
That warmed me, from the divine flame
By which more than a thousand are kindled;
Of the Aeneid I speak, which a mother
Was to me, and was my nurse in making verse:
Without it I never made a dram's weight.
And to have lived over there when
Virgil lived, I would consent to one sun
More than I owe before my release from exile.
These words turned Virgil toward me
With a face that, in silence, said: Be silent:
But the power that wills cannot do all things;
For laughter and tears so closely follow
The passion from which each springs
That in the most truthful they follow the will least.
I did smile, like a man who winks;
Whereupon the shade fell silent and looked at me
In the eyes, where expression fixes itself most deeply.
Ah, so may you bring so great a labor to a good end,
Said he, why did your face just now
Show me a flash of laughter?
Now I am caught on one side and the other;
The one makes me keep silent, the other implores me
To speak: whereupon I sigh, and am understood
By my master, and: Have no fear,
He tells me, of speaking; but speak, and tell him
What he asks with so much concern.
Whereupon I to him: Perhaps you marvel,
Ancient spirit, at the smile I gave;
But I would have greater wonder seize you.
This one, who guides my eyes on high,
Is that Virgil from whom you drew
Strength to sing of men and of gods.
If you thought there was another cause for my smiling,
Dismiss it as untrue; and believe the cause to be
Those words that you said of him.
Already he was bending to embrace the feet
Of my teacher; but he said: Brother,
Do not, for you are a shade, and a shade you see.
And he, rising: Now you can grasp the measure
Of the love for you that warms me,
When I forget our emptiness,
Treating shades as a solid thing.
