When through delights or through pains,
Which some faculty of ours takes in,
The soul gathers itself closely to that faculty,
It seems that it attends to no power any longer;
And this is against that error which believes
That one soul upon another is kindled in us.
And therefore, when one hears a thing or sees it,
Which keeps the soul turned firmly toward itself,
Time goes away, and one does not perceive it,
For one power is that which it heeds,
And another that which has the whole soul;
This one is as if bound, and that one is free.
Of this I had true experience,
Seeing that spirit and marveling:
For fully fifty degrees had risen
The sun, and I had not noticed it, when
We came where those souls with one accord
Cried to us: Here is what you ask for.
A greater opening often has caused to be hedged up,
With a little forkful of his thorns,
A man of the village, when the grape darkens,
Than was the path by which there climbed
My guide and I after him, alone,
As the company departed from us.
One goes to San Leo, and one goes down to Noli;
One climbs up Bismanto and Cacume,
With one's feet alone: but here one must fly;
I mean with the swift wings and with the feathers
Of great desire, behind that guide
Who gave me hope and made light for me.
We climbed within the cleft rock,
And on either side its edge pressed in on us,
And the ground beneath it called for feet and hands.
When we were upon the uppermost rim
Of the high bank, on the open slope:
My master, said I, which way shall we go?
And he to me: Let no step of yours fall back;
Only gain ground up the mountain behind me,
Until some wise guide appears to us.
The summit was so high that it surpassed sight,
And the slope far steeper
Than a line from mid-quadrant to center.
I was weary when I began:
O sweet father, turn and look
How I am left alone if you do not stop.
O son, he said, pull yourself up as far as here,
And he pointed out to me a ledge a little higher up,
Which on that side circles the whole hill.
So did his words spur me on
That I strained, clambering after him,
Until the girdle was beneath my feet.
We both sat down there,
Turned toward the east, whence we had climbed,
For it is wont to hearten one to look back.
My eyes I first directed to the shores below;
Then I raised them to the sun, and marveled
That on the left we were struck by it.
Well did the poet perceive that I stood
Quite astonished at the chariot of light,
Where it passed between us and the north wind.
Whereupon he to me: If Castor and Pollux
Were in the company of that mirror
Which up and down conducts its light,
You would see the ruddy zodiac
Revolve closer still to the Bears,
If it did not stray from its ancient path.
How this may be, if you wish to be able to conceive it,
Gathered within yourself, imagine Zion
With this mountain standing upon the earth
So that both have a single horizon,
And different hemispheres: whence the road
Which Phaethon, to his cost, did not know how to drive along,
You will see how it must pass this mountain
On one flank, while it passes that one on the other,
If your understanding attends quite clearly.
Certainly, my master, said I, thus far
I have not seen so clearly as I discern,
There where my wit seemed wanting:
For the middle circle of the motion above,
Which is called the Equator in a certain art,
And which always remains between the sun and winter,
For the reason you give, stretches away from here
Toward the north, whereas the Hebrews
Saw it toward the hot region.
But if it please you, gladly would I know
How far we have to go, for the hill rises
Higher than my eyes can climb.
And he to me: This mountain is such
That always at the start from below it is hard,
And the higher one goes, the less it hurts.
Therefore, when it shall seem gentle to you
So much so that going up will be as easy for you
As going down with the current by boat;
Then you will be at the end of this path:
There expect to rest from your toil.
I answer no further, and this I know for true.
When he had said his word,
A voice close by rang out: It may be
That you will have need to sit down first.
At the sound of it each of us turned,
And we saw on the left a great boulder
Of which neither I nor he had taken notice before.
Thither we drew; and there were people there
Who stayed in the shade behind the rock,
As one settles down to stay out of negligence.
And one of them who seemed weary to me
Sat and clasped his knees,
Holding his face down low between them.
O my sweet lord, said I, look at
That one who shows himself more negligent
Than if sloth were his sister.
Then he turned toward us and took notice,
Moving his face only up along his thigh,
And said: Now you go up, you who are able.
I knew then who he was; and that distress
Which still quickened my breath a little
Did not keep me from going to him; and afterward,
When I had reached him, he scarcely raised his head,
Saying: Have you seen well how the sun
Drives its chariot on the left shoulder's side?
His sluggish gestures and his curt words
Moved my lips a little to laughter;
Then I began: Belacqua, I do not grieve
For you now; but tell me why seated
Right here you are: are you waiting for a guide,
Or have you taken up your accustomed way again?
And he: O brother, what good does going up do?
For he would not let me go to the torments,
The bird of God that sits upon the gate.
First must heaven turn about me as long
Outside it as I spent in life,
Because I delayed until the end the good sighs;
Unless prayer helps me first,
Rising up from a heart that lives in grace;
What avails any other, which in heaven is not heard?
And already the poet was climbing ahead of me,
And was saying: Come now, see that it is touched,
The meridian, by the sun, for on the shore
Night already covers Morocco with her foot.
