O our Father, who dwell in the heavens,
Not circumscribed, but for the greater love
That you have for the first effects up there,
Praised be your name and your power
By every creature, as it is fitting
To render thanks to your supreme breath.
May the peace of your kingdom come toward us,
For we cannot reach it of ourselves,
If it does not come, with all our skill.
As of their will your angels
Make a sacrifice to you, singing hosanna,
So may men do with theirs.
Give us this day the daily manna,
Without which through this harsh desert
Backward goes whoever labors most to go.
And as we the wrong that we have suffered
Forgive everyone, so do you forgive,
Benign, and do not look upon our merit.
Our strength, which is easily overcome,
Do not put to the test with the ancient adversary,
But free it from him who so spurs it on.
This last prayer, dear Lord,
Is surely not made for us, for there is no need,
But for those who remained behind us.
Thus for themselves and us good passage
Those shades praying for, went beneath the weight,
Like that of which one sometimes dreams,
Unequally tormented all around,
And weary up along the first ledge,
Purging away the murk of the world.
If over there good is ever spoken for us,
What can be said and done for them here
By those whose will has a good root?
Surely one ought to help them wash away the marks
They carried from here, so that, clean and light,
They may pass out of the starry spheres.
Ah, so may justice and pity unburden you
Soon, that you may move the wing
That according to your desire may lift you,
Show on which hand toward the stair
One goes by the shorter way; and if there is more than one passage,
Show us the one that slopes down less steeply:
For this man who comes with me, through the burden
Of Adam's flesh, in which he is clothed,
Is slow in mounting up, against his will.
As for the words they returned to these
Which he whom I followed had spoken,
It was not clear from whom they came;
But it was said: On the right hand along the bank
Come with us, and you will find the pass
That a living person can climb.
And if I were not hindered by the stone
That tames my proud neck,
So that I must carry my face low,
That man who still lives and does not name himself
I would look at, to see if I know him,
And to move him to pity for this burden.
I was an Italian, and born of a great Tuscan:
Guglielmo Aldobrandesco was my father:
I do not know if his name was ever known to you.
The ancient blood and the noble deeds
Of my forebears made me so arrogant
That, not thinking of our common mother,
I held every man in scorn to such a point
That I died of it, as the Sienese know,
And every child in Campagnatico knows it.
I am Omberto: and not to me alone harm
Pride does, for all my kin
It has dragged with it into ruin.
And here I must bear this weight
For it, until I make satisfaction to God,
Since I did not do so among the living, here among the dead.
Listening, I bent my face down;
And one of them, not the one who was speaking,
Twisted beneath the weight that hampers them,
And saw me and knew me and was calling out,
Keeping his eyes with difficulty fixed
On me, who went all bent over with them.
Oh, I said to him, are you not Oderisi,
The honor of Gubbio, and the honor of that art
Which in Paris is called illuminating?
Brother, said he, brighter smile the pages
That Franco of Bologna paints:
The honor is now all his, and mine in part.
Surely I would not have been so courteous
While I lived, because of the great desire
For excellence, on which my heart was set.
For such pride here the penalty is paid;
And I would not yet be here, were it not
That, still able to sin, I turned to God.
O vainglory of human powers,
How briefly the green upon the summit lasts,
Unless it is overtaken by ages of dullness!
Cimabue believed that in painting
He held the field, and now Giotto has the acclaim,
So that the fame of the former is dim.
Thus one Guido has taken from the other
The glory of the tongue; and perhaps born is
He who will drive both the one and the other from the nest.
Worldly clamor is nothing but a breath
Of wind, which now comes from here, and now comes from there,
And changes name because it changes quarter.
What greater renown will you have, if in old age you shed
The flesh from yourself, than if you had died
Before you left off saying num-nums or pennies,
Before a thousand years pass? That is a shorter
Space compared to eternity than a movement of the eyelids
Compared to the sphere that turns most slowly in heaven.
Of him who covers so little of the way
Before me, all Tuscany resounded,
And now there is scarcely a whisper of him in Siena,
Where he was lord when there was crushed
The Florentine fury, which proud
Was at that time, just as now it is a whore.
Your renown is the color of grass,
Which comes and goes, and he drains it of color
Through whom it springs tender from the earth.
And I to him: Your true speech heartens me
To humility, and you flatten a great swelling in me,
But who is he of whom you were just speaking?
That is, he answered, Provenzan Salvani;
He is here because he was presumptuous
In bringing all Siena into his hands.
He has gone thus and goes without rest
Since he died: such coin pays
To make satisfaction whoever is overbold over there.
And I: If that spirit who awaits,
Before repenting, the verge of life,
Remains down below and does not ascend up here,
Unless a good prayer helps him,
Before as much time passes as he lived,
How was his coming granted?
When he lived in greatest glory, he said,
Of his own free will, in the square of Siena,
All shame laid aside, he took his stand:
And there, to draw his friend out of the torment
He was enduring in Charles's prison,
He brought himself to tremble in every vein.
I will say no more, and I know I speak obscurely;
But little time will pass before your neighbors
Will bring it about that you can gloss it.
This deed removed those bounds for him.
