An end had he put to his discourse,
The lofty teacher, and attentively he looked
Into my face to see if I seemed content:
And I, whom a new thirst still goaded,
Outwardly was silent, and inwardly said: Perhaps
The too much asking that I do weighs on him.
But that true father, who perceived
The timid wish that did not disclose itself,
By speaking gave me courage to speak.
Whence I: Master, my sight grows keen
So in your light that I discern clearly
All that your reasoning conveys or describes:
Therefore I pray you, sweet dear father,
That you show me love, to which you trace
Every good deed and its contrary.
Turn toward me, he said, the keen eyes
Of the intellect, and manifest to you will be
The error of the blind who make themselves guides.
The soul, which is created ready to love,
Is moved toward everything that pleases,
As soon as by pleasure it is roused to action.
Your faculty of apprehension from a real being
Draws an image, and within you unfolds it,
So that it makes the soul turn toward it.
And if, once turned, toward it the soul inclines,
That inclining is love; that is nature
Which through pleasure binds itself anew in you.
Then, as fire moves upward,
By its form, which is born to rise
There where it lasts longest in its matter;
So the captive soul enters into desire,
Which is a spiritual motion, and never rests
Until the thing loved makes it rejoice.
Now it can be apparent to you how hidden is
The truth from the people who affirm
Every love to be in itself a praiseworthy thing:
Because perhaps its matter appears
Always to be good; but not every impression
Is good, although the wax is good.
Your words and my mind that follows them,
I answered him, have laid love bare to me;
And this has made me more pregnant with doubt:
For if love is offered to us from without,
And the soul goes with no other foot,
Whether it goes straight or crooked is not to its account.
And he to me: As much as reason sees here
I can tell you; from there on look for my answer
From Beatrice alone, for it is a matter of faith.
Every substantial form that separate
Is from matter, and is united with it,
Has a power peculiar to its kind gathered within itself,
Which, without acting, is not perceived,
Nor does it show itself except by its effect,
As by green leaves in a living plant.
Therefore, whence comes the understanding
Of the first notions, man does not know,
Nor the inclination toward the first objects of desire,
Which are in you as is the zeal in the bee
To make honey; and this first desire
Admits no desert of praise or of blame.
Now, so that to this every other may be gathered,
Innate in you is the power that counsels,
And whose task it is to hold the threshold of assent.
This is the principle from which is drawn
The ground of merit in you, according
As it receives and sifts good and evil loves.
Those who by reasoning went to the bottom
Perceived this innate freedom,
And therefore left moral teaching to the world.
Hence, let us grant that of necessity
Arises every love that is kindled within you,
To hold it back is in your power.
The noble power is what Beatrice means
By free will, and therefore see
That you bear it in mind, if she takes to speaking to you.
The moon, late almost to midnight,
Made the stars seem fewer to us,
Shaped like a great shard all ablaze;
And it ran counter to the heavens along those paths
Which the sun sets aflame when one in Rome
Between the Sardinians and Corsicans sees it as it sets;
And that gentle shade, through whom is named
Pietola more than any Mantuan village,
Had laid down the burden of my load.
Wherefore I, who that reasoning, open and plain,
On my questions had gathered in,
Stood like a man who drowsily wanders in mind.
But this drowsiness was taken from me
Suddenly by people who from behind
Our backs had already turned toward us.
And as Ismenus once saw, and Asopus,
Along their banks by night a frenzy and a throng,
Whenever the Thebans had need of Bacchus;
So round that circle each bends his stride,
From what I saw of those who came,
Whose good will a just love rides.
Soon they were upon us, because at a run
That whole great throng moved;
And two in front cried out, weeping:
Mary ran with haste to the mountain;
And Caesar, to subdue Ilerda,
Struck at Marseille, and then ran into Spain.
Quick, quick, that time may not be lost
Through too little love, cried the others behind;
That zeal for doing good may make grace green again.
O people, in whom sharp fervor now
Makes good perhaps the negligence and delay
Shown by you through lukewarmness in doing good,
This man, who lives, and surely I do not lie to you,
Wishes to go up, if only the sun shines on us again;
Therefore tell us where the opening is nearest.
These were the words of my guide;
And one of those spirits said: Come
Behind us, and you will find the opening.
We are so full of desire to move
That we cannot stay; therefore pardon us
If you take our justice for discourtesy.
I was abbot at San Zeno in Verona,
Under the rule of good Barbarossa,
Of whom Milan still speaks in sorrow.
And one already has a foot in the grave
Who soon will weep for that monastery,
And sad will he be to have had power;
Because his son, hardly whole in body
And worse in mind, and born out of wedlock,
He has put in place of its true shepherd.
I do not know if he said more or else fell silent,
So far beyond us had he already run;
But this I heard, and it pleased me to retain it.
And he who had been my help in every need
Said: Turn this way; see two of them
Coming, biting at sloth.
Behind all the rest they said: Sooner were
The people dead for whom the sea opened
Than Jordan saw their heirs;
And those who did not bear the toil
To the end with the son of Anchises
Gave themselves up to a life without glory.
Then, when so far removed from us were
Those shades that they could no longer be seen,
A new thought entered into me,
From which many others, and diverse, were born;
And so much did I wander from one to another
That in my wandering I closed my eyes,
And my thought I transmuted into a dream.
