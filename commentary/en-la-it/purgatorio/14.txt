Who is this who circles our mountain,
Before death has given him flight,
And opens his eyes at will and closes them?
I do not know who he is; but I know he is not alone:
Ask him, you who draw nearer to him,
And gently, so that he may speak, welcome him.
Thus two spirits, leaning toward one another,
Were talking of me there on the right hand;
Then they turned their faces upward to speak to me:
O, said the one, O soul, who, fixed
Still in the body, go your way toward heaven,
For charity's sake console us, and tell us
Whence you come, and who you are: for you make us
Marvel as much at your grace
As a thing that never was before demands.
And I: Through the midst of Tuscany ranges
A little river that rises in Falterona,
And a hundred miles of course do not sate it.
From its banks I bring this body:
To tell you who I am would be to speak in vain;
For my name does not yet resound much.
If I rightly grasp your meaning
With my understanding, then answered me
The one who spoke first, you speak of the Arno.
And the other said to him: Why hid
This man the name of that river,
As one does with horrible things?
And the shade who had been asked about this
Discharged his debt thus: I do not know, but fitting
Indeed it is that the name of such a valley perish:
For from its beginning, where so swollen is
The rugged mountain from which Peloro is severed,
That in few places does it pass beyond that mark,
As far as where it yields itself up to replenish
What the sky dries up from the sea,
Whence the rivers have what goes with them;
Virtue is so shunned as an enemy
By all, like a snake, either through the ill fortune
Of the place, or through evil custom that goads them:
Hence so changed have they made their nature,
The inhabitants of the wretched valley,
That it seems that Circe had them at pasture.
Among foul swine, worthier of acorns
Than of other foods made for human use,
It first directs its wretched path.
Curs it finds then, coming down,
Snarling more than their strength warrants,
And at them disdainfully it turns its snout away.
It goes on falling, and the larger it grows,
The more it finds dogs becoming wolves,
The accursed and ill-fated ditch.
Then, having descended through more deep pools,
It finds the foxes so full of guile
That they fear no cunning that might catch them.
Nor shall I forbear to speak because another hears me,
And it will be good for this man if he still remembers
What a truthful spirit unfolds to me.
I see your grandson, who becomes
Hunter of those wolves, on the bank
Of the fierce river, and terrifies them all.
He sells their flesh while it is living,
Then kills them like an old wild beast;
Many of life, and himself of honor, he deprives:
Bloody he comes out of the dismal wood;
He leaves it such that in a thousand years from now
It will not have grown wooded again as it was before.
As at the news of harms to come
The face of him who listens is troubled
From whatever quarter the danger may assail him;
So I saw the other soul, who turned
Stood to listen, grow troubled and sad,
Once he had taken the words to heart.
Hearing the one, and the sight of the other,
Made me eager to know their names,
And I made a request for them, mingled with entreaties.
Whereupon the spirit who had spoken to me before
Began again: You wish me to bring myself
To do for you what you will not do for me;
But since God wills that there shine through you
So much of his grace, I shall not be sparing with you:
Therefore know that I am Guido del Duca.
My blood was so parched with envy
That if I had seen a man grow glad,
You would have seen me suffused with livid color.
From my seed I reap such straw.
O human race, why do you set your heart
Where a sharer must be barred?
This is Rinieri; this is the glory and the honor
Of the house of the Calboli, where no one
Has since become heir to his worth.
And not his blood alone has been stripped,
Between the Po and the mountain and the sea and the Reno,
Of the good required for earnest and for play;
For within these bounds it is full
Of poisonous thickets, so that only slowly
Would they now die out through cultivation.
Where is good Licio, and Arrigo Manardi,
Pier Traversaro, and Guido di Carpigna?
O people of Romagna turned bastards!
When does a Fabro take root again in Bologna?
When in Faenza a Bernardin di Fosco,
A noble shoot from lowly grass?
Do not marvel if I weep, Tuscan,
When I remember, along with Guido da Prato,
Ugolin d'Azzo, who lived with us;
Federigo Tignoso and his company;
The house of Traversara, and the Anastagi;
And the one lineage and the other are without heirs;
The ladies and the knights, the toils and the pleasures
To which love and courtesy inclined us,
There where hearts have grown so wicked.
O Brettinoro, why do you not flee away,
Since my family has gone away,
And many people, so as not to be wicked?
Bagnacaval does well, for it breeds no more,
And Castrocaro does ill, and Conio worse,
Which busies itself the more with breeding such counts.
The Pagani will do well, once the demon
Of theirs goes away; but not so that unsullied
Will any testimony of them ever remain.
O Ugolin de' Fantolin, safe
Is your name, since no longer is expected
Anyone who might by degenerating darken it.
But go away now, Tuscan, for now it pleases me
Far more to weep than to speak;
So has your talk gripped my mind.
We knew that those dear souls
Heard us go: therefore by keeping silent
They made us confident of the way.
Then, as we were left alone going onward,
Like lightning when it cleaves the air seemed
A voice that came from ahead, saying:
Whoever takes me will kill me.
And it fled, like thunder that dies away
If suddenly it rends the cloud.
As soon as our hearing had respite from it,
And behold the other, with so great a crash
That it seemed like thunder following close:
I am Aglauro, who became stone.
And then, to draw close to the Poet,
Backward I stepped, and not forward.
Already the air on every side was still,
And he said to me: That was the hard bit
That ought to keep man within his bounds.
But you take the bait, so that the hook
Of the ancient adversary draws you to him;
And therefore little avails either bridle or call.
Heaven calls you, circling round you,
Showing you its eternal beauties;
And your eye looks only to earth:
Hence he who discerns all strikes you.
