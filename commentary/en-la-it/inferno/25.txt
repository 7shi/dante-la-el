At the end of his words the thief
Raised his hands with both the figs,
Saying: Take them, God, for at you I aim them.
From then on the serpents were my friends,
Because one wound itself then about his neck,
As if to say: I will not have you speak further:
And another about his arms, and bound him fast,
Clinching itself so tightly in front,
That he could not give a twitch with them.
Ah, Pistoia, Pistoia, why do you not resolve
To burn yourself to ashes, so that you last no longer,
Since in wrongdoing you surpass your seed?
Through all the dark circles of Hell
I saw no spirit so proud against God,
Not him who fell at Thebes down from the walls.
He departed without speaking one more word:
And I saw a Centaur full of rage
Coming, crying: Where is he, where is the bitter one?
Maremma, I do not think, has so many
Snakes as he had upon his rump,
Up to where our human form begins.
Upon his shoulders, behind the nape,
With wings spread there lay on him a dragon,
And it sets ablaze whoever crosses its path.
My Master said: That is Cacus,
Who beneath the rock of Mount Aventine
Of blood many times made a lake.
He does not go with his brothers along one path,
Because he committed the fraudulent theft
Of the great herd that was near him:
Wherefore his crooked deeds ceased
Under the club of Hercules, who perhaps
Gave him a hundred blows, and he did not feel ten.
While he spoke thus, and the other sped on,
Three spirits came beneath us,
Of whom neither I nor my guide was aware,
Until they cried: Who are you?
Whereupon our talk broke off,
And we attended only to them thereafter.
I did not know them; but it came about,
As it is wont to come about by some chance,
That one had to name the other,
Saying: Where can Cianfa have stayed behind?
Whereupon I, so that my guide might pay heed,
Laid my finger up from chin to nose.
If you are now, reader, slow to believe
What I shall say, it will be no marvel,
For I, who saw it, scarcely grant it to myself.
While I held my brows raised toward them,
And a serpent with six feet flings itself
Before one of them, and clings to him all over.
With its middle feet it clasped his belly,
And with its forefeet it seized his arms;
Then it sank its teeth into both one cheek and the other:
Its hind feet it stretched to his thighs,
And put its tail between them both,
And up his back along the loins it stretched it again.
Ivy never was rooted
To a tree so, as the horrible beast
About his limbs entwined its own:
Then they stuck together, as if of hot wax
They had been made, and mingled their color;
Neither the one nor the other now seemed what it had been:
As there advances ahead of the heat
Up the paper a brown color,
Which is not black, and yet the white dies.
The other two looked on, and each
Said: Alas, Agnel, how you change!
See, already you are neither two nor one.
Already the two heads had become one,
When there appeared to us two figures mingled
In one face, where two were lost.
The arms became two from four lengths;
The thighs with the legs, and the belly and the chest,
Became limbs that had never been seen.
Every former likeness there was effaced:
Where in neither the distorted image
Seemed, and so it went off with slow steps.
As the green lizard, under the great lash
Of the dog days, changing hedges,
Seems a flash of lightning if it crosses the road;
So seemed, coming toward the bellies
Of the other two, a little fiery serpent,
Livid and black as a grain of pepper.
And that part through which is first taken
Our nourishment, it pierced in one of them;
Whereupon it fell stretched out before him.
The pierced one looked at it, but said nothing:
Rather, with feet fixed, he yawned,
Just as if sleep or fever were assailing him.
He gazed at the serpent, and it at him:
The one through the wound, and the other through the mouth,
Smoked heavily, and the smoke streams met.
Let Lucan now be silent where he tells
Of wretched Sabellus and of Nasidius,
And let him give heed to hear what is now told.
Let Ovid be silent about Cadmus and Arethusa:
For though him into a serpent, and her into a fountain,
He turns in his verse, I do not envy him:
For two natures ever face to face
He did not transmute so that both forms
Were ready to exchange their matter.
Together they answered each other by such rules
That the serpent split its tail into a fork,
And the wounded one drew his feet together.
The legs with the thighs to one another
Stuck so fast that in a little while the join
Left no mark that could be seen.
The cleft tail took on the shape
That was being lost there, and its skin
Was growing soft, and that of the man hard.
He drew his arms in through the armpits,
And the two feet of the beast, which were short,
Lengthened as much as those grew short.
Then the hind feet, twisted together,
Became the member that man conceals,
And the wretch from his own had put forth two.
While the smoke veils both one and the other
In a new color, and makes hair grow up
From the one side, and from the other strips it,
The one rose, and the other fell down,
Yet without turning aside the impious lamps
Beneath which each was changing muzzle.
The one who was behind drew it toward his temples;
And from the excess matter that came there
The ears issued from the bare cheeks:
What did not run forward stayed back;
Of that surplus the face made a nose,
And thickened the lips as much as was fitting.
The one who lay thrusts his muzzle forward,
And draws his ears back into his head,
As the snail does its horns:
And the tongue, which he had had whole and ready
Before for speech, splits, and the forked one
Closes back together, and the smoke ceases.
The soul that had become a beast,
Hissing, fled along the valley,
And the other, speaking, spits after him.
Then he turned his new shoulders to him,
And said to the other: Away, let Buoso run,
As I have done, on all fours, along this path.
Thus I saw the seventh ballast
Change and change again; and here may I be excused
By the novelty, if my pen strays at all.
And although my eyes bewildered
Were somewhat, and my spirit dismayed,
Those could not flee so well concealed
That I did not clearly recognize Puccio Sciancato:
And he was the only one who, of the three companions
Who came at first had not been changed:
The other was the one you, Gaville, mourn.
