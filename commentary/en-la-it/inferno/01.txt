In the middle of the journey of our life
I found myself in a dark wood,
for the straight way was lost.
And how hard a thing it is to say what it was like,
this wood so wild and rough and strong,
which in thought renews the fear.
So bitter is it that death is little more so;
but, to tell of the good that I found there,
I shall speak of the other things that I saw there.
I cannot well recount how I entered it;
so full of sleep was I at that point
when I abandoned the true way.
But after I had come to the foot of a hill,
there where that valley ended
which had pierced my heart with fear,
I looked on high, and saw its shoulders
clothed already with the rays of the planet
that leads one straight along every path.
Then a little quieter was the fear
that in the lake of my heart had lasted
the night that I passed with such anguish.
And as one who, with labored breath,
having come out of the deep to the shore,
turns to the perilous water and gazes,
so my spirit, which was still fleeing,
turned back to look again at the pass
that never yet left anyone alive.
And having rested my weary body a little,
I took up my way again along the deserted slope
so that the planted foot was always the lower.
And behold, almost at the beginning of the ascent,
a leopard, light and very swift,
that was covered with a spotted coat.
And it would not move from before my face,
but so hindered my journey
that many times I turned to go back.
It was the hour of the beginning of morning,
and the sun was mounting up with those stars
that were with it when divine love
first set those beautiful things in motion;
so that grounds for good hope were given me
concerning that beast with the gaily marked coat,
by the hour of the day and the sweet season;
but not so much that it did not give me fear,
the sight that appeared to me of a lion.
He seemed to be coming against me
with head held high and with raging hunger,
so that the air seemed to fear him;
and a she-wolf that with all cravings
seemed laden in her leanness,
and had already made many people live in misery.
She brought me such heaviness,
through the fear that came from the sight of her,
that I lost hope of the height.
And as is one who gladly gains,
and the time comes that makes him lose,
who in all his thoughts weeps and grieves,
so the beast without peace made me,
which, coming toward me, little by little
drove me back to where the sun is silent.
While I was tumbling down to a low place,
before my eyes there appeared
one who through long silence seemed hoarse.
When I saw him in the great waste,
Have mercy on me, I cried to him,
whatever you may be, whether shade or living man.
He answered me: Not a man; a man I once was,
and my parents were Lombards,
Mantuans by birth, both of them.
I was born under Julius, though it was late,
and lived in Rome under good Augustus,
in the time of the false and lying gods.
A poet I was, and I sang of that just
son of Anchises who came from Troy
after proud Ilion was burned.
But why do you come to me into such distress?
Why do you not climb the delightful mountain
that is the beginning and cause of all joy?
Are you then that Virgil and that fountain
that pours forth so broad a river of speech?
I answered him with a shamefaced brow.
O honor and light of the other poets,
may the long study and the great love avail me
that have made me search your volume.
You are my master and my author;
you alone are he from whom I took
the beautiful style that has done me honor.
See the beast because of which I turned back;
help me against her, famous sage,
for she makes my veins and pulses tremble.
You must take another way,
he answered, after he saw me weep,
if you wish to escape from this wild place;
for this beast, on account of which you cry out,
lets no one pass along her way,
but so hinders him that she kills him;
and she has a nature so wicked and evil
that she never fills her ravenous desire,
and after feeding she is hungrier than before.
Many are the animals with which she mates,
and more there will yet be, until the Greyhound
comes, who will make her die in pain.
He will feed on neither land nor pewter,
but on wisdom, love, and virtue,
and his birthplace will be between Feltro and Feltro.
Of that humble Italy he will be the salvation
for which the maiden Camilla died,
Euryalus, Turnus, and Nisus, of wounds.
He will chase her through every town
until he has put her back in Hell,
whence envy first sent her forth.
Therefore for your good I think and judge
that you should follow me, and I shall be your guide,
and I shall lead you from here through an eternal place,
where you will hear the desperate cries
of those ancient sorrowing spirits,
each of whom cries out for the second death.
And then you will see those who are content
in the fire, because they hope to come,
whenever it may be, to the blessed people.
To whom, if you then wish to ascend,
there will be a soul worthier of that than I;
with them I shall leave you at my departure:
for that Emperor who reigns on high,
because I was rebellious against his law,
does not will that through me one should come into his city.
In every part he commands, and there he reigns;
there are his city and his lofty seat:
O happy is the one he chooses for that place.
And I to him: Poet, I beseech you,
by that God whom you did not know,
so that I may flee this evil and worse,
that you lead me where you said,
so that I may see the gate of Saint Peter
and those whom you make out to be so sad.
Then he moved, and I followed behind him.
