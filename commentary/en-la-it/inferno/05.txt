Thus I descended from the first circle
down into the second, which girds less space,
and so much more pain, which stings into wailing.
There stands Minos, horribly snarling:
he examines the sins at the entrance,
judges and sends according as he coils.
I say that when the ill-born soul
comes before him, it confesses all;
and that knower of sins
sees what place in Hell is fit for it:
he girds himself with his tail as many times
as the stages he wills it to be sent down.
Always before him many of them stand:
they go by turns, each to judgment;
they speak and hear, and then are turned downward.
O you who come to the abode of pain,
said Minos to me when he saw me,
leaving off the exercise of so great an office,
look how you enter, and in whom you trust:
let not the breadth of the entrance deceive you.
And my guide to him: Why do you keep shouting?
Do not hinder his fated going:
it is so willed there, where one can do
what one wills, and ask no more.
Now the notes of pain begin
to make themselves heard by me: now I have come
there where much weeping strikes me.
I came into a place mute of all light,
which bellows as the sea does in a storm
if it is buffeted by contrary winds.
The infernal gale, which never rests,
drives the spirits with its violent sweep,
whirling and striking them, torments them.
When they come before the ruin,
there the shrieks, and the mourning, and lamentation;
there they blaspheme the divine power.
I understood that to such torment
the carnal sinners were damned,
who subject reason to appetite.
And as their wings carry the starlings away
in the cold season, in a broad and dense flock,
so that blast the evil spirits
this way, that way, down, up, drives them:
no hope ever comforts them,
not of rest, nor even of lesser pain.
And as the cranes go singing their lays,
making of themselves a long line in the air,
so I saw coming, uttering wails,
shades borne by the aforesaid strife:
wherefore I said: Master, who are those
people whom the black air so chastises?
The first of those of whom news
you wish to know, he said to me then,
was empress of many tongues.
To the vice of lust she was so given
that she made desire lawful in her law,
to remove the blame into which she had been brought.
She is Semiramis, of whom one reads
that she succeeded Ninus and was his wife:
she held the land that the Sultan governs.
The next is she who killed herself for love
and broke faith with the ashes of Sychaeus;
the next is lustful Cleopatra.
Helen I saw, for whose sake so evil a
time rolled by, and I saw great Achilles,
who for love fought at the last.
I saw Paris, Tristan; and more than a thousand
shades he pointed out to me and named to me,
whom love parted from our life.
After I had heard my teacher
name the ladies and the knights of old,
pity overcame me, and I was almost lost.
And I began: Poet, gladly
would I speak to those two who go together
and seem to be so light upon the wind.
And he to me: You will see when they are
nearer to us; and you then entreat them
by the love that drives them, and they will come.
As soon as the wind bends them toward us,
I raise my voice: O tormented souls,
come speak to us, if another does not forbid it.
As doves, driven by desire,
with wings open and still, to their sweet nest
come through the air, borne by their will;
so they came out of the band where Dido is,
weeping loudly through the malignant air,
so strong was the affectionate cry.
O living creature, gracious and kind,
who go visiting through the dark air
us who stained the world blood-red;
if the King of the universe were our friend,
we would pray to him for your peace,
since you have pity on our perverse plight.
Of what it pleases you to hear and speak
we shall hear and speak to you,
so long as the wind, as now it does, is silent for us.
The town where I was born lies
on the seashore where the Po descends
to have peace with its followers.
Love, which swiftly takes hold of the noble heart,
seized this man with love for the fair body
that was taken from me, and the manner still wounds me.
Love, which excuses no one loved from loving,
seized me with delight in this man so strongly
that, as you see, it still does not leave me.
Love led us to one death:
Caina awaits him who quenched our life.
These words were offered to us by them.
When I heard those wounded souls,
I bent my face and held it low so long,
until the Poet said to me: What are you thinking?
And I answered, beginning: Alas,
how many sweet thoughts, how much desire
led these two to the painful pass!
Then I turned to them and spoke,
and began: Francesca, your torments
make me sorrowful and full of pity unto tears.
But tell me: in the time of sweet sighs,
by what sign and how did love grant
that you should know your uncertain desires?
And she to me: There is no greater pain
than to remember the happy time
in misery; and this your teacher knows.
But if to know the first root
of our love you have so great a longing,
I shall tell it as one who weeps and speaks.
We were reading one day for delight
of Lancelot, how love gripped him:
we were alone and without any suspicion.
Many times our eyes were drawn up
by that reading, and it drained the color from our faces:
but one point alone was what overcame us.
When we read of the longed-for smile
being kissed by so great a lover,
this man, who never shall be parted from me,
kissed me on the mouth, all trembling:
a Galehaut was the book and he who wrote it:
that day we read no further in it.
While the one spirit said this,
the other wept so that, from pity,
I fainted as though I were dying;
and I fell as a dead body falls.
