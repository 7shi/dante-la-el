Now one of the hard banks carries us onward,
And the smoke of the brook casts shade from above
So that from the fire it saves the water and the banks.
Just as the Flemings between Wissant and Bruges,
Fearing the flood that hurls itself toward them,
Make the barrier so that the sea may flee;
And as the Paduans along the Brenta,
To defend their villages and their castles,
Before Chiarentana feels the heat;
In such a likeness were those banks made,
Although neither so high nor so thick,
Whoever the master was, did he make them.
Already we had drawn away from the wood
So far that I should not have seen where it was,
Even if I had turned myself back;
When we met a troop of souls
That were coming below us, and each one
Looked at us, as people are wont at evening
To look at one another under a new moon;
And they so narrowed their eyes at us,
As the old tailor does at the eye of the needle.
Thus eyed by such a company,
I was recognized by one who took me
By the hem and cried: What a marvel!
And I, when he stretched out his arm to me,
Fixed my eyes on his scorched features
So that his burnt face did not keep
The recognition of him from my understanding;
And lowering my hand toward his face,
I answered: Are you here, Ser Brunetto?
And he: O my son, let it not displease you
If Ser Brunetto Latini a little way with you
Turns back and lets the file go on.
And I to him: As much as I can, I beg it of you;
And if you wish me to sit down with you,
I shall do so, if it pleases this one, for I go with him.
O son, he said, whoever of this flock
Stops for an instant lies for a hundred years
Without fending off the fire when it strikes him.
Therefore go on, for I shall come at your skirts,
And afterward I shall rejoin my band,
Which goes weeping for its eternal losses.
I did not dare descend from the road
To go abreast of him, but my head bowed
I kept, as a man who walks reverently.
He began: What fortune or destiny
Before your last day leads you down here?
And who is this who shows the way?
Up there above in the serene life,
I answered him, I lost my way in a valley
Before my age was full.
Only yesterday morning I turned my back on it:
This one appeared to me as I was returning to it,
And leads me back home by this path.
And he to me: If you follow your star,
You cannot fail to reach the glorious port,
If I discerned aright in the fair life;
And if I had not died so early,
Seeing heaven so kindly disposed toward you,
I should have given you encouragement in the work.
For that ungrateful, malignant people,
Which descended from Fiesole of old
And still partakes of the mountain and the rock,
Will make itself, for your good deeds, your enemy:
And with reason; for among the sour sorbs
It is unfitting to tend the sweet fig.
Old report in the world calls them blind,
A people greedy, envious and proud:
Of their ways see that you cleanse yourself.
Your fortune reserves so much good for you
That one party and the other will hunger
For you: but the grass will be far from the beak.
Let the Fiesolan beasts make fodder
Of themselves, and not touch the plant,
If any still springs up in their dung,
Into which falls the holy seed
Of those Romans who remained there when
The nest of so much malice was made.
If my wish were wholly fulfilled,
I answered him, you would not yet be
Banished from human nature;
For fixed in my mind, and now it grieves my heart,
Is the good and dear fatherly image
Of you, when in the world from time to time
You taught me how a man makes himself eternal:
And how grateful I was for it, while I live,
Must be discerned in my speech.
What you tell of my course I write down,
And keep it, to be glossed with another text,
For a lady who will know how, if I reach her.
But this much I wish to be plain to you:
Provided my conscience does not rebuke me,
For Fortune, however she wills, I am ready.
Such an earnest is not new to my ears:
Therefore let Fortune turn her wheel
As she pleases, and the peasant his mattock.
My master then, over his cheek,
The right one, turned back and looked at me;
Then said: He listens well who takes note of it.
Nor for that do I go on speaking any less
With Ser Brunetto, and I ask who are
His best-known and most eminent companions.
And he to me: To know of some is good:
Of the others it will be praiseworthy for us to keep silent,
For time would be short: so many are they.
In sum, know that all were clerks,
And great men of letters, and of great fame,
With one and the same sin befouled in the world.
Priscian goes with that wretched throng,
And Francesco d'Accorso; also, to see there,
If you had had a craving for such scurf,
You could have seen him who by the Servant of Servants
Was transferred from Arno to Bacchiglione,
Where he left his ill-stretched sinews.
I would tell of more; but my coming along and my speech
Can be no longer, since I see
New smoke rising there on the sand.
People are coming with whom I must not be:
Let my Treasure be commended to you,
In which I still live; and more I do not ask.
Then he turned back and seemed one of those
Who run at Verona for the green cloth
Across the fields; and of these he seemed
The one who wins, and not the one who loses.
