Of a new punishment I must make verses,
And give matter to the twentieth canto
Of the first canticle, which is of the submerged.
I was already wholly intent
On looking down into the uncovered depth,
Which was bathed in anguished tears:
And I saw people through the round valley
Come, keeping silent and weeping, at the pace
That litany processions keep in this world.
As my gaze descended lower upon them,
Wondrously twisted appeared to be
Each one between the chin and the beginning of the chest:
For toward the loins the face was turned,
And backward they had to come,
Because the sight of what lay before them was taken from them.
Perhaps by force of palsy once
Someone was twisted thus completely;
But I have not seen it, nor do I believe it is so.
So may God let you, reader, take fruit
From this reading, think for yourself
How I could keep my face dry,
When our image, from close by,
I saw so twisted that the tears from the eyes
Bathed the buttocks along the cleft.
Indeed I wept, leaning against one of the crags
Of the hard cliff, so that my guide
Said to me: Are you too one of the other fools?
Here pity lives when it is well and truly dead.
Who is more wicked than he
Who brings compassion to divine judgment?
Raise your head, raise it, he said, toward him for whom
The earth opened before the eyes of the Thebans,
Whereupon they all cried: Where are you plunging,
Amphiaraus? Why do you leave the war?
And he did not cease plunging downward
Until he reached Minos, who seizes each one.
See how he has made a breast of his shoulders:
Because he wished to see too far ahead,
Backward he goes and makes a backward path.
See Tiresias, who changed appearance
When from male he became female,
All his limbs changing;
And afterward he had first to strike again
The two entwined serpents with his rod
Before he regained his manly feathers.
Aruns is he who sets his back against his belly,
Who in the mountains of Luni, where the ground is tilled
By the man of Carrara who dwells below,
Had among white marbles the cave
For his dwelling; whence, looking upon the stars
And the sea, his view was not cut off.
And she who covers the breasts
That you do not see, with her loose tresses,
And has on the other side all her hair-bearing skin,
Was Manto, who wandered through many lands,
Then settled there where I was born;
Hence it pleases me that you listen to me a little.
After her father departed this life,
And the city of Bacchus became enslaved,
She went for a long time about the world.
Up in fair Italy lies a lake
At the foot of the Alps that shut in Germany
Above Tyrol, which has the name Benaco.
By a thousand springs and more, I believe, the land is bathed
Between Garda and Val Camonica and the Apennine
With the water that lies still in the said lake.
There is a place in the middle there, where Trent's
Shepherd, and he of Brescia, and he of Verona
Could make the sign of the cross, if he took that road.
Peschiera sits, a strong and handsome bulwark
To face the men of Brescia and Bergamo,
Where the surrounding lowland slopes farthest down.
There it must all pour down,
All that cannot stay in Benaco's bosom,
And becomes a river down through green pastures.
As soon as the water begins to run,
No longer Benaco, but Mincio, it is called
As far as Governolo, where it falls into the Po.
It has not run far when it finds a lowland,
In which it spreads out into the marsh,
And in summer it is apt at times to be noxious.
Passing that way, the cruel virgin
Saw land in the middle of the swamp,
Untilled, and bare of inhabitants.
There, to flee all human company,
She stayed with her servants to practice her arts,
And lived there, and left her empty body.
The men then who were scattered round about
Gathered at that place, which was strong
Because of the swamp it had on every side.
They built the city over those dead bones;
And after her who first chose the place,
They called it Mantua without further casting of lots.
Once its people were more thickly gathered within,
Before the folly of Casalodi
Met with deceit at Pinamonte's hands.
Therefore I counsel you, if ever you hear
My city's origin told otherwise,
Let no lie defraud the truth.
And I: Master, your accounts
Are so certain to me, and so command my faith,
That the others would be dead coals to me.
But tell me of the people who pass on,
If you see any of them worthy of note;
For on this alone my mind rests.
Then he said to me: That one who from his cheek
Extends his beard over his dusky shoulders
Was, when Greece was emptied of males
So that scarcely any remained even for the cradles,
An augur, and with Calchas set the moment
At Aulis to cut the first rope.
Eurypylus was his name, and thus sings of him
My lofty tragedy in one place:
Well do you know it, you who know it all.
That other who is so slight about the flanks
Was Michael Scot, who truly
Knew the game of magic frauds.
See Guido Bonatti, see Asdente,
Who to have tended to his leather and his twine
Would now wish, but too late he repents.
See the wretched women who left the needle,
The shuttle and the spindle, and made themselves diviners;
They cast spells with herbs and with an image.
But come now, for already at the boundary
Of both hemispheres, and touching the wave
Below Seville, are Cain and the thorns.
And already last night the moon was round:
Well should you remember it, for it did you no harm
At times in the deep wood.
Thus he spoke to me, and we went on meanwhile.
