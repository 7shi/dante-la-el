# Love, Poverty, and the Church: Dante's *Paradiso*, Canto XI

In the Heaven of the Sun, Dante encounters souls distinguished by wisdom. His principal speaker is Thomas Aquinas, the Dominican theologian, who now tells the story of Francis of Assisi. The choice is deliberate: a Dominican praises the founder of the Franciscans, before criticizing the failures of his own order. In the following canto, the Franciscan Bonaventure will return the compliment by praising Dominic.

Francis's life becomes more than an exemplary biography. Through the allegory of his marriage to Poverty, Dante asks what it means to follow Christ—and why religious institutions so often lose sight of their founding purpose.

## 1. Earthly Ambition and the Freedom of Heaven

> 1 O insensata cura de' mortali,  
> (O senseless care of mortals,)
>
> 2 quanto son difettivi silogismi  
> (how defective are the syllogisms)
>
> 3 quei che ti fanno in basso batter l'ali!  
> (those that make you beat your wings downward!)

The canto begins with an apostrophe to humanity's misguided preoccupations. A syllogism is a structured argument: Dante's complaint is not simply that human beings reason badly, but that they reason from mistaken assumptions about what makes life worthwhile.

The image of wings beating downward captures this misuse of human capacities. Intelligence and desire ought to lift people toward their highest good; instead, they can become instruments of spiritual descent.

The subsequent catalogue includes law, medicine, ecclesiastical careers, political domination, theft, public business, sensual pleasure, and idleness. These activities are not all morally equivalent. Their common feature is the absorption of human attention in limited, earthly ends.

> 10 quando, da tutte queste cose sciolto,  
> (when, released from all these things,)
>
> 11 con Bëatrice m'era suso in cielo  
> (with Beatrice I was up in heaven)
>
> 12 cotanto glorïosamente accolto.  
> (so gloriously received.)

Dante contrasts these entanglements with his own temporary liberation. The contrast prepares the reader for Francis: Dante has been released from worldly cares through his heavenly journey, while Francis achieved a radical freedom from them during his earthly life.

Beatrice, his guide, connects this liberation with spiritual understanding and grace. Heavenly freedom is not mere inactivity; it is the redirection of attention toward what deserves love.

## 2. Thomas Aquinas and the Need for Distinctions

> 22 Tu dubbi, e hai voler che si ricerna  
> (You are in doubt, and wish that there be sifted)
>
> 23 in sì aperta e 'n sì distesa lingua  
> (in language so clear and so expansive)
>
> 24 lo dicer mio, ch'al tuo sentir si sterna,  
> (my saying, so that it may be laid open to your understanding,)

Thomas knows Dante's unspoken questions because the blessed perceive them in God. Yet heavenly knowledge does not eliminate the need for explanation. Thomas proceeds as a teacher, distinguishing meanings rather than merely announcing conclusions.

Dante is puzzled by two statements from the preceding canto. One concerned the Dominican order as a place where spiritual nourishment is available, provided its members do not stray. The other concerned Solomon's apparently unequalled wisdom.

This canto addresses the first difficulty; the second receives its fuller resolution in Canto XIII. The opening attack on defective reasoning therefore does not reject reason itself. Thomas models reason properly used: careful, illuminated by faith, and directed toward truth.

## 3. Francis and Dominic: Two Complementary Guides

> 35 due principi ordinò in suo favore,  
> (she appointed two princes in her favor,)
>
> 36 che quinci e quindi le fosser per guida.  
> (who on this side and on that would serve as her guides.)
>
> 37 L'un fu tutto serafico in ardore;  
> (The one was wholly seraphic in ardor;)
>
> 38 l'altro per sapïenza in terra fue  
> (the other, through wisdom, on earth was)
>
> 39 di cherubica luce uno splendore.  
> (a radiance of cherubic light.)

The appointing power is divine Providence; the beneficiary is the Church, described immediately beforehand as Christ's bride, joined to him through his blood. Francis and Dominic are the two princes sent to guide her toward her beloved.

Their contrasting descriptions draw on the traditional characteristics of the angelic orders. Seraphim signify burning love, while cherubim signify fullness of knowledge. Francis embodies ardent charity; Dominic embodies illuminating wisdom.

These are complementary gifts, not competing claims to supremacy. Love requires understanding, and understanding should issue in love. Thomas explicitly treats praise of either founder as praise of both, because their work served the same end.

The arrangement also rebukes rivalry between their followers. Both orders arose in the early thirteenth century, emphasizing preaching and forms of religious life less dependent on landed wealth than older monastic institutions. In Dante's Heaven, their original unity of purpose becomes visible again.

## 4. Assisi as the East: The Birth of a Spiritual Sun

> 49 Di questa costa, là dov' ella frange  
> (From this slope, where it breaks)
>
> 50 più sua rattezza, nacque al mondo un sole,  
> (most its steepness, a sun was born into the world,)
>
> 51 come fa questo talvolta di Gange.  
> (as this one sometimes does from the Ganges.)

Dante carefully locates Francis's birthplace on the slopes of Mount Subasio before transforming geography into spiritual symbolism. Francis, born at Assisi around 1181–1182, is a new sun.

The comparison is especially appropriate in the Heaven of the Sun. The souls surrounding Dante radiate wisdom, and Francis's life likewise brings illumination and warmth to the world.

The Ganges evokes the remote East within Dante's geographical imagination. Like the physical sun rising in the east, Francis appears as the beginning of a renewed spiritual day.

> 52 Però chi d'esso loco fa parole,  
> (Therefore whoever speaks of that place,)
>
> 53 non dica Ascesi, ché direbbe corto,  
> (should not say Ascesi, for that would say too little,)
>
> 54 ma Orïente, se proprio dir vuole.  
> (but Orient, if he wishes to speak properly.)

*Ascesi* is a medieval form of Assisi's name. Dante proposes replacing it with *Orient*: the place of rising.

The claim is poetic rather than geographical. A town acquires its fullest meaning through the sacred history that unfolds there. Francis's birth makes Assisi an eastern horizon for the Church, a place from which evangelical renewal dawns.

## 5. Francis's Marriage to Poverty

> 58 ché per tal donna, giovinetto, in guerra  
> (for, on behalf of such a lady, as a young man, into war)
>
> 59 del padre corse, a cui, come a la morte,  
> (with his father he rushed, to whom, as to death,)
>
> 60 la porta del piacer nessun diserra;  
> (no one unlocks the door of pleasure;)

The unnamed lady is Poverty. The relative clause beginning in line 59 refers to her: people welcome Poverty no more readily than they welcome death.

Francis's devotion produces conflict with his father, Pietro Bernardone, a prosperous cloth merchant. Dante recasts their historical confrontation as a chivalric struggle for a lady. The young Francis does not merely abandon wealth; he chooses a beloved whom society rejects.

In the account recalled by the following lines, Francis publicly renounces his father's possessions before the bishop of Assisi. Dante treats that renunciation as a wedding. The language of courtly love is redirected away from social prestige and toward evangelical dispossession.

> 64 Questa, privata del primo marito,  
> (She, deprived of her first husband,)
>
> 65 millecent' anni e più dispetta e scura  
> (for eleven hundred years and more, despised and obscure,)
>
> 66 fino a costui si stette sanza invito;  
> (until this man remained without invitation;)

Poverty's first husband was Christ. Francis therefore renews a bond established by Christ's own life.

The long abandonment is rhetorical, not a literal denial that Christians practiced poverty between Christ and Francis. Dante presents Francis as restoring something essential that the Christian world had largely neglected.

The allegory also exposes the distance between the Church's origins and its later wealth. If Poverty was Christ's beloved, contempt for her may reveal a failure to understand Christ himself.

> 71 sì che, dove Maria rimase giuso,  
> (so that, where Mary remained below,)
>
> 72 ella con Cristo pianse in su la croce.  
> (she wept with Christ upon the cross.)

Poverty accompanies Christ even where his mother cannot: on the cross itself. The personification makes complete dispossession a companion of the Passion. Christ dies stripped of worldly protection, property, and honor.

This is not a simple endorsement of involuntary deprivation or social neglect. Dante's primary subject is freely embraced evangelical poverty: relinquishing possessions and claims to status in order to follow Christ. The startling comparison with Mary measures Poverty's inseparability from the crucified Savior.

## 6. A Brotherhood Drawn by Joy

> 79 tanto che 'l venerabile Bernardo  
> (so much so that the venerable Bernard)
>
> 80 si scalzò prima, e dietro a tanta pace  
> (first took off his shoes, and after such great peace)
>
> 81 corse e, correndo, li parve esser tardo.  
> (ran, and, running, seemed to himself to be slow.)

Bernard of Quintavalle, a wealthy man of Assisi, was among Francis's earliest followers. His removal of his shoes marks a concrete departure from his former way of life and evokes the simplicity of apostolic discipleship.

What draws him is peace. Dante does not represent the first Franciscan community as a collection of people merely enduring deprivation. Francis and Poverty display such concord and happiness that others hurry to join them.

Bernard's sense that he is still too slow conveys the urgency of desire. Spiritual attraction, rather than coercion, generates the brotherhood.

> 82 Oh ignota ricchezza! oh ben ferace!  
> (O unknown riches! O fruitful good!)
>
> 83 Scalzasi Egidio, scalzasi Silvestro  
> (Giles takes off his shoes, Sylvester takes off his shoes)
>
> 84 dietro a lo sposo, sì la sposa piace.  
> (behind the bridegroom, so pleasing is the bride.)

The paradox of unknown riches reverses ordinary valuations. Poverty is rich because she frees her followers to receive goods that possession cannot secure: peace, fraternity, love, and closeness to God.

Giles and Sylvester join the procession, and the repeated action of taking off their shoes makes conversion visible. The marriage allegory expands into the founding of a community: the bride's attractiveness draws followers after the bridegroom.

## 7. Papal Approval: Charisma Within the Church

> 91 ma regalmente sua dura intenzione  
> (but royally his austere intention)
>
> 92 ad Innocenzio aperse, e da lui ebbe  
> (he disclosed to Innocent, and from him received)
>
> 93 primo sigillo a sua religïone.  
> (the first seal upon his religious order.)

Francis approaches Pope Innocent III with royal dignity. The apparent contradiction is central: the man who looks socially insignificant possesses the confidence of someone serving a higher authority.

Innocent's initial approval of Francis's way of life is conventionally dated to 1209–1210. Dante calls it a seal, an authorization confirming the authenticity of the vocation.

Francis's radicalism is thus not presented as separation from the Church. He seeks to renew Christian life within it. Dante can condemn ecclesiastical corruption while still recognizing legitimate ecclesiastical authority.

> 97 di seconda corona redimita  
> (adorned with a second crown)
>
> 98 fu per Onorio da l'Etterno Spiro  
> (was, through Honorius, by the Eternal Spirit)
>
> 99 la santa voglia d'esto archimandrita.  
> (the holy purpose of this shepherd of the flock.)

Pope Honorius III formally approved the Franciscan Rule in 1223. Dante describes this approval as a second crown, with the Holy Spirit acting through the pope.

The elevated term *archimandrite*, a title associated with the head of a religious community, emphasizes Francis's responsibility for his growing flock.

The papal approvals form stages in the recognition of Francis's mission. They will be surpassed, though not annulled, by a final seal given directly by Christ.

## 8. Mission, Martyrdom, and the Final Seal

> 100 E poi che, per la sete del martiro,  
> (And after, through thirst for martyrdom,)
>
> 101 ne la presenza del Soldan superba  
> (in the proud presence of the Sultan)
>
> 102 predicò Cristo e li altri che 'l seguiro,  
> (he preached Christ and the others who followed him,)

These lines refer to Francis's encounter with Sultan al-Kamil in Egypt in 1219, during the Fifth Crusade. Dante interprets the journey through the saint's desire to bear witness to Christ even at the cost of his life.

The historical encounter has acquired different emphases in later retellings. Dante's emphasis is specifically missionary and hagiographical: Francis preaches, hopes for conversion, and desires martyrdom. When he finds the audience unready for conversion, he returns to work in Italy.

His willingness to die continues the logic of his marriage to Poverty. Having surrendered possessions and social standing, he is prepared to surrender life itself.

> 106 nel crudo sasso intra Tevero e Arno  
> (on the rugged rock between Tiber and Arno)
>
> 107 da Cristo prese l'ultimo sigillo,  
> (from Christ he received the final seal,)
>
> 108 che le sue membra due anni portarno.  
> (which his limbs bore for two years.)

The rugged rock is Mount La Verna, where, according to Franciscan tradition, Francis received the stigmata in 1224, two years before his death.

The wounds are the final seal because they authenticate his conformity to Christ in his own flesh. Papal authority recognized his religious mission; now Christ marks his body with the signs of the Passion.

Dante's selection of events gives the biography a clear movement: Francis first chooses Christ's Poverty, then gathers followers, receives ecclesiastical recognition, and finally bears Christ's wounds. His life becomes an increasingly complete imitation of the Savior.

## 9. Death and the Inheritance of Poverty

> 112 a' frati suoi, sì com' a giuste rede,  
> (to his brothers, as to rightful heirs,)
>
> 113 raccomandò la donna sua più cara,  
> (he entrusted his dearest lady,)
>
> 114 e comandò che l'amassero a fede;  
> (and commanded that they love her faithfully;)

Francis's inheritance is paradoxical. He leaves his followers no wealth, but entrusts them with the faithful love of Poverty.

The language of rightful heirs makes the brothers accountable to their founder. Membership in an order does not by itself guarantee fidelity to its purpose. The inheritance must be lived.

This has particular force against the background of later Franciscan disputes over the meaning and practice of poverty. Dante does not explain those controversies here, but the passage supplies a demanding standard by which later developments can be judged.

> 115 e del suo grembo l'anima preclara  
> (and from her lap his illustrious soul)
>
> 116 mover si volle, tornando al suo regno,  
> (wished to depart, returning to its kingdom,)
>
> 117 e al suo corpo non volle altra bara.  
> (and for his body he wished no other bier.)

Francis dies in Poverty's lap. The image recalls accounts of his wish to be laid naked upon the bare earth near death in 1226.

The marriage persists to the end. Poverty is not a temporary discipline discarded after holiness has been achieved; she remains his chosen companion at the passage into eternal life.

Earthly dispossession and heavenly belonging are brought together. The body requires no splendid bier, while the soul returns to its kingdom.

## 10. Dominic's Wandering Flock

> 121 e questo fu il nostro patrïarca;  
> (and this was our patriarch;)
>
> 122 per che qual segue lui, com' el comanda,  
> (therefore whoever follows him, as he commands,)
>
> 123 discerner puoi che buone merce carca.  
> (you can discern that he loads good merchandise.)

Thomas now returns to Dominic, his own order's founder. Francis's greatness establishes the greatness of the man worthy to work beside him in guiding the Church.

The commercial image of good merchandise might seem surprising after the praise of poverty. Its point, however, is to distinguish true spiritual gain from worldly accumulation. A Dominican who genuinely follows Dominic acquires goods that advance salvation.

The qualifying phrase matters: the follower must act as Dominic commands. Institutional identity is not enough.

> 127 e quanto le sue pecore remote  
> (and the more his sheep, far removed)
>
> 128 e vagabunde più da esso vanno,  
> (and wandering, go farther from him,)
>
> 129 più tornano a l'ovil di latte vòte.  
> (the emptier of milk they return to the fold.)

The imagery changes from trade to shepherding. Dominicans are sheep wandering away from their shepherd, enticed by unfamiliar food. The attraction suggests worldly ambitions and pursuits foreign to their founding vocation.

Their failure is measured by an inability to nourish. They return without milk: religious life detached from its proper purpose ceases to feed the community spiritually.

Thomas acknowledges that some remain faithful, but insists that they are few. Because the criticism comes from a Dominican saint, it cannot be dismissed as a rival order's attack. Heavenly charity includes truthful judgment of one's own institution.

> 139 “U' ben s'impingua, se non si vaneggia”».  
> (‘Where one fattens well, if one does not stray.’”)

The closing line explains the earlier statement that troubled Dante. Growing fat is a pastoral metaphor for receiving abundant spiritual nourishment. The Dominican order provides that nourishment—but only when its members remain faithful to their vocation.

The condition is the point. A holy founder and an approved rule offer a path, not an automatic guarantee of holiness.

## Epilogue — The Wealth of Faithful Love

Canto XI moves from the restless calculations of worldly ambition to the freedom of a man who chooses Poverty as his bride. Francis's renunciation bears fruit in joy, fellowship, mission, and an ever closer likeness to Christ.

Yet the canto is also about the Church's continuing responsibility. Francis and Dominic embody complementary forms of renewal: burning love and illuminating wisdom. Their successors honor them not through names, privileges, or institutional pride, but by continuing their work.

The ultimate contrast is therefore not simply between possessions and destitution. It is between attachment that traps the soul and faithful love that makes it free.
