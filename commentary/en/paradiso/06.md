# The Eagle and the Exile: Allegory and History in Dante's *Paradiso*, Canto VI

Canto VI of *Paradiso* is a history of Rome told as a history of divine providence. Its speaker, the emperor Justinian, presents the Roman eagle as the emblem of an authority whose purpose exceeds the ambitions of individual rulers. Rome's victories, its laws, and even its involvement in Christ's death become parts of a single sacred narrative.

Dante and Beatrice are in Mercury, where they encounter souls whose good actions were partly motivated by earthly honor. Unusually, the entire canto consists of one speaker's uninterrupted speech. It also completes a political progression across the poem: Canto VI of *Inferno* concerns Florence, Canto VI of *Purgatorio* concerns Italy, and Canto VI of *Paradiso* concerns the empire and the world.

## 1. The Eagle Turns Back toward the East

> 1 «Poscia che Costantin l'aquila volse  
> (“After Constantine turned the eagle)
>
> 2 contr' al corso del ciel, ch'ella seguio  
> (against the course of heaven, which it followed)
>
> 3 dietro a l'antico che Lavina tolse,  
> (behind the ancient man who took Lavinia as wife,)

The eagle is the central symbol of the canto. Literally, it is Rome's military and imperial emblem; allegorically, it represents the enduring office of universal government. Its successive bearers are temporary, but its providential mission continues.

Constantine “turned” the eagle by transferring the imperial capital from Rome to Constantinople in 330. Dante imagines this eastward movement as contrary to the apparent westward course of the heavens. It also reverses the journey of Aeneas, the “ancient man” who traveled from Troy to Italy and married Lavinia.

The opening thus brings together classical legend, imperial history, and cosmic order. Aeneas's journey supplies Rome with a sacred prehistory: for Dante, the *Aeneid* records more than heroic adventure, because its hero unknowingly prepares the future center of world empire.

The reversal does not, however, mean that imperial legitimacy simply disappeared. The eagle remains the same sacred emblem when it eventually reaches Justinian.

## 2. Justinian: Imperial Law under Divine Love

> 10 Cesare fui e son Iustinïano,  
> (Caesar I was, and Justinian I am,)
>
> 11 che, per voler del primo amor ch'i' sento,  
> (who, by the will of the primal love that I feel,)
>
> 12 d'entro le leggi trassi il troppo e 'l vano.  
> (from within the laws removed the excessive and the useless.)

The distinction between “I was” and “I am” is significant. Justinian's imperial office belonged to mortal life; his personal identity remains in eternity. “Caesar” is a title, not his name, and earthly rank no longer determines his heavenly worth.

Justinian ruled the Eastern Roman Empire from 527 to 565. His great legal compilation, conventionally known as the *Corpus Juris Civilis*, organized and preserved Roman law. Dante compresses this vast enterprise into an act of purification: removing what was redundant or empty.

The “primal love” is God. Law, properly understood, is therefore not merely an instrument of coercion. It participates in the ordering love that governs creation. Justinian's work offers a positive image of political authority: the ruler serves justice rather than private ambition.

> 16 ma 'l benedetto Agapito, che fue  
> (but the blessed Agapetus, who was)
>
> 17 sommo pastore, a la fede sincera  
> (supreme shepherd, toward the true faith)
>
> 18 mi dirizzò con le parole sue.  
> (directed me with his words.)

Justinian says that Pope Agapetus corrected his earlier belief that Christ possessed only one nature. In orthodox Christian doctrine, Christ is one person in two natures, divine and human.

Dante follows a medieval account of Justinian's religious history that does not accurately reproduce the historical chronology: the emperor's major legal work began before Agapetus's visit to Constantinople in 536. The canto reshapes the sequence so that religious correction prepares the way for legislative achievement.

Its exemplary meaning is clear. Spiritual guidance and temporal government should cooperate without becoming identical. The pope corrects the emperor's faith; the emperor carries out his own proper work of law and administration.

## 3. Rome's Beginnings: Sacrifice before Sovereignty

> 34 Vedi quanta virtù l'ha fatto degno  
> (See how much valor has made it worthy)
>
> 35 di reverenza; e cominciò da l'ora  
> (of reverence; and it began from the hour)
>
> 36 che Pallante morì per darli regno.  
> (when Pallas died to give it a kingdom.)

Pallas, the young ally of Aeneas killed by Turnus in the *Aeneid*, becomes an early martyr to Rome's future. He did not historically fight beneath a Roman imperial standard: Dante projects the eagle backward into the legendary events that made Rome possible.

This is how the canto's symbolism works. The eagle gathers many different peoples, institutions, and historical periods into one continuous subject. What appears to be a succession of disconnected conflicts becomes the gradual establishment of a providential authority.

Pallas's death also introduces a moral cost. The empire is not founded solely through triumphant conquest; its beginnings include loyalty, grief, and sacrifice.

> 37 Tu sai ch'el fece in Alba sua dimora  
> (You know that it made its dwelling in Alba)
>
> 38 per trecento anni e oltre, infino al fine  
> (for three hundred years and more, until the end)
>
> 39 che i tre a' tre pugnar per lui ancora.  
> (when the three fought the three for it once more.)

Alba Longa traditionally connected the descendants of Aeneas with the founders of Rome. The “three” fighting “three” are the Roman Horatii and the Alban Curiatii, whose legendary combat decided which city would hold supremacy.

Dante treats the transition from Alba to Rome as another movement of the same imperial destiny. His subsequent catalogue of kings, republican heroes, and wars develops this continuity. Institutions change, but the eagle remains the organizing symbol.

The passage is therefore not a neutral summary of Roman history. It selects and arranges the past to demonstrate that Rome's authority was prepared over centuries.

## 4. Julius Caesar and the Gathering of World History

> 55 Poi, presso al tempo che tutto 'l ciel volle  
> (Then, near the time when all heaven willed)
>
> 56 redur lo mondo a suo modo sereno,  
> (to bring the world to its own serene condition,)
>
> 57 Cesare per voler di Roma il tolle.  
> (Caesar, by Rome's will, takes it up.)

Julius Caesar receives the eagle as history approaches the birth of Christ. The heavenly desire for a “serene” world points toward the peace and political unity that Dante believes prepared humanity for the Incarnation.

Calling Caesar's action “Rome's will” is a strongly interpretive choice. The historical Caesar's rise involved civil conflict and the destruction of republican political arrangements. Dante instead emphasizes his role in the establishment of a universal monarchy.

Behind this reading stands the political ideal also developed in Dante's *Monarchia*: a universal temporal ruler could restrain competing ambitions and secure the peace necessary for human flourishing.

> 61 Quel che fé poi ch'elli uscì di Ravenna  
> (What it did after he came out of Ravenna)
>
> 62 e saltò Rubicon, fu di tal volo,  
> (and crossed the Rubicon, was of such flight,)
>
> 63 che nol seguiteria lingua né penna.  
> (that neither tongue nor pen could follow it.)

The crossing of the Rubicon in 49 BCE began Caesar's civil war against his opponents. Dante transforms the ensuing campaigns into the eagle's astonishing “flight.”

The imagery shifts agency away from Caesar alone. The emperor acts, but the sacred standard also seems to act through him. Military speed becomes a sign of the momentum of providential history.

That symbolism should not be mistaken for an impartial verdict on conquest. The canto makes a theological argument about Rome's destiny, rather than examining the experiences of everyone Rome defeated.

## 5. Augustus: Victory Fulfilled in Peace

> 73 Di quel che fé col baiulo seguente,  
> (Of what it did with the next bearer,)
>
> 74 Bruto con Cassio ne l'inferno latra,  
> (Brutus with Cassius howls in hell,)
>
> 75 e Modena e Perugia fu dolente.  
> (and Modena and Perugia were sorrowful.)

The “next bearer” is Octavian, later Augustus. The word *baiulo*, meaning bearer or carrier, makes the emperor the custodian of an authority larger than himself.

Brutus and Cassius, Caesar's assassins, appear in Dante's lowest Hell alongside Judas. Their punishment expresses the poem's severe judgment on betrayal of the institutions Dante considers providential: Judas betrayed Christ, while Brutus and Cassius betrayed Caesar.

Modena and Perugia recall the violence through which Octavian secured power. Dante does not conceal the suffering, but he incorporates it into a narrative moving toward political unity.

> 79 Con costui corse infino al lito rubro;  
> (With this man it ran as far as the Red Sea shore;)
>
> 80 con costui puose il mondo in tanta pace,  
> (with this man it placed the world in such peace,)
>
> 81 che fu serrato a Giano il suo delubro.  
> (that Janus's temple was closed.)

The closing of the temple of Janus was a Roman sign of peace. Under Augustus, the eagle achieves its temporal goal not simply by overcoming enemies, but by bringing warfare to an end.

For Dante, this Augustan peace forms the historical setting of Christ's birth. Pagan Rome unwittingly prepares the world for Christian salvation. Classical history is neither discarded nor treated as religiously self-sufficient: it becomes a preliminary stage in a larger sacred story.

## 6. The Crucifixion: The Eagle's Most Difficult Glory

> 88 ché la viva giustizia che mi spira,  
> (for the living justice that inspires me,)
>
> 89 li concedette, in mano a quel ch'i' dico,  
> (granted it, in the hand of the one I mean,)
>
> 90 gloria di far vendetta a la sua ira.  
> (the glory of executing vengeance for its wrath.)

The emperor meant here is Tiberius, identified just before this passage as the “third Caesar.” During his reign, Christ was crucified under the Roman governor Pontius Pilate.

Dante makes a startling claim: the eagle's greatest achievement is not military conquest but its role in the Crucifixion. Within his theology, Christ bears the punishment due to fallen humanity, and Roman jurisdiction supplies the universal public authority under which that punishment occurs.

This does not mean that the people responsible for Christ's death acted with innocent intentions. The paradox depends on distinguishing the divine purpose of redemption from the guilt of human actions. Canto VII will explain the difficulty more fully.

> 91 Or qui t'ammira in ciò ch'io ti replìco:  
> (Now marvel here at what I add to you:)
>
> 92 poscia con Tito a far vendetta corse  
> (afterward with Titus it ran to take vengeance)
>
> 93 de la vendetta del peccato antico.  
> (for the vengeance of the ancient sin.)

Titus destroyed Jerusalem in 70 CE. Following a medieval Christian interpretation, Dante presents that destruction as punishment for the Crucifixion: “vengeance” for an act that itself fulfilled divine vengeance against original sin.

The formulation deliberately exposes a theological puzzle. How can the same event be just as an instrument of salvation and unjust as an act committed against Christ?

This passage also carries an anti-Jewish interpretation of Jerusalem's destruction. It must be understood as part of Dante's inherited Christian polemic, not accepted as a historical explanation or as a valid attribution of collective Jewish guilt.

## 7. Charlemagne and the Defense of the Church

> 94 E quando il dente longobardo morse  
> (And when the Lombard tooth bit)
>
> 95 la Santa Chiesa, sotto le sue ali  
> (the Holy Church, beneath its wings)
>
> 96 Carlo Magno, vincendo, la soccorse.  
> (Charlemagne, conquering, came to its aid.)

The “Lombard tooth” personifies the Lombard kingdom as a predator threatening the Church. Charlemagne's defeat of the Lombards in 774 made him the papacy's powerful protector; his imperial coronation followed in 800.

Dante places Charlemagne beneath the eagle's wings, presenting his victories as a continuation of Rome's providential mission. This is a medieval claim of imperial continuity, not a simple account of uninterrupted Roman institutions.

The contrast between biting teeth and sheltering wings gives political history an emblematic clarity. Legitimate imperial power protects the conditions in which spiritual life can flourish.

The scene also prevents the canto's defense of empire from becoming merely an attack on the papacy. Dante's ideal is cooperation between distinct authorities, not the destruction of either one.

## 8. Guelfs and Ghibellines: Two Ways to Betray the Eagle

> 100 L'uno al pubblico segno i gigli gialli  
> (One, against the public standard, the yellow lilies)
>
> 101 oppone, e l'altro appropria quello a parte,  
> (sets up, and the other appropriates that standard for a faction,)
>
> 102 sì ch'è forte a veder chi più si falli.  
> (so that it is hard to see who errs more.)

Justinian now turns sacred history into a judgment on Dante's contemporary Italy.

The Guelfs generally supported the papal side, while the Ghibellines claimed allegiance to the empire, although actual political interests were more complicated. The “yellow lilies” evoke the French and Angevin dynastic emblem, particularly the power of Charles II of Anjou.

Both factions corrupt the eagle's meaning. The Guelfs oppose the universal imperial standard with a dynastic one; the Ghibellines convert that supposedly universal standard into the property of a party. The decisive word is “public”: authority intended for the common good cannot rightfully belong to a faction.

> 103 Faccian li Ghibellin, faccian lor arte  
> (Let the Ghibellines practice, let them practice their schemes)
>
> 104 sott' altro segno, ché mal segue quello  
> (under another standard, for badly follows that one)
>
> 105 sempre chi la giustizia e lui diparte;  
> (always whoever separates justice from it;)

Dante's imperial sympathies do not amount to unconditional support for everyone using the imperial name. A Ghibelline who separates the eagle from justice betrays it as surely as an open enemy does.

This is the canto's central political distinction: allegiance to a symbol is not enough. Power becomes legitimate through its service to justice and the common good. The long Roman history establishes the dignity of the imperial office so that present-day abuses can be measured against it.

## 9. Mercury: Good Works and Imperfect Motives

> 112 Questa picciola stella si correda  
> (This little star is adorned)
>
> 113 d'i buoni spirti che son stati attivi  
> (with the good spirits who were active)
>
> 114 perché onore e fama li succeda:  
> (so that honor and fame might follow them:)

Mercury displays souls whose active lives produced real good but whose motives included the desire for reputation. Justinian's greatness therefore does not remove him from moral scrutiny. Even an emperor serving providential history may love earthly glory too strongly.

These souls are saved, not punished. Their appearance in Mercury makes a difference in spiritual attainment visible to Dante. As explained elsewhere in *Paradiso*, the blessed have their true dwelling together in the Empyrean; the planetary appearances accommodate divine realities to the pilgrim's understanding.

The canto distinguishes the value of an achievement from the purity of the desire behind it. Public service can be admirable without being wholly free of self-interest.

> 124 Diverse voci fanno dolci note;  
> (Different voices make sweet notes;)
>
> 125 così diversi scanni in nostra vita  
> (thus different seats in our life)
>
> 126 rendon dolce armonia tra queste rote.  
> (render sweet harmony among these spheres.)

Unequal degrees of blessedness produce no envy. The souls understand divine justice and desire precisely the order it establishes.

The musical image answers the political disorders described earlier. On earth, difference becomes rivalry because parties seek exclusive advantage. In heaven, difference becomes harmony because every will participates in a common good.

True unity therefore does not require sameness. Like distinct voices in music, different persons can contribute to one order without losing their individuality.

## 10. Romeo: The Just Servant Sent into Exile

> 133 Quattro figlie ebbe, e ciascuna reina,  
> (Four daughters he had, and each a queen,)
>
> 134 Ramondo Beringhiere, e ciò li fece  
> (Raimond Berenger, and this was achieved for him by)
>
> 135 Romeo, persona umìle e peregrina.  
> (Romeo, a humble person and a stranger.)

The canto ends by turning from emperors to Romeo of Villeneuve, the servant of Count Raimond Berenger IV of Provence. The count's four daughters all married kings, and Dante attributes this extraordinary dynastic success to Romeo's service.

The historical Romeo was a substantial administrator rather than simply the wandering stranger of the legend Dante follows. In that legend, an outsider enriches his master, attracts the envy of courtiers, and suffers disgrace despite his innocence.

His story brings the canto's political ideals down to the scale of one human life. Like the ideal emperor, the good servant advances another's welfare rather than treating office as an opportunity for private gain.

> 136 E poi il mosser le parole biece  
> (And then the crooked words moved him)
>
> 137 a dimandar ragione a questo giusto,  
> (to demand an accounting from this just man,)
>
> 138 che li assegnò sette e cinque per diece,  
> (who rendered him seven and five for ten,)
>
> 139 indi partissi povero e vetusto;  
> (then departed poor and old;)

The “crooked words” are envious accusations. They persuade the count to demand an account from a faithful servant who has returned twelve for every ten entrusted to him.

Romeo's financial integrity is made arithmetically plain, but proof does not restore the relationship. He leaves impoverished and aged. Earthly judgment fails precisely where heavenly judgment sees clearly.

The parallel with Dante's own exile is strong. Dante likewise understood himself as a public servant wronged by faction and forced into dependence on others. Romeo becomes a figure through whom the poet can represent the dignity of the unjustly displaced.

> 140 e se 'l mondo sapesse il cor ch'elli ebbe  
> (and if the world knew the heart he had)
>
> 141 mendicando sua vita a frusto a frusto,  
> (begging for his livelihood morsel by morsel,)
>
> 142 assai lo loda, e più lo loderebbe».  
> (it praises him greatly, and would praise him more.”)

The final measure of Romeo's worth is not his administrative success but the courage with which he endured humiliation.

That ending quietly transforms the canto's opening scale. The eagle's conquests encompass continents, yet divine justice also attends to an old man begging for bread. A world dazzled by imperial victories may fail to recognize an equally important moral greatness in suffering borne with integrity.

## Epilogue — From Universal Empire to Unrecognized Goodness

Canto VI presents the Roman eagle as the emblem of a universal justice that no ruler, dynasty, or faction may rightfully possess for itself. Its sacred history culminates in a political demand: authority must serve the common good rather than private ambition.

Yet the canto also looks beyond political achievement. Justinian's desire for fame and Romeo's undeserved disgrace reveal the limits of earthly judgment. Heaven measures both inward motives and hidden endurance. The harmony denied by partisan history is finally found in a justice that recognizes the emperor and the exile alike.
