# Love, Sight, and the First Human Voice: *Paradiso* XXVI

In the Heaven of the Fixed Stars, Dante undergoes examinations on the three theological virtues: faith, hope, and charity. Saint Peter has tested his faith, and Saint James his hope. Now Saint John examines his love. Afterwards, Adam appears and answers questions about humanity's first disobedience, its original language, and its brief innocence.

The canto connects these apparently different subjects through a single concern: how human beings should direct their God-given powers of understanding, loving, and speaking.

## 1. Temporary Blindness and the Examination of Love

> 7 Comincia dunque; e dì ove s'appunta  
> (Begin, then; and say where is directed)
>
> 8 l'anima tua, e fa ragion che sia  
> (your soul, and reckon that there is)
>
> 9 la vista in te smarrita e non defunta:  
> (sight in you lost and not dead:)

Dante begins the canto temporarily blinded by Saint John's radiance. In the preceding canto, he had strained to discover whether John possessed a physical body in Heaven, following a tradition that John had been assumed bodily into paradise. John corrected that belief: his body remains on earth.

The blindness therefore follows an attempt to see more than Dante's present powers allow. Yet John explicitly distinguishes temporary loss from permanent destruction. Dante's sight needs restoration, not replacement.

While physical vision is suspended, intellectual and spiritual attention must become sharper. John asks where Dante's soul “points”: what is the ultimate object of its desire? The examination concerns charity, the theological virtue by which one loves God above all things and other beings in relation to God.

John also promises that Beatrice can restore Dante's sight, comparing her power to the healing performed by Ananias upon Saul in Acts 9. That biblical background makes restored vision a sign of spiritual illumination as well as bodily recovery.

## 2. God as the Beginning and End of Love

> 16 Lo ben che fa contenta questa corte,  
> (The good that makes this court content,)
>
> 17 Alfa e O è di quanta scrittura  
> (is Alpha and O of all the writing)
>
> 18 mi legge Amore o lievemente o forte».  
> (Love reads to me, whether softly or forcefully.”)

Dante's answer identifies God as the supreme object of his love. “This court” is the heavenly community, whose happiness consists in possessing and contemplating divine goodness.

“Alfa e O” means Alpha and Omega, the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet. The expression recalls Revelation, traditionally attributed to John, in which God declares himself the beginning and the end. Dante thus answers John partly through language associated with John's own scriptural authority.

The metaphor of writing makes Dante's entire experience of love into a text. Whatever Love teaches him, quietly or powerfully, begins and ends in God. This does not require the disappearance of his love for Beatrice. Rather, personal love finds its proper meaning within a larger order: created beauty can lead the lover towards the goodness from which it comes.

The canto's task is to establish that this declaration is more than an impressive formula. John now asks what grounds it.

## 3. Reason and Revelation Converge on Divine Goodness

> 28 ché 'l bene, in quanto ben, come s'intende,  
> (for the good, insofar as it is good, as it is understood,)
>
> 29 così accende amore, e tanto maggio  
> (so kindles love, and love so much greater)
>
> 30 quanto più di bontate in sé comprende.  
> (the more goodness it contains within itself.)
>
> 31 Dunque a l'essenza ov' è tanto avvantaggio,  
> (Therefore towards the essence in which there is such superiority,)
>
> 32 che ciascun ben che fuor di lei si trova  
> (that every good found outside it)
>
> 33 altro non è ch'un lume di suo raggio,  
> (is nothing other than a light from its ray,)

Dante begins with a philosophical account of desire: goodness, once apprehended, awakens love. The greater the goodness perceived, the greater the love that should follow.

God is not merely the greatest item in a collection of good things. God is the source of the goodness present in everything else. The image of ray and reflected light expresses the dependence of created goods upon divine goodness.

The reasoning belongs to the medieval synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian theology. Shortly afterwards, Dante invokes the philosopher who explains the first object of love of the eternal substances, generally understood as Aristotle. In Aristotle's account, the highest principle moves the heavens as an object of desire.

Dante's conclusion concerns properly informed love: a mind that truly understands the relation between God and created goodness must love God supremely. The problem is not that finite goods are false, but that human beings mistake their relative goodness for something absolute.

> 40 Sternel la voce del verace autore,  
> (The voice of the truthful author sets it forth,)
>
> 41 che dice a Moïsè, di sé parlando:  
> (who says to Moses, speaking of himself:)
>
> 42 ‘Io ti farò vedere ogne valore’.  
> (‘I shall make you see every excellence’.)
>
> 43 Sternilmi tu ancora, incominciando  
> (You too set it forth for me, when beginning)
>
> 44 l'alto preconio che grida l'arcano  
> (the lofty proclamation that cries out the mystery)

Revelation confirms what philosophical reasoning establishes. God's promise to Moses recalls Exodus 33:19, where God declares that he will make his goodness pass before him. Dante presents divine goodness as the comprehensive source of every excellence.

The second witness is John himself. The “lofty proclamation” is the opening of John's Gospel: the Word exists from the beginning, is with God, and is God; all things come into being through the Word.

Reason and Scripture do not compete here. They converge upon the same truth, although revelation places that truth within the fuller Christian understanding of creation and divine life.

## 4. Charity Becomes Personal: Creation, Redemption, and Hope

> 58 ché l'essere del mondo e l'esser mio,  
> (for the being of the world and my own being,)
>
> 59 la morte ch'el sostenne perch' io viva,  
> (the death that he endured so that I might live,)
>
> 60 e quel che spera ogne fedel com' io,  
> (and what every faithful person hopes for, as I do,)
>
> 61 con la predetta conoscenza viva,  
> (together with the aforesaid living knowledge,)
>
> 62 tratto m'hanno del mar de l'amor torto,  
> (have drawn me from the sea of misdirected love,)
>
> 63 e del diritto m'han posto a la riva.  
> (and have set me on the shore of the right.)

John presses beyond an intellectual account of goodness: what else draws Dante towards God?

Dante names creation, his own existence, Christ's saving death, and the eternal happiness promised to the faithful. Charity arises not only from recognizing what God is, but also from recognizing what God has done.

The movement from “the world” to “my own being,” and from Christ's death to “so that I might live,” makes universal doctrine personally urgent. Dante understands himself as the recipient of gifts he did not create and could not earn by his own power.

The sea of “misdirected love” recalls the poem's larger account of moral disorder. Sin is not simply the possession of desire; it is desire turned towards the wrong object or exercised in the wrong measure. Salvation reorders love rather than extinguishing it.

> 64 Le fronde onde s'infronda tutto l'orto  
> (The leaves with which the whole garden is clothed,)
>
> 65 de l'ortolano etterno, am' io cotanto  
> (of the eternal gardener, I love so much)
>
> 66 quanto da lui a lor di bene è porto».  
> (as goodness is bestowed upon them by him.”)

Dante now extends his answer from God to God's creatures. The universe becomes a garden, and creatures its foliage. God is the eternal gardener whose goodness nourishes the whole.

This is not a rejection of the created world. Proper love of God makes possible a proper love of other beings, according to the goodness God gives them. Charity embraces creation without confusing creation with its source.

The garden imagery also prepares for Adam. Before the canto returns to the first garden, Dante describes the right relationship between its gardener and its living growth.

## 5. Beatrice Restores a Stronger Vision

> 76 così de li occhi miei ogne quisquilia  
> (so from my eyes every mote)
>
> 77 fugò Beatrice col raggio d'i suoi,  
> (Beatrice drove away with the ray of hers,)
>
> 78 che rifulgea da più di mille milia:  
> (which shone from more than a thousand miles away:)
>
> 79 onde mei che dinanzi vidi poi;  
> (so that afterwards I saw better than before;)

Dante's successful declaration of charity is followed by a heavenly hymn. Beatrice then restores his sight, and he sees better than he did before the temporary blindness.

Her healing completes John's promise. The imagery is strikingly intimate: the eyes through which Dante first encountered the transforming power of love now remove the impediments to a higher vision.

Beatrice remains both a person and a bearer of theological meaning. Her illumination represents the assistance by which human understanding becomes capable of receiving truths beyond its unaided powers.

The sequence also suggests a relationship between love and knowledge. After Dante has articulated the proper ordering of love, his capacity to see increases. The canto does not set reason against love: purified love helps make fuller understanding possible.

## 6. Adam: Humanity's First Father in the Divine Mirror

> 91 E cominciai: «O pomo che maturo  
> (And I began: “O fruit that ripe)
>
> 92 solo prodotto fosti, o padre antico  
> (alone were brought forth, O ancient father)
>
> 93 a cui ciascuna sposa è figlia e nuro,  
> (to whom every bride is daughter and daughter-in-law,)

With restored vision, Dante discovers another radiant soul: Adam.

Calling Adam a fruit produced already ripe emphasizes his exceptional beginning. Unlike his descendants, Adam did not pass through infancy and childhood. He entered existence as a mature human being, directly created by God.

The address also places all subsequent human history within one family. Every bride is Adam's daughter through descent, and his daughter-in-law because her husband also descends from him.

This encounter follows the examination in charity for a reason. Dante has just spoken about creation, redemption, and rightly ordered love. Adam's story concerns the original creation of humanity and the first decisive disordering of its will.

> 106 perch' io la veggio nel verace speglio  
> (because I see it in the truthful mirror)
>
> 107 che fa di sé pareglio a l'altre cose,  
> (that makes of itself a likeness of other things,)
>
> 108 e nulla face lui di sé pareglio.  
> (and nothing makes of itself a likeness of him.)

Adam already knows Dante's unspoken questions because he sees them in God, the “truthful mirror.”

The image expresses both divine knowledge and divine transcendence. God perfectly knows and represents created things, but no created thing can provide an adequate likeness of God.

Adam's knowledge is therefore not an independent power of mind-reading. It belongs to the blessed soul's participation in divine illumination. Dante's private desire is visible within the knowledge that embraces all creation.

## 7. The Fall Was a Transgression of the Limit

> 115 Or, figluol mio, non il gustar del legno  
> (Now, my son, not the tasting of the tree)
>
> 116 fu per sé la cagion di tanto essilio,  
> (was in itself the cause of so great an exile,)
>
> 117 ma solamente il trapassar del segno.  
> (but only the passing beyond the boundary.)

Adam distinguishes the material act from its moral meaning. Eating the forbidden fruit was not, considered merely as eating, the essential cause of humanity's exile. The sin lay in violating the limit established by God.

This does not dismiss the commandment. It explains why the act mattered: the human will rejected its proper relationship to its creator.

The “boundary” connects the Fall to one of the *Comedy*'s recurring concerns. Human powers are good, but their goodness does not make them unlimited. Desire and intelligence become destructive when they refuse the conditions under which they were given.

Adam's answer also completes the contrast with Dante's confession of charity. Dante describes love returning to its proper source; Adam describes the will's original departure from that order.

## 8. Adam's Long Exile and the History of Redemption

> 118 Quindi onde mosse tua donna Virgilio,  
> (From the place whence your lady set Virgil in motion,)
>
> 119 quattromilia trecento e due volumi  
> (four thousand three hundred and two revolutions)
>
> 120 di sol desiderai questo concilio;  
> (of the sun I longed for this assembly;)
>
> 121 e vidi lui tornare a tutt' i lumi  
> (and I saw it return to all the lights)
>
> 122 de la sua strada novecento trenta  
> (along its path nine hundred and thirty)
>
> 123 fïate, mentre ch'ïo in terra fu'mi.  
> (times, while I remained upon the earth.)

Adam now gives the chronology of his earthly life and his waiting after death.

The place from which Beatrice summoned Virgil is Limbo. Adam remained there for 4,302 years before Christ's descent to the dead released the righteous ancestors. His earthly life lasted 930 years, following Genesis 5:5.

These numbers belong to medieval biblical chronology. Within the poem's calendar, they place Adam's creation approximately 6,498 years before Dante's journey in 1300.

More important than the arithmetic is the structure of history it expresses: creation, disobedience, prolonged exile, and redemption through Christ. Adam's longing for the heavenly assembly gives that immense chronology an emotional centre.

The reference to Virgil also binds universal history to Dante's particular rescue. Beatrice's intervention at the beginning of the poem forms part of the same larger movement from estrangement towards restored communion.

## 9. Human Language Is Natural, but Languages Change

> 124 La lingua ch'io parlai fu tutta spenta  
> (The language that I spoke was wholly extinguished)
>
> 125 innanzi che a l'ovra inconsummabile  
> (before the work that could not be completed)
>
> 126 fosse la gente di Nembròt attenta:  
> (occupied the people of Nimrod:)

Adam overturns the expectation that his original language survived unchanged until the Tower of Babel. It had already disappeared before the people associated with Nimrod began their impossible undertaking.

The “work that could not be completed” is Babel's tower, whose builders sought to reach Heaven. Their project offers another instance of the human refusal of limits.

This passage also revises the account Dante had offered in *De vulgari eloquentia*, where he treated the original language as surviving among the Hebrews after Babel. Here, linguistic change is more fundamental: even humanity's first language was subject to historical transformation.

> 130 Opera naturale è ch'uom favella;  
> (It is a natural operation that a human being speaks;)
>
> 131 ma così o così, natura lascia  
> (but in this way or that, nature leaves)
>
> 132 poi fare a voi secondo che v'abbella.  
> (you then to act according to what pleases you.)

Adam distinguishes the natural faculty of speech from the particular forms speech takes. Human beings speak by nature; whether they speak one language or another depends upon human practice.

Language is therefore neither wholly arbitrary nor permanently fixed. The capacity is natural, while its vocabulary and forms belong to changing communities.

This helps explain the significance of Dante's own vernacular poetry. A language need not preserve an unaltered sacred origin to communicate the highest truths. Historical, changing human speech can still become the vehicle of theological understanding.

> 134 I s'appellava in terra il sommo bene  
> (I was the earthly name of the highest good)
>
> 135 onde vien la letizia che mi fascia;  
> (from whom comes the joy that clothes me;)
>
> 136 e El si chiamò poi: e ciò convene,  
> (and afterwards he was called El: and this is fitting,)
>
> 137 ché l'uso d'i mortali è come fronda  
> (because the usage of mortals is like a leaf)
>
> 138 in ramo, che sen va e altra vene.  
> (on a branch, which departs and another comes.)

Even the human name for God changes. Adam says that the supreme good was first called “I,” and later “El.” The divine reality remains the same while its human designation alters.

The comparison of linguistic usage to successive leaves recalls the classical image of generations passing like foliage, especially as adapted by Horace to the life of words.

It also echoes the canto's earlier garden imagery. Creatures receive goodness from the eternal gardener, yet their historical forms are transient. Constancy belongs ultimately to God, not to the words by which mortals name him.

## 10. The Few Hours of Earthly Innocence

> 139 Nel monte che si leva più da l'onda,  
> (On the mountain that rises highest above the water,)
>
> 140 fu' io, con vita pura e disonesta,  
> (I remained, with life pure and dishonoured,)
>
> 141 da la prim' ora a quella che seconda,  
> (from the first hour to the one that follows,)
>
> 142 come 'l sol muta quadra, l'ora sesta».  
> (as the sun changes quadrant, the sixth hour.”)

Adam's final answer concerns the duration of his stay in Eden. The highest mountain above the sea is the mountain of Purgatory, whose summit contains the Earthly Paradise.

His residence lasted from the first hour to the hour following the sixth—traditionally understood as roughly from dawn until the beginning of the seventh hour, around noon. This brief interval included both his innocent and his fallen condition.

The contrast with the preceding chronology is immense. A few hours in the garden are followed by centuries of mortal life and millennia of waiting for redemption.

The geography also binds Adam's story to Dante's. Dante has already passed through the garden Adam lost. His ascent retraces, in reverse, humanity's movement away from its original order: purification restores the freedom necessary for rightly directed love.

The canto ends not with an elaborate denunciation of Adam, but with the stark brevity of innocence—and with Adam himself already redeemed.

## Epilogue — Love Restored, Limits Understood

*Paradiso* XXVI joins the examination of charity to the history of humanity's beginnings. Dante explains how love becomes rightly ordered; Adam explains how that order was first broken.

Blindness gives way to clearer sight, misdirected desire to charity, and exile to heavenly belonging. Meanwhile, Adam's account of language distinguishes eternal truth from the changing human forms that express it.

The canto's governing lesson is not that human powers should be suppressed. Love, understanding, and speech reach their fulfilment when they acknowledge their source, their limits, and their proper end in God.
