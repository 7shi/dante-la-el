# The Order of Grace: Allegory and Background in *Paradiso*, Canto XXXII

In Canto XXXII, Dante stands before the celestial rose: the immense assembly of the blessed in the Empyrean, the heaven beyond physical space and time. His final guide, Saint Bernard of Clairvaux, explains its arrangement before preparing him for the vision of God.

The rose is more than a beautiful setting. Its architecture makes salvation history visible: the relationship between the Old and New Testaments, the effects of original sin, the mystery of grace, and the interdependence of the blessed. For Dante, its inhabitants are historical persons, not merely symbols; nevertheless, their positions also reveal the spiritual meaning of history.

## 1. Bernard Becomes a Teacher: Eve and Mary

> 1 Affetto al suo piacer, quel contemplante  
> (Absorbed in his delight, that contemplative)
>
> 2 libero officio di dottore assunse,  
> (freely assumed the office of teacher,)

The contemplative is Bernard, the twelfth-century Cistercian abbot renowned for his spiritual teaching and devotion to Mary. Having replaced Beatrice as Dante's guide, he temporarily turns from contemplation to instruction.

His teaching is freely offered: charity moves him to share what he sees. The passage does not oppose contemplation and intellectual explanation. Instead, Bernard's understanding of heavenly order grows out of his loving attention to it.

> 4 «La piaga che Maria richiuse e unse,  
> (“The wound that Mary closed and anointed,)
>
> 5 quella ch'è tanto bella da' suoi piedi  
> (she who is so beautiful at her feet)
>
> 6 è colei che l'aperse e che la punse.  
> (is she who opened it and pierced it.)

The woman beneath Mary is Eve. The wound is humanity's estrangement from God through the Fall; Mary helps heal it through her role in the Incarnation.

This contrast belongs to the ancient Christian interpretation of Mary as the “new Eve.” Eve's disobedience is answered by Mary's consent to God's will. Dante gives that theological relationship a visible shape by placing the two women in one vertical line.

Yet Eve appears beautiful and blessed. Heaven does not preserve her simply as an accusation against humanity. Her presence shows that the history begun in sin can end in reconciliation.

## 2. The Rose as a Map of Salvation History

> 19 perché, secondo lo sguardo che fée  
> (because, according to the gaze that was directed)
>
> 20 la fede in Cristo, queste sono il muro  
> (by faith in Christ, these women are the wall)
>
> 21 a che si parton le sacre scalee.  
> (at which the sacred tiers divide.)
>
> 22 Da questa parte onde 'l fiore è maturo  
> (On this side where the flower is mature)
>
> 23 di tutte le sue foglie, sono assisi  
> (in all its petals, are seated)
>
> 24 quei che credettero in Cristo venturo;  
> (those who believed in Christ yet to come;)

The women descending beneath Mary form one boundary between the rose's two halves. On one side sit those who, in Dante's Christian understanding of biblical history, believed in the coming Christ; on the other are those who believed in Christ after his coming.

The distinction concerns the direction of faith's gaze: forward in expectation or backward toward an accomplished Incarnation. Both groups belong to one flower.

The preceding list includes Sarah, Rebecca, Judith, and Ruth, identified indirectly as David's great-grandmother. David's penitential *Miserere mei*, the opening of Psalm 51 in modern numbering, recalls his repentance after grave sin. The genealogy of salvation thus includes failure, repentance, and mercy.

Rachel sits with Beatrice. Rachel traditionally represents the contemplative life, making her companionship with Dante's former guide especially appropriate. These women form an ordered sequence of scriptural and spiritual relationships, not merely a catalogue of distinguished ancestors.

The pre-Christian half is already full, while the Christian half still has empty seats. The vision occurs within the poem's historical present, before the completion of human history.

## 3. John the Baptist and the Opposite Boundary

> 31 così di contra quel del gran Giovanni,  
> (so, opposite, that of the great John,)
>
> 32 che sempre santo 'l diserto e 'l martiro  
> (who, holy throughout, the wilderness and martyrdom)
>
> 33 sofferse, e poi l'inferno da due anni;  
> (endured, and then hell for two years;)
>
> 34 e sotto lui così cerner sortiro  
> (and beneath him were likewise assigned to divide)
>
> 35 Francesco, Benedetto e Augustino  
> (Francis, Benedict, and Augustine)

Opposite Mary stands John the Baptist, with major Christian saints beneath him. Their seats form the other dividing line between the two communities of faith.

John is a particularly fitting boundary figure. He announces Christ while belonging to the world awaiting redemption. His holiness from before birth recalls the Gospel account of his response to Christ while still in Elizabeth's womb.

His stay in “hell” means Limbo, not punishment for personal wickedness. According to the Christian tradition Dante follows, the righteous dead awaited Christ's descent to release them. John's roughly two-year wait places even this exceptionally holy figure within the shared chronology of redemption.

Francis, Benedict, and Augustine represent major forms of Western religious life. They are not an exhaustive history of the Church, but their presence makes monastic and communal devotion part of the rose's structure.

Bernard adds that the two forms of faith will ultimately fill the garden equally. Expectation and fulfilment are therefore balanced within one providential design.

## 4. The Children: Salvation Without Personal Achievement

> 42 per nullo proprio merito si siede,  
> (one sits through no merit of one's own,)
>
> 43 ma per l'altrui, con certe condizioni:  
> (but through another's, under certain conditions:)
>
> 44 ché tutti questi son spiriti ascolti  
> (for all these are spirits released)
>
> 45 prima ch'avesser vere elezïoni.  
> (before they had true powers of choice.)

Below the rose's middle level sit those who died before reaching the age of moral responsibility. Their childish faces and voices make their identity perceptible.

These children have not earned their places through freely chosen good actions. Their salvation depends on grace and on the religious conditions Bernard subsequently describes. The passage therefore prevents the rose from being understood simply as a ranking of human accomplishments.

It also introduces a difficult question: if these souls had no opportunity to acquire different personal merits, why do they occupy different levels?

Dante does not dismiss the question. Bernard recognizes it before Dante speaks, making the children's unequal positions the occasion for an inquiry into the limits of human explanations of divine justice.

## 5. Unequal Grace and the Limits of Explanation

> 64 le menti tutte nel suo lieto aspetto  
> (all minds in his joyous presence,)
>
> 65 creando, a suo piacer di grazia dota  
> (creating, he endows with grace at his pleasure)
>
> 66 diversamente; e qui basti l'effetto.  
> (differently; and here let the effect suffice.)

The subject is God, the king whose kingdom rests in love and delight. Bernard explains the differences among the children through the different gifts of grace bestowed at their creation.

This is not a modern account of equality. Dante's heaven contains genuine differences in spiritual capacity and glory. Nevertheless, throughout *Paradiso*, differing degrees of blessedness do not generate envy: every soul's will is fulfilled in agreement with God.

The decisive phrase is “here let the effect suffice.” Bernard identifies divine generosity as the source of difference but does not supply a further explanation for every divine choice. The reader reaches a boundary between knowing that an order is just and comprehending why every distinction within it exists.

> 67 E ciò espresso e chiaro vi si nota  
> (And this is expressly and clearly shown to you)
>
> 68 ne la Scrittura santa in quei gemelli  
> (in Holy Scripture in those twins)
>
> 69 che ne la madre ebber l'ira commota.  
> (who had their anger stirred within their mother.)

The twins are Jacob and Esau, who struggled in Rebecca's womb. Their story in Genesis becomes, especially through Saint Paul's discussion in Romans 9, a central example of divine election preceding personal actions.

Dante invokes that interpretive tradition to explain why the children's different places need not result from different conduct. Their distinctions originate in divine giving, not in achievements they never had time to perform.

The argument is demanding rather than comfortably explanatory. Bernard rejects chance in heaven, but also refuses to make God's purposes entirely transparent to human reasoning.

## 6. Childhood Across the Different Ages of Sacred History

> 76 Bastavasi ne' secoli recenti  
> (In the earliest ages there sufficed,)
>
> 77 con l'innocenza, per aver salute,  
> (together with innocence, for obtaining salvation,)
>
> 78 solamente la fede d'i parenti;  
> (only the faith of the parents;)

Here “earliest ages” means the ages when the world was newly created. Bernard describes a succession of religious conditions governing the salvation of children.

In the first period, innocence together with parental faith was sufficient. Later, circumcision became necessary for males as the sign of the covenant. The poem thus places childhood within sacred history rather than treating all historical periods as religiously identical.

The larger principle is that personal innocence and the inherited consequences of the Fall are not the same thing. A child may have committed no voluntary sin while still needing redemption.

> 82 ma poi che 'l tempo de la grazia venne,  
> (but after the time of grace came,)
>
> 83 sanza battesmo perfetto di Cristo  
> (without the perfect baptism of Christ,)
>
> 84 tale innocenza là giù si ritenne.  
> (such innocence was held down below.)

The “time of grace” is the Christian era inaugurated by Christ. Within the theological scheme Dante adopts, baptism now supplies the required means of incorporation into redeemed life.

“Down below” refers to Limbo, where the poem places unbaptized infants without the bodily torments of the punished damned, but without the vision of God.

This passage should be understood as Dante's historically situated account of salvation, not as an uncontested formulation shared by all Christians. Its severity belongs to the canto's attempt to reconcile original sin, sacramental necessity, divine justice, and the innocence of children.

## 7. Mary and Gabriel: The Human Face of Divine Nearness

> 85 Riguarda omai ne la faccia che a Cristo  
> (Look now upon the face that to Christ)
>
> 86 più si somiglia, ché la sua chiarezza  
> (is most like, for its brightness)
>
> 87 sola ti può disporre a veder Cristo».  
> (alone can prepare you to see Christ”.)

Bernard turns Dante from theological explanation toward Mary. Her resemblance to Christ has an immediate maternal meaning, but it also expresses her exceptional spiritual conformity to him.

The movement is important: Dante cannot reach the final vision merely by acquiring more explanations. His power of sight must be prepared and strengthened.

Mary does not replace Christ as the goal. Her brightness disposes Dante to see him. She represents created humanity brought into the closest possible relationship with the divine through the Incarnation.

> 94 e quello amor che primo lì discese,  
> (and that love which first descended there,)
>
> 95 cantando ‘Ave, Maria, gratïa plena’,  
> (singing ‘Hail, Mary, full of grace’,)
>
> 96 dinanzi a lei le sue ali distese.  
> (spread its wings before her.)

The angel is Gabriel, whom Bernard subsequently identifies through his role at the Annunciation. Calling him “that love” presents angelic identity through the act that most fully expresses it.

His greeting recalls Luke's Gospel and the familiar language of Marian prayer. Heaven renews the announcement through which the Word's assumption of human flesh was proclaimed.

The heavenly court answers his song. What began as a message to one woman becomes a shared celebration of the Incarnation. Dante's progress now takes place through praise as well as instruction.

## 8. Adam and Peter: The Roots of Humanity and the Church

> 118 Quei due che seggon là sù più felici  
> (Those two who sit up there more blessed)
>
> 119 per esser propinquissimi ad Agusta,  
> (through being nearest to the Empress,)
>
> 120 son d'esta rosa quasi due radici:  
> (are, as it were, two roots of this rose:)

The “Empress” is Mary. Beside her sit Adam and Saint Peter, the two figures Bernard calls the rose's roots.

Adam is the father of humanity; Peter is the foundational apostle of the Church. Together they represent natural descent and the community constituted through redemption. Their proximity to Mary gathers creation, the Fall, the Incarnation, and the Church into one compact arrangement.

Bernard describes Adam's forbidden tasting through a bitter verbal reversal: his rash taste caused humanity to taste bitterness. Peter, by contrast, is associated with the keys entrusted to him by Christ.

Nearby sit John the Evangelist and Moses. John represents the prophetic vision of the Church's trials in Revelation, following the traditional attribution of that book; Moses represents the leadership of Israel through the wilderness.

These are not merely prominent individuals occupying prestigious seats. Their relationships make the rose a visible account of how the biblical past converges upon salvation.

## 9. Anne and Lucy: Universal History Becomes Personal

> 133 Di contr' a Pietro vedi sedere Anna,  
> (Opposite Peter you see Anne seated,)
>
> 134 tanto contenta di mirar sua figlia,  
> (so happy to gaze upon her daughter,)
>
> 135 che non move occhio per cantare osanna;  
> (that she does not move her eyes while singing hosanna;)

Saint Anne, traditionally understood as Mary's mother, gazes upon her daughter without interrupting her praise.

Her presence brings intimate family affection into the immense public order of heaven. Beatitude does not erase particular relationships; it perfects them. Anne's maternal delight and her worship are not rival activities.

The scene also balances the canto's hierarchical language of thrones, ranks, and empire. Heaven's order includes the simple joy of a mother looking at her child.

> 136 e contro al maggior padre di famiglia  
> (and opposite the greatest father of a family)
>
> 137 siede Lucia, che mosse la tua donna  
> (sits Lucy, who moved your lady)
>
> 138 quando chinavi, a rovinar, le ciglia.  
> (when you lowered your eyes toward ruin.)

Opposite Adam sits Saint Lucy. Bernard recalls her intervention at the beginning of Dante's journey, when she urged Beatrice to rescue him.

The reference returns the reader to *Inferno* II and the chain of heavenly assistance that initiated the poem. Dante's ascent has never been a solitary achievement: help preceded even his awareness that help was possible.

Lucy's association with light and sight makes her especially appropriate near the completion of a poem concerned with learning how to see. The universal history displayed in the rose now includes Dante's own threatened and rescued life.

## 10. From Instruction to Prayer

> 145 Veramente, ne forse tu t'arretri  
> (Yet, lest perhaps you fall backward,)
>
> 146 movendo l'ali tue, credendo oltrarti,  
> (moving your wings, believing you advance,)
>
> 147 orando grazia conven che s'impetri  
> (through prayer, grace must be obtained,)
>
> 148 grazia da quella che puote aiutarti;  
> (grace from her who can help you;)

The image of flight makes the final danger clear: effort alone does not guarantee ascent. Dante might mistake the movement of his own wings for genuine progress.

This does not make his long education worthless. Rather, it defines its limit. Reason, poetry, moral discipline, and theological understanding have brought him to the threshold, but the direct vision of God requires a further gift.

Bernard therefore turns to Mary's intercession. The repeated emphasis on grace answers the canto's earlier questions about children and unequal blessedness: salvation and vision cannot finally be reduced to personal accomplishment.

> 149 e tu mi seguirai con l'affezione,  
> (and you will follow me with your affection,)
>
> 150 sì che dal dicer mio lo cor non parti».  
> (so that you do not part your heart from my words”.)
>
> 151 E cominciò questa santa orazione:  
> (And he began this holy prayer:)

Dante must follow Bernard not only by understanding the words but by joining them with his heart. Intellectual attention becomes an act of desire and consent.

The canto ends at the threshold of Bernard's prayer, which opens Canto XXXIII. Its final movement is therefore deliberately unfinished: the explanation of heavenly order gives way to the petition that will prepare Dante to encounter its source.

## Epilogue — The Rose as the Visible Shape of Grace

Canto XXXII presents heaven as history transformed into an intelligible, living order. Eve and Mary reveal the passage from injury to healing; the rose's halves unite expectation and fulfilment; its children expose the dependence of salvation upon grace; its saints connect universal redemption with personal affection and assistance.

Yet the canto also limits its own explanations. Bernard can describe the arrangement of heaven without exhausting the reasons for every divine gift. At the end, the proper response is neither intellectual defeat nor self-sufficient certainty, but prayer: understanding must become love before Dante can see the God toward whom the whole rose turns.
