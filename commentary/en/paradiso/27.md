# Joy, Judgment, and Renewal in Dante's *Paradiso* XXVII

Canto XXVII turns from the joy of heaven to the corruption of earthly government, then rises beyond the visible stars to the source of cosmic motion. Its two principal speakers, Saint Peter and Beatrice, expose the distance between the order God gives creation and the disorder human beings make within it.

The dramatic date is 1300, though Dante wrote the canto years later. This distinction matters: events already known to the poet can appear as prophecies spoken during the pilgrim's journey. The canto begins in the heaven of the fixed stars and ends in the Primum Mobile, the outermost moving heaven.

## 1. Heavenly Wealth: Possession Without Craving

> 7 Oh gioia! oh ineffabile allegrezza!  
> (Oh joy! oh ineffable gladness!)
>
> 8 oh vita intègra d'amore e di pace!  
> (oh life complete in love and peace!)
>
> 9 oh sanza brama sicura ricchezza!  
> (oh secure wealth without craving!)

The canto opens with all heaven singing glory to the Trinity. Sight and sound together intoxicate Dante: the universe appears to smile.

These exclamations establish the standard against which earthly corruption will be measured. Heavenly wealth is secure because it cannot be lost, and it is without craving because possession does not generate further hunger. Earthly greed works in the opposite way: accumulation intensifies desire.

The contrast governs the whole canto. Peter will condemn a Church that seeks gold instead of salvation; Beatrice will diagnose the acquisitiveness that overwhelms human society. Their indignation does not interrupt heavenly love. It expresses love's opposition to what damages its objects.

## 2. Peter's Reddening Light and the Vacant Apostolic Seat

> 22 Quelli ch'usurpa in terra il luogo mio,  
> (He who usurps on earth my place,)
>
> 23 il luogo mio, il luogo mio, che vaca  
> (my place, my place, which stands vacant)
>
> 24 ne la presenza del Figliuol di Dio,  
> (in the presence of the Son of God,)
>
> 25 fatt' ha del cimitero mio cloaca  
> (has made of my burial ground a sewer)
>
> 26 del sangue e de la puzza; onde 'l perverso  
> (of blood and stench; at which the perverse one)
>
> 27 che cadde di qua sù, là giù si placa».  
> (who fell from up here, down there is appeased”.)

The four lights before Dante are Peter, James, John, and Adam. Peter becomes increasingly red: Dante compares the transformation to Jupiter exchanging its bright appearance for Mars's fiery coloring. The entire heaven subsequently reddens, and Beatrice's expression changes like that of an innocent woman ashamed by another person's misconduct.

The unnamed usurper is Boniface VIII, pope in 1300. Peter's repeated “my place” insists that the office derives from apostolic responsibility, not its current holder's ambitions. The seat is occupied institutionally but “vacant” before Christ: a devastating judgment of spiritual unworthiness, not a straightforward statement that no pope has been elected.

Peter's burial ground stands for Rome, particularly the sacred place associated with his martyrdom. Turning it into a sewer reverses its meaning: ground sanctified by a martyr's blood becomes polluted by violence and corruption. The beneficiary is Satan, the fallen angel whose satisfaction grotesquely counters heaven's opening joy.

## 3. The Church's Blood, Keys, and Seals

> 40 «Non fu la sposa di Cristo allevata  
> (“The bride of Christ was not nourished)
>
> 41 del sangue mio, di Lin, di quel di Cleto,  
> (with my blood, with Linus's, with that of Cletus,)
>
> 42 per essere ad acquisto d'oro usata;  
> (to be used for the acquisition of gold;)

The Church is Christ's bride, nourished by the blood of Peter and his early successors. Linus and Cletus belong to the earliest succession of Roman bishops; the passage invokes the traditional history of a persecuted, martyr-supported Church.

The contrast is between two kinds of acquisition: gaining eternal life and gaining money. The martyrs gave their lives so that others might enter heavenly happiness, not so that later churchmen could turn spiritual authority into revenue. Dante attacks the betrayal of the Church's purpose through the authority of its own foundational saint.

> 49 né che le chiavi che mi fuor concesse,  
> (nor that the keys that were granted to me,)
>
> 50 divenisser signaculo in vessillo  
> (should become an emblem on a banner)
>
> 51 che contra battezzati combattesse;  
> (that fought against the baptized;)

The keys recall Christ's grant of authority to Peter in Matthew 16:19. They signify the power to bind and loose: an authority directed toward salvation.

On a military banner, however, they authorize warfare against Christians. A sign meant to open heaven becomes an instrument of earthly coercion. The surrounding passage also condemns the division of Christians into favored and rejected political parties. Peter refuses the identification of the universal Church with one faction.

> 52 né ch'io fossi figura di sigillo  
> (nor that I should be the image on a seal)
>
> 53 a privilegi venduti e mendaci,  
> (for privileges sold and mendacious,)
>
> 54 ond' io sovente arrosso e disfavillo.  
> (at which I often blush and flash with fire.)

Papal documents were authenticated by seals bearing the images of Peter and Paul. Peter therefore finds his own likeness used to guarantee corrupt transactions.

The outrage is especially intimate: his image appears to endorse what his life repudiated. The purchased privileges evoke simony, the buying and selling of spiritual things, though the accusation also includes fraudulent or dishonestly granted favors. His fiery appearance makes visible the opposition between apostolic holiness and its commercial exploitation.

## 4. Predatory Shepherds and the Poet's Commission

> 55 In vesta di pastor lupi rapaci  
> (In a shepherd's garb, ravening wolves)
>
> 56 si veggion di qua sù per tutti i paschi:  
> (are seen from up here throughout all pastures:)
>
> 57 o difesa di Dio, perché pur giaci?  
> (O defense of God, why do you still lie idle?)

The wolves in shepherds' clothing combine biblical warnings about false prophets with the traditional description of bishops as shepherds. Those appointed to feed and protect the flock instead consume it.

“From up here” matters: earthly appearances cannot deceive heaven. Rank, vestments, and official claims do not conceal predation. Peter's appeal for divine defense belongs to the language of prophetic lament: it asks why justice delays, without doubting that justice exists.

> 58 Del sangue nostro Caorsini e Guaschi  
> (Of our blood, men of Cahors and Gascony)
>
> 59 s'apparecchian di bere: o buon principio,  
> (prepare themselves to drink: O good beginning,)
>
> 60 a che vil fine convien che tu caschi!  
> (to what vile end must you fall!)

The geographical labels point especially to John XXII, from Cahors, and Clement V, from Gascony, together with the ecclesiastical interests associated with them. Both pontificates lie after the journey's fictional date. Their appearance here is thus prophetic within the narrative but retrospective from the poet's position.

The image horribly reverses martyrdom. The early saints shed their blood to nourish the Church; corrupt successors prepare to drink that blood for their own benefit. The contrast between a good beginning and a degraded outcome anticipates Beatrice's later images of spoiled fruit.

Peter nevertheless predicts providential rescue. His reference to Scipio, Rome's defender against Carthage, connects Christian renewal with Dante's belief that Roman history served a divine purpose. The promised agent of reform remains unspecified.

> 64 e tu, figliuol, che per lo mortal pondo  
> (and you, son, who because of your mortal weight)
>
> 65 ancor giù tornerai, apri la bocca,  
> (will yet return below, open your mouth,)
>
> 66 e non asconder quel ch'io non ascondo».  
> (and do not hide what I do not hide”.)

Dante's mortal body means that he must return to earth. Peter turns that limitation into a vocation: the pilgrim must publicly report heaven's judgment.

This commission reinforces the authorization Dante received from Cacciaguida in Canto XVII. The poem presents its harsh political and ecclesiastical criticism not as private resentment but as testimony the poet has been commanded to deliver.

## 5. Ascending Snow and the Earth Seen from Above

> 70 in sù vid' io così l'etera addorno  
> (upward I thus saw the ether adorned)
>
> 71 farsi e fioccar di vapor trïunfanti  
> (become, and snow with triumphant vapors)
>
> 72 che fatto avien con noi quivi soggiorno.  
> (that had made their stay there with us.)

The preceding lines compare this movement with winter snowfall, when the sun is in Capricorn. Here the snow rises: the blessed souls ascend toward the Empyrean.

Their description as luminous “vapors” belongs to the simile, not to a claim that souls are literally clouds. The reversal of snowfall makes heavenly movement visible through a transformed earthly experience. The souls' temporary appearance in the stellar heaven has accommodated Dante's understanding; their true home is with God.

> 82 sì ch'io vedea di là da Gade il varco  
> (so that I saw beyond Cadiz the passage)
>
> 83 folle d'Ulisse, e di qua presso il lito  
> (mad, of Ulysses, and on this side near the shore)
>
> 84 nel qual si fece Europa dolce carco.  
> (on which Europa became a sweet burden.)

At Beatrice's command, Dante looks down. His movement with the stellar heaven has changed the portion of earth visible beneath him.

The western landmark recalls Ulysses' disastrous voyage beyond the limits marked by the Pillars of Hercules, narrated in *Inferno* XXVI. The eastern landmark recalls Europa, carried away from the Phoenician shore by Jupiter disguised as a bull.

These mythic references place the inhabited world within a landscape of dangerous desire and transgression. Ulysses also provides an implicit contrast with Dante: one traveler passes beyond human limits through an unauthorized venture; the other ascends through grace and guidance. From this height, the earth becomes a little garden plot, diminishing the territorial ambitions that dominate human politics.

## 6. Beatrice and the Ascent Beyond the Stars

> 97 E la virtù che lo sguardo m'indulse,  
> (And the power that her gaze bestowed on me,)
>
> 98 del bel nido di Leda mi divelse,  
> (tore me from Leda's lovely nest,)
>
> 99 e nel ciel velocissimo m'impulse.  
> (and thrust me into the swiftest heaven.)

“Leda's lovely nest” is Gemini, the constellation of the twins Castor and Pollux, mythological children of Leda. Dante has occupied this region of the fixed stars since Canto XXII; it also carries personal significance as his birth sign.

Beatrice's gaze supplies the power of ascent. Immediately before these lines, Dante declares that all natural and artistic beauty combined would seem nothing beside her smile. Her beauty is not a rival attraction distracting him from God: it communicates a divine power that raises him toward God.

Beatrice retains her identity as the woman Dante loved while also embodying the guidance of revealed truth and grace. Their ascent enters the Primum Mobile, the “first moved” heaven, whose swift rotation governs the motions of the heavens beneath it.

## 7. A Heaven Whose Place Is the Divine Mind

> 109 e questo cielo non ha altro dove  
> (and this heaven has no other where)
>
> 110 che la mente divina, in che s'accende  
> (than the divine mind, in which is kindled)
>
> 111 l'amor che 'l volge e la virtù ch'ei piove.  
> (the love that turns it and the power that it rains down.)

Medieval cosmology places the stationary earth at the center of concentric celestial spheres. The Primum Mobile is the outermost moving sphere, but it is not contained within another physical heaven.

Its “where” is the divine mind. Dante pushes spatial language toward its limit: the ultimate explanation of the universe cannot be another object somewhere outside it.

Love causes the sphere's motion, while its power descends through the cosmos. In the philosophical and theological background, celestial motion expresses desire for divine perfection; the heavens transmit ordering influences to the world below.

> 112 Luce e amor d'un cerchio lui comprende,  
> (Light and love embrace it in a circle,)
>
> 113 sì come questo li altri; e quel precinto  
> (as this does the others; and that enclosure)
>
> 114 colui che 'l cinge solamente intende.  
> (only he who girds it understands.)

The encompassing light and love are those of the Empyrean, the heaven beyond physical space and motion. Dante speaks of it as a circle while simultaneously indicating that ordinary geometry cannot adequately describe it.

Light suggests truth and understanding; love suggests the fulfillment of desire. These are not decorative qualities added to the universe but its ultimate ground. Only God fully comprehends this enclosure, so Beatrice's explanation also acknowledges the limits of created understanding.

## 8. The Cosmic Clock and the Roots of Time

> 115 Non è suo moto per altro distinto,  
> (Its motion is not measured by another,)
>
> 116 ma li altri son mensurati da questo,  
> (but the others are measured by this,)
>
> 117 sì come diece da mezzo e da quinto;  
> (as ten by its half and by its fifth;)

The Primum Mobile provides the fundamental measure of celestial motion. Its daily revolution supplies the reference against which other heavenly movements are distinguished and measured.

The numerical comparison invokes five and two, respectively the half and fifth of ten. As a number can be measured by its constituent units, the motions of the other spheres are measured in relation to this primary motion.

This is more than an obsolete astronomical diagram. Dante is establishing that temporal change rests on an ordered structure, even when human life within that structure appears confused.

> 118 e come il tempo tegna in cotal testo  
> (and how time may hold in such a flowerpot)
>
> 119 le sue radici e ne li altri le fronde,  
> (its roots and in the others its branches,)
>
> 120 omai a te può esser manifesto.  
> (can now be manifest to you.)

The abstract explanation becomes a botanical image. Time is a plant rooted in the Primum Mobile, with branches extending through the other heavens.

The image reflects the Aristotelian connection between time and motion: measured celestial movement makes temporal order intelligible. It also prepares the canto's concluding contrast between healthy growth and damaged fruit. Cosmic order produces a coherent living structure; human corruption frustrates the growth of goodness.

## 9. Good Beginnings, Blighted Fruit, and Failed Government

> 124 Ben fiorisce ne li uomini il volere;  
> (The will indeed flowers in human beings;)
>
> 125 ma la pioggia continüa converte  
> (but the continual rain turns)
>
> 126 in bozzacchioni le sosine vere.  
> (true plums into blighted fruit.)

Beatrice abruptly turns from the heavens to human greed. The transition is deliberate: the ordered cosmos makes human disorder more culpable, not less.

People can begin with good intentions, just as a tree produces promising blossoms. But continual corrupting influence prevents those intentions from ripening. The *bozzacchioni* are diseased, swollen plums: apparent growth without genuine fruition.

The metaphor does not make corruption inevitable. Elsewhere Dante firmly defends free will against astrological determinism. Here the emphasis falls on the conditions that repeatedly deform human choices.

> 127 Fede e innocenza son reperte  
> (Faith and innocence are found)
>
> 128 solo ne' parvoletti; poi ciascuna  
> (only in little children; then each)
>
> 129 pria fugge che le guance sian coperte.  
> (flees before their cheeks are covered.)

Faith and innocence disappear before beards cover the children's cheeks. The surrounding examples sharpen the accusation: a child's fasting becomes indiscriminate consumption, and affection for a mother becomes the wish to see her dead.

This is prophetic exaggeration directed at a corrupt society, not a systematic claim that every adult lacks virtue. Its force lies in the reversal of expected development. Growing older should perfect moral capacities; instead, experience becomes an education in appetite and selfishness.

> 139 Tu, perché non ti facci maraviglia,  
> (You, so that you may not marvel,)
>
> 140 pensa che 'n terra non è chi governi;  
> (consider that on earth there is no one who governs;)
>
> 141 onde sì svïa l'umana famiglia.  
> (whence the human family goes so astray.)

There are rulers on earth, but no effective governance worthy of the name. Beatrice identifies a public cause for private moral failure.

This diagnosis belongs to Dante's larger political argument: humanity needs rightly ordered spiritual and temporal guidance. A worldly papacy and a failing imperial authority leave the human family without its proper direction. Peter's attack on corrupt churchmen thus expands into an account of civilization's general disorder.

## 10. The Turning Fleet and the Promise of Renewal

> 142 Ma prima che gennaio tutto si sverni  
> (But before January passes wholly out of winter)
>
> 143 per la centesma ch'è là giù negletta,  
> (because of the hundredth part neglected down there,)
>
> 144 raggeran sì questi cerchi superni,  
> (these supernal circles will send forth such rays,)

The Julian calendar slightly overestimated the solar year. That small discrepancy, approximately the “hundredth part” of a day mentioned here, gradually shifted calendar dates relative to the seasons. Over an enormous interval, January would cease to correspond to its familiar winter position.

Dante makes this slow drift the horizon of a prophecy. The language does not provide a practical date for reform. It asserts that celestial and providential order will correct human disorder before even the apparently stable calendar has undergone such a transformation.

The rays of the heavens signify renewed ordering influence. They should not be taken to abolish human freedom: divine providence works through historical events and human agents as well as through the cosmos.

> 145 che la fortuna che tanto s'aspetta,  
> (that the storm that is so long awaited,)
>
> 146 le poppe volgerà u' son le prore,  
> (will turn the sterns to where the prows are,)
>
> 147 sì che la classe correrà diretta;  
> (so that the fleet will run straight;)
>
> 148 e vero frutto verrà dopo 'l fiore».  
> (and true fruit will come after the flower”.)

Here *fortuna* carries the nautical sense of a storm or powerful wind. The fleet represents collective humanity, including its governing institutions. Its present course is so wrong that salvation requires a reversal: the sterns must turn toward the direction previously occupied by the bows.

Yet the promised result is not permanent upheaval. The fleet will sail straight. Similarly, the final return to the fruit image promises that good beginnings will at last yield good outcomes.

Neither Beatrice nor Peter identifies a definite historical reformer. The canto's certainty concerns providence's ultimate direction, not a decipherable political timetable.

## Epilogue — From Misused Authority to Restored Direction

Canto XXVII sets two orders against each other: heaven, where love produces harmony, and earth, where greed turns offices of service into instruments of predation.

Peter exposes the betrayal of apostolic authority and commissions Dante to speak. Beatrice reveals the cosmic foundation of order, diagnoses the failure of human guidance, and promises renewal. The canto ends not with the destruction of the human community but with its redirection: the fleet will find its course, and the blossom of good intention will finally become genuine fruit.
