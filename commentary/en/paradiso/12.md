# Two Crowns, One Church: Allegory and Background in *Paradiso*, Canto XII

In the Heaven of the Sun, Dante encounters souls distinguished by wisdom. Their light makes spiritual understanding visible, while their coordinated movement expresses the harmony of knowledge perfected by love.

Canto XII completes a carefully balanced pair of episodes. In Canto XI, the Dominican Thomas Aquinas praised Saint Francis and criticized the decline of his own order. Here, the Franciscan Bonaventure praises Saint Dominic before confronting the failings of the Franciscans. The two speeches turn institutional rivalry into mutual recognition: neither religious family can understand its mission apart from the other.

## 1. The Double Rainbow: Difference Without Division

> 10 Come si volgon per tenera nube  
> (As there curve through a delicate cloud)
>
> 11 due archi paralelli e concolori,  
> (two arches, parallel and alike in color,)
>
> 12 quando Iunone a sua ancella iube,  
> (when Juno commands her handmaid,)
>
> 13 nascendo di quel d'entro quel di fori,  
> (the outer one arising from the inner one,)
>
> 14 a guisa del parlar di quella vaga  
> (after the manner of the speech of that wandering nymph)
>
> 15 ch'amor consunse come sol vapori,  
> (whom love consumed as the sun consumes vapors,)

A second circle of twelve luminous souls surrounds the first. Dante compares the circles to a double rainbow, drawing together classical mythology, natural philosophy, and biblical history.

Juno's handmaid is Iris, the rainbow's divine personification. The wandering nymph is Echo, who wasted away through unreturned love for Narcissus until only her voice remained. The outer rainbow, understood here as deriving from the inner, resembles an echo answering an original sound.

These comparisons explain the relationship between the circles: the second answers the first without disrupting its unity. Bonaventure's speech will likewise answer Aquinas's, repeating its structure while exchanging the religious orders involved.

In the following lines, Dante also recalls the rainbow as the sign of God's covenant with Noah. The image therefore means more than beautiful coordination. It places the two circles within a providential order that promises preservation rather than destruction. Pagan stories become poetic instruments within a Christian account of the universe.

## 2. Love Makes One Order Praise the Other

> 31 e cominciò: «L'amor che mi fa bella  
> (and began: “The love that makes me beautiful)
>
> 32 mi tragge a ragionar de l'altro duca  
> (draws me to speak of the other leader)
>
> 33 per cui del mio sì ben ci si favella.  
> (on whose account such good is spoken here of mine.)
>
> 34 Degno è che, dov' è l'un, l'altro s'induca:  
> (It is fitting that, where one is, the other be brought in:)
>
> 35 sì che, com' elli ad una militaro,  
> (so that, as they campaigned together,)
>
> 36 così la gloria loro insieme luca.  
> (so their glory may shine together.)

The speaker, who later identifies himself as Bonaventure, explains that heavenly love moves him to praise Dominic. His beauty is not physical attractiveness but the radiance of charity.

The two leaders are Francis and Dominic, founders of the great mendicant orders of the thirteenth century. These orders brought religious life into towns, universities, and preaching missions, combining forms of poverty with active service.

Dante's arrangement challenges the competition that could divide their followers. Aquinas does not advertise Dominican superiority, nor does Bonaventure insist on Franciscan exclusiveness. Each celebrates what God accomplished through the other founder.

The military language also matters. Francis and Dominic are fellow commanders, not opposing generals. Their methods differ, but their allegiance and purpose are one.

## 3. A Church Rescued by Grace, Not Merit

> 40 quando lo 'mperador che sempre regna  
> (when the emperor who reigns forever)
>
> 41 provide a la milizia, ch'era in forse,  
> (made provision for the army, which was in peril,)
>
> 42 per sola grazia, non per esser degna;  
> (through grace alone, not because it was worthy;)
>
> 43 e, come è detto, a sua sposa soccorse  
> (and, as has been said, came to his bride's aid)
>
> 44 con due campioni, al cui fare, al cui dire  
> (with two champions, at whose deeds, at whose words)
>
> 45 lo popol disvïato si raccorse.  
> (the people who had strayed rallied.)

God is the eternal emperor; the Christian community is his army. In the preceding lines, that army has become slow, uncertain, and depleted. Its restoration is necessary because the Church has failed to live up to the redemption purchased by Christ.

Yet Dante immediately shifts from military to marital imagery. The army is also Christ's bride. Its defense is therefore an act of love, not merely an assertion of power.

The decisive phrase is “through grace alone.” The arrival of the two saints does not reward a healthy institution. It rescues an endangered one. Dante can affirm the Church's divine foundation while condemning the conduct of its members.

The pairing of deeds and words also suggests the complementary missions of the founders: Francis is especially associated with an exemplary life of poverty, Dominic with learned preaching. Neither, however, is reduced to only action or only speech.

## 4. Calaroga: The Birthplace of Faith's Champion

> 52 siede la fortunata Calaroga  
> (stands fortunate Calaroga)
>
> 53 sotto la protezion del grande scudo  
> (under the protection of the great shield)
>
> 54 in che soggiace il leone e soggioga:  
> (in which the lion lies beneath and stands above:)
>
> 55 dentro vi nacque l'amoroso drudo  
> (within it was born the loving champion)
>
> 56 de la fede cristiana, il santo atleta  
> (of the Christian faith, the holy athlete)
>
> 57 benigno a' suoi e a' nemici crudo;  
> (kind to his own and stern toward enemies;)

Calaroga is Caleruega, in Castile, where Dominic was born around 1170. The shield is the quartered royal heraldry of Castile and León: its arrangement places a lion above a castle in one portion and below a castle in another.

Dante introduces the town through an elaborate westward landscape. This complements the imagery surrounding Francis's birthplace in Canto XI, where Assisi is associated with sunrise. Together, the founders suggest a renewal embracing the Christian world.

The word *drudo* here means a devoted lover or champion, not its more disreputable sense of an illicit lover. Dominic loves the faith with personal intensity. The “holy athlete” belongs to a traditional Christian vocabulary of spiritual struggle.

The contrast between kindness and sternness should not be softened into modern tolerance. Dante regards heresy as a grave danger to souls and admires determined opposition to it. Nevertheless, the weapons most clearly attributed to Dominic in this canto are learning, will, preaching, and apostolic authority.

## 5. A Name That Announces a Vocation

> 67 e perché fosse qual era in costrutto,  
> (and so that he might be in name what he was in meaning,)
>
> 68 quinci si mosse spirito a nomarlo  
> (from here an inspiration arose to name him)
>
> 69 del possessivo di cui era tutto.  
> (with the possessive of the one to whom he wholly belonged.)
>
> 70 Domenico fu detto; e io ne parlo  
> (Dominic he was called; and I speak of him)
>
> 71 sì come de l'agricola che Cristo  
> (as of the husbandman whom Christ)
>
> 72 elesse a l'orto suo per aiutarlo.  
> (chose for his garden to help him.)

The name Dominic derives from Latin *dominicus*, meaning “belonging to the Lord.” Bonaventure treats the name as a providential declaration of the child's identity: he belongs entirely to God.

This interpretation follows a series of signs associated with Dominic's infancy. Dante alludes to his mother's prophetic dream, traditionally involving a dog carrying a burning torch, and to a baptismal vision experienced by his godmother. In the hagiographic tradition, these anticipate the light and zeal of his preaching.

Baptism itself is described as a marriage between Dominic and Faith. This answers the marriage of Francis and Poverty in the preceding canto. The saints embody different relationships that renew the same Church.

The agricultural metaphor introduces another aspect of Dominic's work. He is not the owner of the garden but Christ's appointed cultivator. Teaching must nourish something entrusted to him. Later, the branches and streams of his order will extend this work beyond one individual life.

## 6. Poverty Before Prestige, Learning Before Profit

> 74 che 'l primo amor che 'n lui fu manifesto,  
> (for the first love that became manifest in him,)
>
> 75 fu al primo consiglio che diè Cristo.  
> (was for the first counsel that Christ gave.)
>
> 76 Spesse fïate fu tacito e desto  
> (Often he was silent and awake,)
>
> 77 trovato in terra da la sua nutrice,  
> (found upon the ground by his nurse,)
>
> 78 come dicesse: ‘Io son venuto a questo’.  
> (as though he were saying: ‘I have come for this’.)

Christ's counsel is understood here as the call to voluntary poverty. Dominic's childhood habit of lying awake on the ground becomes an anticipatory sign of humility and self-denial.

Dante thus prevents an overly neat opposition between the founders. Francis does not monopolize poverty, just as Dominic does not monopolize wisdom. Their spiritual commitments overlap.

These childhood episodes belong to the conventions of saints' lives: the mature vocation is already discernible in infancy. Their purpose is less to offer a modern psychological biography than to reveal the coherence of a life directed toward God.

> 82 Non per lo mondo, per cui mo s'affanna  
> (Not for the world, for which people now toil)
>
> 83 di retro ad Ostïense e a Taddeo,  
> (in pursuit of Ostiense and Taddeo,)
>
> 84 ma per amor de la verace manna  
> (but for love of the true manna)
>
> 85 in picciol tempo gran dottor si feo;  
> (in a short time he became a great teacher;)

Ostiense is Henry of Susa, an influential canon lawyer. Taddeo is generally identified as Taddeo Alderotti, the celebrated physician of Bologna. Their names represent prestigious professional studies that could lead to wealth and advancement.

Dante's target is not legal or medical knowledge itself, but the motive for pursuing it. Dominic studies for the “true manna”: spiritual nourishment, recalling the food God provided to Israel in the wilderness.

In the Heaven of the Sun, intellectual achievement becomes genuinely luminous only when ordered toward truth and love rather than professional success.

## 7. Rome: Sacred Authority and Corrupt Officeholders

> 88 E a la sedia che fu già benigna  
> (And to the see that once was generous)
>
> 89 più a' poveri giusti, non per lei,  
> (more toward the righteous poor—not through its own fault,)
>
> 90 ma per colui che siede, che traligna,  
> (but through him who occupies it, who goes astray,)

The “see” is the papacy. Bonaventure distinguishes between the institution and the person who occupies its office: the sacred function remains, even when its holder betrays it.

This distinction is central to Dante's criticism of the Church. His denunciations of ecclesiastical corruption do not necessarily imply rejection of ecclesiastical authority. Indeed, Dominic's exemplary action includes seeking authorization from Rome.

> 91 non dispensare o due o tre per sei,  
> (not permission to pay two or three instead of six,)
>
> 92 non la fortuna di prima vacante,  
> (not the benefit of the first vacant position,)
>
> 93 non decimas, quae sunt pauperum Dei,  
> (not tithes, which belong to God's poor,)
>
> 94 addimandò, ma contro al mondo errante  
> (did he ask, but against the erring world)
>
> 95 licenza di combatter per lo seme  
> (permission to fight for the seed)
>
> 96 del qual ti fascian ventiquattro piante.  
> (of which twenty-four plants encircle you.)

The repeated refusals expose the worldly business that can dominate the papal court: financial dispensations, appointments to profitable benefices, and claims upon revenues intended for the poor.

The first request is usually understood as permission to make only partial restitution of money owed or wrongfully acquired. Its compressed accounting language is deliberately unlike the generosity expected of spiritual life.

Dominic asks instead for permission to defend the seed of faith. The twenty-four blessed spirits surrounding Dante are its plants: wisdom is the flourishing result of that seed.

The Latin phrase about tithes carries particular force. It invokes the Church's own language to remind its administrators that wealth entrusted to them is not simply theirs to enjoy.

## 8. The Torrent and the Two-Wheeled Chariot

> 97 Poi, con dottrina e con volere insieme,  
> (Then, with learning and determination together,)
>
> 98 con l'officio appostolico si mosse  
> (with the apostolic commission he set forth)
>
> 99 quasi torrente ch'alta vena preme;  
> (like a torrent driven by a spring on high;)
>
> 100 e ne li sterpi eretici percosse  
> (and against the heretical thickets struck)
>
> 101 l'impeto suo, più vivamente quivi  
> (his force, most vigorously there)
>
> 102 dove le resistenze eran più grosse.  
> (where the resistance was greatest.)

Dominic's learning and determination combine as the pressure behind a mountain torrent. The image gives his preaching both intellectual authority and irresistible energy.

The principal historical background is his activity against heresy in southern France, especially Cathar belief. That setting also belonged to the violent history of the Albigensian Crusade. Dante's passage, however, foregrounds Dominic's apostolic teaching rather than depicting him as a battlefield commander.

The image remains uncompromising: heretical teachings are obstructive growth to be struck down. The ensuing description of streams watering the Catholic garden turns the same force toward nourishment. Dominic's followers extend his mission, making what begins as a torrent into a distributed source of life.

> 106 Se tal fu l'una rota de la biga  
> (If such was one wheel of the two-wheeled chariot)
>
> 107 in che la Santa Chiesa si difese  
> (in which Holy Church defended herself)
>
> 108 e vinse in campo la sua civil briga,  
> (and won in the field her internal conflict,)

Francis and Dominic are the two wheels of one chariot. Neither wheel alone can perform the vehicle's work: the metaphor expresses complementarity as structural necessity.

The conflict is “civil” because it occurs within the Christian world. The Church's danger is not merely an external enemy; it includes internal doctrinal division and moral failure. Its renewal requires both evangelical example and the defense of truth.

## 9. The Franciscans Lose Their Founder's Track

> 112 Ma l'orbita che fé la parte somma  
> (But the track that the highest part made)
>
> 113 di sua circunferenza, è derelitta,  
> (of its circumference is abandoned,)
>
> 114 sì ch'è la muffa dov' era la gromma.  
> (so that there is mold where there was wine-crust.)

The chariot image turns into an accusation. Francis's wheel established a track, but his followers have abandoned it.

The next metaphor moves from wheels to wine. *Gromma* is the crust or deposit formed by wine inside a cask, associated here with sound contents. Mold signals deterioration. A community that once held spiritual richness now shows corruption.

Bonaventure's self-criticism mirrors Aquinas's attack on Dominican decline in Canto XI. Heavenly praise does not conceal institutional failure; it makes clear how far present conduct has fallen below the founder's example.

> 124 ma non fia da Casal né d'Acquasparta,  
> (but it will be neither from Casale nor from Acquasparta,)
>
> 125 là onde vegnon tali a la scrittura,  
> (whence come such men to the written rule,)
>
> 126 ch'uno la fugge e altro la coarta.  
> (that one evades it and the other constricts it.)

Casale refers to Ubertino da Casale, associated with the rigorist Spiritual Franciscans; Acquasparta refers to Matteo d'Acquasparta, associated with a more accommodating approach to the order's discipline.

Bonaventure rejects both distortion by relaxation and distortion by excessive restriction. Matteo represents evasion of the rule; Ubertino represents its constriction. The targets in the last line thus appear in the reverse order of the place-names.

The issue is not a preference for compromise at any cost. It is fidelity to Francis's intention against rival parties that claim exclusive possession of it. Dante's Bonaventure insists that genuine continuity survives, but cannot simply be identified with either faction.

## 10. Bonaventure's Company: Wisdom Beyond Faction

> 127 Io son la vita di Bonaventura  
> (I am the soul of Bonaventure)
>
> 128 da Bagnoregio, che ne' grandi offici  
> (of Bagnoregio, who in great offices)
>
> 129 sempre pospuosi la sinistra cura.  
> (always put worldly concerns second.)

Bonaventure, who lived approximately from 1217 to 1274, was a major theologian, minister general of the Franciscan order, and eventually a cardinal. His experience gave him particular authority to discuss both Franciscan ideals and institutional government.

The “left-hand concern” means temporal or worldly business, subordinate to spiritual care. He does not claim to have avoided administration, but to have kept its priorities properly ordered.

His companions broaden the meaning of wisdom. They include Francis's early followers Illuminato and Augustine; Hugh of Saint Victor; the biblical historian Peter Comestor; Peter of Spain, celebrated here for his logical writings; the prophet Nathan; John Chrysostom; Anselm; the grammarian Donatus; and Rabanus Maurus. Prophecy, theology, scriptural learning, logic, grammar, and humble religious discipleship all find places in the same circle.

> 139 Rabano è qui, e lucemi dallato  
> (Rabanus is here, and beside me shines)
>
> 140 il calavrese abate Giovacchino  
> (the Calabrian abbot Joachim)
>
> 141 di spirito profetico dotato.  
> (endowed with the spirit of prophecy.)

Joachim of Fiore, who died in 1202, developed an influential prophetic interpretation of history associated with the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. His expectations of spiritual renewal later appealed strongly to some Franciscan Spirituals.

His position beside Bonaventure is therefore striking. The historical Bonaventure opposed radical Joachimite interpretations within his order. Their heavenly proximity does not mean that Dante endorses every proposition connected with Joachim. It distinguishes genuine spiritual insight from the controversies and excesses surrounding its historical reception.

This pairing also answers an arresting feature of the first circle: Aquinas stands in heavenly fellowship with Siger of Brabant, despite their earthly intellectual disagreements. Paradise reveals a harmony that polemic alone cannot perceive.

> 142 Ad inveggiar cotanto paladino  
> (To praise so great a champion)
>
> 143 mi mosse l'infiammata cortesia  
> (I was moved by the ardent courtesy)
>
> 144 di fra Tommaso e 'l discreto latino;  
> (of Brother Thomas and his discerning speech;)

Bonaventure ends by returning to the cause of his address: Aquinas's generous tribute to Francis. One act of praise calls forth another, just as one rainbow answers another and one circle responds to another's song.

Courtesy here is more than polished behavior. It is charity expressed through truthful recognition of another's excellence. The canto closes by making that generosity the organizing principle of the entire heavenly company.

## Epilogue — Two Founders, One Work of Renewal

Canto XII presents religious renewal as the restoration of a shared purpose. Francis and Dominic are not rival proprietors of holiness, but complementary servants of a Church rescued by grace.

Its recurring images—rainbows, armies, marriages, gardens, streams, and wheels—express different forms of relationship and cooperation. Against their harmony stand greed, factionalism, and the betrayal of founding ideals.

The canto's deepest lesson lies in its speakers' conduct: wisdom perfected by love praises another community's gifts while honestly judging the failures of its own.
