# Love, Judgment, and the Corruption of Sacred Things: *Paradiso*, Canto IX

Dante remains in the heaven of Venus, where he encounters souls whose earthly lives were strongly shaped by love. Their presence raises a central question: how can a force that once led people into disorder become part of their blessedness?

The canto answers by distinguishing love's underlying power from its mistaken objects. Human desire can be redirected toward God; conversely, religious authority can be corrupted when it turns toward money and political advantage. The movement from former lovers to corrupt churchmen makes this one of the *Paradiso*'s sharpest examinations of rightly and wrongly ordered love.

The souls appear in Venus to make their histories intelligible to Dante. Their ultimate dwelling, like that of all the blessed, is the Empyrean: the planetary heavens represent differences in the manifestation of grace, not separate destinations outside God's presence.

## 1. Charles Martel: History Awaits Its Judgment

> 4 ma disse: «Taci e lascia muover li anni»;  
> (but he said: “Be silent and let the years move on”;)
>
> 5 sì ch'io non posso dir se non che pianto  
> (so that I can say only that weeping)
>
> 6 giusto verrà di retro ai vostri danni.  
> (justly deserved will follow upon your wrongs.)

The canto begins by completing Dante's conversation with Charles Martel of Anjou, the prince encountered in Canto VIII—not the eighth-century Frankish ruler of the same name. Charles has foretold injuries to his descendants but forbids Dante to disclose their particulars.

The address to “bella Clemenza” is usually understood as referring to Charles's daughter, though identification with his wife has also been proposed. Behind the passage lie the tangled Angevin successions, especially the displacement of Charles's line from the throne of Naples in favor of his brother Robert.

The prophecy distinguishes historical delay from divine indifference. Political injustice may prosper temporarily, but its apparent success does not determine its ultimate meaning. Dante can promise judgment without possessing—or being permitted to publish—its timetable.

Charles then turns back toward God. This withdrawal establishes a governing perspective: earthly dynastic struggles matter, but they are not the final object of heavenly attention.

## 2. Cunizza: One Family Root, Two Different Fires

> 29 là onde scese già una facella  
> (from where once descended a little flame)
>
> 30 che fece a la contrada un grande assalto.  
> (that made a great assault upon the region.)
>
> 31 D'una radice nacqui e io ed ella:  
> (From one root were born both I and it:)

The speaker is Cunizza da Romano. The “little flame” is her brother Ezzelino III da Romano, whose power and violence devastated parts of northeastern Italy in the thirteenth century. Dante has already placed Ezzelino among the tyrants immersed in boiling blood in *Inferno* XII.

The image also recalls the story that Ezzelino's mother dreamed of giving birth to a burning torch. Here it becomes an emblem of destructive political energy.

Brother and sister emerge from the same family “root,” yet reach radically different ends. Their contrast continues the preceding canto's concern with inheritance and individual character: neither ancestry nor celestial influence fixes a person's moral destiny.

> 32 Cunizza fui chiamata, e qui refulgo  
> (I was called Cunizza, and here I shine)
>
> 33 perché mi vinse il lume d'esta stella;  
> (because the light of this star overcame me;)

Venus signifies the power of love, and Cunizza acknowledges having been overcome by it. Medieval accounts associate her with several marriages and love affairs, most famously with the troubadour Sordello, whom Dante meets in the *Purgatorio*.

Her placement is deliberately surprising. A woman remembered for sexual irregularity appears in heaven, while her politically formidable brother suffers among the violent. Dante refuses to make social reputation the measure of salvation.

Venus does not excuse wrongdoing by making it inevitable. Rather, its influence identifies the powerful natural disposition that, through freedom and grace, could eventually be turned toward a higher good.

## 3. Earthly Fame as a Second Life

> 39 grande fama rimase; e pria che moia,  
> (great fame remained; and before it dies,)
>
> 40 questo centesimo anno ancor s'incinqua:  
> (this hundredth year will yet return five times:)
>
> 41 vedi se far si dee l'omo eccellente,  
> (see whether a person should make himself excellent,)
>
> 42 sì ch'altra vita la prima relinqua.  
> (so that the first life may leave another behind.)

Cunizza points to the neighboring soul, later identified as Folco of Marseille. His earthly reputation, she predicts, will endure for another five centuries.

The “other life” is the life of a person's name and achievement in human memory. This is not a substitute for eternal blessedness. It is a secondary form of survival through which excellence can continue to benefit the living.

The passage creates a productive tension. Dante repeatedly exposes the vanity of worldly reputation, yet he does not treat all concern for remembrance as worthless. Fame can preserve worthy action and artistic accomplishment rather than merely flatter ambition.

Cunizza immediately contrasts such excellence with the contemporary inhabitants of northeastern Italy. They neither seek an honorable legacy nor learn from repeated suffering. Their failure prepares the canto's series of political prophecies.

## 4. The Veneto: Violence, Betrayal, and Heavenly Justice

> 46 ma tosto fia che Padova al palude  
> (but soon it shall be that Padua at the marsh)
>
> 47 cangerà l'acqua che Vincenza bagna,  
> (will change the water that bathes Vicenza,)
>
> 48 per essere al dover le genti crude;  
> (because its people are harsh toward their duty;)

The changed water is an ominous image of bloodshed. The prophecy is generally connected with Padua's defeat near Vicenza in 1314, during its conflict with Cangrande della Scala.

The poem's fictional journey occurs in 1300, so events already known during its composition can appear as predictions. Dante uses this retrospective prophetic form not merely to demonstrate supernatural knowledge, but to interpret political history as morally consequential.

Cunizza also foretells the violent end of the proud lord of Treviso, usually identified as Rizzardo da Camino, assassinated in 1312. Military aggression and aristocratic confidence alike stand beneath a judgment their perpetrators cannot see.

> 52 Piangerà Feltro ancora la difalta  
> (Feltre too will weep for the wrongdoing)
>
> 53 de l'empio suo pastor, che sarà sconcia  
> (of its impious shepherd, which will be shameful)
>
> 54 sì, che per simil non s'entrò in malta.  
> (so greatly that none entered Malta for its like.)

The “shepherd” is Alessandro Novello, bishop of Feltre. In 1314 he handed over fugitives from Ferrara to the authorities who executed them. Dante portrays this betrayal as an offering of human blood made to demonstrate political loyalty.

“Malta” is traditionally understood as a notorious prison, often identified with one used for ecclesiastical offenders. The precise reference is disputed, but the force of the comparison is clear: the bishop's crime surpasses offenses already associated with exemplary punishment.

The pastoral title makes the betrayal worse. A bishop should shelter and guide; this one delivers vulnerable people to death. The grotesque description of him as a “courteous priest” later in the passage turns the language of generosity into bitter irony.

> 61 Sù sono specchi, voi dicete Troni,  
> (Above are mirrors, which you call Thrones,)
>
> 62 onde refulge a noi Dio giudicante;  
> (from which God in judgment shines upon us;)
>
> 63 sì che questi parlar ne paion buoni».  
> (so that these words seem good to us”.)

Cunizza explains why severe denunciation can belong to heavenly speech. The angelic order called the Thrones mirrors divine judgment to the blessed.

The image of mirrors emphasizes reception rather than private authority. Cunizza is not indulging a family feud or regional resentment: the poem presents her judgment as illuminated by God's justice. Whether readers accept every historical application or not, that distinction is essential to Dante's conception of prophetic speech.

## 5. Heavenly Language: Joy, Light, and Mutual Knowledge

> 70 Per letiziar là sù fulgor s'acquista,  
> (Through rejoicing up there, radiance is gained,)
>
> 71 sì come riso qui; ma giù s'abbuia  
> (as a smile is here; but below grows dark)
>
> 72 l'ombra di fuor, come la mente è trista.  
> (the outward shade, as the mind is sorrowful.)

As Folco prepares to speak, Dante explains the visible language of the afterlife. Heavenly joy expresses itself as increasing brightness, just as earthly joy appears in a smile. By contrast, infernal sorrow darkens the outward appearance of the soul.

Light is therefore more than decoration. It makes spiritual life perceptible. The blessed do not conceal their inward condition behind misleading surfaces: what they are becomes visible through their radiance.

> 80 Già non attendere' io tua dimanda,  
> (Indeed I would not wait for your request,)
>
> 81 s'io m'intuassi, come tu t'inmii».  
> (if I could enter into you as you enter into me”.)

Dante asks why Folco waits to answer a desire he already knows. The extraordinary verbs *intuarsi* and *inmiarsi* imagine entering into another person's interior life.

Just before this, Dante says that Folco's vision enters into God. The blessed know Dante's desire through divine knowledge, not through an independent power that competes with God.

These invented words express communion without the destruction of individuality. Dante and Folco remain distinct persons, yet heavenly love permits an understanding deeper than ordinary speech. Language itself has to expand to suggest that relation.

## 6. Folco of Marseille: From Troubadour Love to Sacred Vocation

> 94 Folco mi disse quella gente a cui  
> (Folco was what those people called me to whom)
>
> 95 fu noto il nome mio; e questo cielo  
> (my name was known; and this heaven)
>
> 96 di me s'imprenta, com' io fe' di lui;  
> (takes my imprint, as I took its;)

Folco first locates his birthplace through an elaborate description of the Mediterranean. He comes from Marseille, which he identifies partly through its ancient history of bloodshed during the Roman civil wars.

He is the historical Folquet de Marselha, an Occitan troubadour who later entered the Cistercian order and became bishop of Toulouse. The reciprocal imprint is central: Venus once impressed its loving disposition upon him; now his blessed presence contributes to the heaven's manifestation of that power.

His biography also poses a historical difficulty. As bishop, Folco supported the campaign against heresy associated with the Albigensian Crusade. Dante presents him as an authoritative Christian figure, whereas modern readers may find his involvement in persecution deeply troubling. Recognizing that distance helps distinguish Dante's theological portrait from an unqualified modern endorsement.

> 97 ché più non arse la figlia di Belo,  
> (for no more fiercely burned the daughter of Belus,)
>
> 98 noiando e a Sicheo e a Creusa,  
> (wronging both Sychaeus and Creusa,)
>
> 99 di me, infin che si convenne al pelo;  
> (than I, as long as it suited my hair;)

The daughter of Belus is Dido, whose love for Aeneas compromises her fidelity to her dead husband Sychaeus and implicates the memory of Aeneas's dead wife Creusa. Folco claims to have burned no less intensely while his age—suggested by his hair—was appropriate to such passion.

He also compares himself with Phyllis, abandoned by Demophoon, and Hercules in his love for Iole. Classical stories supply the vocabulary of overwhelming desire.

Folco does not deny the intensity of his former life. His salvation shows that intense desire need not simply be extinguished: its direction can change.

## 7. Why the Blessed Do Not Repent in Heaven

> 34 ma lietamente a me medesma indulgo  
> (but gladly I forgive myself)
>
> 35 la cagion di mia sorte, e non mi noia;  
> (the cause of my allotted place, and it does not grieve me;)
>
> 36 che parria forse forte al vostro vulgo.  
> (which might perhaps seem difficult to your common people.)

Cunizza's earlier words anticipate the canto's central theological explanation. She can look upon the history that brought her here without continuing self-torment.

This is not a declaration that everything she did was innocent. Nor does it mean that repentance was unnecessary. It means that the blessed no longer exist in the condition of unresolved guilt: forgiveness and purification have accomplished their work.

> 103 Non però qui si pente, ma si ride,  
> (Yet here one does not repent, but rejoices,)
>
> 104 non de la colpa, ch'a mente non torna,  
> (not in the fault, which does not return to mind,)
>
> 105 ma del valor ch'ordinò e provide.  
> (but in the power that ordered and provided.)

Folco makes the distinction explicit. Heaven rejoices not in sin but in the divine power that has brought salvation out of a history containing sin.

The statement about memory need not imply the erasure of personal biography: these souls can still describe their earthly passions. Rather, wrongdoing no longer returns as a stain upon their blessed consciousness or a source of renewed anguish. This accords with the purification of sinful memory represented by Lethe in the *Purgatorio*.

> 106 Qui si rimira ne l'arte ch'addorna  
> (Here one contemplates the art that adorns)
>
> 107 cotanto affetto, e discernesi 'l bene  
> (so great an affection, and discerns the good)
>
> 108 per che 'l mondo di sù quel di giù torna.  
> (for which the world above turns the one below.)

The blessed perceive divine craftsmanship in the ordering of love and creation. What appears below as a confused history of impulses and accidents can be understood above within providence.

This does not make evil necessary or good. It means that evil cannot finally defeat God's capacity to redeem. The canto's allegory of Venus rests on that distinction: the natural energy of love can survive the correction of its misuse.

## 8. Rahab: The Outsider Who Enters Sacred History

> 115 Or sappi che là entro si tranquilla  
> (Now know that there within takes her rest)
>
> 116 Raab; e a nostr' ordine congiunta,  
> (Rahab; and joined to our order,)
>
> 117 di lei nel sommo grado si sigilla.  
> (our order is sealed with her in its highest rank.)

The next soul is Rahab, the prostitute of Jericho who sheltered Joshua's spies and helped them escape. Her story appears in Joshua 2 and 6; the New Testament praises her faith and her deeds.

Her presence intensifies the challenge already posed by Cunizza. A person whose social identity might invite condemnation can occupy an exalted place in salvation history.

Traditional Christian interpretation also saw Rahab as a figure of the Gentile Church: an outsider brought into the people of God through faith. Her place in Venus makes love's redirection especially striking. A history associated with sexual commerce culminates in heavenly peace.

> 118 Da questo cielo, in cui l'ombra s'appunta  
> (By this heaven, in which comes to a point the shadow)
>
> 119 che 'l vostro mondo face, pria ch'altr' alma  
> (that your world casts, before any other soul)
>
> 120 del trïunfo di Cristo fu assunta.  
> (of Christ's triumph she was taken up.)

Dante follows the medieval cosmographical belief that Earth's shadow reaches as far as Venus. Symbolically, this gives Venus a threshold position: it is the last heaven touched by the shadow of the earthly world.

The image does not mean that its blessed souls remain sinful. It suggests the special relation of the lower heavens to earthly limitations and the histories through which grace has overcome them.

Rahab was the first soul received into this heaven from Christ's triumph—the liberation of the righteous associated with his descent into the realm of the dead. Dante is not calling her the first saved soul in every heaven.

> 124 perch' ella favorò la prima gloria  
> (because she furthered the first glory)
>
> 125 di Iosüè in su la Terra Santa,  
> (of Joshua in the Holy Land,)
>
> 126 che poco tocca al papa la memoria.  
> (which little touches the pope's memory.)

Rahab's help in Joshua's conquest becomes part of a Christian typological pattern: the entry into the promised land anticipates the greater victory of salvation.

The last line abruptly redirects the poem toward contemporary politics. Rahab helped secure the Holy Land; the pope scarcely remembers it. Within Dante's crusading outlook, this neglect exposes a failure of spiritual priorities. The ancient outsider serves sacred history more faithfully than its present institutional guardian.

## 9. The Florin: A Flower That Turns Shepherds into Wolves

> 130 produce e spande il maladetto fiore  
> (produces and spreads the accursed flower)
>
> 131 c'ha disvïate le pecore e li agni,  
> (that has led astray the sheep and the lambs,)
>
> 132 però che fatto ha lupo del pastore.  
> (because it has made a wolf of the shepherd.)

The subject is Florence, Dante's own city. The “accursed flower” is the gold florin, stamped with the Florentine lily and widely influential in European commerce and finance.

The flower, ordinarily an image of beauty and growth, becomes the vehicle of spiritual corruption. Its circulation enables a kind of anti-evangelization: money spreads a distorted hierarchy of values.

The pastoral transformation is especially severe. The clergy do not merely neglect the flock; acquisitiveness turns their guardians into predators. The canto thus contrasts lovers whose desire has been redeemed with religious leaders whose desire has become destructive.

> 133 Per questo l'Evangelio e i dottor magni  
> (For this the Gospel and the great doctors)
>
> 134 son derelitti, e solo ai Decretali  
> (are abandoned, and only to the Decretals)
>
> 135 si studia, sì che pare a' lor vivagni.  
> (does study attend, as appears from their margins.)

The Decretals are collections of papal legal decisions. Their heavily annotated margins testify to intense professional attention.

Dante's target is not simply the existence of church law. It is the displacement of the Gospel and major theological authorities by legal expertise pursued for income, office, jurisdiction, and advantage.

Sacred learning has become instrumental. Instead of asking what brings human beings toward God, churchmen study what consolidates institutional and personal power.

## 10. Nazareth, Rome, and the Promise of Purification

> 136 A questo intende il papa e ' cardinali;  
> (To this attend the pope and the cardinals;)
>
> 137 non vanno i lor pensieri a Nazarette,  
> (their thoughts do not go to Nazareth,)
>
> 138 là dove Gabrïello aperse l'ali.  
> (there where Gabriel opened his wings.)

Nazareth recalls the Annunciation and therefore the beginning of the Incarnation: God's entry into human history through humility and self-giving love.

Against that event, the financial and legal preoccupations of the hierarchy appear as a reversal of Christianity's center. Those entrusted with proclaiming divine generosity have organized their attention around acquisition.

The passage also completes the canto's pattern of direction. Charles turns toward God; the blessed perceive one another in God; corrupt churchmen direct their minds away from the places and events that should orient their vocation.

> 139 Ma Vaticano e l'altre parti elette  
> (But the Vatican and the other chosen parts)
>
> 140 di Roma che son state cimitero  
> (of Rome that have been a burial ground)
>
> 141 a la milizia che Pietro seguette,  
> (for the soldiery that followed Peter,)
>
> 142 tosto libere fien de l'avoltero».  
> (will soon be free of the adultery”.)

The final image sets contemporary corruption against the graves of the martyrs. The Vatican evokes Saint Peter's burial, while Rome's other sacred sites preserve the memory of Christians who gave their lives for their faith.

“Adultery” is ecclesiastical and allegorical. The Church, understood as Christ's bride, betrays its proper love by attaching itself to wealth and worldly power.

The promised liberation remains historically indefinite. Attempts to identify it with a single coming ruler or political event are uncertain. Its essential function is prophetic: present corruption will not possess the sacred inheritance forever. The canto ends not by rejecting the Church's holiness, but by insisting that its betrayal cannot be final.

## Epilogue — Love Redeemed, Sacred Power Judged

Canto IX makes an unsettling distinction between reputation and spiritual reality. Cunizza, Folco, and Rahab carry histories of passionate or socially compromised love, yet appear in heavenly light. Bishops, popes, and cardinals possess sacred offices, yet may turn their responsibilities into instruments of betrayal.

The difference is not between people who have desired and people who have not. It is between desire redirected toward its true good and desire fastened upon destructive substitutes. Venus becomes the heaven of love transformed; the florin becomes the emblem of love misdirected. Between them, Dante measures both personal salvation and the moral condition of Christian society.
