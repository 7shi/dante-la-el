# From Holy Warriors to the Eagle of Justice: Dante's *Paradiso*, Canto XVIII

Canto XVIII moves from Dante's private anxiety about exile to a public question: what makes authority just? It begins in Mars, among souls associated with warfare for the faith, and ends in Jupiter, where blessed spirits form the eagle of imperial justice. Between these scenes, Beatrice redirects Dante's attention, heavenly movement becomes writing, and a vision of divine order inspires an attack on the corruption of the Church.

## 1. Bitter Knowledge and Heavenly Consolation

> 1 Già si godeva solo del suo verbo  
> (Already was rejoicing alone in his own thought)
>
> 2 quello specchio beato, e io gustava  
> (that blessed mirror, and I was tasting)
>
> 3 lo mio, temprando col dolce l'acerbo;  
> (my own, tempering the bitter with the sweet;)

The “blessed mirror” is Cacciaguida, Dante's ancestor, who has just foretold Dante's exile and explained his obligation to write about what he has seen. Like other blessed souls, Cacciaguida reflects divine light; his identity as a “mirror” presents his knowledge and happiness as received from God.

Dante's thoughts combine bitterness and sweetness. Exile will bring humiliation and dependence, but his prophetic mission gives that suffering purpose and promises lasting recognition. The opening therefore carries the emotional consequences of Canto XVII into this new vision.

> 4 e quella donna ch'a Dio mi menava  
> (and that lady who was leading me to God)
>
> 5 disse: «Muta pensier; pensa ch'i' sono  
> (said: “Change your thought; consider that I am)
>
> 6 presso a colui ch'ogne torto disgrava».  
> (near him who relieves every wrong”.)

Beatrice does not deny the wrongs Dante will suffer. She places them within the larger jurisdiction of divine justice. Her nearness to God assures him that earthly injustice is neither ultimate nor invisible.

The description of her as the woman leading him to God also defines her role throughout *Paradiso*. She remains the beloved woman of Dante's earlier poetry, but her beauty and instruction now guide desire beyond itself.

## 2. Beatrice's Beauty Is a Reflection, Not the Destination

> 16 fin che 'l piacere etterno, che diretto  
> (so long as the eternal delight, which directly)
>
> 17 raggiava in Bëatrice, dal bel viso  
> (shone into Beatrice, from her beautiful face)
>
> 18 mi contentava col secondo aspetto.  
> (was satisfying me with its reflected appearance.)

God's delight shines directly into Beatrice and reaches Dante through her. The “second aspect” is this reflected manifestation: Dante encounters divine goodness through a created person rather than immediately in its source.

The passage does not dismiss human love as an obstacle. Instead, rightly understood love becomes a means of ascent. Beatrice's beauty is valuable precisely because it communicates something beyond herself.

> 19 Vincendo me col lume d'un sorriso,  
> (Overcoming me with the light of a smile,)
>
> 20 ella mi disse: «Volgiti e ascolta;  
> (she said to me: “Turn and listen;)
>
> 21 ché non pur ne' miei occhi è paradiso».  
> (for paradise is not only in my eyes”.)

Beatrice gently corrects the exclusiveness of Dante's attention. Even her sacred beauty must not become something he possesses or contemplates to the exclusion of everything else.

Her instruction also governs the reader's attention. Paradise is not merely the culmination of Dante's personal love story: it includes history, community, moral responsibility, and the right ordering of power.

## 3. Mars: The Heavenly Tree and the Warriors of Faith

> 28 El cominciò: «In questa quinta soglia  
> (He began: “On this fifth threshold)
>
> 29 de l'albero che vive de la cima  
> (of the tree that lives from its summit)
>
> 30 e frutta sempre e mai non perde foglia,  
> (and always bears fruit and never loses a leaf,)

Cacciaguida describes the heavens as a tree nourished from above. An earthly tree draws life through its roots; this heavenly tree lives from its summit, God. Its perpetual fruit and unfading leaves express the inexhaustibility of blessed life.

The “fifth threshold” is Mars, the fifth planetary heaven. Its traditional association with warfare is transformed by the luminous cross formed by its souls. Military distinction appears here under the sign of Christ's sacrifice, not simply as earthly conquest.

> 43 Così per Carlo Magno e per Orlando  
> (Thus for Charlemagne and for Roland)
>
> 44 due ne seguì lo mio attento sguardo,  
> (my attentive gaze followed two of them,)
>
> 45 com' occhio segue suo falcon volando.  
> (as an eye follows its falcon in flight.)
>
> 46 Poscia trasse Guiglielmo e Rinoardo  
> (Then William and Rainouart drew)
>
> 47 e 'l duca Gottifredi la mia vista  
> (and Duke Godfrey my sight)
>
> 48 per quella croce, e Ruberto Guiscardo.  
> (along that cross, and Robert Guiscard.)

The named warriors bring together biblical history, medieval history, and heroic literature. Earlier in the roll call come Joshua and Judas Maccabeus; here Dante names figures associated with the medieval Christian military imagination.

Charlemagne and Roland recall the epic tradition of the *Song of Roland*. William and Rainouart belong to the French epic cycle associated with William of Orange: Rainouart is a giant warrior who becomes a Christian. Godfrey of Bouillon was a leader of the First Crusade, while Robert Guiscard was the Norman conqueror active in southern Italy.

Dante treats these diverse figures as participants in a providential history. That reflects a medieval Christian idealization of warfare for the faith, not a neutral account of their campaigns. The cross supplies the theological framework within which their deeds are remembered.

## 4. Ascending to Jupiter: A Change in Beauty and Color

> 55 e vidi le sue luci tanto mere,  
> (and I saw her eyes so pure,)
>
> 56 tanto gioconde, che la sua sembianza  
> (so joyful, that her appearance)
>
> 57 vinceva li altri e l'ultimo solere.  
> (surpassed her other and her latest accustomed appearances.)

Dante recognizes his ascent through a change in Beatrice. Her increasing beauty functions as an index of his increasing proximity to God—and of his growing capacity to perceive divine glory.

Spiritual advancement is therefore registered through perception rather than through an ordinary description of travel. The guide becomes more radiant as the pilgrim becomes capable of receiving more.

> 67 tal fu ne li occhi miei, quando fui vòlto,  
> (such was the change before my eyes, when I turned,)
>
> 68 per lo candor de la temprata stella  
> (because of the whiteness of the temperate star,)
>
> 69 sesta, che dentro a sé m'avea ricolto.  
> (the sixth, which had received me within itself.)

The surrounding comparison describes a fair-skinned woman losing the redness of a blush. Likewise, the red light of Mars gives way to Jupiter's white radiance.

Jupiter was traditionally considered a temperate and beneficent planet, associated with justice and kingship. The transition thus changes both the visual atmosphere and the moral subject: from martial service to righteous government.

These planetary appearances should not be mistaken for permanent compartments of heaven. In Dante's cosmology, the blessed truly dwell in the Empyrean; they appear in particular spheres to make different aspects of blessedness intelligible to him.

## 5. A Community Becomes a Language

> 73 E come augelli surti di rivera,  
> (And as birds risen from a riverbank,)
>
> 74 quasi congratulando a lor pasture,  
> (as though rejoicing together over their feeding grounds,)
>
> 75 fanno di sé or tonda or altra schiera,  
> (make of themselves now a round formation, now another,)
>
> 76 sì dentro ai lumi sante creature  
> (so holy creatures within the lights)
>
> 77 volitando cantavano, e faciensi  
> (sang as they flew, and made themselves)
>
> 78 or D, or I, or L in sue figure.  
> (now D, now I, now L in their formations.)

Jupiter's souls arrange themselves into letters. The comparison to flocking birds makes their coordination seem both natural and joyful: no visible coercion imposes their order.

This is an image of community in which individuality and common purpose are compatible. Each soul remains a distinct light, yet their movements together produce intelligible meaning.

The first letters anticipate the word *DILIGITE*, “love”. The political lesson of Jupiter thus begins with rightly directed love, not merely with regulations or penalties. Just order arises when distinct wills cooperate in a shared good.

## 6. Poetry Must Recover Heaven's Command

> 85 illustrami di te, sì ch'io rilevi  
> (illuminate me with yourself, so that I may render)
>
> 86 le lor figure com' io l'ho concette:  
> (their figures as I have apprehended them:)
>
> 87 paia tua possa in questi versi brevi!  
> (let your power appear in these brief verses!)

Dante addresses the “Pegasean goddess”, a Muse invoked in the preceding lines. Classical inspiration is called upon to help represent a Christian revelation.

The immediate difficulty is one of translation between media. Dante must turn living, luminous formations into remembered language and written verse. His appeal acknowledges the distance between an experience of heaven and the poem that attempts to preserve it.

> 91 ‘DILIGITE IUSTITIAM’, primai  
> (‘LOVE JUSTICE’, first)
>
> 92 fur verbo e nome di tutto 'l dipinto;  
> (were the verb and noun of the whole inscription;)
>
> 93 ‘QUI IUDICATIS TERRAM’, fur sezzai.  
> (‘YOU WHO JUDGE THE EARTH’, were last.)

The complete inscription quotes Wisdom 1:1: “Love justice, you who judge the earth”. Its thirty-five letters have appeared successively through the coordinated movements of the blessed.

The imperative addresses those who govern and judge. Authority is not justified merely by possession, inheritance, or successful force: rulers must love justice.

That formulation reaches beyond correct procedure. A ruler who outwardly obeys rules while inwardly pursuing greed or domination still fails the command. Jupiter's message concerns the moral orientation from which public decisions proceed.

## 7. From the Letter M to the Imperial Eagle

> 94 Poscia ne l'emme del vocabol quinto  
> (Then in the M of the fifth word)
>
> 95 rimasero ordinate; sì che Giove  
> (they remained arranged; so that Jupiter)
>
> 96 pareva argento lì d'oro distinto.  
> (looked like silver marked there with gold.)

The fifth word is *TERRAM*, whose final letter is M. The souls remain in that shape, their golden light standing against Jupiter's silvery whiteness.

The written command is about to become a political emblem. The M is often understood as having a shape suited to the transformation, and it has also invited associations with monarchy. The essential point is the continuity: the eagle develops out of Scripture's demand for justice.

> 106 e quïetata ciascuna in suo loco,  
> (and when each had settled in its place,)
>
> 107 la testa e 'l collo d'un'aguglia vidi  
> (I saw the head and neck of an eagle)
>
> 108 rappresentare a quel distinto foco.  
> (depicted by that patterned fire.)

As additional lights rise and settle, the eagle's head and neck appear; the remaining souls complete the figure. This is the eagle of Roman imperial authority.

For Dante, universal temporal government could restrain rivalry and greed by establishing a common order of justice. The heavenly eagle represents that vocation in its ideal form, not an unconditional endorsement of every emperor.

Its construction is also significant: the emblem of sovereignty consists of many blessed persons. Rightful authority is pictured through concord rather than through the solitary magnificence of one ruler.

> 109 Quei che dipinge lì, non ha chi 'l guidi;  
> (He who paints there has no one to guide him;)
>
> 110 ma esso guida, e da lui si rammenta  
> (but he himself guides, and from him is recalled)
>
> 111 quella virtù ch'è forma per li nidi.  
> (that power which gives form within the nests.)

God is the artist of this living image. Unlike human artists, he needs no model or instructor.

The difficult reference to nests connects divine artistry with the formative power of nature: the power that shapes actual birds derives from God. The heavenly eagle and the natural world therefore share a source, but God's creative act depends on no prior example.

## 8. Heavenly Justice and the Smoke That Obscures It

> 115 O dolce stella, quali e quante gemme  
> (O sweet star, what splendid and numerous gems)
>
> 116 mi dimostraro che nostra giustizia  
> (showed me that our justice)
>
> 117 effetto sia del ciel che tu ingemme!  
> (is an effect of the heaven that you adorn like a gem!)

Dante addresses Jupiter and its luminous souls. Their arrangement reveals the association between this heaven and justice on earth.

Within medieval cosmology, celestial influences help shape earthly dispositions and activities. They do not, however, abolish human freedom. Jupiter's influence cannot excuse unjust rulers or mechanically produce good government.

The image establishes an ordered relationship between heaven and earth while leaving human beings responsible for how they act.

> 118 Per ch'io prego la mente in che s'inizia  
> (Therefore I pray to the mind in which originate)
>
> 119 tuo moto e tua virtute, che rimiri  
> (your motion and your power, that it look again)
>
> 120 ond' esce il fummo che 'l tuo raggio vizia;  
> (at the source of the smoke that corrupts your ray;)

The prayer passes beyond the planet to God, the source of its motion and power. Dante asks him to attend to the earthly “smoke” that obstructs justice.

The contrast is sharp: heaven communicates light, while corrupt human institutions produce obscurity. The following lines locate the immediate source of that smoke in the commercial abuse of ecclesiastical power.

## 9. The Church as Marketplace and Spiritual Battlefield

> 121 sì ch'un'altra fïata omai s'adiri  
> (so that now once again he may be angry)
>
> 122 del comperare e vender dentro al templo  
> (at the buying and selling within the temple)
>
> 123 che si murò di segni e di martìri.  
> (which was built with miracles and martyrdoms.)

Dante invokes Christ's expulsion of the merchants from the Temple. He asks for a renewed cleansing of the Church, whose foundations are miracles and the sacrifices of martyrs.

Buying and selling suggest simony and, more broadly, the conversion of spiritual authority into financial advantage. The offense is especially grave because the institution being exploited exists through the witness of people who surrendered their lives rather than sought worldly profit.

> 127 Già si solea con le spade far guerra;  
> (Once it was customary to wage war with swords;)
>
> 128 ma or si fa togliendo or qui or quivi  
> (but now it is waged by taking away here or there)
>
> 129 lo pan che 'l pïo Padre a nessun serra.  
> (the bread that the loving Father withholds from no one.)

The “bread” points to spiritual nourishment, particularly sacramental communion. Dante attacks the use of excommunication and related ecclesiastical penalties as instruments of political warfare.

The accusation is not that spiritual discipline can never be legitimate. It is that access to sacred goods is being manipulated to advance worldly interests.

This creates a dark contrast with Mars. There, warfare was presented through the ideal of service to faith; here, spiritual authority itself has become a weapon of faction and ambition.

## 10. The Pope Who Prefers the Baptist on a Coin

> 130 Ma tu che sol per cancellare scrivi,  
> (But you who write only in order to erase,)
>
> 131 pensa che Pietro e Paulo, che moriro  
> (consider that Peter and Paul, who died)
>
> 132 per la vigna che guasti, ancor son vivi.  
> (for the vineyard you lay waste, are still alive.)

The unnamed pope is generally identified as John XXII, whose pontificate began in 1316. Although the poem's journey is set in 1300, its denunciation can address events and figures contemporary with Dante's later composition.

Writing “only in order to erase” is commonly understood as issuing ecclesiastical censures with a view to their profitable cancellation. Sacred jurisdiction becomes a transaction.

The vineyard is the Church. Peter and Paul died for it, but they remain alive in heaven: the pope's conduct is therefore measured against living apostolic witnesses, not merely against a distant institutional past.

> 133 Ben puoi tu dire: «I' ho fermo 'l disiro  
> (You may indeed say: “I have fixed my desire)
>
> 134 sì a colui che volle viver solo  
> (so firmly on him who chose to live alone)
>
> 135 e che per salti fu tratto al martiro,  
> (and who through dancing was brought to martyrdom,)
>
> 136 ch'io non conosco il pescator né Polo».  
> (that I know neither the fisherman nor Paul”.)

The solitary martyr is John the Baptist, who lived in the wilderness and was executed after the dance of Herodias's daughter. But the supposed devotion contains a monetary joke: the Baptist appeared on Florence's gold florin.

Dante gives the pope an imagined confession of greed. His love of the Baptist is really love of the coin bearing the saint's image. So absorbing is that attachment that he no longer recognizes Peter, the fisherman, or Paul.

The canto ends by reversing its heavenly imagery. In Jupiter, lights become sacred writing and an emblem of justice. On earth, a sacred image on money becomes the focus of an idolatrous appetite.

## Epilogue — Justice Must Govern Power

Canto XVIII enlarges Dante's concern from his own unjust suffering to the institutions responsible for justice on earth. Beatrice directs him beyond private consolation; Mars presents an ideal of faithful service; Jupiter turns a community of blessed souls into Scripture and then into the imperial eagle.

Against that luminous order stands the smoke of ecclesiastical greed. The canto's governing distinction is not simply between Church and Empire, but between authority devoted to its proper purpose and authority corrupted by appetite. Its central command applies to every holder of power: love justice.
