# Light Beyond Language: Allegory and Background in Dante's *Paradiso*, Canto XXIII

In Canto XXIII, Dante and Beatrice are in the heaven of the Fixed Stars, the eighth sphere of the medieval cosmos. Here they witness the triumph of Christ, surrounded by the redeemed, followed by the glorification of Mary. Before Dante undergoes examinations in faith, hope, and charity in the following cantos, he sees the heavenly reality toward which those virtues lead.

The canto moves between overwhelming light and familiar earthly images: a bird awaiting dawn, a garden, a mother and child. These images do not reduce heaven to ordinary experience. They give the reader ways of approaching a reality that Dante repeatedly declares beyond the powers of sight, memory, and poetry.

## 1. The Bird Awaiting Dawn: Love as Attentive Expectation

> 7 previene il tempo in su aperta frasca,  
> (anticipates the hour upon an open bough,)
>
> 8 e con ardente affetto il sole aspetta,  
> (and with burning affection awaits the sun,)
>
> 9 fiso guardando pur che l'alba nasca;  
> (gazing fixedly, intent only that dawn should break;)

The canto opens with an extended comparison between Beatrice and a bird waiting for daylight so that it can see and feed its young. Its love makes the labor of finding food welcome rather than burdensome.

The bird's anticipation gives the coming revelation an emotional shape before its theological meaning becomes clear. As sunlight will enable the bird to nourish its nestlings, Christ's appearing will nourish Dante's understanding. Beatrice's guidance is thus presented through the tenderness and vigilance of parental care.

> 10 così la donna mïa stava eretta  
> (so my lady stood upright)
>
> 11 e attenta, rivolta inver' la plaga  
> (and attentive, turned toward the region)
>
> 12 sotto la quale il sol mostra men fretta:  
> (beneath which the sun shows least haste:)

Beatrice looks toward the region of the sky associated with the sun at its highest point, where its movement appears least hurried. Her stillness is not inactivity: it is concentrated expectation.

Dante does not yet know what she anticipates. He learns to desire through watching her desire. This is characteristic of Beatrice's role throughout the *Paradiso*: she directs him beyond herself, educating both his intellect and his love.

## 2. Christ's Triumph: The Harvest of the Heavens

> 19 e Bëatrice disse: «Ecco le schiere  
> (and Beatrice said: “Behold the hosts)
>
> 20 del trïunfo di Cristo e tutto 'l frutto  
> (of Christ's triumph and all the fruit)
>
> 21 ricolto del girar di queste spere!».  
> (gathered from the turning of these spheres!”)

The word “triumph” evokes the public procession celebrating a victorious Roman commander. Dante transforms that political spectacle into a Christian revelation: Christ's victory is over sin and death, and the blessed are its living evidence.

The agricultural image adds another dimension. The redeemed are the “fruit” of the heavens' movement. In Dante's cosmology, celestial influences help shape earthly life under divine providence; the heavens are instruments within an ordered creation, not independent powers that determine salvation. Their ultimate harvest is the fulfillment of human life in God.

> 28 vid' i' sopra migliaia di lucerne  
> (I saw above thousands of lamps)
>
> 29 un sol che tutte quante l'accendea,  
> (a sun that kindled every one of them,)
>
> 30 come fa 'l nostro le viste superne;  
> (as ours does the lights above;)

Christ appears as a sun illuminating the innumerable lights of the blessed. Their glory is real, but it is received: the redeemed shine through participation in Christ's light.

Immediately before this, Dante compares the spectacle to Trivia—the classical goddess associated with Diana and the moon—among the stars. Classical mythology supplies a beautiful preliminary image, but the Christian vision surpasses it. The central light is not merely the most splendid member of a constellation: it is the source of the others' radiance.

## 3. Divine Wisdom and Power: The Reopened Road

> 37 Quivi è la sapïenza e la possanza  
> (There are the wisdom and the power)
>
> 38 ch'aprì le strade tra 'l cielo e la terra,  
> (that opened the roads between heaven and earth,)
>
> 39 onde fu già sì lunga disïanza».  
> (for which there once was such long yearning.”)

Dante cannot sustain the brightness before him. Beatrice explains that he is encountering the divine wisdom and power embodied in Christ. The pairing recalls Saint Paul's description of Christ as the power and wisdom of God in 1 Corinthians 1:24.

The opened roads refer to reconciliation between humanity and God through the Incarnation and Christ's redemptive work. Heaven is not reached solely by humanity's ascent: the way becomes possible because God first enters human history.

The “long yearning” evokes the expectation of redemption before Christ. Dante's personal journey is therefore situated within sacred history. What he now sees fulfills a desire extending far beyond his own life.

## 4. Ecstasy and the Enlargement of Human Capacity

> 43 la mente mia così, tra quelle dape  
> (my mind likewise, amid those feasts)
>
> 44 fatta più grande, di sé stessa uscìo,  
> (made greater, went out of itself,)
>
> 45 e che si fesse rimembrar non sape.  
> (and what it became it cannot remember.)

Dante compares his mind to lightning breaking out of a cloud because it has expanded beyond the cloud's capacity to contain it. Medieval natural philosophy supplies the image; mystical experience supplies its deeper significance.

The mind does not simply fail before divine revelation. It is first “made greater,” then carried beyond its ordinary limits. The language describes ecstasy in its literal sense of standing outside oneself.

The “feasts” continue the canto's imagery of nourishment. Divine truth feeds and enlarges the intellect, yet what the intellect experiences can exceed what memory later preserves. Dante distinguishes between having an experience and being able to reconstruct it in language.

> 46 «Apri li occhi e riguarda qual son io;  
> (“Open your eyes and look upon what I am;)
>
> 47 tu hai vedute cose, che possente  
> (you have seen things such that capable)
>
> 48 se' fatto a sostener lo riso mio».  
> (you have been made of sustaining my smile.”)

Beatrice's smile, previously too powerful for Dante to bear, has now become accessible. The apparently forced grammar of these lines emphasizes the transformation: he has been *made capable*.

Spiritual ascent changes the observer, not merely the objects observed. Dante can receive more beauty because divine revelation has strengthened his powers. Beatrice's smile expresses blessed joy, but it also marks a new stage in his education.

## 5. The Sacred Poem at the Limits of Speech

> 61 e così, figurando il paradiso,  
> (and so, in depicting paradise,)
>
> 62 convien saltar lo sacrato poema,  
> (the sacred poem must make a leap,)
>
> 63 come chi trova suo cammin riciso.  
> (like someone who finds the path cut off.)

Dante declares that even the combined gifts of the Muses could not adequately describe Beatrice's smile. Consequently, the poem must “leap”: it must pass over what it cannot represent.

This is more than a conventional declaration that something is extraordinarily beautiful. The poem makes its own limitation part of its account of heaven. A finite language cannot contain the fullness of divine reality.

Calling the work a “sacred poem” also raises the stakes. Dante claims a religious purpose for his poetry while acknowledging that this purpose exposes it to demands beyond ordinary artistic competence.

> 67 non è pareggio da picciola barca  
> (it is no stretch of sea for a little boat)
>
> 68 quel che fendendo va l'ardita prora,  
> (that which the daring prow goes cleaving,)
>
> 69 né da nocchier ch'a sé medesmo parca.  
> (nor for a helmsman who spares himself.)

The metaphor shifts from a blocked road to a dangerous sea. Poetry becomes navigation, requiring both boldness and strenuous effort.

Dante's humility is therefore not a retreat from ambition. He admits that his vessel is mortal while insisting on the seriousness of its voyage. The reader should recognize both the inadequacy of the medium and the courage required to use it.

## 6. The Heavenly Garden: Mary, the Apostles, and Merciful Light

> 73 Quivi è la rosa in che 'l verbo divino  
> (There is the rose in which the divine Word)
>
> 74 carne si fece; quivi son li gigli  
> (became flesh; there are the lilies)
>
> 75 al cui odor si prese il buon cammino».  
> (at whose fragrance the good path was taken.”)

Beatrice turns Dante's attention away from her face toward the heavenly garden. Mary is the rose in whom the Word became flesh, an explicit reference to the Incarnation and to the language of John 1:14.

The lilies are commonly understood as the apostles, whose teaching and example directed humanity toward salvation. Their “fragrance” suggests an influence that attracts rather than coerces.

The garden gathers individual lives into a single flourishing order. It also anticipates the great celestial rose of the later *Paradiso*, though Mary's identification as a rose here is a distinct image.

> 85 O benigna vertù che sì li 'mprenti,  
> (O gracious power that so stamps them with your impress,)
>
> 86 sù t'essaltasti, per largirmi loco  
> (you raised yourself on high, to grant me room)
>
> 87 a li occhi lì che non t'eran possenti.  
> (for my eyes there, which were not strong enough for you.)

Christ rises beyond Dante's direct sight, allowing him to contemplate the blessed without being overwhelmed by their source of illumination.

This withdrawal is an act of generosity, not abandonment. Divine power accommodates itself to human weakness. Dante can now see the effects of Christ's light because he is spared the full intensity of its origin.

## 7. Gabriel's Crown: The Annunciation Remembered in Heaven

> 94 per entro il cielo scese una facella,  
> (through the heaven descended a little flame,)
>
> 95 formata in cerchio a guisa di corona,  
> (formed into a circle like a crown,)
>
> 96 e cinsela e girossi intorno ad ella.  
> (and it encircled her and revolved around her.)

A fiery angel descends and circles Mary, making a living crown. The angel is traditionally identified as Gabriel, the messenger of the Annunciation.

That identification joins Mary's heavenly glory to her earthly assent to God. The angel who announced the Incarnation now celebrates its fulfillment. The crown is not a static ornament but an action of love: movement itself becomes praise.

> 103 «Io sono amore angelico, che giro  
> (“I am angelic love, who circle)
>
> 104 l'alta letizia che spira del ventre  
> (the lofty joy that breathes from the womb)
>
> 105 che fu albergo del nostro disiro;  
> (that was the dwelling of our desire;)

The angel names itself through its defining activity: “angelic love.” Mary's womb was the dwelling of Christ, the object of angelic desire.

The bodily language matters. This heavenly vision does not discard the material world as worthless. At its center stands the mystery that the divine Word entered a human body. Mary's glory celebrates that union of divinity and humanity.

The angel's music is correspondingly beyond earthly comparison. Dante says that even the sweetest terrestrial melody would sound like thunder beside it: heavenly harmony transforms the scale by which beauty is judged.

## 8. Mary's Ascent and the Mantle of the Cosmos

> 112 Lo real manto di tutti i volumi  
> (The royal mantle of all the revolving spheres)
>
> 113 del mondo, che più ferve e più s'avviva  
> (of the world, which burns more fiercely and grows more alive)
>
> 114 ne l'alito di Dio e nei costumi,  
> (in the breath of God and in his ways,)

The “royal mantle” is the Primum Mobile, the ninth sphere, which surrounds the lower moving heavens. In Dante's universe, it receives divine influence and communicates motion to the spheres within it.

Its swift movement expresses the intensity of its relation to God. Medieval cosmology here becomes a language of desire: the universe moves because it is ordered toward divine goodness.

Beyond the moving heavens lies the Empyrean, the dwelling of God and the blessed, outside ordinary spatial and temporal conditions.

> 118 però non ebber li occhi miei potenza  
> (therefore my eyes did not have the power)
>
> 119 di seguitar la coronata fiamma  
> (to follow the crowned flame)
>
> 120 che si levò appresso sua semenza.  
> (that rose after her offspring.)

The crowned flame is Mary, ascending after Christ, her “offspring.” The phrase preserves the startling intimacy of their relationship: the Savior whom heaven adores is also her son.

Dante's sight cannot follow her. His powers have increased, but they remain limited. The canto repeatedly stages revelation as a measured process, not a sudden possession of everything that can be known.

## 9. The Blessed as Children: Love Made Visible

> 121 E come fantolin che 'nver' la mamma  
> (And like a little child who toward its mother)
>
> 122 tende le braccia, poi che 'l latte prese,  
> (stretches its arms, after taking milk,)
>
> 123 per l'animo che 'nfin di fuor s'infiamma;  
> (because of the feeling that flames even outward;)

The domestic image recalls the bird and its young at the canto's opening. Once again, nourishment produces a gesture of love.

The child reaches toward its mother *after* being fed. Its movement therefore expresses more than need: it is affection overflowing from satisfaction. Dante uses this distinction to suggest heavenly desire, which remains ardent without being the painful craving of deprivation.

> 124 ciascun di quei candori in sù si stese  
> (each of those radiances stretched upward)
>
> 125 con la sua cima, sì che l'alto affetto  
> (with its summit, so that the deep affection)
>
> 126 ch'elli avieno a Maria mi fu palese.  
> (that they bore toward Mary became clear to me.)

The luminous forms of the blessed extend upward after Mary, making their inward love outwardly visible. Heavenly light is not impersonal radiance: it expresses feeling, intention, and relationship.

The blessed then sing the Marian antiphon “Regina celi,” associated with Easter rejoicing. Their song links Mary's glorification to Christ's Resurrection. Dante's lasting delight in it contrasts with the earlier failure of memory: he cannot retain every vision, but its joy remains.

## 10. Heavenly Treasure, Earthly Exile, and Peter's Keys

> 133 Quivi si vive e gode del tesoro  
> (There one lives and rejoices in the treasure)
>
> 134 che s'acquistò piangendo ne lo essilio  
> (that was acquired weeping in the exile)
>
> 135 di Babillòn, ove si lasciò l'oro.  
> (of Babylon, where the gold was left behind.)

Babylon evokes the biblical exile of Israel and, allegorically, earthly life understood as separation from the heavenly homeland.

The passage reverses ordinary economic values. The true treasure is acquired through faithful living amid suffering, whereas material gold must be left behind. Heavenly wealth consists in what can survive death: love, holiness, and communion with God.

The imagery also carries particular force in a poem written by an exile. Dante places the pain of displacement within a larger Christian account of humanity's journey home.

> 136 Quivi trïunfa, sotto l'alto Filio  
> (There triumphs, beneath the exalted Son)
>
> 137 di Dio e di Maria, di sua vittoria,  
> (of God and of Mary, in his victory,)
>
> 138 e con l'antico e col novo concilio,  
> (and with the ancient and the new assembly,)
>
> 139 colui che tien le chiavi di tal gloria.  
> (he who holds the keys of such glory.)

The final figure is Saint Peter, identified by the keys Christ grants him in Matthew 16:19. The ancient and new assemblies encompass the blessed of the old and new covenants.

Peter's authority remains explicitly subordinate to Christ. His triumph is participation in divine victory, not an independent sovereignty. The ending also prepares the next canto, in which Peter will examine Dante on faith: the pilgrim must articulate the belief whose glorious fulfillment he has just witnessed.

## Epilogue — Learning to Receive the Light

Canto XXIII presents heaven as a reality that both exceeds and enlarges human capacity. Christ illuminates the redeemed, Mary embodies the mystery of the Incarnation, and Beatrice teaches Dante how to look without mistaking the guide for the destination.

Its central movement is from expectation to revelation, and from overwhelming revelation to grateful love. Birds, flowers, mothers, children, harvests, and treasure make that movement imaginable. Yet the poem's acknowledged gaps remain essential: divine reality can be genuinely encountered without being fully contained by memory or speech.
