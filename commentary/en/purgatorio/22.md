# Poetry, Conversion, and the Discipline of Desire: *Purgatorio*, Canto XXII

Canto XXII follows Dante, Virgil, and the Roman poet Statius from the terrace of avarice and prodigality into the terrace of gluttony. Its central concern is the education of desire: how love, poetry, repentance, and bodily discipline can redirect human appetites toward their proper ends.

Statius provides the canto's great example of this transformation. Through him, Dante imagines classical poetry preparing a reader for Christian revelation—even when the poet himself cannot enter the salvation his words help another person discover.

## 1. The Ascent: Becoming Lighter Through Purification

> 7 E io più lieve che per l'altre foci  
> (And I, lighter than at the other passageways,)
>
> 8 m'andava, sì che sanz' alcun labore  
> (went onward, so that without any effort)
>
> 9 seguiva in sù li spiriti veloci;  
> (I followed upward the swift spirits;)

An angel has just erased another of the seven marks of sin inscribed on Dante's forehead at the entrance to Purgatory. These marks represent the seven capital sins; their gradual removal makes his ascent progressively easier.

Physical lightness expresses a spiritual principle. Sin does not make people free: it burdens them, hindering their movement toward the good. Purification restores the will's natural capacity to rise toward God. Dante therefore experiences moral improvement not merely as submission to a rule but as liberation from resistance within himself.

The angel's blessing adapts the biblical beatitude concerning those who hunger and thirst for righteousness. Here the emphasis falls on thirst, leaving hunger particularly appropriate to the terrace of gluttony now ahead. Bodily appetite is becoming the language through which spiritual desire is understood.

## 2. Friendship: Virtuous Love Awakens Love

> 10 quando Virgilio incominciò: «Amore,  
> (when Virgil began: “Love,)
>
> 11 acceso di virtù, sempre altro accese,  
> (kindled by virtue, always kindled another,)
>
> 12 pur che la fiamma sua paresse fore;  
> (provided that its flame appeared outwardly;)

Virgil explains why he already loves Statius. When the satirist Juvenal entered Limbo, he told Virgil of Statius's devotion to him. Even before meeting Statius, Virgil responded with affection.

The image of fire makes love communicable: one flame lights another. But Virgil carefully specifies love “kindled by virtue.” This is not simply the claim that every intense desire deserves reciprocation. It is an account of friendship founded on recognized goodness.

The passage also recalls Francesca's language about love in *Inferno* V. Where Francesca presents love as an irresistible force that excuses surrender, Virgil describes an affection grounded in virtue and made possible by truthful communication. Dante thus distinguishes love that educates desire from love used to evade responsibility.

The encounter also creates a community across generations. Books and reports of admiration allow poets separated by death and historical time to become friends.

## 3. Statius's Surprise: The Sin Was Prodigality

> 34 Or sappi ch'avarizia fu partita  
> (Now know that avarice was separated)
>
> 35 troppo da me, e questa dismisura  
> (too far from me, and this lack of measure)
>
> 36 migliaia di lunari hanno punita.  
> (thousands of lunar cycles have punished.)

Virgil assumes that Statius's long stay among the avaricious means that he had hoarded wealth. Statius corrects him: his fault was the opposite, excessive spending.

The crucial word is *dismisura*, lack of measure. Virtue does not consist in escaping one extreme by rushing into its contrary. A person can be enslaved to wealth by grasping it too tightly or by distributing and consuming it without judgment.

This reflects the ethical tradition, especially associated with Aristotle, in which virtue occupies a rightly determined mean between opposed vices. Generosity is neither miserliness nor wastefulness: it is the proper use of possessions.

> 49 E sappie che la colpa che rimbecca  
> (And know that the fault which counters)
>
> 50 per dritta opposizione alcun peccato,  
> (through direct opposition any sin,)
>
> 51 con esso insieme qui suo verde secca;  
> (together with it dries up its greenery here;)

The opposing sins undergo purification together because they deform the same underlying appetite. The image of drying greenery suggests that vice loses its living power: its capacity to sprout again is being extinguished.

The arrangement also recalls *Inferno* VII, where hoarders and squanderers collide while pushing enormous weights. In Hell their opposition produces endless accusation. In Purgatory both extremes can be corrected because the souls acknowledge their faults and desire healing.

## 4. Reading Virgil: Poetry as a Moral Turning Point

> 40 ‘Per che non reggi tu, o sacra fame  
> (‘Why do you not govern, O sacred hunger)
>
> 41 de l'oro, l'appetito de' mortali?’,  
> (for gold, the appetite of mortals?’,)

Statius credits his repentance to a passage in Virgil's *Aeneid*. The source is the story of Polydorus, murdered for his wealth, and Virgil's denunciation of the *auri sacra fames*: the accursed hunger for gold that drives human beings to crime.

Dante's wording creates a famous interpretive difficulty. The Latin *sacra* can have the sinister meaning “accursed,” whereas the Italian *sacra* readily suggests “sacred.” On a common reading, Statius turns Virgil's condemnation of greed into an appeal for a properly ordered desire for wealth to restrain reckless expenditure. The precise force of Dante's reformulation remains debated.

What is clear is that Statius does not merely admire Virgil's language. He applies it to his own conduct and recognizes a fault he had failed to see.

> 43 Allor m'accorsi che troppo aprir l'ali  
> (Then I perceived that too widely spreading their wings)
>
> 44 potean le mani a spendere, e pente'mi  
> (the hands could be in spending, and I repented)
>
> 45 così di quel come de li altri mali.  
> (of that as well as of my other evils.)

The winged hands are a vivid image of uncontrolled expenditure. What might look like freedom or generosity becomes an appetite escaping rational government.

Poetry saves Statius from moral blindness, but not automatically. Its words must be understood, brought to bear on personal conduct, and followed by repentance. His account presents reading as an ethical activity: a text becomes transformative through the reader's response.

## 5. The Backward Lamp: A Poet Who Illuminates Others

> 67 Facesti come quei che va di notte,  
> (You acted like someone who walks at night,)
>
> 68 che porta il lume dietro e sé non giova,  
> (who carries the light behind and does not benefit himself,)
>
> 69 ma dopo sé fa le persone dotte,  
> (but makes the people behind him knowledgeable,)

Statius now reveals that Virgil led him not only toward moral reform but toward Christianity. The comparison of Virgil to a person carrying a lamp behind himself is the canto's governing image.

Virgil's poetry illuminates later readers, yet its author does not receive the saving benefit they find in it. Within the poem's Christian framework, natural reason and classical culture possess genuine power, but they cannot by themselves supply the revelation and faith necessary for salvation.

Statius particularly invokes Virgil's Fourth Eclogue, which announces a renewed age, returning justice, and a new offspring descending from heaven. Written within the political and literary world of the late Roman Republic, the poem was subsequently read by Christians as anticipating Christ. Dante builds on that Christian interpretive tradition rather than offering a modern historical account of Virgil's intentions.

> 73 Per te poeta fui, per te cristiano:  
> (Through you I became a poet, through you a Christian:)

The line gives Virgil a double spiritual fatherhood. He awakens Statius's poetic vocation and prepares him to recognize Christian truth.

Yet the parallel is painfully unequal. Statius will ascend to heaven; Virgil will return to Limbo. Dante honors the power of pagan poetry without removing the theological boundary that makes Virgil's position tragic.

## 6. Conversion and Concealment: The Cost of Fear

> 79 e la parola tua sopra toccata  
> (and your word touched upon above)
>
> 80 si consonava a' nuovi predicanti;  
> (was in harmony with the new preachers;)
>
> 81 ond' io a visitarli presi usata.  
> (therefore I adopted the practice of visiting them.)

Virgil's poetry does not function as a substitute for Christianity. Rather, Statius hears a harmony between the poem and the preaching of Christians and begins seeking them out.

His conversion develops through contact with a community. He admires Christian conduct, grieves over persecution, and assists the persecuted. Poetry opens a possibility; teaching and lived example give that possibility substance.

Dante places this development under the emperor Domitian, following a Christian tradition that portrayed his reign as a period of persecution. The historical scope of such persecution is debated. More fundamentally, there is no secure historical evidence that Statius became a Christian: his conversion belongs to Dante's imaginative reconstruction of the ancient poet.

> 89 di Tebe poetando, ebb' io battesmo;  
> (of Thebes in poetry, I received baptism;)
>
> 90 ma per paura chiuso cristian fu'mi,  
> (but through fear I was a hidden Christian,)
>
> 91 lungamente mostrando paganesmo;  
> (for a long time displaying paganism;)

Statius says that he was baptized before reaching a particular stage in the composition of the *Thebaid*, his epic about the war between the sons of Oedipus. Nevertheless, he concealed his faith.

Dante distinguishes conversion from the courage to live openly by its implications. Statius's fear produced spiritual lukewarmness, for which he spent more than four hundred years on the terrace of sloth.

Here sloth means more than ordinary laziness. It is deficient energy in pursuing the good: knowing what deserves love but failing to commit oneself wholeheartedly. Statius's story consequently includes both the power of revelation and the long work required to overcome an incomplete response.

## 7. Limbo: The Dignity and Limit of Classical Culture

> 100 «Costoro e Persio e io e altri assai»,  
> (“These and Persius and I and many others”,)
>
> 101 rispuose il duca mio, «siam con quel Greco  
> (my guide replied, “are with that Greek)
>
> 102 che le Muse lattar più ch'altri mai,  
> (whom the Muses suckled more than any other,)
>
> 103 nel primo cinghio del carcere cieco;  
> (in the first circle of the blind prison;)

Statius asks after other ancient writers, and Virgil describes their shared existence in Limbo. “That Greek” is Homer, represented as the supreme recipient of the Muses' nourishment.

The image preserves the greatness of classical poetry. These writers remain a distinguished intellectual community, discussing poetry and Mount Parnassus, the traditional home of the Muses. Yet their dwelling is still part of the “blind prison.”

Dante refuses an easy resolution between cultural admiration and his account of salvation. Literary achievement and moral dignity are real goods, but they do not abolish the absence of Christian faith. Limbo is therefore neither a dismissal of antiquity nor an alternative heaven.

> 112 Védeisi quella che mostrò Langia;  
> (There is seen the woman who showed Langia;)
>
> 113 èvvi la figlia di Tiresia, e Teti,  
> (there are the daughter of Tiresias, and Thetis,)

Virgil also names figures from the mythological world of Statius's poetry. The woman who showed the spring Langia is Hypsipyle, an important figure in the *Thebaid*. Authors and their poetic subjects inhabit a common imaginative geography.

The daughter of Tiresias introduces a well-known difficulty. If she is Manto, the reference appears to conflict with *Inferno* XX, where Manto is placed among the diviners. Commentators have proposed alternative identifications or explanations, but the discrepancy remains unresolved.

## 8. Dante the Listener: Learning How to Write

> 127 Elli givan dinanzi, e io soletto  
> (They went ahead, and I alone)
>
> 128 di retro, e ascoltava i lor sermoni,  
> (behind, and I listened to their conversations,)
>
> 129 ch'a poetar mi davano intelletto.  
> (which gave me understanding for composing poetry.)

After reaching the sixth terrace, the three travelers turn right, following their established practice of circling the mountain. Virgil and Statius walk ahead in conversation; Dante follows and listens.

The positioning dramatizes literary apprenticeship. Dante learns through attentive reception of predecessors rather than by declaring independence from them.

It also extends the image of the backward-facing lamp. Once again, those who go ahead illuminate someone following behind. Dante receives instruction from both a pagan master and a poet whom he imagines as that master's Christian disciple.

The understanding he gains concerns more than technique. Their conversation has linked poetry with friendship, ethical self-recognition, and revelation. The *Comedy* itself aspires to unite those powers in the service of its readers.

## 9. The Inverted Tree: Appetite Meets a Boundary

> 133 e come abete in alto si digrada  
> (and as a fir narrows upward)
>
> 134 di ramo in ramo, così quello in giuso,  
> (from branch to branch, so that one downward,)
>
> 135 cred' io, perché persona sù non vada.  
> (I believe, so that no person may climb up.)

An extraordinary tree interrupts the poets' conversation. Its fruit smells delicious, and clear water falls from the rock above onto its leaves. But unlike a fir, which becomes narrower toward its top, this tree becomes narrower toward its base.

Dante explicitly interprets the shape as preventing ascent. The food is attractive but inaccessible: appetite encounters a limit it cannot simply overcome through bodily effort.

The tree belongs to the discipline of the gluttonous. The next canto will make the penitential effects of hunger and thirst more explicit; here the setting already establishes the lesson. Desire must learn not to treat every attractive object as something immediately available for consumption.

> 141 gridò: «Di questo cibo avrete caro».  
> (cried: “Of this food you will have scarcity”.)

The voice from the foliage announces deprivation. The old expression *avrete caro* means that the travelers will lack this food, not that they will cherish it.

The scene evokes Eden's association of fruit, appetite, and prohibition, although the connection is not fully explained here. Its immediate purpose is corrective rather than merely punitive. Food and water are not evil; the problem is the appetite that refuses measure.

## 10. Temperance: Feeding the Body Without Serving It

> 142 Poi disse: «Più pensava Maria onde  
> (Then it said: “Mary thought more about how)
>
> 143 fosser le nozze orrevoli e intere,  
> (the wedding might be honorable and complete,)
>
> 144 ch'a la sua bocca, ch'or per voi risponde.  
> (than about her mouth, which now intercedes for you.)

The voice begins a series of examples of temperance with Mary at the wedding at Cana. When the wine ran out, she drew Jesus's attention to the hosts' difficulty. Dante emphasizes her concern for the dignity of the celebration rather than for her own consumption.

Temperance therefore begins not with hostility toward bodily pleasure but with freedom from self-centered appetite. Mary attends to other people's needs. Her intercession in heaven continues the concern for others displayed at Cana.

The voice then recalls ancient Roman women satisfied with water and Daniel's refusal of royal food. These examples combine classical and biblical traditions: disciplined appetite belongs to a broad moral inheritance.

> 148 Lo secol primo, quant' oro fu bello,  
> (The first age, as beautiful as gold,)
>
> 149 fé savorose con fame le ghiande,  
> (made acorns savory through hunger,)
>
> 150 e nettare con sete ogne ruscello.  
> (and every stream nectar through thirst.)

The classical Golden Age supplies an image of desire satisfied without luxury. Hunger makes simple food delicious; thirst makes ordinary water seem divine.

The point is not that pleasure must disappear. Properly ordered appetite makes pleasure possible without extravagance. Instead of endlessly multiplying desirable objects, a person can recover the capacity to delight in enough.

> 151 Mele e locuste furon le vivande  
> (Honey and locusts were the foods)
>
> 152 che nodriro il Batista nel diserto;  
> (that nourished the Baptist in the wilderness;)

John the Baptist concludes the examples. His wilderness diet, drawn from the Gospels, associates bodily restraint with prophetic greatness.

Together, the examples show several forms of temperance: care for others, resistance to luxury, simplicity, and dedication to a divine vocation. The body is nourished, but nourishment does not become life's governing purpose.

## Epilogue — Learning What Desire Is For

Canto XXII joins literary education to the purification of appetite. Statius learns to recognize his excesses, receives Christian truth through poetry and personal encounter, and gradually overcomes the failures that survived his conversion.

Virgil embodies both the greatness and the limit of classical wisdom: he can illuminate a road he cannot himself complete. Dante follows, learning how poetry might guide its readers toward moral and spiritual change.

The inaccessible fruit brings these themes into bodily focus. Whether the object is money, knowledge, friendship, or food, salvation requires more than desire's intensity. Desire must learn its proper measure, its proper object, and its responsibility to others.
