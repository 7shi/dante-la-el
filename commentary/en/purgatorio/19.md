# The Siren and the Earth: Allegory in *Purgatorio*, Canto XIX

Canto XIX moves from an alluring dream to a painful awakening, and then to a meeting with a pope who discovered the emptiness of worldly ambition. Its governing question is how desire can mistake something degrading for something beautiful—and how it can learn to see truly again.

Dante begins on the terrace of sloth and climbs to the fifth terrace, where avarice and prodigality are purged. His dream prepares him for all three remaining terraces: those devoted to excessive attachment to wealth, food, and sexual pleasure.

## 1. Before Dawn: A Dream at the Threshold of Truth

> 4 —quando i geomanti lor Maggior Fortuna  
> (—when the geomancers their Greater Fortune)
>
> 5 veggiono in orïente, innanzi a l'alba,  
> (see in the east, before dawn,)
>
> 6 surger per via che poco le sta bruna—,  
> (rise along a path that remains dark for it only briefly—,)

The canto opens at the coldest part of the night, when the previous day's warmth can no longer resist the chill associated with earth, moon, and Saturn. The astrological setting places Dante's dream just before sunrise.

Geomancy was a form of divination using patterns of points marked on the ground. The “Greater Fortune,” or *Fortuna Maior*, was one of its favorable figures; Dante evokes a configuration of eastern stars resembling it. Its path will remain dark only briefly because dawn is approaching.

In medieval literary tradition, dreams near dawn could carry particular significance. This is the second of the *Purgatorio*'s three major dreams, and its movement from deception to exposure anticipates the movement from darkness into daylight. The astronomical introduction therefore establishes both the hour and a threshold between confused perception and clearer understanding.

## 2. Desire Manufactures Its Own Beauty

> 7 mi venne in sogno una femmina balba,  
> (there came to me in a dream a stammering woman,)
>
> 8 ne li occhi guercia, e sovra i piè distorta,  
> (cross-eyed, and crooked upon her feet,)
>
> 9 con le man monche, e di colore scialba.  
> (with maimed hands, and pale in complexion.)

The woman initially appears as a collection of impairments: defective speech, sight, movement, and action. Within the dream's allegory, these outward features express the inward disorder of the pleasures she represents. Her appearance is not the canto's final account of her power, however. That power emerges through Dante's response.

The dream is especially unsettling because temptation does not arrive already beautiful. Dante helps make it so.

> 12 così lo sguardo mio le facea scorta  
> (so my gaze made nimble for her)
>
> 13 la lingua, e poscia tutta la drizzava  
> (the tongue, and then straightened all of her)
>
> 14 in poco d'ora, e lo smarrito volto,  
> (in a short while, and her faded face,)
>
> 15 com' amor vuol, così le colorava.  
> (as love wills, so it gave color to.)

Dante's gaze releases the woman's speech, straightens her body, and colors her face. The transformation dramatizes desire's ability to embellish its object. What seems irresistibly attractive may owe its attractiveness to the person looking at it.

The phrase “as love wills” recalls the language of love poetry, in which the lover's attention magnifies the beloved's beauty. Here that process becomes dangerous. Attraction does not guarantee goodness: love can illuminate, but disordered love can also fabricate.

This develops the preceding cantos' explanation that love underlies human action. The central moral problem is not the existence of desire, but its object, measure, and direction.

## 3. The Siren's Promise: Pleasure Without Departure

> 19 «Io son», cantava, «io son dolce serena,  
> (“I am,” she sang, “I am a sweet siren,)
>
> 20 che ' marinari in mezzo mar dismago;  
> (who lead sailors astray in the midst of the sea;)
>
> 21 tanto son di piacere a sentir piena!  
> (so full of pleasure to hear am I!)

Once transformed, the woman identifies herself through song. Her appeal operates through pleasurable sensation, but its consequence is disorientation: sailors lose their course.

Navigation provides a moral analogy. Human life is a journey toward its proper destination, ultimately God. The siren offers an experience so absorbing that the traveler forgets where he ought to go. Pleasure becomes destructive when it ceases to support the journey and becomes a substitute for its destination.

> 22 Io volsi Ulisse del suo cammin vago  
> (I turned Ulysses, eager for his journey,)
>
> 23 al canto mio; e qual meco s'ausa,  
> (toward my song; and whoever grows accustomed to me,)
>
> 24 rado sen parte; sì tutto l'appago!».  
> (rarely departs from me; so completely do I satisfy him!”.)

The reference to Ulysses draws on the classical tradition of the Sirens. Dante knew that tradition through Latin literature and medieval transmission rather than through direct reading of Homer's Greek.

Her boast should not be treated as an impartial account. In the familiar story, Ulysses hears the Sirens while bound to his ship's mast and escapes them. The dream-siren presents herself as victorious, appropriating the prestige of a famous traveler.

Her most revealing promise is complete satisfaction. Elsewhere in Dante's moral universe, no finite good can finally satisfy the human heart. The siren claims precisely what earthly pleasure cannot deliver. Habit then reinforces deception: whoever becomes accustomed to her rarely leaves.

## 4. The Holy Woman and the Exposure of Corruption

> 26 quand' una donna apparve santa e presta  
> (when a woman appeared, holy and ready,)
>
> 27 lunghesso me per far colei confusa.  
> (beside me, to put that other woman to shame.)

The second woman interrupts the seduction. Unlike the siren, she does not acquire her qualities through Dante's admiring gaze: she appears already holy and decisive.

Her identity remains unspecified. She has been interpreted as divine grace, holy wisdom, or an intervention associated with Beatrice or Lucia. The text does not require a single identification. Her function is clearer than her name: she brings an authority capable of breaking the dream's enchantment.

The opposition is therefore not simply between woman and reason. One female figure embodies deceptive pleasure; another initiates liberation from it.

> 31 L'altra prendea, e dinanzi l'apria  
> (He seized the other, and opened her in front,)
>
> 32 fendendo i drappi, e mostravami 'l ventre;  
> (tearing her garments, and showed me her belly;)
>
> 33 quel mi svegliò col puzzo che n'uscia.  
> (that woke me with the stench that came from it.)

Responding to the holy woman, Virgil exposes the siren's body. Conventionally understood, Virgil represents human reason, now acting under the prompting of a higher moral or spiritual authority.

The exposure reverses the earlier transformation. Dante's gaze had clothed disorder in beauty; Virgil strips away the covering. Song and pleasing appearance give way to an intolerable smell.

The belly brings the revelation down to the bodily center of appetite. Yet the point is not that the body itself is evil. Rather, desire has mistaken a corrupting attachment for complete fulfillment. Awakening begins when the hidden consequences of that attachment become perceptible.

## 5. The Angel and Virgil: Learning Where to Look

> 49 Mosse le penne poi e ventilonne,  
> (Then he moved his feathers and fanned us,)
>
> 50 ‘Qui lugent’ affermando esser beati,  
> (declaring “those who mourn” to be blessed,)
>
> 51 ch'avran di consolar l'anime donne.  
> (for they shall have their souls endowed with consolation.)

After waking, Dante follows Virgil to the passage upward. An angel directs them between walls of rock and fans them with his wings. Such encounters mark progress through Purgatory and the removal of the burdens symbolized by the seven letters cut into Dante's forehead.

The angel invokes the Beatitude from Matthew 5:4: those who mourn shall be comforted. Its promise answers the siren's counterfeit consolation. She offers immediate satisfaction that becomes captivity; repentance accepts present sorrow in order to reach genuine comfort.

Dante nevertheless remains troubled by the dream.

> 58 «Vedesti», disse, «quell'antica strega  
> (“You saw,” he said, “that ancient witch)
>
> 59 che sola sovr' a noi omai si piagne;  
> (who alone is now lamented above us;)
>
> 60 vedesti come l'uom da lei si slega.  
> (you saw how a person frees himself from her.)

Virgil identifies the siren as the common temptation underlying the sins still to be purged above them. Avarice, gluttony, and lust differ in their immediate objects, but all involve excessive attachment to limited earthly goods.

Calling her “ancient” makes her more than a private dream-image. She represents a recurring human susceptibility: taking a lesser good for the highest good.

> 62 li occhi rivolgi al logoro che gira  
> (turn your eyes toward the lure that)
>
> 63 lo rege etterno con le rote magne».  
> (the eternal King swings with the mighty wheels”.)

A *logoro* is a falconer's lure, used to recall a hawk. Virgil imagines God drawing human attention upward through the great revolving heavens.

The image offers an alternative to the siren's attraction. Dante does not have to abolish desire; he must answer a better summons. The following comparison with a falcon rising eagerly toward its food makes spiritual ascent energetic and appetitive, not merely prohibitive.

The dream began with a gaze that made an object falsely attractive. Its lesson ends with a command to redirect that gaze.

## 6. The Fifth Terrace: Souls Bound to the Earth

> 73 ‘Adhaesit pavimento anima mea’  
> (“My soul has clung to the pavement”)
>
> 74 sentia dir lor con sì alti sospiri,  
> (I heard them say with such deep sighs,)
>
> 75 che la parola a pena s'intendea.  
> (that the words could scarcely be understood.)

On the fifth terrace, Dante sees souls lying face downward. They recite a verse from Psalm 118 in the Vulgate numbering, Psalm 119 in most modern English Bibles.

Their bodily position makes the psalm visible. These souls had attached themselves to earthly possessions; now they cannot lift their faces from the earth.

The original psalm continues by asking God for renewed life according to his word. Although Dante quotes only its opening here, its scriptural context places abasement within a movement toward restoration.

> 118 Sì come l'occhio nostro non s'aderse  
> (Just as our eye did not raise itself)
>
> 119 in alto, fisso a le cose terrene,  
> (on high, fixed upon earthly things,)
>
> 120 così giustizia qui a terra il merse.  
> (so justice here plunged it toward the earth.)

The speaker explicitly explains the *contrapasso*: the punishment corresponds to the sin. The downward-looking eye recalls both Dante's troubled posture after the dream and Virgil's command to look upward.

In Purgatory, however, correspondence is also correction. The souls undergo their former spiritual condition knowingly, as a means of being freed from it.

> 121 Come avarizia spense a ciascun bene  
> (As avarice extinguished toward every good)
>
> 122 lo nostro amore, onde operar perdési,  
> (our love, whereby the power to act was lost,)
>
> 123 così giustizia qui stretti ne tene,  
> (so justice here holds us fast,)
>
> 124 ne' piedi e ne le man legati e presi;  
> (bound and confined in feet and hands;)

Avarice is not merely the possession of money. It is an attachment that suppresses love for other goods and prevents good action. The hands and feet that failed to serve worthwhile ends are now restrained.

This also connects the fifth terrace with the preceding terrace of sloth. Different distortions of love can produce related failures: spiritual inactivity, neglected duties, and an inability to respond freely to what is good.

## 7. Adrian V: The Weight of the Papal Mantle

> 99 scias quod ego fui successor Petri.  
> (know that I was Peter's successor.)

The soul identifies himself in Latin as a former pope. He is Adrian V, born Ottobuono Fieschi, a member of the powerful Ligurian family associated with the title of counts of Lavagna.

His nearby description of the river between Sestri Levante and Chiavari locates his family geographically rather than announcing its name directly. Dante thus introduces a man marked by both inherited nobility and the highest ecclesiastical office.

Adrian was elected pope in July 1276 and died the following month.

> 103 Un mese e poco più prova' io come  
> (For a month and a little more I experienced how)
>
> 104 pesa il gran manto a chi dal fango il guarda,  
> (the great mantle weighs on one who keeps it from the mud,)
>
> 105 che piuma sembran tutte l'altre some.  
> (so that all other burdens seem like feathers.)

The papal mantle signifies office and responsibility. To keep it out of the mud is to preserve its dignity from corruption.

From outside, supreme office may appear to be the culmination of ambition. From within, Adrian describes it as an almost incomparable burden. The distinction is between enjoying a position's prestige and faithfully discharging its obligations.

His brief reign becomes the setting for a radical change of vision. At the summit of worldly advancement, he discovers not fulfillment but the weight of service.

## 8. At the Summit of Ambition, the Heart Remains Unsatisfied

> 106 La mia conversïone, omè!, fu tarda;  
> (My conversion, alas, was late;)
>
> 107 ma, come fatto fui roman pastore,  
> (but, as soon as I was made Roman shepherd,)
>
> 108 così scopersi la vita bugiarda.  
> (so I discovered the deceitful life.)

Adrian's “conversion” is not a change from one religion to another. It is a reorientation of the heart away from avarice and toward God.

His elevation reveals the deception of his previous life. The office he had reached exposes the inadequacy of the ambitions that helped define him. Dante presents this inner transformation poetically; it should not be mistaken for a fully documented historical account of Adrian's motives.

> 109 Vidi che lì non s'acquetava il core,  
> (I saw that there the heart did not find rest,)
>
> 110 né più salir potiesi in quella vita;  
> (nor could one rise higher in that life;)
>
> 111 per che di questa in me s'accese amore.  
> (therefore love for this life was kindled in me.)

These lines supply the canto's clearest answer to the siren. She promised complete satisfaction; Adrian reached the highest available rank and found that his heart still lacked rest.

The contrast recalls the Augustinian understanding of the restless heart: created goods cannot provide the final fulfillment found in God. Adrian does not conclude that all goods or all offices are worthless. He discovers that none can bear the weight of ultimate desire.

There is also a pointed reversal of “ascent.” He could climb no higher in earthly hierarchy, but that very limit awakened love for another life. Purgatory now gives him a genuine upward journey.

## 9. Beyond Office: A Pope Becomes a Fellow Servant

> 133 «Drizza le gambe, lèvati sù, frate!»,  
> (“Straighten your legs, rise up, brother!”,)
>
> 134 rispuose; «non errar: conservo sono  
> (he replied; “do not err: a fellow servant am I)
>
> 135 teco e con li altri ad una podestate.  
> (with you and with the others under one authority.)

Learning that the soul was a pope, Dante kneels. Adrian tells him to stand.

“Brother” replaces the language of rank with the language of spiritual kinship. The pope and the pilgrim are fellow servants under God. Adrian does not deny that ecclesiastical office had significance on earth; he denies that its former holder should receive the same ceremonial deference here.

The response also echoes the biblical scene in Revelation in which an angel refuses a human being's worship and identifies himself as a fellow servant.

> 136 Se mai quel santo evangelico suono  
> (If ever that holy Gospel utterance)
>
> 137 che dice ‘Neque nubent’ intendesti,  
> (which says “they shall not marry” you understood,)
>
> 138 ben puoi veder perch' io così ragiono.  
> (you can clearly see why I reason thus.)

Adrian invokes Christ's teaching that those in the resurrection neither marry nor are given in marriage. The Gospel passage concerns marriage directly; Adrian applies its principle to the ending of earthly relationships and dignities.

The application is especially apt because a bishop could be understood as spiritually married to his church. Adrian's former bond of office no longer establishes his status among these souls.

His refusal of homage completes his conversion from ambition. The man who once occupied the highest rank now insists on a shared condition of service.

## 10. Alagia: Family, Example, and the Hope of Prayer

> 142 Nepote ho io di là c'ha nome Alagia,  
> (I have a niece over there whose name is Alagia,)
>
> 143 buona da sé, pur che la nostra casa  
> (good in herself, provided that our house)
>
> 144 non faccia lei per essempro malvagia;  
> (does not make her wicked by example;)
>
> 145 e questa sola di là m'è rimasa».  
> (and she alone remains to me over there”.)

Adrian ends not with the papacy but with his niece Alagia Fieschi, wife of Moroello Malaspina. The reference also connects the canto with a family whose hospitality mattered during Dante's exile.

Alagia's goodness is personal, not a guaranteed inheritance from a noble house. Indeed, Adrian fears that family example could corrupt her. The canto therefore distinguishes inherited status from moral worth one last time.

His statement that she alone remains to him is best understood in the context of spiritual help, not simply as a claim that every other relative has died. She is the person on earth in whose goodness—and implicitly whose prayers—he can place hope.

Earlier, Dante offered to obtain assistance for him among the living. Adrian's final words quietly indicate where such assistance might be found. Public dignity has given way to dependence on one good person's love.

## Epilogue — From Enchanted Desire to Clear Sight

Canto XIX traces the correction of desire through a sequence of changed perceptions. Dante makes the siren beautiful, sees her exposed, and learns to turn his eyes toward a higher attraction. On the next terrace, souls who once looked only at earthly things must confront that attachment until they are free of it.

Adrian V gives the dream's lesson a human history. His achievement of supreme office reveals the falseness of the promise that worldly success can satisfy the heart.

The canto does not demand the destruction of desire. It demands its liberation: from appearances that deceive, habits that bind, and ambitions that mistake earthly elevation for the soul's true ascent.
