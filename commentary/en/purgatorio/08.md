# Dusk, Grace, and Earthly Affection in Dante's *Purgatorio*, Canto VIII

Canto VIII takes place at evening in the valley of the rulers, a sheltered part of Ante-Purgatory where souls who postponed repentance wait before beginning their ascent. Dante, still alive, is accompanied by Virgil and the Mantuan poet Sordello.

The canto moves between a communal drama of temptation and protection and intimate conversations about friendship, marriage, family, and exile. Its central question is how earthly attachments can survive without obstructing love of God.

## 1. Evening and the Ache of Separation

> 1 Era già l'ora che volge il disio  
> (It was already the hour that turns longing)
>
> 2 ai navicanti e 'ntenerisce il core  
> (for sailors and softens the heart)
>
> 3 lo dì c'han detto ai dolci amici addio;  
> (on the day they have said farewell to dear friends;)

The canto begins not with punishment but with homesickness. Evening turns sailors' thoughts toward the people they have left behind. In the following lines, the distant sound of a bell similarly wounds a newly departed pilgrim with love.

These images make the souls' situation intelligible through ordinary human experience. They, too, are travellers separated from familiar company. Yet their ultimate home lies ahead, with God, rather than behind them on earth.

The passage also anticipates Dante's exile. Although the fictional journey takes place in 1300, before his banishment from Florence, the poet writes with knowledge of the separation and dependence that exile would bring. The canto's closing prophecy will make that connection explicit.

## 2. The Evening Hymn: Turning Longing into Prayer

> 13 ‘Te lucis ante’ sì devotamente  
> (‘Before the ending of the light’ so devoutly)
>
> 14 le uscìo di bocca e con sì dolci note,  
> (came from its mouth and with such sweet notes,)
>
> 15 che fece me a me uscir di mente;  
> (that it made me forget myself;)

One soul begins *Te lucis ante terminum*, the Latin hymn traditionally sung at Compline, the final prayer office of the day. The other souls join in, looking toward heaven.

The hymn asks the Creator to protect sleepers through the night, ward off troubling dreams and nocturnal defilement, and restrain the enemy. Its words therefore prepare for the serpent's appearance: the danger against which the souls pray will shortly become visible.

The scene redirects the longing of the opening. Instead of remaining enclosed within memories of absent companions, desire becomes communal prayer. Dante's self-forgetfulness suggests the power of sacred song to draw attention away from the isolated self. Here, musical sweetness assists devotion rather than distracting from the journey toward God.

## 3. The Thin Veil of Allegory

> 19 Aguzza qui, lettor, ben li occhi al vero,  
> (Sharpen here, reader, your eyes well upon the truth,)
>
> 20 ché 'l velo è ora ben tanto sottile,  
> (for the veil is now indeed so thin,)
>
> 21 certo che 'l trapassar dentro è leggero.  
> (that surely passing through it is easy.)

Dante interrupts the narrative to instruct his reader. What follows must be read on two levels: as an event within his journey and as an image of spiritual realities.

The “veil” is the literal story through which a deeper meaning becomes perceptible. Angels, a serpent, a sheltered garden, and prayer form an unusually recognizable symbolic arrangement: divine protection confronts temptation.

Yet the simplicity of the symbols should not make us flatten the episode into a mechanical code. These souls are already destined for salvation. The scene need not imply that they might finally be lost; rather, it dramatizes their dependence on grace and the defeat of the enemy. Their ultimate security comes from God, not from self-sufficiency.

## 4. The Angels: Protection Rather Than Vengeance

> 26 due angeli con due spade affocate,  
> (two angels with two flaming swords,)
>
> 27 tronche e private de le punte sue.  
> (truncated and deprived of their points.)

The flaming swords recall the angelic guardians stationed at Eden after humanity's expulsion in Genesis. Dante will eventually reach the Earthly Paradise at the summit of Mount Purgatory; this smaller garden anticipates it.

But these swords have no points. The unusual detail is often understood as distinguishing protective justice from destructive punishment: the angels repel the enemy rather than execute judgment upon the souls. Their appearance emphasizes mercy as well as power.

Their green clothing and wings also suggest hope, the defining confidence of souls whose purification will certainly end in blessedness.

> 37 «Ambo vegnon del grembo di Maria»,  
> (“Both come from the bosom of Mary”,)
>
> 38 disse Sordello, «a guardia de la valle,  
> (said Sordello, “to guard the valley,)
>
> 39 per lo serpente che verrà vie via».  
> (because of the serpent that will come shortly”.)

Sordello identifies the angels' mission with Mary's care. This continues a pattern established earlier in the poem: heavenly assistance reaches Dante through intercession, not simply through his own initiative.

Mary also stands opposite Eve in Christian tradition. Where Eve is associated with humanity's yielding to the serpent, Mary's obedience is associated with redemption. Protection coming from Mary therefore deepens the episode's relationship to the story of the Fall.

Dante responds by drawing close to Virgil. His instinctive fear reminds us that, unlike the souls around him, he remains a living pilgrim whose spiritual journey is unfinished.

## 5. Nino Visconti: Friendship and Intercession

> 53 giudice Nin gentil, quanto mi piacque  
> (noble Judge Nino, how much it pleased me)
>
> 54 quando ti vidi non esser tra ' rei!  
> (when I saw you were not among the damned!)

The recognition of Nino Visconti brings personal warmth into the ceremonial setting. Nino, who died in 1296, had been ruler of Gallura in Sardinia and a prominent figure in Pisan politics. His title “judge” denotes the ruler of a Sardinian *giudicato*, not merely a judicial officer.

Dante's delight is specifically delight in Nino's salvation. Earthly friendship survives death, but its deepest concern has changed: what matters most is that the friend is not damned.

When Dante explains that he is still alive, Nino and Sordello recoil in astonishment. The living traveller is an extraordinary sign of divine grace—and someone who can carry requests back to earth.

> 71 dì a Giovanna mia che per me chiami  
> (tell my Giovanna to cry out for me)
>
> 72 là dove a li 'nnocenti si risponde.  
> (there where the innocent are answered.)

Nino asks Dante to seek the prayers of his daughter Giovanna. In the theology of the *Purgatorio*, the living can assist the dead through intercessory prayer.

This is a crucial transformation of family affection. Love does not merely preserve memories or mourn absence; it can act for another person's spiritual good. The opening sorrow of separation thus finds a possible answer in communion across the boundary of death.

## 6. Remarriage, Heraldry, and the Persistence of Hurt

> 73 Non credo che la sua madre più m'ami,  
> (I do not believe that her mother loves me any longer,)
>
> 74 poscia che trasmutò le bianche bende,  
> (since she changed her white headbands,)
>
> 75 le quai convien che, misera!, ancor brami.  
> (which she must, poor woman, long for again.)

Nino contrasts his daughter's hoped-for prayers with the conduct of her mother, Beatrice d'Este. The white headbands signify widowhood; changing them means remarrying. Her new husband was Galeazzo Visconti of Milan.

The complaint shows how strongly earthly feeling persists in Ante-Purgatory. Nino is saved, but he still experiences abandonment, wounded affection, and concern for family honour. His subsequent generalization about women's inconstancy belongs to this aggrieved speech; it should not simply be adopted as an authoritative moral conclusion.

His prediction that Beatrice will again desire her widow's clothing looks toward the misfortunes associated with her new marriage and the reversals of the Milanese Visconti.

> 79 Non le farà sì bella sepultura  
> (It will not make so fine a tomb for her,)
>
> 80 la vipera che Melanesi accampa,  
> (the viper that leads the Milanese into the field,)
>
> 81 com' avria fatto il gallo di Gallura».  
> (as the cock of Gallura would have done”.)

The viper is the emblem of the Milanese Visconti; the cock represents Nino's Gallura. Through heraldry, private disappointment becomes a comparison between dynasties.

Nino argues that fidelity to his memory would have brought Beatrice greater honour than her new alliance. Dante describes his emotion as a measured, righteous zeal, but the speech also exposes the entanglement of love with status and remembrance. Such attachments are not simply erased in Purgatory: they must be purified.

## 7. Three Stars: The Virtues That Orient Hope

> 89 E io a lui: «A quelle tre facelle  
> (And I to him: “At those three little flames)
>
> 90 di che 'l polo di qua tutto quanto arde».  
> (with which the pole on this side wholly burns”.)

Dante turns from Nino's troubled marriage to the southern sky. The three bright stars are conventionally understood as the theological virtues: faith, hope, and charity.

These virtues orient human beings toward God. Their appearance above the waiting souls complements the hymn and angelic protection: salvation depends on more than political distinction, family loyalty, or unaided moral effort.

> 91 Ond' elli a me: «Le quattro chiare stelle  
> (Then he to me: “The four bright stars)
>
> 92 che vedevi staman, son di là basse,  
> (that you saw this morning are low on the other side,)
>
> 93 e queste son salite ov' eran quelle».  
> (and these have risen where those were”.)

The four stars seen earlier in the poem represent the cardinal virtues: prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance.

Their changing positions do not imply that the theological virtues abolish the cardinal virtues. Rather, the movement of the heavens creates a symbolic succession. At nightfall, when the souls pray and await protection, faith, hope, and charity become especially prominent.

The celestial order also contrasts with the instability of earthly politics and marriages. Human allegiances shift painfully; the heavens disclose a reliable moral pattern.

## 8. The Serpent: Eden Recalled and the Enemy Repelled

> 98 la picciola vallea, era una biscia,  
> (the little valley, there was a snake,)
>
> 99 forse qual diede ad Eva il cibo amaro.  
> (perhaps like the one that gave Eve the bitter food.)

The serpent enters from the valley's unprotected side. Dante's explicit reference to Eve identifies the creature with the primal temptation.

The setting intensifies the parallel: the snake moves among grass and flowers, suggesting that evil can approach through an attractive environment rather than an obviously terrifying one. It repeatedly turns its head and licks its back, appearing almost harmlessly absorbed in grooming.

The “bitter food” is the fruit whose apparent desirability concealed disastrous consequences. Temptation promises satisfaction while producing alienation and suffering.

> 106 Sentendo fender l'aere a le verdi ali,  
> (Hearing the air cleft by the green wings,)
>
> 107 fuggì 'l serpente, e li angeli dier volta,  
> (the serpent fled, and the angels turned,)
>
> 108 suso a le poste rivolando iguali.  
> (flying back upward together to their stations.)

There is no prolonged battle. The sound of the approaching angels is sufficient to drive the serpent away.

The brevity matters. This is not a struggle between equal powers: evil operates under limits, and divine protection decisively defeats its approach. The angels then resume their stations, showing that care continues after the immediate danger passes.

The episode answers the evening hymn. What the souls requested in prayer has been enacted before Dante's eyes.

## 9. Corrado Malaspina: Family Love Refined into Virtue

> 118 Fui chiamato Currado Malaspina;  
> (I was called Corrado Malaspina;)
>
> 119 non son l'antico, ma di lui discesi;  
> (I am not the elder, but I descended from him;)
>
> 120 a' miei portai l'amor che qui raffina».  
> (toward my own I bore the love that is refined here”.)

Corrado Malaspina, who died in 1294, belonged to a noble family established in the Val di Magra and surrounding Lunigiana. He distinguishes himself from his grandfather, also named Corrado.

His final line offers one of the canto's clearest statements about purification. He loved his own people—his family or house—with an attachment now being refined. The problem is not necessarily that family love is false, but that it can become excessive or exclusive.

Purgatory redirects such love rather than annihilating it. Loyalty must become compatible with a wider and properly ordered charity.

> 128 che vostra gente onrata non si sfregia  
> (that your honoured family does not strip itself)
>
> 129 del pregio de la borsa e de la spada.  
> (of the distinction of purse and sword.)

Dante praises the Malaspina for generosity and martial worth: the “purse” signifies liberality, while the “sword” signifies courage and noble action.

This praise is especially relevant in the valley of rulers. Political and aristocratic life can distract people from their spiritual obligations, yet nobility is not inherently condemned. Wealth and power can serve others when governed by virtue.

The family's reputation also challenges a corrupt public world. Against misdirected leadership, Dante imagines a household preserving standards of honour and hospitality.

## 10. The Prophecy: Reputation Becomes Lived Experience

> 133 Ed elli: «Or va; che 'l sol non si ricorca  
> (And he: “Now go; for the sun will not lie down again)
>
> 134 sette volte nel letto che 'l Montone  
> (seven times in the bed that the Ram)
>
> 135 con tutti e quattro i piè cuopre e inforca,  
> (with all four feet covers and straddles,)

The Ram is Aries. Corrado measures the future by the sun's annual return to that constellation: fewer than seven years will pass before Dante learns the truth of his praise personally.

The prophecy points toward Dante's relations with the Malaspina during exile, particularly his documented service to the family in 1306. What lies in the future for Dante the character is already known to Dante the poet.

> 136 che cotesta cortese oppinïone  
> (before that courteous opinion)
>
> 137 ti fia chiavata in mezzo de la testa  
> (will be nailed into the middle of your head)
>
> 138 con maggior chiovi che d'altrui sermone,  
> (with greater nails than another person's speech,)
>
> 139 se corso di giudicio non s'arresta».  
> (unless the course of judgment is halted”.)

The image of the nails is forceful: experience will fix Dante's conviction more securely than hearsay could.

There is consolation here, but also an implicit wound. Dante will discover the Malaspina's generosity because exile will make him dependent upon the hospitality of others. Their virtue will become real to him through his own vulnerability.

The final condition places that future within divine judgment. Human history is not outside providence, even when its course brings displacement and suffering.

## Epilogue — Love Preserved, Purified, and Protected

Canto VIII begins with the pain of leaving loved ones and ends with the promise of hospitality in exile. Between those moments, prayer turns longing toward God, angels repel the serpent, and the souls reveal attachments that death has not erased.

Its governing idea is that salvation does not destroy human affection. Friendship, marriage, family loyalty, and generosity retain their value, but they must be freed from possessiveness and wrongly ordered desire. Under divine protection, the souls learn to love without making any earthly person or household their final home.
