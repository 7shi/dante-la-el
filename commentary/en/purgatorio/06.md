# From the Dice Game to a Divided Italy: Allegory and Background in *Purgatorio* VI

Canto VI begins with souls asking for prayers and ends with Dante denouncing the political disorder of Italy. Between these apparently different subjects stands a single question: what binds people together? The dead depend on the living through prayer; citizens should recognize obligations to one another; religious and political authorities should cooperate without usurping each other's responsibilities.

The setting is still Ante-Purgatory, where souls await admission to the mountain's penitential terraces. Dante and Virgil are among people who died violently but repented before death. Their requests for help lead first to a theological discussion, then to an encounter whose warmth exposes the failures of earthly society.

## 1. The Dice Game: A Crowd Seeking Favors

> 1 Quando si parte il gioco de la zara,  
> (When the game of zara breaks up,)
>
> 2 colui che perde si riman dolente,  
> (the one who loses remains sorrowful,)
>
> 3 repetendo le volte, e tristo impara;  
> (repeating the throws, and sadly learns;)

Dante opens with an everyday scene rather than a sacred image. Zara was a gambling game played with dice. The loser stays behind, mentally rehearsing the throws and learning through disappointment, while the crowd follows the winner in hope of sharing his good fortune.

The comparison establishes a recognizable social world of attention, petition, and advantage. Its main purpose is not to assign every detail a separate spiritual meaning: Dante chiefly needs the image of someone moving through an insistent crowd.

> 10 Tal era io in quella turba spessa,  
> (Such was I in that dense crowd,)
>
> 11 volgendo a loro, e qua e là, la faccia,  
> (turning toward them, here and there, my face,)
>
> 12 e promettendo mi sciogliea da essa.  
> (and by promising I freed myself from it.)

Like the winner, Dante escapes by acknowledging requests. But the souls seek something more valuable than money: they want him to obtain prayers for them when he returns to the living.

The simile transforms a worldly exchange into an image of spiritual dependence. Dante possesses a rare opportunity—the ability to carry messages between the dead and the living—and must use it responsibly. Their salvation is assured; what they hope to shorten is their waiting.

## 2. Violent Death and the Limits of Earthly Judgment

> 22 Pier da la Broccia dico; e qui proveggia,  
> (I mean Pierre de la Brosse; and let her take heed here,)
>
> 23 mentr' è di qua, la donna di Brabante,  
> (while she is on this side, the lady of Brabant,)
>
> 24 sì che però non sia di peggior greggia.  
> (so that on this account she may not belong to a worse flock.)

The crowd includes victims of murder, political hostility, and private revenge. Their presence prepares for the canto's later indictment of Italy: factional violence has already appeared through its individual victims.

Pierre de la Brosse was a courtier of Philip III of France, executed in 1278 after losing royal favor. His fall became associated with his conflict with Philip's queen, Marie of Brabant. Immediately before naming him, Dante reports Pierre's claim that envy and hatred, not guilt, caused his death.

The warning to Marie distinguishes human verdicts from divine judgment. A condemned man may be among the saved, while someone secure in worldly power may still risk damnation. The “worse flock” means the damned. Yet the warning leaves repentance possible: Marie must reconsider her conduct while she remains alive.

Dante's placement of Pierre should not be mistaken for a modern historical finding. It is a moral and poetic judgment, shaped by the accounts available to him.

## 3. Prayer Does Not Overturn Divine Justice

> 37 ché cima di giudicio non s'avvalla  
> (for the summit of judgment is not lowered)
>
> 38 perché foco d'amor compia in un punto  
> (because a fire of love accomplishes in an instant)
>
> 39 ciò che de' sodisfar chi qui s'astalla;  
> (what whoever waits here must satisfy;)

The souls' requests raise an apparent contradiction. Dante remembers a passage in Virgil's *Aeneid* suggesting that prayer cannot change divine decrees. If that is true, why ask the living to pray?

Virgil answers that intercession does not compromise justice. What must be satisfied can be fulfilled through the love expressed in another person's prayer. Justice is not overruled; its demand is met through charity.

The “fire of love” contrasts with an exclusively quantitative understanding of punishment. Spiritual restoration is not simply a fixed number of days that no relationship can affect. In the Christian community Dante imagines, one person's love can genuinely benefit another.

> 40 e là dov' io fermai cotesto punto,  
> (and where I established that point,)
>
> 41 non s'ammendava, per pregar, difetto,  
> (no deficiency was made good by praying,)
>
> 42 perché 'l priego da Dio era disgiunto.  
> (because the prayer was separated from God.)

The background is *Aeneid* VI, where the unburied Palinurus asks to cross the infernal river before the prescribed conditions have been fulfilled. The Sibyl rejects the hope that prayer can bend the decrees of the gods.

Dante's Virgil distinguishes that pagan situation from Christian intercession. Prayer directed outside the saving relationship with God does not possess the efficacy attributed to the charitable prayers sought here. This is Dante's Christian reinterpretation of his classical source, not simply a restatement of Roman religion.

## 4. Beatrice: The Explanation Beyond Virgil

> 46 Non so se 'ntendi: io dico di Beatrice;  
> (I do not know whether you understand: I mean Beatrice;)
>
> 47 tu la vedrai di sopra, in su la vetta  
> (you will see her above, upon the summit)
>
> 48 di questo monte, ridere e felice».  
> (of this mountain, smiling and happy.”)

Virgil offers an explanation, but directs Dante to Beatrice for fuller understanding. Immediately before these lines, he describes her as the light that will stand between truth and Dante's intellect.

The allegorical distinction is important without being absolute. Virgil represents the achievements of human reason, classical learning, and moral wisdom; Beatrice is associated with revelation and a wisdom illuminated by faith. Virgil can bring Dante toward theological truth, but cannot supply its ultimate illumination.

Beatrice is also the woman Dante longs to see. Intellectual instruction and personal love are therefore inseparable. The prospect of meeting her renews his desire to climb, though Virgil cautions that the journey will take longer than Dante imagines. Desire can energize spiritual progress, but cannot abolish its necessary stages.

## 5. Sordello: Dignity Transformed into Fellowship

> 61 Venimmo a lei: o anima lombarda,  
> (We came to it: O Lombard soul,)
>
> 62 come ti stavi altera e disdegnosa  
> (how you stood proud and disdainful)
>
> 63 e nel mover de li occhi onesta e tarda!  
> (and in the movement of your eyes dignified and slow!)

The solitary soul is Sordello, a thirteenth-century poet from the Mantuan region who wrote in Occitan. He belonged to the international culture of troubadours and aristocratic courts. His surviving poetry includes political and moral criticism, making him an apt presence at the beginning of Dante's own political denunciation.

His reserve suggests self-possession rather than the restless competition seen elsewhere in the canto. Dante's subsequent comparison of him to a resting lion reinforces that controlled dignity.

> 74 dicendo: «O Mantoano, io son Sordello  
> (saying: “O Mantuan, I am Sordello)
>
> 75 de la tua terra!»; e l'un l'altro abbracciava.  
> (of your land!”; and each embraced the other.)

Sordello responds as soon as Virgil names Mantua. He does not yet know that he is meeting the great Roman poet: a shared homeland is sufficient to provoke affection.

That fact drives the canto's transition. Civic belonging here produces welcome, not hostility. Dante will contrast this immediate embrace between strangers with the violence of living people who inhabit the same city.

The encounter does not condemn local affection. Instead, it shows what such affection should become: a foundation for fellowship rather than an excuse for exclusion or faction.

## 6. Italy: A Ship Without a Helmsman

> 76 Ahi serva Italia, di dolore ostello,  
> (Ah, enslaved Italy, lodging-place of sorrow,)
>
> 77 nave sanza nocchiere in gran tempesta,  
> (ship without a helmsman in a great storm,)
>
> 78 non donna di province, ma bordello!  
> (not mistress of provinces, but a brothel!)

The embrace interrupts the narrative with one of the poem's most famous apostrophes. Dante turns from the travelers to address Italy itself.

The images describe both political failure and moral degradation. Italy is enslaved rather than free, a shelter for suffering rather than flourishing, and a vessel endangered by the absence of competent direction. The final contrast opposes its Roman dignity as a ruler of provinces to the shame of a household whose proper order has collapsed.

Italy in 1300 was not a unified nation-state. It contained communes, lordships, kingdoms, and papal territories, with overlapping allegiances and recurring conflict. Dante's proposed remedy is not modern national independence: he looks toward a restored universal imperial authority capable of restraining partisan power.

> 82 e ora in te non stanno sanza guerra  
> (and now within you they do not remain without war,)
>
> 83 li vivi tuoi, e l'un l'altro si rode  
> (your living people, and one gnaws at another)
>
> 84 di quei ch'un muro e una fossa serra.  
> (among those whom one wall and one moat enclose.)

The strongest accusation concerns violence within cities. Common defenses should mark a shared civic life, yet those inside them consume one another.

Sordello and Virgil recognize a bond across centuries. Living neighbors fail to recognize the bond established by their own walls. The encounter in the afterlife thus becomes a standard by which earthly politics is judged.

## 7. Law, Empire, and the Church's Proper Limits

> 88 Che val perché ti racconciasse il freno  
> (What good is it that he repaired your bridle,)
>
> 89 Iustinïano, se la sella è vòta?  
> (Justinian, if the saddle is empty?)
>
> 90 Sanz' esso fora la vergogna meno.  
> (Without it the shame would be less.)

The ship becomes a horse. Italy possesses a bridle—the body of law—but lacks the rider needed to direct it.

Justinian, the sixth-century Eastern Roman emperor, commissioned the great codification of Roman law later known as the *Corpus Juris Civilis*. For Dante, this legal inheritance provides an instrument of public justice. But a sophisticated legal order cannot govern itself.

The unused bridle makes the failure more disgraceful: Italy possesses the means of restraint without the authority willing to apply them.

> 91 Ahi gente che dovresti esser devota,  
> (Ah, people who ought to be devout,)
>
> 92 e lasciar seder Cesare in la sella,  
> (and let Caesar sit in the saddle,)
>
> 93 se bene intendi ciò che Dio ti nota,  
> (if you rightly understand what God indicates to you,)

The rebuke is directed at churchmen who interfere with temporal government. “Caesar” represents legitimate imperial authority, while the appeal to God recalls the Gospel distinction between what belongs to Caesar and what belongs to God.

Dante is not arguing for a secular society in the modern sense. He understands both spiritual and temporal authority within a providential order. His objection is to their confusion: the Church should guide spiritual life, not prevent the proper exercise of civil government.

## 8. The Absent Ruler and the Widowed City

> 97 O Alberto tedesco ch'abbandoni  
> (O German Albert, who abandon)
>
> 98 costei ch'è fatta indomita e selvaggia,  
> (her who has become untamed and savage,)
>
> 99 e dovresti inforcar li suoi arcioni,  
> (and ought to straddle her saddlebows,)

Albert I of Habsburg, king of the Romans from 1298 to 1308, becomes the principal target of Dante's anger at imperial neglect. Although Albert was never crowned emperor, Dante addresses him as the ruler responsible for exercising imperial authority in Italy.

The horse metaphor continues: Italy has become unmanageable because its rightful rider has failed to govern it. Dante also blames Albert's father, Rudolf, for concentrating on interests north of the Alps.

The accusation reflects Dante's political hopes rather than a neutral account of the rulers' constraints. In his interpretation, their absence permits local factions and powerful families to pursue private advantage without effective restraint.

> 112 Vieni a veder la tua Roma che piagne  
> (Come to see your Rome, which weeps)
>
> 113 vedova e sola, e dì e notte chiama:  
> (widowed and alone, and day and night calls:)
>
> 114 «Cesare mio, perché non m'accompagne?».  
> (“My Caesar, why do you not keep me company?”.)

Rome appears as a deserted wife. This personification makes imperial absence an emotional and moral betrayal, not merely an administrative deficiency.

The repeated summons to come and see, which structures the surrounding passage, demands direct confrontation with suffering. Dante wants the ruler to witness what neglect has produced. Rome's loneliness also forms another contrast with Sordello's embrace: where companionship should exist, abandonment prevails.

## 9. Providence Under Question

> 118 E se licito m'è, o sommo Giove  
> (And if I am permitted, O highest Jove)
>
> 119 che fosti in terra per noi crucifisso,  
> (who were crucified on earth for us,)
>
> 120 son li giusti occhi tuoi rivolti altrove?  
> (are your just eyes turned elsewhere?)

Dante's complaint rises from human rulers to God. “Highest Jove” is a classical designation redirected toward the Christian God, unmistakably identified through the Crucifixion. The language joins the Roman and Christian dimensions of Dante's vision.

The question is deliberately dangerous: has divine justice ceased to watch Italy? The introductory request for permission acknowledges the boldness of asking it.

This is not a settled denial of providence. It is the language of anguish, in which belief in divine justice makes visible injustice especially difficult to endure.

> 121 O è preparazion che ne l'abisso  
> (Or is it a preparation that in the abyss)
>
> 122 del tuo consiglio fai per alcun bene  
> (of your counsel you make for some good)
>
> 123 in tutto de l'accorger nostro scisso?  
> (wholly cut off from our discernment?)

Dante proposes another possibility: present disorder may prepare a good that human beings cannot yet perceive.

The canto does not identify that good or explain away the suffering. Instead, it distinguishes forceful moral judgment from complete knowledge of history. Dante can condemn the failures he sees while admitting that God's ultimate purposes remain beyond his sight.

This limitation recalls Virgil's earlier deference to Beatrice. Neither theological difficulty nor political catastrophe can be fully mastered by unaided human understanding.

## 10. Florence: Irony and the Fever of Constant Change

> 127 Fiorenza mia, ben puoi esser contenta  
> (My Florence, you may indeed be content)
>
> 128 di questa digression che non ti tocca,  
> (with this digression, which does not touch you,)
>
> 129 mercé del popol tuo che si argomenta.  
> (thanks to your people, who are so resourceful.)

The address to Florence is bitterly ironic. Dante pretends that his own city is exempt from the disorder he has described, then demonstrates that it exemplifies it.

The possessive “my” matters. Dante speaks as a Florentine whose attachment survives his condemnation. Exiled in 1202, he writes with knowledge of the factional struggles that the pilgrim, traveling in 1300, has not yet personally suffered in their fullest form.

In the following lines, supposed virtues become accusations: justice is constantly on people's lips, and eagerness to hold office masks political ambition.

> 142 verso di te, che fai tanto sottili  
> (compared with you, who make such subtle)
>
> 143 provedimenti, ch'a mezzo novembre  
> (provisions that by the middle of November)
>
> 144 non giugne quel che tu d'ottobre fili.  
> (what you spin in October does not last.)

Dante mockingly ranks Florence above ancient Athens and Sparta as a maker of laws. The praise collapses into an image of legislation spun like thread and discarded almost immediately.

The issue is not simply that laws change. It is that constant alteration reveals the absence of a stable conception of justice. Florence repeatedly changes laws, currency, offices, and civic arrangements without curing the antagonisms beneath them.

> 149 vedrai te somigliante a quella inferma  
> (you will see yourself resembling that sick woman)
>
> 150 che non può trovar posa in su le piume,  
> (who cannot find rest upon the feathers,)
>
> 151 ma con dar volta suo dolore scherma.  
> (but by turning over seeks relief from her pain.)

The final comparison strips political restlessness of its claim to progress. Florence resembles a sick woman turning in a feather bed: movement offers momentary relief but does not heal the illness.

This ending also reverses Sordello's composed stillness. His dignity rests on self-command; Florence's agitation reveals an inability to govern itself.

## Epilogue — The Bonds That Save, the Divisions That Wound

Canto VI moves from petitions for prayer to a diagnosis of political disease. Its unity lies in the contrast between relationships that restore and rivalries that destroy. Prayer allows one person's love to help another; Sordello's embrace turns shared origin into fellowship. Italy and Florence, by contrast, convert common life into faction, ambition, and violence.

Dante's political remedies belong to his medieval vision of Church and Empire. The broader moral challenge remains clear: laws and institutions cannot secure peace when those entrusted with them pursue private power instead of the common good.
