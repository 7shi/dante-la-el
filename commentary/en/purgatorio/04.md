# Learning to Climb: Allegory and Background in *Purgatorio*, Canto IV

Canto IV brings Dante and Virgil from their conversation with Manfred to the steep ascent of Mount Purgatory and their encounter with Belacqua, a soul whose habitual laziness survives in his gestures and speech. They remain in **Ante-Purgatory**, outside the gate of Purgatory proper.

The canto joins philosophical reflection, physical exertion, astronomy, and affectionate comedy. Its central question is how a human being learns to move toward the good: attention must be gathered, desire strengthened, understanding corrected, and freedom exercised within the conditions established by divine justice.

## 1. The Soul's Unity and the Power of Attention

> 3 l'anima bene ad essa si raccoglie,  
> (the soul gathers itself wholly to it,)
>
> 4 par ch'a nulla potenza più intenda;  
> (it seems to attend to no other faculty;)
>
> 5 e questo è contra quello error che crede  
> (and this goes against that error which believes)
>
> 6 ch'un'anima sovr' altra in noi s'accenda.  
> (that one soul is kindled above another within us.)

Dante begins with an experience familiar to anyone absorbed in pleasure, pain, or an engrossing conversation: concentrated attention can make everything else disappear from awareness. The soul gathers itself around what one of its faculties perceives.

He treats this experience as evidence for the soul's unity. Medieval philosophical psychology distinguished powers such as sensation, imagination, and intellect. Dante's point is that these are powers of one soul, not independent souls operating inside the same person. His argument belongs broadly to the Aristotelian and scholastic account of human nature, opposed to theories that divide human life among distinct souls.

The immediate occasion is his absorption in Manfred's story in the preceding canto. Listening and wondering, Dante has failed to notice the passing time. The sun has climbed fifty degrees above the horizon—roughly three hours and twenty minutes after sunrise.

This opening also establishes an important spiritual ambiguity. Concentration makes profound listening possible, but it can conceal other obligations. Dante has listened well; now he must recognize that the journey requires movement as well as attention.

## 2. The Narrow Passage: Conversion Cannot Be Comfortable

> 22 che non era la calla onde salìne  
> (than was the passage by which ascended)
>
> 23 lo duca mio, e io appresso, soli,  
> (my guide, and I behind him, alone,)
>
> 24 come da noi la schiera si partìne.  
> (when the company departed from us.)

The comparison begun in the preceding lines makes this entrance extraordinarily narrow: a farmer may block a larger opening in a hedge with a small forkful of thorns when the grapes begin to ripen. Dante and Virgil must squeeze through something smaller.

The rustic image makes the sacred landscape tangible. Purgatory is not an abstract diagram of moral improvement; it has constrictions, sharp surfaces, and awkward passages.

The narrow entrance also recalls the Gospel image of the narrow gate and difficult way leading to life. Dante does not explicitly announce that connection, but the landscape invites it. Spiritual renewal requires leaving the broad, accommodating routes of ordinary habit.

The departure of Manfred's company reinforces the transition. Their conversation has offered knowledge and reassurance, but they cannot perform Dante's ascent for him. He must follow his own guide into the difficult passage.

## 3. The Wings of Desire and the Work of Hands

> 27 con esso i piè; ma qui convien ch'om voli;  
> (with the feet alone; but here one must fly;)
>
> 28 dico con l'ale snelle e con le piume  
> (I mean with the swift wings and with the feathers)
>
> 29 del gran disio, di retro a quel condotto  
> (of great desire, behind that guide)
>
> 30 che speranza mi dava e facea lume.  
> (who gave me hope and provided light.)

Dante has compared the mountain with difficult Italian terrain: San Leo, Noli, Bismantova, and Cacume. Those places can be negotiated on foot; this ascent supposedly requires flight.

He immediately explains that the wings are metaphorical. They belong to desire: the longing strong enough to carry a person beyond what ordinary inclination would attempt. In Purgatory, desire is not simply suppressed. It must be directed toward its proper object.

Virgil supplies both hope and illumination. As Dante's guide, he embodies the capacities of human reason, moral instruction, and poetic wisdom. These can reveal a route and encourage perseverance, though they do not replace the grace on which the larger journey depends.

> 31 Noi salavam per entro 'l sasso rotto,  
> (We were climbing within the split rock,)
>
> 32 e d'ogne lato ne stringea lo stremo,  
> (and on every side its edges pressed us close,)
>
> 33 e piedi e man volea il suol di sotto.  
> (and the ground beneath demanded feet and hands.)

The promised flight becomes strenuous scrambling. This is not a contradiction but the point of the metaphor: spiritual desire does not abolish bodily effort; it makes effort possible.

Dante's living body distinguishes him from the souls around him. His progress requires muscles, balance, and endurance. Allegorically, aspiration becomes meaningful through action. Wanting the summit must take the form of finding the next foothold.

## 4. Guidance Makes the Next Step Possible

> 46 «Figliuol mio», disse, «infin quivi ti tira»,  
> (“My son,” he said, “pull yourself as far as here,”)
>
> 47 additandomi un balzo poco in sùe  
> (pointing out to me a ledge a little higher)
>
> 48 che da quel lato il poggio tutto gira.  
> (that circles the whole mountain on that side.)

Exhausted, Dante has appealed to Virgil as his sweet father. Virgil answers as a father, calling him his son and directing him toward a nearby ledge.

The instruction is practical rather than grandiose. Dante cannot see the summit, and its apparent height threatens to overwhelm him. Virgil does not demand that he comprehend or conquer the entire mountain at once. He gives him a reachable intermediate goal.

This is an important model of moral education. The final good may exceed a beginner's imaginative grasp, but the next action can still be identified. Hope becomes effective when it is attached to something one can actually do.

The ledge also permits legitimate rest. Dante will shortly encounter negligence, but the canto does not equate every pause with laziness. Rest after effort can restore attention and allow understanding to catch up with experience.

## 5. A Different Sky Requires a Different Perspective

> 55 Li occhi prima drizzai ai bassi liti;  
> (First I directed my eyes toward the low shores;)
>
> 56 poscia li alzai al sole, e ammirava  
> (then I raised them to the sun, and wondered)
>
> 57 che da sinistra n'eravam feriti.  
> (that we were being struck by it from the left.)

Seated facing east, Dante notices sunlight arriving from his left, from the northern side of the sky. For someone accustomed to the inhabited northern world of medieval geography, this is disorienting.

The explanation is geographical. Mount Purgatory stands in the Southern Hemisphere, where the sun's course appears on the opposite side from that familiar to Dante in Italy.

The episode gives physical substance to his displacement. He has not merely entered a place with different moral rules; he has entered a world in which familiar habits of orientation no longer suffice.

> 68 dentro raccolto, imagina Sïòn  
> (gathered inwardly, imagine Zion)
>
> 69 con questo monte in su la terra stare  
> (with this mountain standing upon the earth)
>
> 70 sì, ch'amendue hanno un solo orizzòn  
> (so that both have a single horizon)
>
> 71 e diversi emisperi; onde la strada  
> (and different hemispheres; hence the road)

Zion means Jerusalem. In Dante's sacred geography, Jerusalem and Mount Purgatory stand at opposite points of the globe. They therefore share a horizon circle while occupying opposite hemispheres.

The road introduced in the final quoted line is the sun's apparent celestial path. Virgil's explanation, including references to the zodiac and the equator, teaches Dante to reason from the earth's spherical structure rather than from local expectation.

The geography also has theological force. Jerusalem, the place of Christ's Passion, stands opposite the mountain where souls are prepared for heavenly life. Redemption and purification form a symbolic axis through the earth.

Yet the astronomy is not merely decorative allegory. Dante wants the physical explanation to be understood. Its spiritual implication emerges through the act of learning: apparent disorder can arise from a limited viewpoint rather than from a disordered universe.

## 6. Why the Mountain Becomes Easier

> 88 Ed elli a me: «Questa montagna è tale,  
> (And he to me: “This mountain is such,)
>
> 89 che sempre al cominciar di sotto è grave;  
> (that it is always hard at the beginning below;)
>
> 90 e quant' om più va sù, e men fa male.  
> (and the higher one goes, the less it hurts.)

Dante asks how far they still have to climb. Virgil answers not with a distance but with a law of spiritual progress.

Ordinary mountains often become harder as exhaustion accumulates. This mountain becomes easier. Its difficulty reflects the climber's remaining disorder: the resistance of habits and desires not yet aligned with the good.

The principle connects classical ethics with Christian purification. Repeated good action can form virtuous habits, making right conduct less strained. In the larger theology of the poem, grace heals the will so that goodness becomes increasingly free and wholehearted.

> 91 Però, quand' ella ti parrà soave  
> (Therefore, when it shall seem gentle to you)
>
> 92 tanto, che sù andar ti fia leggero  
> (so much that going upward will be easy for you)
>
> 93 com' a seconda giù andar per nave,  
> (as going downstream in a boat,)
>
> 94 allor sarai al fin d'esto sentiero;  
> (then you will be at the end of this path;)

The destination is defined by a transformed relation to movement. What once required exhausting resistance will become as natural as travelling with a current.

This does not mean that virtue becomes mere passivity. Rather, the division within the will disappears. A purified person no longer experiences the good principally as an external demand opposed to inward desire.

Purgatory therefore aims beyond punishment or endurance. Its goal is freedom: the ability to desire and perform the good without the old obstruction.

## 7. Belacqua: A Body Shaped by Delay

> 106 E un di lor, che mi sembiava lasso,  
> (And one of them, who seemed weary to me,)
>
> 107 sedeva e abbracciava le ginocchia,  
> (sat and clasped his knees,)
>
> 108 tenendo 'l viso giù tra esse basso.  
> (keeping his face lowered between them.)

A voice interrupts Virgil's account of the climb. Behind a large rock, Dante finds souls resting in the shade. One sits folded around himself, with his face lowered between his knees.

The posture reverses the movement of ascent. Dante has been pulling himself upward, raising his eyes, and seeking a route. This figure curls inward and makes himself almost immovable.

He is Belacqua, traditionally identified with a Florentine maker of musical instruments known to Dante. Early commentators associate him with a reputation for laziness, although the biographical details are less secure than the vivid literary portrait.

His posture is comic, but it also gives habit a visible form. The tendencies developed in earthly life remain recognizable in the soul's manner of presenting itself.

> 114 e disse: «Or va tu sù, che se' valente!».  
> (and said: “Now go on up, you who are so capable!”.)

Belacqua punctures Dante's strenuous determination with a teasing challenge. The remark brings the poem down from astronomical exposition and elevated spiritual aspiration to the familiar speech of an acquaintance.

His irony should not simply be adopted as the canto's wisdom. Dante must indeed continue upward. Nevertheless, Belacqua exposes the possibility of self-importance in spiritual effort: climbing does not entitle the pilgrim to regard another soul with easy superiority.

## 8. Recognition, Affection, and the Assurance of Salvation

> 121 Li atti suoi pigri e le corte parole  
> (His sluggish gestures and his brief words)
>
> 122 mosser le labbra mie un poco a riso;  
> (moved my lips a little toward laughter;)
>
> 123 poi cominciai: «Belacqua, a me non dole  
> (then I began: “Belacqua, it no longer grieves me)
>
> 124 di te omai; ma dimmi: perché assiso  
> (about you now; but tell me: why seated)

Dante responds with a slight smile, not a denunciation. Belacqua's economical speech and minimal movements make him immediately recognizable.

More importantly, Dante's anxiety about his acquaintance has been relieved. Belacqua is in the realm of the saved. Whatever his negligence, he is not eternally lost.

This distinction governs the comedy. Dante can laugh affectionately because Belacqua's ultimate destiny is secure. Purgatory preserves individual personality without making a person's worst habit the final truth about them.

The question begun in the last quoted line asks why Belacqua remains sitting there: does he await guidance, or has he simply resumed his old habits? His answer complicates the joke. His delay is not merely a fresh refusal to climb; divine justice requires him to wait.

## 9. Delayed Repentance and the Help of Prayer

> 130 Prima convien che tanto il ciel m'aggiri  
> (First the heavens must revolve around me for as long)
>
> 131 di fuor da essa, quanto fece in vita,  
> (outside it, as they did during life,)
>
> 132 per ch'io 'ndugiai al fine i buon sospiri,  
> (because I delayed until the end the good sighs,)

Belacqua belongs to the late-repentant souls of Ante-Purgatory. Having postponed repentance until the end of life, he must ordinarily wait outside the gate for a period equal to his earthly lifetime.

The good sighs are the sighs of contrition. His last repentance was real and sufficient for salvation, but it did not make the earlier misuse of time meaningless.

The correspondence is exact: someone who delayed turning toward God now experiences delay before entering the purifying terraces. Yet this is not the hopeless repetition of Hell. Belacqua waits within a future that is assured.

He should therefore not be confused with the souls purging sloth on a higher terrace. His present condition concerns delayed repentance and exclusion from Purgatory proper.

> 133 se orazïone in prima non m'aita  
> (unless prayer first comes to my aid)
>
> 134 che surga sù di cuor che in grazia viva;  
> (that rises from a heart living in grace;)
>
> 135 l'altra che val, che 'n ciel non è udita?».  
> (what does the other avail, which is not heard in heaven?”.)

Prayer can shorten the waiting. The living and the dead remain joined within the Christian communion of saints, and charity can cross the boundary of death.

Belacqua specifies prayer arising from a heart living in grace. The emphasis is not on words functioning as a mechanical transaction. Effective intercession belongs to a living relationship with God.

This qualification also prevents the mountain from becoming a monument to isolated self-improvement. Personal effort matters, but no soul's journey is wholly private. Others can assist through love and prayer.

The canto thus balances two truths: Dante must undertake the climb himself, and salvation is never a matter of self-sufficiency.

## 10. The Sun's Reminder: Time Must Be Used

> 136 E già il poeta innanzi mi saliva,  
> (And already the poet was climbing ahead of me,)
>
> 137 e dicea: «Vienne omai; vedi ch'è tocco  
> (and was saying: “Come now; see that there has been reached)
>
> 138 meridïan dal sole e a la riva  
> (the meridian by the sun, and at the shore)
>
> 139 cuopre la notte già col piè Morrocco».  
> (night already covers Morocco with its foot.”)

Virgil is already moving before Dante's conversation has fully released him. His final summons returns the canto to the passage of time.

The sun has reached the meridian: it is noon at Mount Purgatory. The reference to night advancing over Morocco places this local moment within the poem's global geography. Sunlight and darkness continue their courses regardless of an individual's absorption.

The structure circles back to the opening. Dante first failed to notice time while listening to Manfred; he now risks lingering with Belacqua. Neither encounter is worthless, but even valuable attention must be integrated with the purpose of the journey.

Virgil's answer to the temptation to remain is neither an elaborate argument nor a rejection of affection. It is a summons to resume the next necessary action.

## Epilogue — From Divided Effort to Willing Ascent

Canto IV presents purification as the education of attention, desire, and time. The narrow passage demands commitment; the climb demands effort; the unfamiliar sky demands a corrected perspective. Belacqua shows both the lasting consequences of negligence and the saving reach of repentance and communal prayer.

The mountain's deepest promise is that effort will not remain equally painful forever. As the soul is healed, ascent becomes easier because desire itself changes. The journey ends not when freedom has been exhausted by obedience, but when freedom has learned to move willingly toward the good.
