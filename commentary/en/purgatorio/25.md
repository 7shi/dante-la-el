# The Soul and Its Shadow: Allegory and Background in *Purgatorio* XXV

Canto XXV connects two apparently different subjects: the formation of human life and the purification of sexual desire. As Dante, Virgil, and Statius climb from the terrace of gluttony toward the terrace of lust, Dante asks how souls without earthly bodies can become thin. The answer develops into an account of generation, the creation of the rational soul, and the formation of the visible shades encountered throughout the afterlife.

The canto's physiology belongs to medieval natural philosophy, not modern biology. Yet its larger concern remains recognizable: how bodily life, thought, emotion, and personal identity belong together—and how desire can mark the whole person.

## 1. The Afternoon Ascent: Time Must Become Action

> 1 Ora era onde 'l salir non volea storpio;  
> (It was an hour when the ascent allowed no hindrance;)
>
> 2 ché 'l sole avëa il cerchio di merigge  
> (for the sun had the meridian circle)
>
> 3 lasciato al Tauro e la notte a lo Scorpio:  
> (left to Taurus, and the night to Scorpio:)

The astronomical description places the journey at roughly two in the afternoon. The sun has passed the meridian, leaving that position to Taurus; on the opposite side of the heavens, night has similarly yielded it to Scorpio.

This elaborate clock has a practical purpose. The travelers cannot afford delay, since ascent on Mount Purgatory depends on daylight. More broadly, Purgatory is the realm where time can still accomplish something: souls move toward the completion of their purification.

The narrow stair forces the three poets into single file. Their physical progress provides the setting for intellectual progress, but Dante must learn without ceasing to climb. Understanding and moral effort proceed together.

## 2. The Young Stork: Desire Held Back by Hesitation

> 10 E quale il cicognin che leva l'ala  
> (And like the young stork that raises its wing)
>
> 11 per voglia di volare, e non s'attenta  
> (from a desire to fly, and does not dare)
>
> 12 d'abbandonar lo nido, e giù la cala;  
> (to abandon the nest, and lowers it down;)
>
> 13 tal era io con voglia accesa e spenta  
> (so was I, with desire kindled and extinguished)
>
> 14 di dimandar, venendo infino a l'atto  
> (to ask, coming as far as the gesture)
>
> 15 che fa colui ch'a dicer s'argomenta.  
> (made by someone preparing to speak.)

Dante's curiosity repeatedly overcomes his hesitation, then retreats. The young stork makes this movement visible: its wings are ready for an activity it cannot yet confidently undertake.

The comparison also characterizes Dante's education. He possesses the capacity for understanding but still needs encouragement to exercise it. Virgil recognizes his unspoken question and urges him to release the bow of speech, already drawn back to its arrowhead.

Both images concern stored energy awaiting action. In a canto about the development of human powers, even the pupil's hesitation becomes a small example of potential seeking fulfillment.

## 3. The Hungry Dead: Why Bodiless Souls Can Grow Thin

> 20 e cominciai: «Come si può far magro  
> (and began: “How can one grow thin)
>
> 21 là dove l'uopo di nodrir non tocca?».  
> (where the need for nourishment does not apply?”.)

Dante is still thinking about the emaciated souls on the terrace of gluttony. Their appearance seems to contradict their condition: if they no longer need food, what could make them waste away?

The question exposes a difficulty underlying the entire poem. Dante's dead speak, weep, suffer, and recognize one another, yet their mortal bodies remain elsewhere. What kind of embodiment makes these encounters possible?

> 22 «Se t'ammentassi come Meleagro  
> (“If you remembered how Meleager)
>
> 23 si consumò al consumar d'un stizzo,  
> (was consumed as a firebrand was consumed,)
>
> 24 non fora», disse, «a te questo sì agro;  
> (this would not be”, he said, “so difficult for you;)

Virgil first invokes Meleager, whose life, according to classical myth, was bound to a piece of wood. His mother preserved the brand but later burned it, bringing about his death.

The example does not explain the actual mechanism of the shades. It loosens Dante's assumption that bodily change must result from ordinary bodily causes: Meleager's life could be consumed through something outside his body.

> 25 e se pensassi come, al vostro guizzo,  
> (and if you considered how, at your sudden movement,)
>
> 26 guizza dentro a lo specchio vostra image,  
> (your image moves suddenly within the mirror,)
>
> 27 ciò che par duro ti parrebbe vizzo.  
> (what seems hard would seem soft to you.)

The mirror supplies a more familiar analogy. An image changes with the person it reflects, although it does not possess that person's flesh.

Together, the examples prepare Dante to understand a visible form that responds to something other than its own material needs. The shade's appearance expresses the soul's condition. Virgil then asks Statius to explain the process itself.

## 4. Generation: Medieval Physiology and the Formative Power

> 37 Sangue perfetto, che poi non si beve  
> (Perfect blood, which then is not drunk)
>
> 38 da l'assetate vene, e si rimane  
> (by the thirsty veins, and remains)
>
> 39 quasi alimento che di mensa leve,  
> (like food that you remove from the table,)

Statius begins surprisingly far back: to explain the body after death, he first explains the body before birth.

According to the physiology presented here, refined blood remains after the body's nutritional requirements have been met. It receives a formative power in the heart and, after further refinement, becomes the male generative seed.

This account draws on Aristotelian natural philosophy as developed in medieval medicine and scholastic thought. Its governing idea is that living matter requires an organizing principle: something must make the developing organism a coordinated whole rather than an accidental collection of parts.

> 46 Ivi s'accoglie l'uno e l'altro insieme,  
> (There the one and the other come together,)
>
> 47 l'un disposto a patire, e l'altro a fare  
> (the one disposed to undergo, and the other to act)
>
> 48 per lo perfetto loco onde si preme;  
> (because of the perfect place from which it is pressed;)

The two substances meet in the womb. Within this historical theory, the female contribution supplies receptive matter, while the male contribution supplies the active formative principle, whose power derives from its origin in the heart.

This unequal assignment of roles belongs to the gendered assumptions of ancient and medieval reproductive theory; it is not an accurate account of conception. For Dante's argument, however, its central function is to introduce the *formative power*. That power will eventually explain not only the development of bodily organs but also the soul's visible appearance after death.

## 5. Plant, Animal, Human: Life Develops Through Ordered Powers

> 52 Anima fatta la virtute attiva  
> (The active power having become a soul)
>
> 53 qual d'una pianta, in tanto differente,  
> (like that of a plant, differing in this respect,)
>
> 54 che questa è in via e quella è già a riva,  
> (that this is on its way and that already at the shore,)

The developing being first possesses the kind of life associated with plants: nourishment and growth. Here, “soul” means the principle of life, not yet the fully developed rational soul.

The embryo and the plant differ in their destination. Vegetative life is the plant's completed condition, but only a stage in human development. Dante makes this distinction through the language of travel: one is still on the road, while the other has reached shore.

That image echoes the pilgrims' own condition. A genuine achievement can remain incomplete when measured against the end toward which it is directed.

> 55 tanto ovra poi, che già si move e sente,  
> (it then works so much that it already moves and feels,)
>
> 56 come spungo marino; e indi imprende  
> (like a sea sponge; and then undertakes)
>
> 57 ad organar le posse ond' è semente.  
> (to furnish with organs the powers of which it is the seed.)

The organism next acquires sensation and movement, compared here to the rudimentary life of a marine creature. It then develops the organs through which its capacities can operate.

This is an account of individual development, not a theory of evolution. Its sequence follows the traditional hierarchy of vegetative, sensitive, and rational life.

The emphasis falls on continuity: a formative principle progressively realizes capacities already implicit in its activity. Nevertheless, Statius will insist that rational humanity requires more than the completion of a purely natural process.

## 6. The Rational Soul: Divine Creation and Personal Unity

> 64 sì che per sua dottrina fé disgiunto  
> (so that in his teaching he made separate)
>
> 65 da l'anima il possibile intelletto,  
> (from the soul the possible intellect,)
>
> 66 perché da lui non vide organo assunto.  
> (because he saw no organ employed by it.)

The unnamed thinker is generally identified as Averroes, the influential commentator on Aristotle. The “possible intellect” is the capacity to receive intelligible forms and thus to understand.

As Dante presents the disputed position, the absence of a bodily organ for intellectual activity led Averroes to separate this intellect from the individual soul. The issue has enormous consequences for the poem: if intellectual life does not belong to an enduring individual, what becomes of personal responsibility, memory, and judgment?

Statius answers by defending the unity of the human person.

> 70 lo motor primo a lui si volge lieto  
> (the First Mover turns joyfully toward it)
>
> 71 sovra tant' arte di natura, e spira  
> (over so great a work of nature, and breathes)
>
> 72 spirito novo, di vertù repleto,  
> (a new spirit, filled with power,)
>
> 73 che ciò che trova attivo quivi, tira  
> (which draws whatever it finds active there)
>
> 74 in sua sustanzia, e fassi un'alma sola,  
> (into its own substance, and becomes a single soul,)
>
> 75 che vive e sente e sé in sé rigira.  
> (which lives and feels and turns itself upon itself.)

Once the brain's organization is complete, God creates the rational spirit. Nature prepares the organism, but rational life is a direct divine gift.

Crucially, the new spirit does not remain alongside separate vegetable and animal souls. It incorporates their powers into “a single soul.” The human being lives, senses, and reflects through one unified principle.

The final phrase describes reflexive consciousness: the soul can turn inward upon itself. The divine gift thus establishes both intellectual capacity and personal inwardness. This unity makes the individual's continued existence after death intelligible within the poem.

## 7. Death: The Soul Retains Its Identity and Powers

> 79 Quando Làchesis non ha più del lino,  
> (When Lachesis has no more flax,)
>
> 80 solvesi da la carne, e in virtute  
> (it is loosed from the flesh, and potentially)
>
> 81 ne porta seco e l'umano e 'l divino:  
> (carries away with it both the human and the divine:)
>
> 82 l'altre potenze tutte quante mute;  
> (all the other powers entirely mute;)
>
> 83 memoria, intelligenza e volontade  
> (memory, intelligence, and will)
>
> 84 in atto molto più che prima agute.  
> (in action far keener than before.)

Lachesis, one of the classical Fates, supplies the image of a life-thread reaching its end. Dante incorporates this pagan figure into a Christian account of death without treating her as an independent ruler of human destiny.

The soul separates from the flesh but retains its powers. Those dependent on bodily organs become inactive; memory, understanding, and will operate with heightened intensity.

The distinction between possessing a power and exercising it is essential. Death does not destroy the soul's relationship to its bodily life, even though the earthly organs are gone.

Statius next explains that the soul arrives at one of two shores: that of Acheron for the damned, or the Tiber's mouth for those destined for Purgatory. The individual survives not as an anonymous spiritual fragment but as a person whose moral direction has become decisive.

## 8. The Aerial Body: Inner Desire Becomes Visible

> 88 Tosto che loco lì la circunscrive,  
> (As soon as a place there circumscribes it,)
>
> 89 la virtù formativa raggia intorno  
> (the formative power radiates around)
>
> 90 così e quanto ne le membra vive.  
> (in the same manner and measure as in the living limbs.)

The formative power that organized the living body now acts upon the surrounding air. It produces a visible likeness through which the soul can manifest itself.

Statius compares the process to moist air taking on the colors of a rainbow. He then compares the resulting form's movement to a flame following its fire. These analogies suggest a dependent appearance: the visible form belongs to, and follows, the soul.

This aerial body explains how shades can speak, laugh, weep, and display recognizable features. It is not their resurrected flesh; the resurrection remains a future event.

> 106 Secondo che ci affliggono i disiri  
> (According as desires afflict us)
>
> 107 e li altri affetti, l'ombra si figura;  
> (and the other emotions, the shade takes shape;)
>
> 108 e quest' è la cagion di che tu miri».  
> (and this is the cause of what you marvel at”.)

Here Dante finally receives his answer. The gluttons' thinness is not caused by a biological shortage of nourishment. Their shades express the hunger and longing through which they undergo purification.

The explanation also offers a key to the poem's imaginative method. Invisible spiritual realities become visible forms. Desire is not merely described: it acquires a face, a posture, and an apparent body.

The long discussion of embryology is therefore structurally necessary. The same formative continuity that explains a person's earthly embodiment also makes the person's inward condition perceptible after death.

## 9. The Terrace of Lust: Fire and the Discipline of Sight

> 112 Quivi la ripa fiamma in fuor balestra,  
> (There the cliff shoots flame outward,)
>
> 113 e la cornice spira fiato in suso  
> (and the terrace breathes a wind upward)
>
> 114 che la reflette e via da lei sequestra;  
> (that turns it back and keeps it away from the edge;)

The travelers reach the seventh and highest terrace, where lust is purified. Flame projects from the mountainside, while an upward wind bends it back, leaving a narrow passage beside the precipice.

Fire is appropriate to a sin conventionally understood as burning desire. Yet this is not simply the destructive fire of punishment. In Purgatory, suffering works toward restoration: the ardor that once escaped proper measure becomes the instrument through which desire is reordered.

The canto's movement from generation to lust is consequently deliberate. Sexual power is part of the created goodness of human life; the problem lies in its disorder, not in embodiment itself.

> 118 Lo duca mio dicea: «Per questo loco  
> (My guide was saying: “Through this place)
>
> 119 si vuol tenere a li occhi stretto il freno,  
> (one must keep a tight rein upon the eyes,)
>
> 120 però ch'errar potrebbesi per poco».  
> (because one could go astray through a small lapse”.)

Virgil's warning is immediately practical: a moment's inattention could lead Dante into the flames or over the edge.

It also carries moral force on the terrace of lust. Sight can awaken and direct desire; the eyes therefore require governance. The image of reins suggests disciplined guidance rather than the destruction of a natural faculty.

Dante must divide his attention between the souls and his own footsteps. Even the desire to observe something spiritually important does not excuse neglect of the path.

## 10. Hymns and Exemplars: Desire Learns a Different Order

> 121 ‘Summae Deus clementïae’ nel seno  
> (‘God of highest clemency’ within the heart)
>
> 122 al grande ardore allora udi' cantando,  
> (of the great burning I then heard sung,)

The souls sing a Latin hymn associated with the night office. Its appeal for the purification of the heart and the restraint of lust makes it especially appropriate here.

Their suffering is joined to prayer. Purification requires not merely enduring a painful condition but actively turning desire toward God.

> 127 Appresso il fine ch'a quell' inno fassi,  
> (After the ending that is made to that hymn,)
>
> 128 gridavano alto: ‘Virum non cognosco’;  
> (they cried aloud: ‘I know not a man’;)
>
> 129 indi ricominciavan l'inno bassi.  
> (then they began the hymn again in low voices.)

The first exemplary voice is Mary's, quoting her response to Gabriel at the Annunciation in Luke 1:34. Her words introduce virginal purity as the opposite of lust's disorder.

Mary's chastity is not presented as mere withdrawal. Within the larger Christian narrative, it accompanies her assent to divine purpose. Desire and freedom find their right direction in that assent.

> 130 Finitolo, anco gridavano: «Al bosco  
> (Having finished it, they also cried: “To the woods)
>
> 131 si tenne Diana, ed Elice caccionne  
> (Diana kept, and drove Elice out of them)
>
> 132 che di Venere avea sentito il tòsco».  
> (who had felt the poison of Venus”.)

The second example comes from classical mythology. Diana preserves her chastity and expels Elice, better known as Callisto, after her sexual involvement with Jupiter.

Dante places pagan and biblical examples within the same program of moral instruction. Diana represents the guarding of chastity, while Venus's “poison” figures erotic desire as something that can overwhelm judgment.

The example also requires historical caution: in Ovid's account, Callisto suffers sexual violence. Dante's compressed moral use of the story does not adequately represent that victimization; it draws on a tradition of extracting exemplary meanings from complicated myths.

> 133 Indi al cantar tornavano; indi donne  
> (Then they returned to singing; then women)
>
> 134 gridavano e mariti che fuor casti  
> (they called out, and husbands who were chaste)
>
> 135 come virtute e matrimonio imponne.  
> (as virtue and marriage enjoined.)

The final category prevents chastity from being equated solely with virginity. Faithful wives and husbands also exemplify it.

The moral distinction is therefore not simply between sexual activity and abstinence. It is between disordered appetite and desire governed by virtue and rightful commitment. Marriage has its own form of chastity.

> 138 con tal cura conviene e con tai pasti  
> (with such treatment and with such nourishment it is necessary)
>
> 139 che la piaga da sezzo si ricuscia.  
> (that the last wound should be sewn closed.)

The canto ends with the language of healing. Lust is the final wound to be closed on the ascent, and the souls' regimen combines fire, prayer, and exemplary nourishment.

The reference to “nourishment” also recalls the opening question about hunger. The body's need for food has given way to the soul's need for what can restore it.

## Epilogue — The Visible Shape of Desire

Canto XXV explains why Dante's souls can look and act like embodied persons, but its significance extends beyond the mechanics of the afterlife. Human life forms a unity: growth, sensation, thought, memory, and will belong to the same enduring individual.

That unity makes desire consequential. What the soul loves affects the form through which it becomes visible. On the terrace of lust, the goal is not to erase embodied love but to heal its disorder. The canto moves from the creation of life to the correction of desire, presenting purification as the restoration of the whole person.
