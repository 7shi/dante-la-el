# Purgatorio VII: The Limits of Reason and the Responsibilities of Kings

Canto VII moves from an encounter between poets to a gathering of rulers. Its setting is still **Antepurgatory**, below the entrance to Purgatory proper: here, souls who delayed repentance must wait before beginning their purification.

Sordello, the medieval Mantuan poet, has already embraced Virgil as a fellow countryman. Now he discovers exactly whom he has welcomed. Their conversation about salvation and the mountain's rules leads to a sheltered valley where negligent princes await their ascent. Throughout, Dante asks what poetry, reason, political power, and inherited greatness can accomplish—and where they depend on something beyond themselves.

## 1. Sordello Recognizes the Master Poet

> 13 tal parve quelli; e poi chinò le ciglia,  
> (such that man appeared; and then he lowered his eyes,)
>
> 14 e umilmente ritornò ver' lui,  
> (and humbly turned back toward him,)
>
> 15 e abbracciòl là 've 'l minor s'appiglia.  
> (and embraced him where the lesser person takes hold.)

Sordello's first embrace, in the preceding canto, expressed the equality of two men from Mantua. This second embrace acknowledges a hierarchy: he now knows that the stranger is Virgil. His gesture, generally understood as an embrace low on the body or at the knees, expresses a younger poet's reverence for his master.

The scene gives literary greatness an authority distinct from political rank. Before Dante surveys kings and emperors, he shows a poet receiving homage. Yet this authority has limits: the honored Virgil will shortly explain that his greatness could not secure salvation.

> 17 mostrò ciò che potea la lingua nostra,  
> (showed what our language could do,)
>
> 18 o pregio etterno del loco ond' io fui,  
> (O eternal glory of the place from which I came,)

Sordello praises Virgil both as the supreme practitioner of Latin poetry and as Mantua's lasting glory. The historical Sordello was a thirteenth-century troubadour who wrote in Occitan, not Latin. His praise therefore reaches across linguistic and historical divisions to claim Virgil as part of a shared cultural inheritance.

There is also a political connection. Sordello's poetry included a celebrated lament that rebuked contemporary rulers for their lack of courage. Dante makes this poet of aristocratic criticism the guide who will identify—and judge—the princes in the valley.

## 2. Virgil's Exclusion: Virtue Without Saving Faith

> 25 Non per far, ma per non fare ho perduto  
> (Not through doing, but through not doing, I have lost)
>
> 26 a veder l'alto Sol che tu disiri  
> (the sight of the high Sun that you desire)
>
> 27 e che fu tardi per me conosciuto.  
> (and that was known by me too late.)

The “high Sun” is God. Virgil describes his loss not as punishment for some particular wicked deed but as the consequence of an absence: he did not possess the faith necessary for salvation within Dante's Christian account of history.

The wording also anticipates the negligent rulers. They, too, failed to do something required of them. But their eventual repentance distinguishes their condition from Virgil's: their waiting will end, whereas his exclusion from the vision of God will not.

Virgil often represents natural reason in the poem. Here, that allegorical role becomes painfully personal. Reason can recognize much that is true and good, but it cannot independently supply the supernatural conditions of salvation.

> 34 quivi sto io con quei che le tre sante  
> (there I dwell with those who the three holy)
>
> 35 virtù non si vestiro, e sanza vizio  
> (virtues did not put on, and without vice)
>
> 36 conobber l'altre e seguir tutte quante.  
> (knew the others and followed them all.)

The three holy virtues are **faith, hope, and charity**, traditionally called the theological virtues because they orient the soul directly toward God. The “others” are the cardinal virtues: prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance.

Virgil's account of Limbo preserves the dignity of virtuous non-Christians while insisting that moral excellence and salvation are not identical. Its inhabitants suffer no bodily torture, but they remain deprived of divine vision. His earlier mention of infants who died before baptism makes the problem still more troubling: exclusion is not simply a measure of personal wrongdoing.

Dante does not allow admiration for classical civilization to erase this theological boundary. Instead, he makes the reader feel the sorrow it creates.

## 3. Nightfall: Why the Will Cannot Ascend Alone

> 55 non però ch'altra cosa desse briga,  
> (not, however, because anything else caused obstruction,)
>
> 56 che la notturna tenebra, ad ir suso;  
> (than the darkness of night, to going upward;)
>
> 57 quella col nonpoder la voglia intriga.  
> (that entangles the will with inability.)

Asked whether someone would physically prevent a nighttime ascent, Sordello explains that no external guard is necessary. Darkness itself makes ascent impossible. He illustrates the restriction by tracing a line on the ground that the travelers could not cross upward after sunset.

Literally, this is a law of the mountain. Allegorically, it suggests that spiritual progress requires divine illumination. The desire to rise cannot make itself effective merely by willing harder.

The connection with Virgil's “high Sun” is important. God is the ultimate light toward which the mountain leads, while daylight supplies the visible condition of ascent. Natural reason and human effort remain necessary, but they do not constitute an independent route to redemption.

Sordello adds that movement sideways or downward remains possible. The distinction is pointed: activity is not necessarily progress. One may move without drawing nearer to the good.

## 4. The Valley: Beauty Beyond Human Art

> 73 Oro e argento fine, cocco e biacca,  
> (Gold and fine silver, scarlet and white lead,)
>
> 74 indaco, legno lucido e sereno,  
> (indigo, wood bright and clear,)
>
> 75 fresco smeraldo in l'ora che si fiacca,  
> (fresh emerald at the moment it is broken,)
>
> 76 da l'erba e da li fior, dentr' a quel seno  
> (by the grass and flowers, within that hollow)
>
> 77 posti, ciascun saria di color vinto,  
> (placed, each would be surpassed in color,)
>
> 78 come dal suo maggiore è vinto il meno.  
> (as the lesser is surpassed by its greater.)

The travelers discover a recess in the mountainside whose vegetation outshines precious metals, pigments, and gemstones. The “wood” probably refers to an exotic coloring material, though its precise identification is uncertain.

Dante describes natural beauty through the materials of painting and luxury, then declares those materials inadequate. The valley surpasses what human wealth can purchase and human art can reproduce.

This is particularly appropriate to a gathering of rulers. Their former world was one of display, expensive fabrics, splendid objects, and courtly magnificence. Here, a beauty they neither commissioned nor own exceeds all such achievements.

The setting resembles the literary **locus amoenus**, the delightful place of shade, flowers, and repose. It also anticipates the Earthly Paradise at the mountain's summit. But it is not that final garden: the princes are still waiting below the place of active purification. Their surroundings offer consolation without making further ascent unnecessary.

## 5. The Salve Regina: Kings Become Exiles and Petitioners

> 82 ‘Salve, Regina’ in sul verde e 'n su' fiori  
> (‘Hail, Queen’ upon the greenery and upon the flowers)
>
> 83 quindi seder cantando anime vidi,  
> (from there I saw souls sitting and singing,)
>
> 84 che per la valle non parean di fuori.  
> (who because of the valley were not visible from outside.)

The souls sing the **Salve Regina**, the Marian prayer that addresses the Virgin as queen and mother of mercy. Its language describes humanity as exiled children of Eve, sighing in a valley of tears and longing to see Jesus.

The correspondence between prayer and setting is deliberate. These souls sit in a literal valley, separated from their ultimate homeland. Its flowers do not cancel their exile; beauty and longing coexist.

For former sovereigns, addressing Mary as queen also reorders authority. Those who once received petitions now petition someone higher. Earthly rank becomes subordinate to a spiritual sovereignty founded not on force but on mercy.

> 88 Di questo balzo meglio li atti e ' volti  
> (From this ledge better the gestures and faces)
>
> 89 conoscerete voi di tutti quanti,  
> (you will discern of every one of them,)
>
> 90 che ne la lama giù tra essi accolti.  
> (than down in the hollow gathered among them.)

Sordello keeps Dante and Virgil on a raised ledge because they can observe the souls more clearly from there. This practical arrangement also shapes the canto's moral perspective.

The travelers look at rulers rather than approaching them as subjects or courtiers. Faces, postures, silence, and song disclose truths that ceremonial splendor once concealed. Political judgment becomes an act of reading outward signs of inward recognition and remorse.

## 6. Rudolf and Ottokar: Imperial Neglect and Reconciled Enemies

> 94 Rodolfo imperador fu, che potea  
> (was Emperor Rudolf, who could)
>
> 95 sanar le piaghe c'hanno Italia morta,  
> (heal the wounds that have killed Italy,)
>
> 96 sì che tardi per altri si ricrea.  
> (so that only slowly through others is she revived.)

The highest-seated ruler is Rudolf I of Habsburg, elected king of the Romans in 1273. Dante calls him emperor, although he never received an imperial coronation in Rome.

His fault is a neglected opportunity. Dante believed that a properly functioning universal empire could restrain factional violence and secure peace. Rudolf's failure to intervene decisively in Italy therefore represents more than an unsuccessful foreign policy: he left unfulfilled the protective responsibilities of his office.

The wounded body of Italy recalls the fierce political lament in the preceding canto. Here, however, denunciation becomes an image of penitence. Rudolf visibly recognizes that he failed to do what he should have done.

> 100 Ottacchero ebbe nome, e ne le fasce  
> (Ottokar was his name, and in swaddling clothes)
>
> 101 fu meglio assai che Vincislao suo figlio  
> (he was far better than Wenceslaus his son)
>
> 102 barbuto, cui lussuria e ozio pasce.  
> (bearded, whom lust and idleness feed.)

Beside Rudolf sits Ottokar II of Bohemia, his former enemy, killed fighting Rudolf's forces in 1278. In the valley, Ottokar appears to comfort him. Their proximity reveals how salvation transforms earthly antagonism: dynastic rivalry no longer determines their relationship.

Sordello contrasts Ottokar with his son Wenceslaus II. The exaggeration is deliberately humiliating: the father in infancy surpassed the fully grown son. A beard establishes adulthood, not moral maturity.

This begins a recurring argument: political power can pass through a family without virtue passing with it.

## 7. The French Kings: Grief Over a Corrupted Successor

> 109 Padre e suocero son del mal di Francia:  
> (They are father and father-in-law of the evil of France:)
>
> 110 sanno la vita sua viziata e lorda,  
> (they know his vicious and filthy life,)
>
> 111 e quindi viene il duol che sì li lancia.  
> (and from this comes the grief that so pierces them.)

The two grieving figures are **Philip III of France** and **Henry I of Navarre**. They are respectively the father and father-in-law of Philip IV of France, whom Dante calls “the evil of France.”

Philip III, previously identified by his small nose, died in 1285 during the disastrous retreat from his campaign against Aragon. Henry's daughter Joan married Philip IV, linking the French and Navarrese dynasties.

Their bodily gestures—one beating his breast, the other resting his cheek on his hand—turn dynastic prestige into dynastic sorrow. They now recognize the moral character of the ruler whom their family connections helped place in power.

Philip IV is among Dante's most persistent political targets. Elsewhere, the poem attacks his government's greed and its dealings with the Church. Here, Dante makes the judgment intimate: the king's own family grieves over what he has become.

## 8. Peter and Charles: Virtue Is Not a Hereditary Possession

> 112 Quel che par sì membruto e che s'accorda,  
> (The one who appears so strongly built and joins in harmony,)
>
> 113 cantando, con colui dal maschio naso,  
> (singing, with the one of the manly nose,)
>
> 114 d'ogne valor portò cinta la corda;  
> (wore the cord girded with every kind of worth;)

The strongly built ruler is **Peter III of Aragon**; the prominent-nosed companion is **Charles I of Anjou**. They had been bitter rivals over Sicily, especially after the Sicilian Vespers of 1282 displaced Angevin rule from the island.

Now they sing together. Like Rudolf and Ottokar, they embody a reconciliation unavailable within the competitive logic of earthly politics. Their harmony does not erase the historical consequences of their actions, but it shows that their wills are no longer organized around enmity.

The image of Peter girded with every excellence presents virtue almost as a knightly equipment. Yet the following account asks whether his descendants inherited anything more substantial than territories.

> 119 Iacomo e Federigo hanno i reami;  
> (James and Frederick possess the kingdoms;)
>
> 120 del retaggio miglior nessun possiede.  
> (neither possesses the better inheritance.)
>
> 121 Rade volte risurge per li rami  
> (Rarely does there rise again through the branches)
>
> 122 l'umana probitate; e questo vole  
> (human uprightness; and this is willed by)
>
> 123 quei che la dà, perché da lui si chiami.  
> (the one who gives it, so that it may be asked of him.)

James II of Aragon and Frederick of Sicily possess kingdoms but, in Sordello's judgment, lack their father's better inheritance: moral excellence. The young figure behind Peter is traditionally identified as Alfonso III, whose early death ended the hope of a more worthy continuation.

The metaphor of family branches becomes a theological argument. Goodness is not a secure biological property. God wills that it should be sought from its true giver rather than treated as an automatic consequence of ancestry.

This does not make moral effort irrelevant. It denies the dynasty's claim to own excellence. Hereditary entitlement must answer to a standard that heredity itself cannot guarantee.

## 9. Angevin Decline and an English Exception

> 127 Tant' è del seme suo minor la pianta,  
> (So much inferior to its seed is the plant,)
>
> 128 quanto, più che Beatrice e Margherita,  
> (as, more than Beatrice and Margaret,)
>
> 129 Costanza di marito ancor si vanta.  
> (Constance still boasts of her husband.)

The compressed comparison concerns Charles II of Anjou, the inferior “plant” sprung from Charles I. Constance, Peter III's wife, has more reason to boast of her husband than Charles I's wives, Beatrice and Margaret, have of theirs.

The comparison establishes distinctions rather than a simple division into good and bad dynasties. Peter surpasses Charles I, yet Charles I himself surpasses his son. Dante refuses to let allegiance to one political house determine every judgment of individual worth.

The wider context is the suffering of territories ruled by deficient successors. Dynastic decline matters because its consequences fall on communities, not merely on a family's reputation.

> 130 Vedete il re de la semplice vita  
> (See the king of the simple life)
>
> 131 seder là solo, Arrigo d'Inghilterra:  
> (sitting there alone, Henry of England:)
>
> 132 questi ha ne' rami suoi migliore uscita.  
> (he has in his branches a better outcome.)

Henry III of England provides a counterexample: his son Edward I is presented as a better dynastic outcome.

Henry's “simple life” suggests a comparatively unassuming character, though simplicity need not imply great political ability. The important point is the reversal of the preceding pattern. A descendant can surpass a parent as readily as fall below one.

Dante's claim is therefore not that history inevitably declines. It is that moral worth cannot be predicted from a family tree. Each ruler remains responsible for what he makes of inherited power.

## 10. William of Montferrat: The Subjects Pay for Their Rulers' Wars

> 134 guardando in suso, è Guiglielmo marchese,  
> (looking upward, is Marquis William,)
>
> 135 per cui e Alessandria e la sua guerra  
> (because of whom both Alessandria and its war)
>
> 136 fa pianger Monferrato e Canavese».  
> (make Montferrat and Canavese weep.”)

The final figure is **William VII, Marquis of Montferrat**, a major power in northern Italian politics. Captured at Alessandria in 1290, he died in captivity in 1292. The warfare associated with his capture and its aftermath, including his son's efforts at revenge, brought suffering to Montferrat and Canavese.

His low position completes the descent through the valley's political ranks, from emperor to regional lord. But the closing emphasis is not on rank. It is on those who weep because rulers pursue power and vengeance.

The canto thus ends by turning away from the powerful men themselves toward the communities affected by them. Even when rulers are ultimately saved, the earthly suffering produced by their government remains real.

## Epilogue — Greatness Must Answer to Grace and Duty

Canto VII brings poetic greatness, moral virtue, and political authority under a common judgment. Virgil possesses extraordinary wisdom but cannot attain salvation through that wisdom alone. The princes possessed power but often failed to perform the duties attached to it. Their descendants inherited kingdoms without necessarily inheriting excellence.

Yet the valley is also a place of hope. Former enemies sing together, rulers acknowledge a higher sovereignty, and remorse replaces earthly self-importance.

The canto's central distinction is between **possessing an advantage and using it rightly**. Learning, ancestry, office, and even the desire to improve are not sufficient in themselves. Genuine ascent requires grace—and a will that responds to it through responsible action.
