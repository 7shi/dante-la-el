# Hunger, Love, and the Discipline of Desire in *Purgatorio* XXIV

Canto XXIV takes place on the sixth terrace of Purgatory, where excessive attachment to food and drink is purified through hunger and thirst. Dante walks with Virgil and Statius while speaking to his old friend Forese Donati, whom he encountered in the preceding canto.

The setting makes bodily appetite the immediate subject, but the canto explores desire more broadly: affection for friends, poetic inspiration, political ambition, and the longing for salvation. Its governing distinction is not between desire and its absence, but between desire that follows a rightful guide and desire that refuses restraint.

## 1. Emaciated Souls and the Wonder of a Living Body

> 4 e l'ombre, che parean cose rimorte,  
> (and the shades, who seemed things dead a second time,)
>
> 5 per le fosse de li occhi ammirazione  
> (through the hollows of their eyes amazement)
>
> 6 traean di me, di mio vivere accorte.  
> (drew from me, having become aware that I was alive.)

The souls look as though they have died again: their punishment has stripped even their shadow-bodies of fullness. Yet these seemingly lifeless figures are intensely alert. Their hollow eyes register astonishment at Dante, whose living body makes him an extraordinary visitor.

This reversal establishes the terrace's spiritual logic. Bodily depletion accompanies inward renewal. Those who once gave excessive attention to eating now undergo a hunger that loosens appetite's domination.

Unlike the punishments of Hell, this suffering is corrective and willingly accepted. The souls are not being taught that nourishment is evil; they are learning that bodily satisfaction must not become the governing purpose of life.

## 2. Piccarda: Heavenly Fulfillment Beyond Earthly Appearance

> 13 «La mia sorella, che tra bella e buona  
> (“My sister, who between beautiful and good)
>
> 14 non so qual fosse più, trïunfa lieta  
> (I do not know which she was more, triumphs joyfully)
>
> 15 ne l'alto Olimpo già di sua corona».  
> (in high Olympus already in her crown.”)

Forese answers Dante's question about his sister Piccarda Donati. The balance between “beautiful” and “good” joins outward attractiveness to inward excellence, but her heavenly crown places the final emphasis on spiritual fulfillment.

“Olympus,” the mountain of the classical gods, here names the Christian heaven. Dante regularly makes inherited classical language serve Christian meanings.

Piccarda will appear in *Paradiso* III, where she explains how she was forcibly removed from religious life. Here, before Dante hears that history, Forese announces its ultimate outcome: she is saved. Her joyful fulfillment contrasts with the unfulfilled bodily hunger surrounding the speakers.

> 16 Sì disse prima; e poi: «Qui non si vieta  
> (So he first spoke; and then: “Here it is not forbidden)
>
> 17 di nominar ciascun, da ch'è sì munta  
> (to name each person, since so drained is)
>
> 18 nostra sembianza via per la dïeta.  
> (our appearance away by the diet.)

The penitential “diet” has made the souls difficult to recognize. Forese therefore supplies names where faces can no longer identify their owners.

The contrast matters: bodily appearance has diminished, but personal identity remains. Salvation does not dissolve individuals into anonymity. Their histories and relationships survive, though their former dependence on physical appearance and worldly standing is being dismantled.

## 3. A Pope Among the Hungry: Rank Offers No Exemption

> 22 ebbe la Santa Chiesa in le sue braccia:  
> (held Holy Church in his arms:)
>
> 23 dal Torso fu, e purga per digiuno  
> (he was from Tours, and purges through fasting)
>
> 24 l'anguille di Bolsena e la vernaccia».  
> (the eels of Bolsena and the vernaccia wine.”)

The face Forese has indicated belongs to Pope Martin IV, a French pope whose pontificate lasted from 1281 to 1285. Dante identifies him through his association with Tours and through a gastronomic reputation: the eels of Lake Bolsena and vernaccia wine. Medieval anecdotes elaborated on his fondness for these delicacies, sometimes describing eels prepared with wine.

The juxtaposition is deliberately sharp. A man who held the universal Church in his care is remembered through particular foods and a particular appetite. High office does not exempt anyone from the moral consequences of ordinary habits.

Yet the placement is also merciful. Martin is in Purgatory, not Hell: his salvation is assured. Dante can criticize a pope without treating his entire existence as corrupt. The terrace subjects ecclesiastical and secular distinction alike to the same corrective justice.

## 4. Gentucca: The Unexpected Hospitality of Exile

> 43 «Femmina è nata, e non porta ancor benda»,  
> (“A woman has been born, and does not yet wear a headcloth,”)
>
> 44 cominciò el, «che ti farà piacere  
> (he began, “who will make pleasing to you)
>
> 45 la mia città, come ch'om la riprenda.  
> (my city, however people may reproach it.)

Bonagiunta da Lucca predicts that a woman will make his native city welcome to Dante. He has already murmured her name: Gentucca. Her lack of the headcloth conventionally associated with married women indicates that, at the poem's dramatic date of 1300, she is still unmarried.

Her precise historical identity and the circumstances of her kindness remain uncertain. The passage promises a welcome, not necessarily a romantic relationship.

The prophecy belongs to the canto's background of exile. Dante's banishment from Florence, beginning in 1302, will make him dependent on others' hospitality. A city with an unfavorable reputation will become dear to him through one person's goodness. Individual charity complicates collective prejudice.

There is also a quiet correspondence with the terrace: where appetite formerly sought possession and consumption, Gentucca represents the sustaining goodness of a gift freely offered.

## 5. The *Dolce Stil Novo*: Writing Under Love's Dictation

> 52 E io a lui: «I' mi son un che, quando  
> (And I to him: “I am one who, when)
>
> 53 Amor mi spira, noto, e a quel modo  
> (Love inspires me, takes note, and in that manner)
>
> 54 ch'e' ditta dentro vo significando».  
> (which he dictates within I go on expressing.”)

Bonagiunta has recognized Dante as the author of *Donne ch'avete intelletto d'amore*, a canzone incorporated into the *Vita nuova*. Dante answers by describing not his fame but his method: he receives an inward prompting from Love and seeks to express it faithfully.

The verbs imply attention and service. The poet listens, records, and communicates rather than treating language merely as an opportunity for rhetorical display.

This is not simply a modern claim that good poetry expresses spontaneous personal feelings. “Love” belongs to a medieval literary and philosophical vocabulary in which erotic experience can disclose questions about virtue, nobility, and spiritual transformation. Within the *Comedy*, inward inspiration must ultimately answer to a larger moral order.

> 55 «O frate, issa vegg' io», diss' elli, «il nodo  
> (“O brother, now I see,” he said, “the knot)
>
> 56 che 'l Notaro e Guittone e me ritenne  
> (that held the Notary and Guittone and me)
>
> 57 di qua dal dolce stil novo ch'i' odo!  
> (on this side of the sweet new style that I hear!)
>
> 58 Io veggio ben come le vostre penne  
> (I see clearly how your pens)
>
> 59 di retro al dittator sen vanno strette,  
> (follow closely behind the dictator,)
>
> 60 che de le nostre certo non avvenne;  
> (which certainly did not happen with ours;)

The “Notary” is Giacomo da Lentini, a leading poet of the Sicilian school; Guittone is Guittone d'Arezzo, an influential Tuscan predecessor. Bonagiunta includes himself among poets whose writing lacked the fidelity Dante has described.

The “dictator” is Love as one who dictates, not a political ruler. The new poets' pens follow their source closely. The phrase *dolce stil novo*, “sweet new style,” became the conventional name for a major current in medieval Italian lyric poetry.

This remains Dante's staged account of literary history, not a neutral classification accepted by everyone involved. Its placement among the gluttons is especially suggestive: faithful poetry requires a discipline analogous to rightly ordered appetite. Neither verbal skill nor desire is condemned; each must follow an appropriate guide.

## 6. Florence and the Longing for Another Shore

> 76 «Non so», rispuos' io lui, «quant' io mi viva;  
> (“I do not know,” I answered him, “how long I shall live;)
>
> 77 ma già non fïa il tornar mio tantosto,  
> (but my return will certainly not be so soon,)
>
> 78 ch'io non sia col voler prima a la riva;  
> (that I shall not first be at the shore in desire;)
>
> 79 però che 'l loco u' fui a viver posto,  
> (because the place where I was set to live,)
>
> 80 di giorno in giorno più di ben si spolpa,  
> (from day to day strips itself more of goodness,)
>
> 81 e a trista ruina par disposto».  
> (and seems appointed for grievous ruin.”)

Forese asks when he will see Dante again. Dante cannot know the date of his death, but says that his desire will reach the shore of the next life before he does. Florence's worsening condition makes earthly life increasingly painful.

The verb *si spolpa*, suggesting the stripping away of flesh, echoes the terrace's emaciated bodies. But the similarity exposes a moral opposition. The penitents lose bodily fullness while gaining spiritual health; Florence loses goodness and approaches ruin.

Dante's longing therefore connects private sorrow with civic judgment. His home no longer provides a secure moral home. The canto places beside bodily hunger another appetite: the desire for a community governed by justice rather than destructive rivalry.

## 7. Corso Donati: Unchecked Ambition Becomes Helpless Motion

> 82 «Or va», diss' el; «che quei che più n'ha colpa,  
> (“Now go,” he said; “for the one most to blame for it,)
>
> 83 vegg' ïo a coda d'una bestia tratto  
> (I see dragged at the tail of a beast)
>
> 84 inver' la valle ove mai non si scolpa.  
> (toward the valley where guilt is never purged.)
>
> 85 La bestia ad ogne passo va più ratto,  
> (The beast at every step goes faster,)
>
> 86 crescendo sempre, fin ch'ella il percuote,  
> (ever increasing, until it strikes him,)
>
> 87 e lascia il corpo vilmente disfatto.  
> (and leaves the body shamefully mangled.)

Forese predicts the end of his brother Corso Donati, a leading figure among Florence's Black Guelfs and a major participant in the factional conflicts that produced Dante's exile.

Corso died in 1308 while fleeing his enemies. Historical accounts differ in detail, but associate his death with an attempted escape on horseback and violent capture or killing. Dante transforms that history into a prophetic image of bodily destruction and damnation.

The beast's increasing speed gives ambition a visible shape. A man who sought to direct civic affairs is now dragged by a force he cannot control. Whether or not the beast is assigned a single allegorical identity, the reversal is clear: ungoverned desire ends in the loss of self-government.

> 91 Tu ti rimani omai; ché 'l tempo è caro  
> (Now remain behind; for time is precious)
>
> 92 in questo regno, sì ch'io perdo troppo  
> (in this kingdom, so that I lose too much)
>
> 93 venendo teco sì a paro a paro».  
> (by coming alongside you thus at an equal pace.”)

Forese must leave because his slower conversation with Dante delays his purification. His haste contrasts with Corso's acceleration. Both brothers move rapidly, but one moves willingly toward freedom, the other helplessly toward punishment.

Affection itself is not rejected. Forese's departure shows love accepting a proper order: friendship must not obstruct the soul's movement toward God.

## 8. The Forbidden Tree and the Education of Wanting

> 108 quasi bramosi fantolini e vani  
> (like eager and unsuccessful little children)
>
> 109 che pregano, e 'l pregato non risponde,  
> (who beg, and the one begged does not answer,)
>
> 110 ma, per fare esser ben la voglia acuta,  
> (but, to make their desire thoroughly keen,)
>
> 111 tien alto lor disio e nol nasconde.  
> (holds high what they desire and does not hide it.)

The souls stretch their hands toward fruit they cannot obtain. Dante compares them to children watching something desirable held beyond their reach.

The image captures appetite's dependence and vulnerability. It also depicts the difficult method of this purification: the desired object remains visible, so the souls cannot simply escape temptation by forgetting it.

The tree does not demonstrate that food is intrinsically wrong. Instead, it separates wanting from immediate satisfaction. The penitents learn to endure desire without assuming that desire itself confers a right to possession.

> 115 «Trapassate oltre sanza farvi presso:  
> (“Pass beyond without drawing near:)
>
> 116 legno è più sù che fu morso da Eva,  
> (higher up is a tree that was bitten by Eve,)
>
> 117 e questa pianta si levò da esso».  
> (and this plant sprang from it.”)

An unseen voice connects this tree with the tree of the knowledge of good and evil in Eden, located above them in the Earthly Paradise.

The connection expands the meaning of appetite. Eve's forbidden eating involved not merely food but disobedience: the refusal to accept a limit established by God. The terrace revisits that original pattern wherever desire treats its own impulse as sufficient authority.

The command to pass without approaching consequently becomes an exercise in restored obedience.

## 9. Centaurs and Gideon: Appetite Can Disable a Community

> 121 «Ricordivi», dicea, «d'i maladetti  
> (“Remember,” it said, “the accursed ones)
>
> 122 nei nuvoli formati, che, satolli,  
> (formed in the clouds, who, sated,)
>
> 123 Tesëo combatter co' doppi petti;  
> (fought Theseus with their double breasts;)

The first warning recalls the centaurs, creatures combining human and equine bodies. Their association with clouds comes from the myth of Ixion and the cloud-figure Nephele.

At the wedding feast of Pirithous, drunken centaurs attempted to seize women, provoking a battle with the Lapiths in which Theseus fought. Excess at a shared meal became sexual violence and communal disorder.

Their “double breasts” emphasize the mixed form through which Dante presents appetite's degradation of rational conduct. The warning concerns not bodily existence itself, but reason's surrender to uncontrolled impulse.

> 124 e de li Ebrei ch'al ber si mostrar molli,  
> (and the Hebrews who showed themselves soft in drinking,)
>
> 125 per che no i volle Gedeon compagni,  
> (for which Gideon did not want them as companions,)
>
> 126 quando inver' Madïan discese i colli».  
> (when toward Midian he descended the hills.”)

The second example comes from Judges 7. Gideon's soldiers are tested by how they drink, and only three hundred are retained for the campaign against Midian.

The biblical episode chiefly emphasizes that victory depends on God rather than overwhelming military numbers. Dante adapts the drinking test to the terrace's lesson, making undisciplined drinking an image of unfitness for demanding service.

Classical myth and Scripture thus converge: appetite is not merely a private concern. When it overwhelms self-command, it can make people dangerous or unreliable members of a community.

## 10. The Angel: Hunger Transformed, Not Extinguished

> 148 tal mi senti' un vento dar per mezza  
> (such a wind I felt strike across the middle of)
>
> 149 la fronte, e ben senti' mover la piuma,  
> (my forehead, and clearly felt the feather move,)
>
> 150 che fé sentir d'ambrosïa l'orezza.  
> (which made the breeze smell of ambrosia.)

At the terrace's exit, an angel directs the travelers upward. The movement of its wing across Dante's forehead marks the removal of another of the seven signs of sin inscribed there at Purgatory's entrance.

The sensation resembles a fragrant May breeze. “Ambrosia,” the food of the classical gods, brings the language of heavenly nourishment into a scene where earthly appetite has been disciplined.

The terrace ends not with an abolition of sensory delight, but with delight made gentle and liberating. Fragrance and touch no longer provoke grasping; they communicate grace.

> 151 E senti' dir: «Beati cui alluma  
> (And I heard it said: “Blessed are those whom it illumines)
>
> 152 tanto di grazia, che l'amor del gusto  
> (with so much grace that the love of taste)
>
> 153 nel petto lor troppo disir non fuma,  
> (does not kindle excessive desire in their breast,)
>
> 154 esurïendo sempre quanto è giusto!».  
> (always hungering as much as is right!”)

The concluding blessing adapts the Gospel beatitude concerning those who hunger and thirst for righteousness. Here the emphasis falls on grace and the right measure of appetite.

The image of desire smoking in the breast recalls an inward fire capable of clouding judgment. Grace does not remove the capacity to enjoy food; it prevents enjoyment from becoming excessive demand.

The final line states the canto's positive goal. The blessed still hunger. Their hunger has become proportionate, rightly directed, and compatible with freedom.

## Epilogue — Learning What and How to Desire

Canto XXIV moves from wasted bodies to a fragrant angelic breeze, examining desire through friendship, poetry, civic life, and forbidden fruit. Its contrasts are decisive: Florence loses goodness while penitents gain it; Corso is dragged toward ruin while Forese hastens toward freedom; appetite demands possession while the faithful poet listens.

Purification does not make the person incapable of wanting. It teaches the person how to want rightly. The canto's destination is therefore neither starvation nor emotional emptiness, but a freedom in which hunger, love, and expression follow their proper good.
