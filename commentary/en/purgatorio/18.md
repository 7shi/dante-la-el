# Love, Freedom, and the Discipline of Desire in *Purgatorio* XVIII

Canto XVIII takes place on the fourth terrace of Purgatory, where souls are purified of sloth, or *acedia*. Dante understands this sin not simply as laziness, but as insufficient eagerness to pursue the good. Before the penitents appear, Virgil explains how love arises and why human beings remain responsible for what they love.

The canto therefore joins philosophical argument to dramatic action. Its first half examines desire and freedom; its second shows souls learning to love with the energy they lacked in life.

## 1. A Teacher Who Encourages Questions

> 7 Ma quel padre verace, che s'accorse  
> (But that true father, who perceived)
>
> 8 del timido voler che non s'apriva,  
> (the timid wish that did not reveal itself,)
>
> 9 parlando, di parlare ardir mi porse.  
> (by speaking, gave me courage to speak.)

Virgil has finished the explanation begun in the preceding canto, where he identified love as the source of both virtue and sin. Dante understands the argument but still wants to know what love itself is. He hesitates because he fears becoming burdensome.

Virgil recognizes the unspoken question. His fatherliness consists not in demanding passive acceptance, but in encouraging understanding. The exchange presents education as an act of rightly directed love: the teacher cares for the learner's development, while the learner's desire for knowledge deserves encouragement rather than suppression.

This matters on the terrace of sloth. Dante's renewed intellectual thirst already contrasts with spiritual indifference. Wanting to understand the good is part of learning to pursue it.

## 2. How Attraction Becomes Love

> 22 Vostra apprensiva da esser verace  
> (Your apprehensive faculty, from a real being,)
>
> 23 tragge intenzione, e dentro a voi la spiega,  
> (draws a representation, and unfolds it within you,)
>
> 24 sì che l'animo ad essa volger face;  
> (so that it makes the mind turn toward it;)

Virgil describes a process grounded in medieval Aristotelian psychology. The faculty of perception apprehends something real and presents an inward representation of it. The term *intenzione* here means a mental representation, not an intention in the modern sense of a plan.

Love therefore involves an encounter between the world and the perceiving soul. An object does not simply enter us unchanged: it becomes something apprehended, imagined, and experienced as attractive.

> 25 e se, rivolto, inver' di lei si piega,  
> (and if, having turned, it inclines toward it,)
>
> 26 quel piegare è amor, quell' è natura  
> (that inclination is love, that is nature)
>
> 27 che per piacer di novo in voi si lega.  
> (which through pleasure binds itself anew within you.)

The decisive movement is an inward inclination toward what has been perceived. This is love in its broad Dantean sense: not merely romantic affection, but the attraction underlying human action.

That breadth is essential. Love can animate charity, ambition, friendship, greed, or the pursuit of knowledge. Calling an impulse love does not yet establish its moral worth. It identifies the soul's movement toward something that appears desirable.

## 3. Desire Seeks Rest—but Not Every Love Is Good

> 31 così l'animo preso entra in disire,  
> (so the captivated soul enters into desire,)
>
> 32 ch'è moto spiritale, e mai non posa  
> (which is a spiritual movement, and never rests)
>
> 33 fin che la cosa amata il fa gioire.  
> (until the beloved thing makes it rejoice.)

Immediately before these lines, Virgil compares desire to fire rising toward its natural place. In medieval cosmology, the elements possessed characteristic tendencies of motion: fire rose toward the fiery region above the air.

The analogy presents desire as purposeful movement. Once attracted, the soul seeks enjoyment of the beloved object. Its restlessness is not inherently sinful; it belongs to the structure of love.

Yet the comparison also creates a problem. If desire follows naturally from attraction, does the soul possess any more freedom than a flame?

> 37 però che forse appar la sua matera  
> (because perhaps its matter appears)
>
> 38 sempre esser buona, ma non ciascun segno  
> (always to be good, but not every impression)
>
> 39 è buono, ancor che buona sia la cera».  
> (is good, although the wax may be good.”)

The wax and its impression distinguish the natural capacity for love from the particular form that love takes. Good wax can receive a bad imprint; likewise, the goodness of the capacity to love does not guarantee the goodness of every actual attachment.

Dante thus rejects the claim that love automatically justifies itself. The moral question concerns what love seeks and how it seeks it. This challenges any romantic or philosophical doctrine that treats passion as innocent merely because it feels natural or irresistible.

## 4. Natural Impulses and the Limits of Reason

> 46 Ed elli a me: «Quanto ragion qui vede,  
> (And he to me: “As much as reason sees here,)
>
> 47 dir ti poss' io; da indi in là t'aspetta  
> (I can tell you; beyond that, look)
>
> 48 pur a Beatrice, ch'è opra di fede.  
> (only to Beatrice, for it is a matter of faith.)

Dante draws the apparent consequence of Virgil's account: if external things awaken love, and love drives action, how can anyone deserve praise or blame?

Virgil answers while marking the boundary of his authority. He represents the achievements of human reason, especially classical philosophy. Beatrice will guide Dante into truths requiring revelation and a fuller theological understanding.

The distinction does not make reason useless. Virgil can establish meaningful human freedom. But he cannot independently explain every aspect of the soul's origin or its relation to divine grace.

> 58 che sono in voi sì come studio in ape  
> (which are in you just as diligence in a bee)
>
> 59 di far lo mele; e questa prima voglia  
> (to make honey; and this first desire)
>
> 60 merto di lode o di biasmo non cape.  
> (admits no desert of praise or blame.)

The opening phrase refers back to humanity's first principles of knowledge and its attraction toward primary objects of desire. These belong to our created constitution, as honey-making belongs to the bee.

The comparison distinguishes what is given by nature from what becomes morally accountable through choice. The basic desire for good or happiness is not itself a moral achievement or failure.

Virgil is not declaring every developed appetite innocent. He is identifying a foundational inclination that precedes deliberate judgment. Responsibility arises in what human beings subsequently approve, cultivate, or restrain.

## 5. Free Will Guards the Threshold

> 61 Or perché a questa ogn' altra si raccoglia,  
> (Now, so that every other desire may conform to this one,)
>
> 62 innata v'è la virtù che consiglia,  
> (innate in you is the faculty that deliberates,)
>
> 63 e de l'assenso de' tener la soglia.  
> (and it must guard the threshold of assent.)

The image of a threshold gives abstract psychology a concrete form. An attraction may approach the mind, but it does not thereby possess the right to govern conduct. Deliberative judgment must determine whether to admit it.

This is the canto's central moral distinction: experiencing an inclination and consenting to it are not identical. Freedom need not mean choosing every feeling that first arises. It means possessing the capacity to judge and regulate one's response.

The faculty is innate, but its proper exercise requires vigilance. A door exists to be guarded, not merely to stand open.

> 70 Onde, poniam che di necessitate  
> (Therefore, let us suppose that of necessity)
>
> 71 surga ogne amor che dentro a voi s'accende,  
> (every love arises that is kindled within you,)
>
> 72 di ritenerlo è in voi la podestate.  
> (within you is the power to restrain it.)

Virgil grants the strongest version of Dante's objection for the sake of argument. Even supposing that love arises necessarily, it does not follow that surrender to it is necessary.

This preserves the basis of ethics. Without some freedom regarding assent and action, praise, blame, repentance, and moral instruction would lose their meaning. Purgatory itself depends on that freedom: its souls are not simply being punished for mechanical reactions, but learning to direct their loves rightly.

Virgil subsequently identifies this power with what Beatrice calls free will, linking his philosophical explanation to Dante's future theological education.

## 6. From Night-Time Drowsiness to Holy Urgency

> 76 La luna, quasi a mezza notte tarda,  
> (The moon, late by almost midnight,)
>
> 77 facea le stelle a noi parer più rade,  
> (made the stars appear fewer to us,)
>
> 78 fatta com' un secchion che tuttor arda;  
> (shaped like a bucket that glows all over;)

The late-rising, waning moon establishes the nocturnal setting. Its brightness makes the stars seem less numerous, while the glowing bucket comparison gives the celestial scene a familiar, earthly shape.

After the intellectual effort of the discussion, Dante becomes drowsy. This is ordinary bodily fatigue, not necessarily a moral failure. Nevertheless, its placement on the terrace of sloth creates a pointed contrast with what follows: a company of souls rushes toward the poets.

> 94 cotal per quel giron suo passo falca,  
> (so around that terrace it curves its stride,)
>
> 95 per quel ch'io vidi di color, venendo,  
> (from what I saw of those approaching,)
>
> 96 cui buon volere e giusto amor cavalca.  
> (whom good will and righteous love ride.)

The souls are imagined as mounts ridden by good will and rightly ordered love. Love still supplies the motive force, just as Virgil's argument maintained, but now its direction is good.

Their running constitutes a corrective *contrapasso*. Those who once delayed in doing good must now move with unremitting eagerness. Their punishment is also a cure, training desire through repeated action.

Dante introduces the runners through a comparison with nocturnal worshippers of Bacchus near the Theban rivers Ismenus and Asopus. Pagan religious frenzy becomes a literary counterpart to Christian zeal. The crucial difference is the force directing the movement: these souls are propelled by righteous love rather than uncontrolled ecstasy.

## 7. Mary and Caesar: Two Images of Prompt Action

> 100 «Maria corse con fretta a la montagna;  
> (“Mary ran with haste to the mountain country;)
>
> 101 e Cesare, per soggiogare Ilerda,  
> (and Caesar, to subdue Ilerda,)
>
> 102 punse Marsilia e poi corse in Ispagna».  
> (struck at Marseille and then ran into Spain.”)

The first runners proclaim examples of energetic action. Mary's journey recalls Luke 1:39, where she goes with haste into the hill country to visit Elizabeth. Her promptness embodies active charity: she does not delay in responding to another person.

Caesar supplies the classical counterpart. During the civil war in 49 BCE, he attacked Marseille and proceeded to Spain, where he campaigned against Pompeian forces near Ilerda, modern Lleida.

The pairing does not equate military conquest with Christian charity in every respect. Both figures exemplify the particular virtue being taught here: readiness to act. Dante's admiration for Roman imperial history allows a pagan commander to stand beside a biblical figure as a lesson against sluggishness.

> 103 «Ratto, ratto, che 'l tempo non si perda  
> (“Quickly, quickly, so that time may not be lost)
>
> 104 per poco amor», gridavan li altri appresso,  
> (through too little love,” cried the others behind,)
>
> 105 «che studio di ben far grazia rinverda».  
> (“so that zeal in doing good may make grace green again.”)

The runners name their former defect precisely: *poco amor*, too little love. Sloth is not an excess of rest considered in isolation; it is a deficiency of commitment to the good.

The image of grace becoming green again suggests renewed vegetation. Spiritual life must flourish rather than merely survive in a dormant state. Their cries turn Virgil's account of love into a practical demand: understanding the good must become energetic pursuit of it.

## 8. The Abbot of San Zeno and Corrupted Authority

> 118 Io fui abate in San Zeno a Verona  
> (I was abbot at San Zeno in Verona)
>
> 119 sotto lo 'mperio del buon Barbarossa,  
> (under the rule of the good Barbarossa,)
>
> 120 di cui dolente ancor Milan ragiona.  
> (of whom Milan still speaks sorrowfully.)

One runner identifies himself as a former abbot of the Benedictine monastery of San Zeno in Verona. He is traditionally identified as Gherardo, though the historical identification is uncertain.

Frederick I Barbarossa places him in the twelfth century. Milan's continuing sorrow recalls the emperor's destruction of the city in 1162. Calling Barbarossa good while acknowledging Milan's suffering reflects Dante's complex imperial sympathies: approval of imperial authority does not erase the pain associated with its exercise.

The abbot does not stop to converse. His refusal to interrupt the run demonstrates that the terrace's corrective discipline takes priority even over ordinary courtesy.

> 124 perché suo figlio, mal del corpo intero,  
> (because his son, unsound in body,)
>
> 125 e de la mente peggio, e che mal nacque,  
> (and worse in mind, and who was born illegitimate,)
>
> 126 ha posto in loco di suo pastor vero».  
> (he has put in the place of its true shepherd.”)

The unnamed father is Alberto I della Scala, lord of Verona, who installed his illegitimate son Giuseppe as abbot of San Zeno. The surrounding lines predict Alberto's approaching death and reckoning. Since Dante sets the journey in 1300 and Alberto died in 1301, the speech functions as a prophecy within the poem's chronology.

Its central charge is the subordination of religious office to dynastic power. A monastery needs a true shepherd, not a ruler's beneficiary.

The attack also employs medieval prejudices about bodily impairment and illegitimate birth; neither condition is itself evidence of moral unfitness. The enduring institutional criticism concerns nepotism and the replacement of spiritual responsibility with family advantage. On this terrace, the episode extends the question of proper commitment from individual conduct to ecclesiastical government.

## 9. Those Who Abandoned the Journey

> 133 Di retro a tutti dicean: «Prima fue  
> (Behind all the others they were saying: “Earlier was)
>
> 134 morta la gente a cui il mar s'aperse,  
> (dead the people for whom the sea opened,)
>
> 135 che vedesse Iordan le rede sue.  
> (than Jordan saw its heirs.)

At the rear of the procession, two souls proclaim examples of failed perseverance.

The first recalls the Israelites who crossed the Red Sea but died in the wilderness before their descendants entered the Promised Land. The background includes the refusal to advance into Canaan and the consequent sentence of wandering in Numbers 14.

Dante emphasizes their failure to complete a divinely directed journey. Liberation was not enough: they also needed trust and perseverance. The example mirrors the pilgrim's own situation. Leaving captivity begins the spiritual life, but does not complete it.

> 136 E quella che l'affanno non sofferse  
> (And that people which did not endure the hardship)
>
> 137 fino a la fine col figlio d'Anchise,  
> (to the end with the son of Anchises,)
>
> 138 sé stessa a vita sanza gloria offerse».  
> (gave itself over to a life without glory.”)

The second example comes from Book V of Virgil's *Aeneid*. Some of Aeneas's companions remain in Sicily instead of continuing the difficult voyage toward Italy. Aeneas, the son of Anchises, carries a destiny that will eventually lead to Rome.

Here the failure is defined by abandoning the effort before its proper conclusion. The biblical and classical examples form a pair: Israel falls short of the Promised Land, while some Trojans relinquish participation in the journey toward Rome.

Both contrast with Mary and Caesar. Prompt beginnings matter, but so does endurance. Right love must persist when the good becomes difficult.

## 10. Thought Passes into Dream

> 142 del qual più altri nacquero e diversi;  
> (from which many other and different thoughts were born;)
>
> 143 e tanto d'uno in altro vaneggiai,  
> (and so far I wandered from one to another,)
>
> 144 che li occhi per vaghezza ricopersi,  
> (that through wandering thought I closed my eyes,)
>
> 145 e 'l pensamento in sogno trasmutai.  
> (and I transformed thought into a dream.)

Once the runners disappear, Dante's thoughts proliferate and lose their deliberate direction. Wakeful reflection becomes dreaming.

The ending should not be reduced to an accusation that Dante has succumbed to sinful sloth. He remains a living person subject to fatigue, and sleep has a legitimate place in the journey. Nevertheless, the movement from disciplined reasoning to mental wandering subtly recalls the canto's concern with attention and self-government.

It also prepares the next canto's dream, in which attraction and judgment will take visible, symbolic form. What Virgil has explained philosophically will become something Dante must experience imaginatively.

## Epilogue — Love Must Be Judged, Then Put into Motion

*Purgatorio* XVIII argues that love is the energy of human action, but not its automatic justification. Attraction may arise before deliberate choice; moral responsibility lies in the capacity to examine it, withhold assent, and direct conduct toward the good.

The running penitents reveal the complementary danger. It is not enough to refuse wrongful love: one must also pursue rightful love with sufficient zeal. Through philosophy, historical examples, and the spectacle of ceaseless movement, the canto presents freedom as both discernment and perseverance—the ability to choose the good and keep moving toward it.
