# Purgatorio XIV: Envy, a Corrupted Landscape, and the Call of Heaven

Canto XIV takes place on the second terrace of Purgatory, where souls are purified of envy. Their eyes are sewn shut: those who looked resentfully at another person's happiness must now learn to live without that hostile gaze. Dante, still alive and able to see, encounters Guido del Duca and Rinieri da Calboli. Their conversation expands into a condemnation of Tuscany and Romagna before returning to the spiritual choice underlying their corruption: whether to seek happiness in earthly competition or in goods that everyone can share.

## The Living Visitor and the Education of Sight

> 1 «Chi è costui che 'l nostro monte cerchia  
> (“Who is this who circles our mountain)
>
> 2 prima che morte li abbia dato il volo,  
> (before death has given him flight,)
>
> 3 e apre li occhi a sua voglia e coverchia?».  
> (and opens his eyes at will and closes them?”.)

The speakers immediately notice what distinguishes Dante from themselves: he can open and close his eyes freely. On this terrace, such an ordinary action becomes evidence of an extraordinary privilege.

The punishment belongs to Dante's system of *contrapasso*, in which a bodily condition exposes and corrects a spiritual disorder. Envy involves looking at someone else's good as though it diminished one's own. The souls' enforced blindness interrupts that habit and makes them dependent on others.

The reference to death giving the soul “flight” also establishes Dante's exceptional position. He has reached the mountain before the normal separation of soul and body. Yet the envious souls do not resent his privilege. They approach him courteously and ask for consolation: their response already shows the charity replacing their former vice.

## The Unnamed River and the Unimportant Name

> 16 E io: «Per mezza Toscana si spazia  
> (And I: “Through the midst of Tuscany stretches)
>
> 17 un fiumicel che nasce in Falterona,  
> (a little river that rises on Falterona,)
>
> 18 e cento miglia di corso nol sazia.  
> (and a hundred miles of course do not satisfy it.)

Dante identifies his homeland indirectly through the Arno, which rises on Monte Falterona and passes through Tuscany toward the sea. The river supplies the geographical structure for the denunciation that follows: its course will become a journey through successive forms of human degradation.

Calling it a “little river” initially makes this a modest description. Soon, however, the river valley will carry enormous moral significance. A familiar landscape becomes a map of corrupted communities.

> 20 dirvi ch'i' sia, saria parlare indarno,  
> (to tell you who I am would be to speak in vain,)
>
> 21 ché 'l nome mio ancor molto non suona».  
> (for my name does not yet resound very far”.)

Dante withholds his own name because it is not yet widely known. The qualification “yet” distinguishes the pilgrim within the story, whose journey occurs in 1300, from the poet writing afterward.

His refusal also prepares a small moral correction. When Dante later requests the spirits' names, Guido observes that he wants others to provide what he himself has withheld. Even a privileged pilgrim must learn reciprocity.

The omission of the Arno's name receives a harsher interpretation. Rinieri asks whether Dante has avoided it as one avoids naming something horrible. Guido turns that suggestion into an indictment of the entire valley.

## Virtue as an Enemy: The Valley of Circe

> 37 vertù così per nimica si fuga  
> (virtue is thus driven away as an enemy)
>
> 38 da tutti come biscia, o per sventura  
> (by everyone, like a snake, either through the misfortune)
>
> 39 del luogo, o per mal uso che li fruga:  
> (of the place, or through evil custom that goads them:)

The valley's deepest corruption is not simply that its inhabitants commit particular wrongs. Their moral perception has become inverted: they treat virtue itself as something dangerous or repulsive.

Guido offers two possible explanations, an unfortunate influence associated with the place or the pressure of entrenched bad habits. The alternatives reflect medieval interest in the relationship between environment and character. But neither removes the canto's emphasis on responsibility. Its final speech will insist that human beings continually choose earthly bait over heavenly beauty.

> 40 ond' hanno sì mutata lor natura  
> (therefore they have so changed their nature,)
>
> 41 li abitator de la misera valle,  
> (the inhabitants of the wretched valley,)
>
> 42 che par che Circe li avesse in pastura.  
> (that it seems Circe had them at pasture.)

Circe, the classical enchantress who transformed human beings into animals, supplies the governing image. The inhabitants remain physically human, but their conduct has reduced them to beasts.

The transformation is ethical rather than literal. Reason should enable people to govern appetite, violence, and deception; habitual vice instead makes them resemble animals ruled by those impulses. Circe's imagined pasture turns Tuscany into a grotesque enclosure in which human dignity has been surrendered.

## The Arno's Bestiary: Pigs, Curs, Wolves, and Foxes

> 43 Tra brutti porci, più degni di galle  
> (Among filthy pigs, more deserving of acorns)
>
> 44 che d'altro cibo fatto in uman uso,  
> (than of other food prepared for human use,)
>
> 45 dirizza prima il suo povero calle.  
> (it first directs its impoverished course.)

The pigs represent the inhabitants of the Casentino, the upper Arno valley. Their animal food marks their estrangement from properly human life. The accusation is one of degraded appetite and brutishness, although the image should not be reduced to a rigid correspondence with a single deadly sin.

The river's “poor” course suggests that it passes through a landscape impoverished morally as well as contemptible in Guido's judgment.

> 46 Botoli trova poi, venendo giuso,  
> (Then it finds curs, coming downstream,)
>
> 47 ringhiosi più che non chiede lor possa,  
> (more snarling than their strength warrants,)
>
> 48 e da lor disdegnosa torce il muso.  
> (and disdainfully turns its muzzle away from them.)

The curs are the people of Arezzo, mocked for aggression disproportionate to their power. Dante turns geography into satire: the Arno's bend away from Arezzo becomes a gesture of disgust.

The river itself acquires a “muzzle,” extending the animal imagery beyond the inhabitants. The whole landscape seems drawn into this moral bestiary.

> 49 Vassi caggendo; e quant' ella più 'ngrossa,  
> (It goes descending; and the more it swells,)
>
> 50 tanto più trova di can farsi lupi  
> (the more it finds dogs becoming wolves,)
>
> 51 la maladetta e sventurata fossa.  
> (the accursed and ill-fated ditch.)

The wolves are the Florentines. Their image conveys predatory greed and violence: Florence is not merely noisy and threatening, like Arezzo, but capable of devouring others.

As the river grows, the moral danger grows with it. Physical descent becomes the framework for an apparent progression toward more destructive forms of vice.

> 53 trova le volpi sì piene di froda,  
> (it finds the foxes so full of fraud,)
>
> 54 che non temono ingegno che le occùpi.  
> (that they fear no cunning that might catch them.)

The foxes are the Pisans, associated here with deception so accomplished that they believe themselves beyond capture.

This is partisan political invective, not impartial regional description. Nevertheless, its larger ethical claim exceeds local hostility: communities lose their humanity when appetite, aggression, predation, and fraud become their governing habits.

## Fulcieri da Calboli: Political Power as a Hunt

> 58 Io veggio tuo nepote che diventa  
> (I see your nephew becoming)
>
> 59 cacciator di quei lupi in su la riva  
> (a hunter of those wolves upon the bank)
>
> 60 del fiero fiume, e tutti li sgomenta.  
> (of the savage river, and he terrifies them all.)

Guido now addresses Rinieri directly. The nephew is Fulcieri da Calboli, who became podestà of Florence in 1303 and was associated with the violent repression of opponents of the Black Guelph regime.

For the pilgrim in 1300, this is prophecy; for the poet composing the canto later, it refers to events already known. Such retrospective prophecy allows Dante to place contemporary political suffering within the moral perspective of the afterlife.

The Florentine wolves now become hunted animals. Yet their hunter is no agent of justice: the imagery implicates rulers and victims in a political world organized around predation.

> 61 Vende la carne loro essendo viva;  
> (He sells their flesh while it is alive;)
>
> 62 poscia li ancide come antica belva;  
> (then he kills them like an old beast;)
>
> 63 molti di vita e sé di pregio priva.  
> (he deprives many of life and himself of honor.)

The language combines hunting, commerce, and slaughter. Political authority becomes a means of treating people as marketable flesh.

The decisive judgment falls in the last line. Fulcieri can destroy his victims' lives, but his conduct simultaneously destroys his own honor. Apparent worldly success is therefore a deeper defeat.

Rinieri's distressed reaction makes the prophecy personal. A family's inherited prestige offers no protection against the moral choices of its descendants.

## Guido's Confession: Why Envy Cannot Share

> 82 Fu il sangue mio d'invidia sì rïarso,  
> (My blood was so scorched by envy,)
>
> 83 che se veduto avesse uom farsi lieto,  
> (that if it had seen a man becoming happy,)
>
> 84 visto m'avresti di livore sparso.  
> (you would have seen me suffused with lividness.)
>
> 85 Di mia semente cotal paglia mieto;  
> (From my own seed I reap such straw;)

Having condemned others, Guido identifies his own sin. Another person's happiness once produced visible distress in him. Envy is therefore more than wanting what someone else possesses: it makes the good of another painful.

His agricultural metaphor acknowledges responsibility. His present suffering grows from what he himself planted. The voice denouncing public corruption is not claiming personal innocence; it speaks from penitence.

> 86 o gente umana, perché poni 'l core  
> (O human race, why do you set your heart)
>
> 87 là 'v' è mestier di consorte divieto?  
> (where exclusion of a fellow sharer is necessary?)

These lines contain the canto's central diagnosis. Human beings attach themselves to goods whose possession involves rivalry: wealth, office, prestige, and power. If happiness depends on having more than someone else, another person's success naturally becomes threatening.

Guido points toward a different order of goods, developed more explicitly in the following canto. Spiritual good does not diminish through being shared. Love and participation in divine goodness can unite people rather than divide them.

The political disorder of the Arno valley thus has a spiritual root: a mistaken understanding of what happiness consists in.

## Romagna's Thorns and the Memory of Courtesy

> 94 ché dentro a questi termini è ripieno  
> (for within these boundaries it is filled)
>
> 95 di venenosi sterpi, sì che tardi  
> (with poisonous thickets, so that slowly)
>
> 96 per coltivare omai verrebber meno.  
> (would they now diminish through cultivation.)

Guido turns from Tuscany to Romagna, bounded in his account by the Po, the Apennines, the Adriatic, and the Reno. The earlier metaphor of sowing and reaping becomes a larger image of neglected cultivation.

The region is overgrown with poisonous vegetation. Corruption has become so deeply rooted that reform would require prolonged labor. Noble families, which should have preserved public virtue, have instead helped spread the disorder.

Against this present Guido recalls figures associated with an older culture of generosity and honorable conduct, including Lizio, Arrigo Mainardi, Pier Traversaro, and Guido di Carpigna.

> 109 le donne e ' cavalier, li affanni e li agi  
> (the ladies and the knights, the hardships and the ease)
>
> 110 che ne 'nvogliava amore e cortesia  
> (to which love and courtesy made us aspire)
>
> 111 là dove i cuor son fatti sì malvagi.  
> (there where hearts have become so wicked.)

This is the emotional center of Guido's nostalgia. “Courtesy” means more than polished manners: it includes generosity, hospitality, honorable companionship, and a refusal to make self-interest the sole measure of action.

The remembrance is idealized, not a neutral historical survey. Its purpose is to set a social ideal against the degraded present.

It also reveals Guido's transformation. The man who once suffered at other people's happiness now grieves for the disappearance of other people's excellence. His sorrow has moved from resentful comparison toward love of a common good.

## When Extinction Preserves a Family's Honor

> 115 Ben fa Bagnacaval, che non rifiglia;  
> (Bagnacavallo does well, for it produces no more offspring;)
>
> 116 e mal fa Castrocaro, e peggio Conio,  
> (and Castrocaro does badly, and Conio worse,)
>
> 117 che di figliar tai conti più s'impiglia.  
> (which troubles itself further with begetting such counts.)

Guido's lament becomes bitterly paradoxical. Normally, the continuation of a distinguished family would promise stability and preserve its achievements. Here, failing to produce descendants becomes a blessing.

The places stand for their ruling families. The point is not a general condemnation of parenthood, but an attack on hereditary prestige unsupported by virtue. A title can be transmitted; moral worth cannot.

> 121 O Ugolin de' Fantolin, sicuro  
> (O Ugolino de' Fantolini, secure)
>
> 122 è 'l nome tuo, da che più non s'aspetta  
> (is your name, since no longer is anyone expected)
>
> 123 chi far lo possa, tralignando, scuro.  
> (who could make it dark by degenerating.)

Ugolino's reputation is safe because no further descendant is expected to disgrace it. Family extinction becomes the only reliable protection against degeneration.

The passage reverses the worldly desire to secure immortality through lineage. The canto repeatedly questions earthly mechanisms for preserving value: political office becomes predation, fame becomes vulnerable, and inheritance cannot guarantee goodness.

## Cain and Aglauros: Envy Heard as Thunder

> 133 ‘Anciderammi qualunque m'apprende’;  
> (‘Whoever finds me will kill me’;)

After Dante and Virgil leave the souls behind, an unidentified voice suddenly delivers Cain's words from Genesis 4:14.

Cain murdered Abel after God favored Abel's offering. His story shows envy becoming fratricide: another person's good appears intolerable, so the envious person attacks the person who possesses it. The cry expresses Cain's resulting fear and alienation.

Its thunderous delivery gives the warning an almost physical force. On a terrace whose penitents cannot see, moral examples arrive through hearing.

> 139 «Io sono Aglauro che divenni sasso»;  
> (“I am Aglauros, who became stone”;)

The second voice belongs to Aglauros, whose story appears in Ovid's *Metamorphoses*. Envious of her sister Herse, whom Mercury desired, she obstructed the god and was transformed into stone.

Her punishment makes envy's inward rigidity visible. Unable to welcome another's happiness, she becomes incapable of movement altogether.

Cain and Aglauros bring biblical and classical traditions together. One destroys his brother; the other attempts to block her sister's good fortune. Both violate bonds that should have encouraged affection rather than rivalry.

## The Bit, the Hook, and the Upward Gaze

> 143 ed el mi disse: «Quel fu 'l duro camo  
> (and he said to me: “That was the hard bit)
>
> 144 che dovria l'uom tener dentro a sua meta.  
> (that should keep a person within due bounds.)

Virgil explains that the terrifying examples are a restraining “bit,” like the metal instrument used to control a horse. Earlier examples of charity on this terrace encouraged souls toward love; these examples of envy's consequences restrain them from evil.

The animal metaphor now applies to moral discipline. Human beings must learn to govern impulses that otherwise reduce them to the condition described in Guido's bestiary.

> 145 Ma voi prendete l'esca, sì che l'amo  
> (But you take the bait, so that the hook)
>
> 146 de l'antico avversaro a sé vi tira;  
> (of the ancient adversary draws you to itself;)
>
> 147 e però poco val freno o richiamo.  
> (and therefore restraint or summons is of little use.)

The “ancient adversary” is Satan. Earthly attractions operate as bait: they seem desirable while concealing the means of captivity.

Yet Virgil's accusation preserves human responsibility. People take the bait. Neither warnings nor invitations can accomplish their purpose while desire remains committed to the wrong objects.

> 148 Chiamavi 'l cielo e 'ntorno vi si gira,  
> (Heaven calls you and revolves around you,)
>
> 149 mostrandovi le sue bellezze etterne,  
> (showing you its eternal beauties,)
>
> 150 e l'occhio vostro pur a terra mira;  
> (and your eye still looks only toward the earth;)
>
> 151 onde vi batte chi tutto discerne».  
> (therefore he who discerns everything chastises you”.)

The canto ends by returning to sight. Its opening question concerned Dante's freely moving eyes; its conclusion asks where human beings choose to direct their gaze.

Within Dante's medieval cosmology, the revolving heavens display an order whose beauty should draw the mind toward God. Humanity instead looks downward, fastening desire on the competitive goods Guido identified.

The final chastisement belongs to the God who sees without distortion. On this mountain, punishment is corrective: it works to heal the vision that envy has corrupted.

## Epilogue — From Rivalry to Shared Good

Canto XIV connects a private vice with the breakdown of public life. Envy turns another person's happiness into an injury; societies governed by that logic become places of predation, deception, and inherited corruption.

Yet Guido's confession shows that the pattern can change. His former resentment has become grief for lost virtue, and his grief points toward goods that need not exclude a fellow sharer. The canto's ultimate movement is therefore away from competition for earthly possession and toward a restored vision of happiness as something fulfilled, rather than diminished, through love.
