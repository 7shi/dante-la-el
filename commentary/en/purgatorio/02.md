# Dawn, Deliverance, and Delay: The Allegory of *Purgatorio*, Canto II

Canto II takes place on the shore of Mount Purgatory, where Dante and Virgil have arrived after leaving Hell. An angel brings a boatload of souls destined for salvation; among them Dante recognizes his friend Casella, whose singing briefly captivates everyone. Then Cato, the mountain's guardian, interrupts their pleasure and sends them toward the ascent.

The episode brings together the canto's governing contrasts: divine purpose and human hesitation, spiritual liberation and earthly attachment, the power of art and the necessity of moving beyond it.

## 1. Dawn: A New Life That Must Begin

> 7 sì che le bianche e le vermiglie guance,  
> (so that the white and crimson cheeks,)
>
> 8 là dov' i' era, de la bella Aurora  
> (there where I was, of beautiful Aurora)
>
> 9 per troppa etate divenivan rance.  
> (through advancing age were turning orange.)

The canto's opening astronomy locates the sunrise within Dante's imagined geography. Jerusalem and Mount Purgatory occupy opposite sides of the globe: sunset at Jerusalem corresponds to sunrise at the mountain. Night rises near the Ganges carrying Libra, the constellation opposite the springtime sun.

This elaborate coordination makes the shore part of a universal order. The pilgrims have escaped Hell's darkness into a world governed by light, time, and the possibility of change.

Aurora, the classical personification of dawn, has cheeks that change from white and red to orange as morning advances. Her “age” is only the passage of minutes, but that passage matters. In Purgatory, unlike Hell, time can be used for progress—and can also be wasted.

> 10 Noi eravam lunghesso mare ancora,  
> (We were still alongside the sea,)
>
> 11 come gente che pensa a suo cammino,  
> (like people who think about their journey,)
>
> 12 che va col cuore e col corpo dimora.  
> (who go in their hearts and remain with their bodies.)

Dante and Virgil already desire to move, yet physically remain where they are. The separation between inward intention and outward action introduces the canto's central problem.

Their hesitation is understandable: they do not know the way. Nevertheless, wanting to advance is not the same as advancing. By the canto's end, a similar suspension will affect the entire company.

## 2. The Angel: A Power Beyond Human Instruments

> 28 gridò: «Fa, fa che le ginocchia cali.  
> (he cried: “Make haste, make haste to lower your knees.)
>
> 29 Ecco l'angel di Dio: piega le mani;  
> (Behold the angel of God: fold your hands;)
>
> 30 omai vedrai di sì fatti officiali.  
> (from now on you will see ministers of this kind.)

A distant light approaches across the sea. As its shape becomes recognizable, Virgil commands Dante to kneel and join his hands in reverence.

These are “officials” of a divinely ordered realm. In Hell, supernatural guardians commonly obstruct, threaten, or punish; here, angels carry out the work of salvation. Virgil recognizes their authority, but he does not command it.

Allegorically, the encounter establishes the relation between human reason and divine assistance. Virgil can identify what deserves reverence and instruct Dante in the proper response. Yet the power that brings souls to Purgatory comes from beyond him.

> 31 Vedi che sdegna li argomenti umani,  
> (See how he disdains human instruments,)
>
> 32 sì che remo non vuol, né altro velo  
> (so that he wants no oar, nor any other sail)
>
> 33 che l'ali sue, tra liti sì lontani.  
> (than his wings, between shores so far apart.)

The angel needs neither oars nor a manufactured sail. His wings suffice for a journey beyond ordinary human navigation.

The point is not that human skill is intrinsically worthless, but that salvation cannot be achieved by technical ingenuity alone. The angel's effortless passage also contrasts with the disastrous sea voyage of Ulysses in *Inferno* XXVI. Ulysses reaches sight of the mountain through an unauthorized pursuit of knowledge; these souls reach it through divine provision.

## 3. The Boat and the Exodus: Salvation as a Collective Journey

> 41 con un vasello snelletto e leggero,  
> (with a little vessel, swift and light,)
>
> 42 tanto che l'acqua nulla ne 'nghiottiva.  
> (so much so that the water swallowed none of it.)

The boat scarcely enters the water. Its extraordinary lightness suits both its angelic propulsion and its passengers, who are souls without mortal bodies.

The scene reverses the infernal crossing under Charon. There, a boat carries the damned toward punishment amid terror and curses. Here, the vessel brings the saved toward purification, and its passengers sing together.

> 46 ‘In exitu Isräel de Aegypto’  
> (‘When Israel went out of Egypt’)
>
> 47 cantavan tutti insieme ad una voce  
> (they were all singing together with one voice)
>
> 48 con quanto di quel salmo è poscia scripto.  
> (with all that is written afterward in that psalm.)

The souls sing the Exodus psalm, numbered 113 in the Vulgate; its opening corresponds to Psalm 114 in most modern Bibles.

The historical departure of Israel from Egypt supplies the canto with an explicit allegorical pattern. Christian interpretation understood that departure as an image of liberation from sin, as well as a figure of the soul's passage toward its eternal home. The sea crossing therefore means more than transportation: it marks release from bondage.

Their singing “with one voice” is equally important. Purgatory begins with a community united in worship. Its inhabitants must be purified individually, but their hope and destination are shared.

## 4. Fellow Pilgrims: The Limits of Knowledge

> 61 E Virgilio rispuose: «Voi credete  
> (And Virgil replied: “You believe)
>
> 62 forse che siamo esperti d'esto loco;  
> (perhaps that we are familiar with this place;)
>
> 63 ma noi siam peregrin come voi siete.  
> (but we are pilgrims just as you are.)

The arriving souls ask Dante and Virgil for directions. Virgil answers by acknowledging their common ignorance.

The word *peregrin* suggests both pilgrims and strangers. All are outside familiar territory; all must discover how to proceed. The ascent begins not with mastery but with humility.

This also qualifies Virgil's role as guide. He remains indispensable, yet his experience of Hell does not make him an expert in the realm of purification. Read allegorically, reason must recognize the limits of its knowledge rather than pretend to possess spiritual authority it lacks.

> 67 L'anime, che si fuor di me accorte,  
> (The souls, who had become aware of me,)
>
> 68 per lo spirare, ch'i' era ancor vivo,  
> (through my breathing, that I was still alive,)
>
> 69 maravigliando diventaro smorte.  
> (grew pale with wonder.)

Dante's breathing reveals his exceptional condition. Unlike the others, he has reached this shore before death.

His living body makes the journey a possibility of conversion within earthly life. What the dead undergo as purification, Dante must learn from and communicate to people who can still change their conduct.

Yet wonder at him also becomes a distraction. The souls crowd around the living visitor and momentarily forget their own ascent. Even innocent curiosity can interrupt the work for which they have arrived.

## 5. Casella's Embrace: Love Survives, Possession Does Not

> 79 Ohi ombre vane, fuor che ne l'aspetto!  
> (Oh shades, empty except in appearance!)
>
> 80 tre volte dietro a lei le mani avvinsi,  
> (three times I clasped my hands behind it,)
>
> 81 e tante mi tornai con esse al petto.  
> (and as many times brought them back to my breast.)

One soul steps forward affectionately, and Dante tries to embrace it. His hands encounter no solid body.

The three unsuccessful embraces recall classical scenes, especially Aeneas's attempt to embrace his dead father Anchises in Virgil's *Aeneid*. Dante carries the emotional language of classical epic into a Christian account of the afterlife.

The shade is not unreal: its identity, memory, and affection persist. What is absent is the bodily solidity Dante expects. His habitual way of expressing love no longer works, and he must learn to recognize presence without physical possession.

> 88 Rispuosemi: «Così com' io t'amai  
> (He answered me: “Just as I loved you)
>
> 89 nel mortal corpo, così t'amo sciolta:  
> (in the mortal body, so I love you, now unbound:)

The speaker is Casella, a musician and friend of Dante about whom little secure biographical information survives. Dante recognizes him through his voice.

Casella's answer affirms continuity across death. Purification does not require the destruction of personal identity or the denial of every earthly affection.

The problem is therefore not friendship itself. It is whether affection supports the movement toward God or becomes a reason to stop. That distinction will govern the musical episode that follows.

## 6. The Delayed Passage: Divine Justice and Jubilee Mercy

> 94 Ed elli a me: «Nessun m'è fatto oltraggio,  
> (And he to me: “No wrong has been done to me,)
>
> 95 se quei che leva quando e cui li piace,  
> (if he who takes passengers when and whom he pleases,)
>
> 96 più volte m'ha negato esto passaggio;  
> (has several times denied me this passage;)
>
> 97 ché di giusto voler lo suo si face:  
> (for his will is formed from a just will:)

Dante wonders why Casella has taken so long to arrive. Casella explains that the angel repeatedly declined to transport him, but insists that this was no injustice.

The angel's apparent freedom is not arbitrary power. His will conforms to divine justice. Casella's acceptance contrasts with the resentment and self-justification so common among the damned.

The poem does not explain the precise reason for each refusal. Its emphasis falls instead on Casella's trust in a providential ordering he does not challenge.

> 98 veramente da tre mesi elli ha tolto  
> (nevertheless, for three months he has taken)
>
> 99 chi ha voluto intrar, con tutta pace.  
> (whoever has wished to enter, in complete peace.)

These three months are generally connected with the Jubilee of 1300, proclaimed by Pope Boniface VIII. The Jubilee offered extraordinary penitential benefits to pilgrims visiting designated Roman basilicas.

Dante imaginatively extends this historical moment of mercy into the otherworld: the angel's passage has become freely available to those wishing to board. This is poetic theology, not a straightforward statement of official doctrine about the transportation of souls.

The connection also sharpens the canto's language of pilgrimage. The earthly journey to Rome and the spiritual journey toward God belong to the same imaginative framework.

## 7. The Tiber's Mouth: Two Routes After Death

> 103 A quella foce ha elli or dritta l'ala,  
> (Toward that river mouth he has now directed his wing,)
>
> 104 però che sempre quivi si ricoglie  
> (because there continually gathers)
>
> 105 qual verso Acheronte non si cala».  
> (whoever does not descend toward Acheron.”)

Casella has identified the gathering place as the mouth of the Tiber, where the river enters the sea. The angel has now returned there for another company.

Dante's geography distinguishes two destinations after death. Those who descend toward Acheron enter Hell; the saved gather near the Tiber for passage to Purgatory.

The Tiber connects this departure point with Rome, the center of the Western Church and the destination of Jubilee pilgrims. Its location thus gives spiritual geography an institutional and historical center.

But the river mouth remains a departure point, not the final goal. Arrival within the community of the saved begins a further journey of purification.

## 8. Casella's Song: The Genuine Consolation of Art

> 106 E io: «Se nuova legge non ti toglie  
> (And I: “If no new law takes from you)
>
> 107 memoria o uso a l'amoroso canto  
> (memory or practice of the song of love)
>
> 108 che mi solea quetar tutte mie doglie,  
> (which used to quiet all my sorrows,)

Dante asks whether Casella can still exercise the musical gift that once comforted him. The question acknowledges that earthly abilities may now be subject to another order.

His request is deeply human. After the terrors and exhaustion of Hell, he seeks the familiar relief of a friend's music. The poem does not represent that relief as false or worthless.

Nevertheless, something that quiets sorrow does not necessarily cure its deepest cause. Consolation can help a traveler continue, but it can also make remaining still seem sufficient.

> 112 ‘Amor che ne la mente mi ragiona’  
> (‘Love that speaks within my mind’)
>
> 113 cominciò elli allor sì dolcemente,  
> (he then began so sweetly,)
>
> 114 che la dolcezza ancor dentro mi suona.  
> (that the sweetness still sounds within me.)

Casella sings Dante's own canzone, “Amor che ne la mente mi ragiona.” Dante later discusses this poem in the *Convivio*, interpreting its beloved lady as Philosophy.

The song therefore cannot simply be dismissed as crude sensual temptation. It belongs to Dante's serious poetic and intellectual life. Its beauty is so powerful that the narrator still inwardly hears it.

That enduring sweetness makes the episode self-critical rather than merely censorious. Dante examines the limits of an art he continues to love: even elevated poetry and philosophical consolation cannot replace the journey toward God.

## 9. Enchantment and Rebuke: When a Good Becomes a Delay

> 115 Lo mio maestro e io e quella gente  
> (My master and I and those people)
>
> 116 ch'eran con lui parevan sì contenti,  
> (who were with him seemed so content,)
>
> 117 come a nessun toccasse altro la mente.  
> (as though nothing else touched anyone's mind.)

The music absorbs everyone, including Virgil. No one appears to remember another concern.

This unanimity resembles the earlier psalm-singing, but its direction differs. The Exodus psalm accompanies a divinely guided passage; Casella's performance arrests the travelers' movement.

The distinction is not simply sacred music versus secular music. It concerns the ordering of attention. Beauty becomes dangerous when its immediate satisfaction excludes a more necessary purpose.

> 120 gridando: «Che è ciò, spiriti lenti?  
> (shouting: “What is this, sluggish spirits?)
>
> 121 qual negligenza, quale stare è questo?  
> (What negligence, what lingering is this?)
>
> 122 Correte al monte a spogliarvi lo scoglio  
> (Run to the mountain to strip off the crust)
>
> 123 ch'esser non lascia a voi Dio manifesto».  
> (that does not allow God to be manifest to you.”)

The speaker is Cato of Utica, the ancient Roman whom Dante has made guardian of Purgatory. His historical association with liberty helps explain this surprising role: he oversees a realm where souls become free from sin.

Cato condemns delay, not explicitly the song itself. His command identifies the proper task: removing the *scoglio*, the encrustation or covering that prevents a clear vision of God.

Salvation is assured for these souls, but their desires still require purification. Their freedom must become active and rightly directed. To be capable of enjoying beauty is not yet to be ready for the ultimate good.

## 10. The Scattered Doves: From Absorption Back to Movement

> 127 se cosa appare ond' elli abbian paura,  
> (if something appears of which they are afraid,)
>
> 128 subitamente lasciano star l'esca,  
> (suddenly they leave the food alone,)
>
> 129 perch' assaliti son da maggior cura;  
> (because they are assailed by a greater concern;)

Dante compares the listeners to doves peacefully feeding until something frightens them away. The image is gentle: these are not predatory or vicious creatures.

Their food is a natural good, just as musical pleasure and friendship are real goods. What changes is the arrival of a “greater concern,” which displaces their immediate absorption.

The simile expresses an ordering of priorities rather than a denial of appetite. Spiritual discipline means recognizing when something more important requires the abandonment of present pleasure.

> 130 così vid' io quella masnada fresca  
> (so I saw that newly arrived company)
>
> 131 lasciar lo canto, e fuggir ver' la costa,  
> (leave the song and flee toward the slope,)
>
> 132 com' om che va, né sa dove rïesca;  
> (like someone who goes without knowing where he will emerge;)
>
> 133 né la nostra partita fu men tosta.  
> (nor was our departure any less swift.)

The souls move before they fully understand the route. Their uncertainty remains, but their paralysis has ended.

Dante and Virgil hurry away too. Neither the living poet nor his venerable guide stands outside the rebuke. The canto includes its own authors and authorities within the frailty it depicts.

Its ending therefore reverses its opening: those who previously traveled only “with the heart” now move with urgency. Knowledge is still incomplete, but intention has become action.

## Epilogue — Learning to Love Without Standing Still

Canto II presents purification as the education of desire. Friendship, music, philosophy, and wonder all retain their value, but none may become a substitute for the highest end.

The angel brings liberation; the psalm gives that liberation a biblical pattern; Casella reveals the persistence of human affection; Cato restores the urgency of the journey. Together, they show that salvation is not merely arrival on a safe shore. It is the beginning of learning how to love good things without allowing them to halt the ascent.
