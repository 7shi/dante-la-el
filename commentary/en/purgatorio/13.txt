We were at the summit of the stair,
where for the second time is cut back
the mountain that frees those climbing it from evil.
There a ledge likewise girdles
the hillside round, as does the first;
except that its arc bends more sharply.
No image is there, nor any mark that shows:
the cliff appears bare, and bare appears the path
with the livid colour of the rocky ground.
“If here we wait for people to ask,”
the poet reasoned, “I fear perhaps
that our choice will bring too much delay.”
Then he fixed his eyes upon the sun;
made his right side the centre of his movement,
and turned the left part of himself.
“O sweet light, trusting in which I enter
upon this new path, lead us,”
he said, “as one should be led within this place.
You warm the world, you shine upon it;
unless another reason urges otherwise,
your rays must always be our guides.”
As far as here is reckoned by a thousand paces,
so far had we already gone there,
in little time, through our eager will;
and towards us were heard flying,
though not seen, spirits speaking
gracious invitations to the table of love.
The first voice that passed flying
‘They have no wine’ it said aloud,
and went on repeating it behind us.
And before it could no longer be heard at all
through drawing farther away, another ‘I am Orestes’
passed crying out, and likewise did not stop.
“Oh!” I said, “father, what voices are these?”
And as I asked, behold the third,
saying: ‘Love those from whom you received harm’.
And the good master: “This terrace scourges
the sin of envy, and therefore are
the cords of the scourge drawn from love.
The bridle must be of the contrary sound;
I believe you will hear it, as I judge,
before you reach the passage of forgiveness.
But fix your eyes intently through the air,
and you will see people sitting before us,
and each is seated along the cliff.”
Then I opened my eyes wider than before;
I looked ahead, and saw shades with cloaks
no different from the colour of the stone.
And when we were a little farther on,
I heard them cry: ‘Mary, pray for us’:
cry ‘Michael’ and ‘Peter’ and ‘All saints’.
I do not believe there walks the earth today
a man so hard that he would not be pierced
by compassion at what I next saw;
for when I had come so close to them
that their gestures became clear to me,
through my eyes I was drained by deep sorrow.
They seemed to me covered in coarse haircloth,
and one supported another with the shoulder,
and all were supported by the cliff.
So the blind who lack possessions
stand at places of pardon to ask for their needs,
and one bows his head upon another,
so that pity may swiftly be awakened in others,
not only through the sound of their words,
but through the sight, which pleads no less.
And as the sun brings no benefit to the blind,
so to the shades here, of whom I now speak,
the light of heaven will not grant itself;
for an iron wire pierces the eyelids of them all
and sews them together, as with a wild sparrowhawk
is done because it will not remain still.
It seemed to me, as I walked, an outrage
to see others without being seen:
so I turned to my wise counsellor.
Well he knew what the silent one wished to say;
and therefore did not await my question,
but said: “Speak, and be brief and pointed.”
Virgil was coming beside me on that side
of the ledge from which one can fall,
because no parapet encircles it;
on my other side were the devout
shades, who through the terrible stitching
pressed tears so hard that they bathed their cheeks.
I turned to them and: “O people certain,”
I began, “of seeing the light on high
which is the sole concern of your desire,
so may grace soon dissolve the scum
of your conscience, that clear
through it may flow the river of the mind,
tell me, for it will be pleasing and dear to me,
whether any soul here among you is Italian;
and perhaps it will benefit her if I learn it.”
“O my brother, each is a citizen
of one true city; but you mean
that she lived in Italy as a pilgrim.”
This I seemed to hear in answer
a little farther ahead than where I stood,
so I made myself heard still farther on.
Among the others I saw a shade expectant
in appearance; and should anyone ask ‘How?’,
she was raising her chin like a blind person.
“Spirit,” I said, “who subdues yourself to rise,
if you are the one who answered me,
make yourself known to me by place or name.”
“I was Sienese,” she replied, “and with these
others here I mend my sinful life,
weeping to him to grant himself to us.
Wise I was not, although Sapìa
I was called, and in the misfortunes of others I was
far happier than in my own good fortune.
And so that you may not think I deceive you,
hear whether I was, as I tell you, foolish,
already descending the arc of my years.
My fellow citizens were near Colle,
joined in battle with their adversaries,
and I was praying to God for what he willed.
There they were routed and turned to the bitter
steps of flight; and seeing the pursuit,
I felt a joy beyond all others,
so much that I turned my defiant face upward,
crying to God: “Now I fear you no more!”,
as the blackbird did for a little fair weather.
I desired peace with God at the very end
of my life; and still would not be
my debt diminished through penance,
were it not that he held me in remembrance,
Pier Pettinaio, in his holy prayers,
who, through charity, felt compassion for me.
But who are you, who about our condition
go asking, and bear your eyes unbound,
as I believe, and speak while breathing?”
“My eyes,” I said, “will yet be taken from me here,
but for a short time, for slight is the offence
committed through their having been turned with envy.
Far greater is the fear in which is held
my soul concerning the torment below,
for already the burden down there weighs upon me.”
And she to me: “Who then has led you
up here among us, if you expect to return below?”
And I: “This one who is with me and says nothing.
And I am alive; therefore ask me,
chosen spirit, if you wish me to move
my mortal feet again for you down there.”
“Oh, this is so strange a thing to hear,”
she replied, “that it is a great sign that God loves you;
therefore help me sometimes with your prayer.
And I ask you, by what you most desire,
if ever you tread the land of Tuscany,
that you restore my good name among my kinsfolk.
You will find them among those foolish people
who place their hopes in Talamone, and will lose there
more hope than in searching for the Diana;
but the admirals will lose still more there.”
