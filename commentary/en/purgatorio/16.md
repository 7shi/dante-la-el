# Smoke, Freedom, and the Two Suns: Allegory in *Purgatorio* XVI

At the center of the *Divine Comedy*, Dante asks a central moral question: if the world is corrupt, who is responsible? The stars, human nature, or human choices?

The setting makes the question urgent. Dante and Virgil are crossing the third terrace of Purgatory, where souls are purified of wrath. Within blinding smoke, Dante encounters Marco Lombardo, whose explanation moves from individual freedom to the responsibilities of Church and empire. The canto connects the darkness of anger with a wider failure of moral and political guidance.

## 1. Wrath as a Darkness That Blinds

> 5 come quel fummo ch'ivi ci coperse,  
> (as that smoke which covered us there,)
>
> 6 né a sentir di così aspro pelo,  
> (nor to the touch of so harsh a texture,)
>
> 7 che l'occhio stare aperto non sofferse;  
> (that the eye could not endure staying open;)

The canto begins by comparing the smoke to the deepest imaginable darkness. Even an infernal night beneath a cloud-covered, starless sky would not obscure Dante's sight so completely.

The smoke is the terrace's *contrapasso*: a condition that reveals the nature of the sin being purged. Wrath clouds judgment, prevents people from seeing others clearly, and turns the surrounding world into something hostile. Here that inward blindness becomes a physical environment.

Unlike punishment in Hell, however, this suffering has a healing purpose. The souls undergo darkness so that they can recover clear sight. Dante's reference to the smoke's harsh texture makes the experience more than visual: anger is abrasive as well as obscuring.

> 8 onde la scorta mia saputa e fida  
> (wherefore my knowledgeable and faithful escort)
>
> 9 mi s'accostò e l'omero m'offerse.  
> (came close to me and offered me his shoulder.)

Virgil's response is practical and gentle. He supplies the bodily support that Dante cannot provide for himself.

Allegorically, reason offers guidance when passion has disabled clear perception. Yet the scene also emphasizes trust: Dante must accept dependence rather than insist on finding his way alone. The offered shoulder answers wrath with patient companionship.

## 2. The Lamb of God and the Recovery of Concord

> 19 Pur ‘Agnus Dei’ eran le loro essordia;  
> (Only ‘Lamb of God’ were their beginnings;)
>
> 20 una parola in tutte era e un modo,  
> (one wording was in them all, and one manner,)
>
> 21 sì che parea tra esse ogne concordia.  
> (so that every harmony seemed to exist among them.)

The souls pray the *Agnus Dei*, the liturgical invocation of Christ as the Lamb of God who takes away the sins of the world. Its petitions for mercy and peace directly oppose the violence of wrath.

The lamb also embodies meekness: power expressed through willing sacrifice rather than retaliation. The penitents are learning to desire the opposite of their former sin.

Their shared words and manner matter as much as the prayer's content. Wrath separates people into competing wills; these voices act together. Although smoke prevents visible community, prayer creates an audible one. Dante can hear the concord that he cannot yet see.

## 3. A Living Traveler Meets Marco Lombardo

> 37 Allora incominciai: «Con quella fascia  
> (Then I began: “With that covering)
>
> 38 che la morte dissolve men vo suso,  
> (which death dissolves I am going upward,)
>
> 39 e venni qui per l'infernale ambascia.  
> (and I came here through infernal anguish.)

Dante tells the unseen speaker that he still possesses his mortal body. The “covering” is the flesh, which death separates from the soul and eventually dissolves.

His journey is therefore exceptional: he has passed through Hell and is ascending Purgatory while alive. This gives his encounter a double purpose. He receives instruction for his own salvation, but he can also carry that instruction back to the living world.

The image of bodily covering also distinguishes Dante from the souls around him. They are undergoing purification after death; he must learn to redirect his choices before death.

> 46 «Lombardo fui, e fu' chiamato Marco;  
> (“I was a Lombard, and was called Marco;)
>
> 47 del mondo seppi, e quel valore amai  
> (I knew the world, and loved that worth)

Marco Lombardo is historically elusive. Medieval sources associate him with the courts of northern Italy and remember him as a discerning, outspoken figure. The geographical label “Lombard” could refer broadly to northern Italy rather than only to the modern region of Lombardy.

Dante makes his worldly experience central. Marco understands human conduct and values the ethical excellence that contemporary society neglects.

His presence among the wrathful also complicates his authority. He is not a faultless observer condemning others from outside the problem. He speaks as someone whose own disorder is being healed. His diagnosis of society emerges from penitence.

## 4. Why the Stars Cannot Bear the Blame

> 67 Voi che vivete ogne cagion recate  
> (You who are living attribute every cause)
>
> 68 pur suso al cielo, pur come se tutto  
> (only to heaven above, just as if everything)
>
> 69 movesse seco di necessitate.  
> (it moved along with itself by necessity.)

Dante asks why the world is empty of virtue and full of wrongdoing. Marco answers by attacking a familiar excuse: blaming celestial influence.

In medieval cosmology, the heavens were widely understood to affect earthly bodies and human dispositions. Marco does not simply dismiss that framework. His objection is to turning influence into necessity—to imagining that the movements of the heavens compel every human action.

The distinction matters because determinism provides an escape from responsibility. If the stars make us act, moral failure becomes someone else's—or something else's—fault.

> 70 Se così fosse, in voi fora distrutto  
> (If this were so, in you would be destroyed)
>
> 71 libero arbitrio, e non fora giustizia  
> (free choice, and there would be no justice)
>
> 72 per ben letizia, e per male aver lutto.  
> (in having joy for good, and grief for evil.)

Marco tests determinism against divine justice. Reward and punishment would be unjust if people could not choose their conduct.

This argument reaches beyond the immediate conversation. The entire moral structure of the *Comedy* depends upon meaningful human responsibility. Hell, Purgatory, and Paradise would become unintelligible if souls were merely instruments of celestial forces.

Purgatory especially requires freedom: its souls are not simply suffering consequences, but learning to love and will rightly.

## 5. Freedom Must Be Exercised and Nourished

> 73 Lo cielo i vostri movimenti inizia;  
> (Heaven initiates your impulses;)
>
> 74 non dico tutti, ma, posto ch'i' 'l dica,  
> (I do not say all, but, even supposing I said so,)
>
> 75 lume v'è dato a bene e a malizia,  
> (light is given you to discern good and evil,)
>
> 76 e libero voler; che, se fatica  
> (and free will; which, if hardship)
>
> 77 ne le prime battaglie col ciel dura,  
> (in its first battles with heaven it endures,)
>
> 78 poi vince tutto, se ben si notrica.  
> (afterward conquers all, if it is well nourished.)

Marco's account allows for strong natural inclinations. Freedom does not mean that resisting an impulse is effortless.

The “light” is the rational capacity to distinguish good from evil. Free will enables a person to act upon that distinction rather than simply follow an appetite. Marco even grants, for argument's sake, more celestial influence than he actually accepts: even then, influence would not abolish choice.

The military metaphor presents moral life as struggle. The nutritional metaphor adds something equally important: freedom must be cultivated. Good habits, instruction, and sound institutions help the will become capable of resisting disordered impulses.

> 79 A maggior forza e a miglior natura  
> (To a greater power and a better nature)
>
> 80 liberi soggiacete; e quella cria  
> (you are subject in freedom; and that creates)
>
> 81 la mente in voi, che 'l ciel non ha in sua cura.  
> (the mind within you, which heaven has not in its charge.)

The greater power is God. Human rational life comes from a source higher than the material heavens.

“Subject in freedom” expresses an apparent paradox at the heart of Dante's theology. Dependence upon God does not negate freedom; God creates the rational and voluntary capacities that make freedom possible.

The stars may affect bodily temperament, but they do not possess ultimate authority over the mind. Consequently, humanity must look within itself for the cause of its moral failures.

## 6. The Childlike Soul and the Education of Desire

> 88 l'anima semplicetta che sa nulla,  
> (the little simple soul that knows nothing,)
>
> 89 salvo che, mossa da lieto fattore,  
> (except that, set in motion by a joyful maker,)
>
> 90 volontier torna a ciò che la trastulla.  
> (it gladly turns toward what delights it.)

Marco describes the newly created soul as a small child. Its first movement is not calculated wickedness but an inexperienced attraction to pleasure.

The “joyful maker” is God. Because the soul originates in goodness, it naturally seeks what seems good and delightful. Its problem is that it does not initially know how to distinguish passing satisfactions from its true fulfillment.

This is not a claim that human beings need no redemption. It is an explanation of how desire becomes misdirected: the impulse toward happiness can attach itself to inadequate objects.

> 91 Di picciol bene in pria sente sapore;  
> (At first it tastes a little good;)
>
> 92 quivi s'inganna, e dietro ad esso corre,  
> (there it deceives itself, and runs after it,)
>
> 93 se guida o fren non torce suo amore.  
> (unless a guide or rein redirects its love.)

The “little good” is not necessarily evil in itself. The mistake is to pursue a limited good as though it were sufficient.

Marco therefore describes moral education as the redirection of love, not its elimination. Desire needs both guidance and restraint: someone must show it where to go, and something must prevent it from rushing into harmful attachments.

The distinction prepares the canto's political argument. Society needs institutions because human freedom, though real, does not automatically produce wisdom.

## 7. Laws Exist—but Who Applies Them?

> 94 Onde convenne legge per fren porre;  
> (Hence law was needed to impose a rein;)
>
> 95 convenne rege aver, che discernesse  
> (a ruler was needed, who could discern)
>
> 96 de la vera cittade almen la torre.  
> (of the true city at least the tower.)

Law supplies the “rein” introduced in the previous passage. The ruler supplies direction.

The “true city” evokes the heavenly community and the right ordering of human life toward its ultimate end. The ruler need not fulfill the Church's spiritual office, but must at least recognize the distant landmark toward which human society should be oriented.

Politics, in this account, is not merely the management of competing appetites. Its purpose includes making an ordered, virtuous common life possible.

> 97 Le leggi son, ma chi pon mano ad esse?  
> (The laws exist, but who puts a hand to them?)
>
> 98 Nullo, però che 'l pastor che procede,  
> (No one, because the shepherd who goes before,)
>
> 99 rugumar può, ma non ha l'unghie fesse;  
> (can chew the cud, but does not have divided hooves;)

The failure is not a shortage of legislation. It is the absence of proper enforcement and leadership.

The “shepherd” is the pope. Marco's strange animal image draws on the biblical dietary laws, under which clean land animals both chew the cud and have divided hooves. In the traditional allegorical reading relevant here, chewing suggests spiritual knowledge or meditation, while divided hooves suggest proper discrimination.

The pope may possess religious teaching, yet fail to distinguish spiritual authority from temporal government. By pursuing worldly goods, the shepherd teaches the flock to pursue them too. Public example defeats the moral purpose of law.

## 8. The Two Suns: Distinct Spiritual and Temporal Guides

> 106 Soleva Roma, che 'l buon mondo feo,  
> (Rome, which made the world good, used)
>
> 107 due soli aver, che l'una e l'altra strada  
> (to have two suns, which the one and the other road)
>
> 108 facean vedere, e del mondo e di Deo.  
> (made visible, both of the world and of God.)

The “two suns” are the empire and the papacy. One guides humanity in earthly affairs; the other guides it toward spiritual salvation.

The image asserts the dignity and distinct function of both authorities. It challenges political imagery in which the pope resembles the sun and the emperor the moon, receiving his light from papal power. Dante instead gives each office its own illuminating role.

This is not modern secularism. Both authorities belong within a providential order. Nor is Marco offering a precise history of an age when the institutions functioned perfectly together: Rome represents an ideal of rightly ordered authority against which the present is judged.

> 109 L'un l'altro ha spento; ed è giunta la spada  
> (One has extinguished the other; and the sword is joined)
>
> 110 col pasturale, e l'un con l'altro insieme  
> (with the pastoral staff, and the one together with the other)
>
> 111 per viva forza mal convien che vada;  
> (by sheer necessity must go badly;)
>
> 112 però che, giunti, l'un l'altro non teme:  
> (because, joined, neither fears the other:)

The sword signifies temporal government; the pastoral staff signifies spiritual care. Their fusion destroys the distinction between their functions and removes an important restraint: neither authority can correct the other when both are possessed by the same power.

Dante's criticism reflects the struggles between popes and emperors that shaped medieval Italy, as well as his own experience of factional politics and exile. His target is not Christianity but the political ambition of its institutional leadership.

> 127 Dì oggimai che la Chiesa di Roma,  
> (Say henceforth that the Church of Rome,)
>
> 128 per confondere in sé due reggimenti,  
> (by confusing within itself two governments,)
>
> 129 cade nel fango, e sé brutta e la soma».  
> (falls into the mud, and soils itself and its burden.”)

The confusion harms both the Church and what it carries: its office, responsibilities, and the community entrusted to it.

Dante responds by recalling the biblical exclusion of Levi's descendants from a territorial inheritance like that of the other tribes of Israel. He reads this as a precedent for distinguishing sacred service from the possession of worldly dominion.

The governing principle is separation of responsibilities, not hostility between religion and political life.

## 9. Lombardy's Lost Courtesy and Its Surviving Witnesses

> 115 In sul paese ch'Adice e Po riga,  
> (In the land that the Adige and Po water,)
>
> 116 solea valore e cortesia trovarsi,  
> (worth and courtesy used to be found,)
>
> 117 prima che Federigo avesse briga;  
> (before Frederick encountered strife;)

Marco moves from general theory to northern Italian history. The region watered by the Adige and Po becomes evidence of what institutional conflict has done to public character.

“Frederick” is generally understood as Emperor Frederick II, whose struggles with the papacy helped intensify the divisions of thirteenth-century Italy.

“Courtesy” here is more than politeness. It includes generosity, honorable conduct, and the cultivated virtues associated with noble households and courts. Marco's contrast between past and present presents political disorder as a cause of cultural and ethical deterioration.

> 124 Currado da Palazzo e 'l buon Gherardo  
> (Conrad da Palazzo and the good Gherardo)
>
> 125 e Guido da Castel, che mei si noma,  
> (and Guido da Castel, who is better named,)
>
> 126 francescamente, il semplice Lombardo.  
> (in the French manner, the straightforward Lombard.)

These three elderly men preserve the standards that the surrounding world has abandoned. Conrad da Palazzo, Gherardo da Camino, and Guido da Castello represent the survival of honorable public and courtly conduct.

The description of Guido as “simple” is complimentary: it suggests sincerity and straightforwardness rather than stupidity.

Their survival also supports Marco's argument about freedom. A corrupt age does not make virtue impossible. These men remain capable of choosing differently, and their example silently rebukes their contemporaries.

## 10. Gherardo's Reputation and the Light Beyond the Smoke

> 139 Per altro sopranome io nol conosco,  
> (By any other byname I do not know him,)
>
> 140 s'io nol togliessi da sua figlia Gaia.  
> (unless I were to take it from his daughter Gaia.)

When Dante asks which Gherardo Marco means, Marco is astonished. Gherardo's reputation should make further identification unnecessary.

The reference is to Gherardo da Camino, lord of Treviso, celebrated for generosity and hospitality. His daughter Gaia supplies a possible identifying association, but the precise force of the reference remains disputed. Commentators have differed over whether her reputation is being invoked favorably or unfavorably; the text does not securely settle that question.

What is clear is Marco's assumption that moral character can become a person's defining name. “Good Gherardo” needs no elaborate genealogy.

> 142 Vedi l'albor che per lo fummo raia  
> (See the dawnlike light that shines through the smoke)
>
> 143 già biancheggiare, e me convien partirmi  
> (already growing white, and I must depart)
>
> 144 (l'angelo è ivi) prima ch'io li paia».  
> ((the angel is there) before I appear to him.”)

Light now penetrates the darkness. The scene's physical movement completes its intellectual movement: Dante has received an explanation of how moral blindness arises and how it can be overcome.

Marco must return rather than accompany the travelers to the angel who guards the ascent. His insight does not mean that his purification is finished.

The encounter therefore preserves a distinction between knowing the truth and fully embodying it. Marco can illuminate Dante's understanding while still needing the healing discipline of his own terrace.

## Epilogue — Freedom Needs Clear Sight and Faithful Guides

*Purgatorio* XVI rejects the excuse that human wrongdoing is imposed by the stars or made unavoidable by nature. People possess reason and free will, but those powers require cultivation, good examples, and properly ordered institutions.

The canto's images make one connected argument. Smoke shows anger's blindness; shared prayer restores concord; the childlike soul needs guidance; and the two suns represent authorities that must illuminate different paths without swallowing one another.

The world's darkness is humanly sustained, not cosmically inevitable. Its remedy begins with responsible choice—and with guides who lead toward the good rather than obscure it.
