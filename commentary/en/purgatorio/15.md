# Purgatorio XV: From Envy's Scarcity to the Abundance of Love

Canto XV follows Dante and Virgil from the terrace of envy to the terrace of wrath. Between these two disorders of love, Dante places a lesson about what makes goodness different from material wealth: spiritual goods increase through being shared. The canto's changing conditions of sight—sunlight, angelic brilliance, inward visions, and finally smoke—turn that lesson into an experience of learning to see.

## 1. Afternoon Light and the Difficulty of Seeing

> 4 tanto pareva già inver' la sera  
> (so much, toward evening, there already seemed)
>
> 5 essere al sol del suo corso rimaso;  
> (to remain to the sun of its course;)
>
> 6 vespero là, e qui mezza notte era.  
> (it was afternoon there, and here it was midnight.)

The opening comparison measures the remaining daylight against the interval between sunrise and the end of the third hour: approximately three hours. It is therefore about three in the afternoon on the mountain of Purgatory. “Here” refers to Italy, the geographical standpoint of Dante as narrator, where it is midnight.

This coordination of distant places belongs to the poem's carefully imagined geography. Purgatory stands opposite Jerusalem on the globe; the pilgrim's progress takes place within a coherent cosmic order.

The afternoon setting also matters spiritually. Time remains available for ascent, but daylight is declining. Dante's purification must continue through changing conditions of illumination.

> 22 così mi parve da luce rifratta  
> (so I seemed by reflected light)
>
> 23 quivi dinanzi a me esser percosso;  
> (there before me to be struck;)
>
> 24 per che a fuggir la mia vista fu ratta.  
> (therefore my sight was quick to withdraw.)

Dante has just compared the approaching brightness to a ray reflected from water or a mirror. Although *rifratta* resembles the modern word “refracted,” the preceding description concerns reflection: the incoming and outgoing rays make equal angles with the perpendicular, represented by the path of a falling stone.

The scientific comparison prepares a spiritual difficulty. Dante can describe how light behaves, but he cannot yet endure the heavenly light approaching him. Understanding a phenomenon intellectually is not the same as possessing the inward capacity to receive it.

## 2. The Angel and the Education of Desire

> 31 Tosto sarà ch'a veder queste cose  
> (Soon it will be that seeing these things)
>
> 32 non ti fia grave, ma fieti diletto  
> (will not be burdensome to you, but will be a delight)
>
> 33 quanto natura a sentir ti dispuose».  
> (as great as nature fitted you to feel”.)

Virgil identifies the radiance as an angel coming to invite them upward. His reassurance explains an important principle of Purgatory: the pilgrim's capacity for goodness is being transformed.

Heavenly beauty is not intrinsically painful. It feels overwhelming because Dante is not yet fully prepared for it. Purification will make the same light delightful. Salvation therefore involves more than the removal of wrongdoing; it restores the ability to enjoy what is truly good.

> 38 e ‘Beati misericordes!’ fue  
> (and ‘Blessed are the merciful!’ was)
>
> 39 cantato retro, e ‘Godi tu che vinci!’.  
> (sung behind us, and ‘Rejoice, you who overcome!’.)


The Latin phrase comes from the Beatitudes: “Blessed are the merciful” (Matthew 5:7). It answers the envy purified on the terrace they are leaving.

Envy grieves at another person's happiness. Mercy moves in the opposite direction, responding generously to another person's need. The angel's blessing celebrates not simply Dante's departure from a location but his progress toward a different relationship with others.

The accompanying victory cry presents purification as a conquest of disordered desire, not a conquest over fellow human beings.

## 3. Envy Begins Where Possession Means Exclusion

> 49 Perché s'appuntano i vostri disiri  
> (Because your desires fix themselves)
>
> 50 dove per compagnia parte si scema,  
> (where a share is diminished by sharing,)
>
> 51 invidia move il mantaco a' sospiri.  
> (envy works the bellows of your sighs.)

As they climb, Dante asks about something said by Guido del Duca, the “spirit of Romagna” encountered in the preceding canto. Guido had condemned humanity's attachment to goods that cannot admit partners without reducing someone's portion.

Virgil explains the mechanism behind that complaint. Wealth, political power, property, and prestige are often pursued as limited possessions. If another person gains more, I imagine that less remains for me. Desire organized around such goods readily becomes envy.

The image of the bellows makes envy an active generator of misery: it keeps pumping out the sighs produced by rivalry. Virgil does not claim that material necessities are evil. His criticism concerns treating divisible goods as the highest objects of desire and therefore making other people appear chiefly as competitors.

## 4. Divine Goodness Grows Through Sharing

> 67 Quello infinito e ineffabil bene  
> (That infinite and ineffable good)
>
> 68 che là sù è, così corre ad amore  
> (which is up there, so hastens toward love)
>
> 69 com' a lucido corpo raggio vene.  
> (as a ray comes to a shining body.)
>
> 70 Tanto si dà quanto trova d'ardore;  
> (It gives itself in proportion to the ardor it finds;)

Dante objects that distributing a good among more owners should make each owner poorer. Virgil answers that this reasoning still assumes the conditions of earthly possession.

God, the “infinite and ineffable good,” is not a finite substance cut into smaller portions. Divine generosity responds to the soul's capacity for love. The limitation lies not in the supply of goodness but in the recipient's openness to it.

The light imagery connects this teaching to the angel's brightness. Dante's difficulty seeing the angel dramatizes the very problem Virgil now explains: the good is present, but the receiver must become capable of receiving it.

> 73 E quanta gente più là sù s'intende,  
> (And the more people there are who love up there,)
>
> 74 più v'è da bene amare, e più vi s'ama,  
> (the more good there is to love, and the more love there is,)
>
> 75 e come specchio l'uno a l'altro rende.  
> (and like a mirror each reflects it to the other.)

Heaven reverses envy's arithmetic. More blessed souls mean more persons to love and more love communicated among them. Another person's happiness becomes an enlargement of one's own happiness rather than a threat to it.

The mirror image develops the canto's earlier account of reflected light. Here reflection becomes a model of communion: each soul receives divine goodness and returns it to others.

This does not mean that God becomes more infinite as the number of the blessed increases. Rather, the community's enjoyment and mutual communication of goodness increase. Charity makes another person's good genuinely shareable.

## 5. Beatrice, Reason, and the Remaining Wounds

> 76 E se la mia ragion non ti disfama,  
> (And if my reasoning does not satisfy your hunger,)
>
> 77 vedrai Beatrice, ed ella pienamente  
> (you will see Beatrice, and she fully)
>
> 78 ti torrà questa e ciascun' altra brama.  
> (will take away this and every other longing.)

Virgil has given a rational explanation, but he acknowledges that Dante may still hunger for fuller understanding. Beatrice will complete what his reasoning cannot yet accomplish.

Throughout the poem, Virgil represents the dignity and limitations of human reason, while Beatrice is associated with revealed truth and divine wisdom. These roles do not make reason worthless. Virgil's teaching is necessary, but it prepares Dante for an understanding that exceeds its own resources.

The language of hunger also makes knowledge a form of desire. Intellectual uncertainty belongs to the larger education of wanting that takes place throughout Purgatory.

> 79 Procaccia pur che tosto sieno spente,  
> (Strive only that soon there may be erased,)
>
> 80 come son già le due, le cinque piaghe,  
> (as the two already are, the five wounds,)
>
> 81 che si richiudon per esser dolente».  
> (which close through sorrow”.)

The wounds are the seven letters *P*, for *peccatum*, “sin,” inscribed on Dante's forehead at Purgatory's gate. Two have now been removed, corresponding to pride and envy; five remain.

Virgil redirects Dante from speculation to the practical work of repentance. Understanding spiritual generosity matters, but it must be accompanied by the healing of desire.

The paradox is that these wounds close through sorrow. Penitential pain is medicinal: remorse heals the damage that sinful pleasure has left behind.

## 6. Mary: Correction Without Wrath

> 88 e una donna, in su l'entrar, con atto  
> (and a woman, at the entrance, with the manner)
>
> 89 dolce di madre dicer: «Figliuol mio,  
> (gentle of a mother, saying: “My son,)
>
> 90 perché hai tu così verso noi fatto?  
> (why have you acted this way toward us?)
>
> 91 Ecco, dolenti, lo tuo padre e io  
> (Behold, sorrowing, your father and I)

Upon reaching the third terrace, Dante enters an ecstatic vision. He sees Mary finding the young Jesus in the Temple after she and Joseph have searched anxiously for him. The source is Luke 2:41–50.

The crucial detail is her “gentle” maternal manner. She expresses distress and asks for an explanation, but she does not surrender to anger. Meekness here does not mean indifference, silence, or an inability to correct someone. It means that correction remains governed by love.

As on other terraces, Mary provides the first and highest example of the virtue opposed to the sin being purged. Against wrath, Dante places a tenderness capable of speaking honestly without becoming vindictive.

## 7. Pisistratus: Power Restrained by Mercy

> 104 «Che farem noi a chi mal ne disira,  
> (“What shall we do to one who wishes us harm,)
>
> 105 se quei che ci ama è per noi condannato?»,  
> (if one who loves us is condemned by us?”,)

The second vision draws on classical history. The wife of Pisistratus, ruler of Athens, demands punishment for a young man who has embraced their daughter. Pisistratus answers with this measured question.

The anecdote comes from the classical tradition of exemplary conduct, notably Valerius Maximus. Dante pairs pagan and biblical examples because moral truth can be recognized outside the history of Christian revelation.

The scene shifts from family anxiety to the use of public power. Pisistratus possesses the authority to punish, but he refuses to let wounded pride dictate his judgment. He distinguishes an act motivated by affection from one motivated by hostility.

His response embodies clemency: strength expressed through restraint rather than retaliation. The ruler's calm exposes the disproportion between the offense and the vengeance his wife demands.

## 8. Stephen: Love for the Persecutor

> 111 ma de li occhi facea sempre al ciel porte,  
> (but of his eyes he continually made gates toward heaven,)
>
> 112 orando a l'alto Sire, in tanta guerra,  
> (praying to the high Lord, amid such violence,)
>
> 113 che perdonasse a' suoi persecutori,  
> (that he would forgive his persecutors,)
>
> 114 con quello aspetto che pietà diserra.  
> (with that expression which unlocks compassion.)

The third vision shows the martyrdom of Stephen, narrated in Acts 7. As an enraged crowd stones him, he prays for his killers to be forgiven.

The examples have become progressively more demanding: Mary responds gently to anxiety, Pisistratus restrains punishment after an affront, and Stephen loves those who are actually killing him. His prayer also recalls Christ's forgiveness of his executioners.

Stephen's eyes become “gates toward heaven.” While his body falls toward the earth, his attention remains directed toward God. This orientation allows him to answer violence without reproducing it.

The scene gives concrete form to Virgil's lesson about charity. Love ceases to be a transaction dependent on receiving favorable treatment. Stephen seeks good even for people who deny his own.

## 9. True Visions and the Awakening of the Will

> 115 Quando l'anima mia tornò di fori  
> (When my soul returned outward)
>
> 116 a le cose che son fuor di lei vere,  
> (to the things that are real outside it,)
>
> 117 io riconobbi i miei non falsi errori.  
> (I recognized my not-false errors.)

Coming out of his trance, Dante recognizes that the scenes were not physically occurring around him. Yet he calls them “not-false errors.”

The paradox distinguishes external perception from inward truth. Dante had momentarily experienced the visions as present events; in that sense, his perception had wandered from his immediate surroundings. But the visions were not deceptive in their moral content or spiritual purpose.

They make goodness visible so that it can affect desire. Purgatory educates through imagination and feeling as well as through argument.

> 130 Ciò che vedesti fu perché non scuse  
> (What you saw was so that you do not excuse yourself from)
>
> 131 d'aprir lo core a l'acque de la pace  
> (opening your heart to the waters of peace)
>
> 132 che da l'etterno fonte son diffuse.  
> (which are poured forth from the eternal fountain.)

Virgil explains that the visions were given to open Dante's heart to peace. Their purpose is not aesthetic admiration alone, but moral receptivity.

The image of flowing water complements the earlier imagery of light. Peace, like divine radiance, comes from an inexhaustible source. The recipient's task is to become open to it.

Virgil also explains that his questioning was meant to restore Dante's purposeful movement. Spiritual experience must lead back into attentive action: seeing examples of virtue is preparation for practicing it.

## 10. Wrath's Smoke and the Loss of Clear Sight

> 142 Ed ecco a poco a poco un fummo farsi  
> (And behold, little by little, a smoke forming)
>
> 143 verso di noi come la notte oscuro;  
> (toward us, dark as night;)
>
> 144 né da quello era loco da cansarsi.  
> (nor was there any place to escape from it.)
>
> 145 Questo ne tolse li occhi e l'aere puro.  
> (This deprived us of sight and pure air.)

The canto ends as Dante and Virgil enter the smoke of the terrace of wrath. Its full penitential setting unfolds in the next canto, but its symbolic force is already unmistakable.

Anger clouds judgment. It prevents people from seeing others clearly and contaminates the shared atmosphere in which they live. The smoke makes this inward disorder physically unavoidable.

The ending also contrasts two forms of impaired vision. At the beginning, Dante cannot bear the angel's light because its goodness exceeds his present capacity. At the end, he cannot see because wrath's darkness obstructs perception. Purification must both remove the obstruction and strengthen the power to receive light.

## Epilogue — Learning to Share the Light

Canto XV connects envy and wrath through their common distortion of love. Envy treats another person's good as a loss; wrath turns injury or frustration into a demand for retaliation.

Against both, Dante sets charity: goodness that increases through sharing, correction without hatred, power restrained by mercy, and forgiveness even under persecution. Virgil explains these truths, the visions embody them, and the changing light makes them felt.

The ascent toward freedom requires more than knowing what goodness is. Dante must learn to welcome it in others—and to keep loving when his own vision grows dark.
