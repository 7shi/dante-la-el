# Learning to Rise: Allegory and Background in *Purgatorio*, Canto XII

Canto XII completes Dante's passage through the first terrace of Purgatory, where pride is purified. The proud carry crushing stones that force their bodies downward; Dante, although still alive, must also learn to lower his gaze. On the pavement he discovers images of pride brought to ruin. Then an angel directs him toward the next terrace, and his ascent becomes unexpectedly easier.

The canto's central movement is from oppressive weight to spiritual freedom. Humility does not diminish human beings: it releases them from the self-importance that prevents their ascent.

## 1. Sharing the Yoke: Dante Learns Humility

> 1 Di pari, come buoi che vanno a giogo,  
> (Side by side, like oxen that go under the yoke,)
>
> 2 m'andava io con quell' anima carca,  
> (I was going with that burdened soul,)
>
> 3 fin che 'l sofferse il dolce pedagogo.  
> (as long as the gentle teacher allowed it.)

The burdened soul is Oderisi of Gubbio, the manuscript illuminator with whom Dante spoke in the preceding canto. Oderisi has exposed the instability of artistic fame: one celebrated master soon gives way to another. Dante, himself a poet ambitious for recognition, has particular reason to listen.

The comparison with yoked oxen gives their companionship a deliberately humble form. Dante walks beside the penitent rather than looking down upon him. The shared posture suggests that pride is not merely someone else's sin.

Virgil, the “gentle teacher,” eventually tells Dante to move ahead. Sympathy must contribute to the pilgrim's moral progress, not prevent it.

> 7 dritto sì come andar vuolsi rife'mi  
> (Upright, as one ought to walk, I made myself again)
>
> 8 con la persona, avvegna che i pensieri  
> (in my body, although my thoughts)
>
> 9 mi rimanessero e chinati e scemi.  
> (remained bowed down and diminished.)

Dante straightens physically, but his thoughts remain lowered. “Diminished” here suggests an abatement of self-importance after Oderisi's lesson.

The distinction between bodily position and inward disposition is crucial. Humility is not simply a bent back. Its purpose is to change how the person understands himself. Dante can stand upright without immediately returning to the spiritual posture of pride.

## 2. The Pavement as a Book of Memory

> 16 Come, perché di lor memoria sia,  
> (As, so that their memory may endure,)
>
> 17 sovra i sepolti le tombe terragne  
> (above the buried, tombs level with the ground)
>
> 18 portan segnato quel ch'elli eran pria,  
> (bear depicted what they were before,)

Virgil tells Dante to look beneath his feet. The terrace's pavement bears images resembling the figures carved upon floor tombs in medieval churches. Such monuments preserved the appearance, identity, or social standing of the dead and prompted the living to remember them.

Here, however, remembrance has a penitential purpose. The proud must look downward, encountering not an affirmation of their importance but a record of pride's consequences.

These pavement images complement the sculpted examples of humility that Dante saw on the terrace's wall in Canto X. Together they provide a double education: imitate humility and recognize the destructive logic of arrogance. The landscape itself becomes a moral book, read through movement, posture, and attention.

## 3. Pride Against Heaven: Lucifer, the Giants, and Babel

> 25 Vedea colui che fu nobil creato  
> (I saw him who was created noble)
>
> 26 più ch'altra creatura, giù dal cielo  
> (more than any other creature, down from heaven)
>
> 27 folgoreggiando scender, da l'un lato.  
> (descending like lightning, on one side.)

The first example is Lucifer. Created more exalted than any other creature, he falls because he refuses his proper relation to God. The lightning-like descent recalls Christ's declaration in Luke 10:18 that he saw Satan fall from heaven like lightning.

Lucifer establishes the governing pattern of the entire sequence: the desire to rise above one's rightful condition produces a catastrophic fall. His original excellence makes the warning sharper. Pride can corrupt genuine gifts, not merely imaginary superiority.

The adjacent images of Briareus and the defeated Giants give this pattern a classical counterpart. Their assault upon divine authority ends in bodies stretched across the earth. Biblical and pagan histories converge within Dante's Christian interpretation of moral order.

> 34 Vedea Nembròt a piè del gran lavoro  
> (I saw Nimrod at the foot of the great work)
>
> 35 quasi smarrito, e riguardar le genti  
> (as though bewildered, and looking at the peoples)
>
> 36 che 'n Sennaàr con lui superbi fuoro.  
> (who in Shinar were proud with him.)

The “great work” is the Tower of Babel. Medieval tradition associated Nimrod with the attempt to build a tower reaching heaven, although Genesis does not explicitly name him as its builder.

Babel displays pride as a collective enterprise. Human cooperation becomes an attempt at self-exaltation, and the result is the confusion of languages. Nimrod, once imagined as a commanding leader, now looks helplessly at his companions.

For a poet, this example has particular force: pride damages the language through which human beings understand one another. Its punishment is not only defeat but the disintegration of community.

## 4. Gifts Turned into Claims of Superiority

> 37 O Nïobè, con che occhi dolenti  
> (O Niobe, with what sorrowful eyes)
>
> 38 vedea io te segnata in su la strada,  
> (I saw you depicted upon the road,)
>
> 39 tra sette e sette tuoi figliuoli spenti!  
> (among your seven and seven children slain!)

Niobe, whose story appears in Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, boasted that her fourteen children made her superior to Latona, the mother of Apollo and Diana. The divine children killed Niobe's sons and daughters, destroying the very advantage on which she had based her claim.

Her example concerns the conversion of a gift into a title to superiority. Motherhood becomes an occasion for contempt, and the sculpture shows the terrible aftermath.

Dante nevertheless addresses her with emotional intensity. The moral lesson does not require indifference to suffering. The apostrophe makes the defeated figure present as a grieving person, not merely an abstract illustration.

The nearby image of Saul, dead upon his own sword at Mount Gilboa, extends the warning to kingship. Political elevation, like parental abundance, offers no immunity from ruin.

> 43 O folle Aragne, sì vedea io te  
> (O foolish Arachne, thus I saw you)
>
> 44 già mezza ragna, trista in su li stracci  
> (already half spider, sorrowful upon the shreds)
>
> 45 de l'opera che mal per te si fé.  
> (of the work that was made to your harm.)

Arachne challenged Minerva, or Pallas Athena, in weaving. Dante depicts her midway through transformation into a spider, beside the torn remains of her work.

Her story is especially relevant after Dante's encounter with Oderisi. Artistic accomplishment can become spiritually dangerous when the maker treats excellence as proof of independence from, or superiority to, divine authority.

The unfinished metamorphosis also makes the moral deformation visible: pride has distorted the artist herself. The accompanying example of Rehoboam, fleeing after his threatening policies provoke rebellion, transfers the same lesson from artistic mastery to political command.

## 5. Blood, Ruin, and the Hidden Word for Humanity

> 55 Mostrava la ruina e 'l crudo scempio  
> (It showed the destruction and the cruel slaughter)
>
> 56 che fé Tamiri, quando disse a Ciro:  
> (that Tomyris wrought, when she said to Cyrus:)
>
> 57 «Sangue sitisti, e io di sangue t'empio».  
> (“You thirsted for blood, and I fill you with blood.”)

Tomyris, queen of the Massagetae, defeated the Persian king Cyrus. According to the historical tradition Dante follows, she placed his severed head in a vessel of blood, answering his appetite for conquest with a grotesquely literal satisfaction.

The episode expresses a logic of reversal: the conqueror receives what he desired in a form that exposes its horror. Dante's selection emphasizes the self-destructive appetite beneath imperial ambition.

Other images in this final group similarly bring power and possessiveness to violent endings. Alcmaeon kills his mother Eriphyle, whose acceptance of a fatal necklace had helped bring about her husband's death. Sennacherib is murdered by his sons in a temple. The Assyrians flee after Judith kills Holofernes. Private corruption, royal arrogance, and military domination belong to the same moral landscape.

> 61 Vedeva Troia in cenere e in caverne;  
> (I saw Troy in ashes and in hollow ruins;)
>
> 62 o Ilïón, come te basso e vile  
> (O Ilium, how low and abject)
>
> 63 mostrava il segno che lì si discerne!  
> (the image discerned there showed you!)

Troy concludes the catalogue by expanding the scale from individuals to an entire civilization. Its legendary grandeur survives as ashes and emptiness. Virgil's presence makes the example particularly resonant: the *Aeneid* begins from the destruction of Troy and the displacement of its survivors.

The catalogue also contains an elaborate acrostic. Four tercets begin with **V**, through forms of *Vedea*; four begin with **O**, through direct addresses; and four begin with **M**, through *Mostrava*. The final tercet repeats **V–O–M** in its three lines.

Read with the medieval interchangeability of **V** and **U**, the letters form **UOM**, “man” or “human being.” The famous names thus conceal a universal designation. The spectacle is not merely about exceptional monsters or distant tyrants: it is about humanity's recurring temptation to pride.

## 6. Art That Makes the Living Confront the Truth

> 67 Morti li morti e i vivi parean vivi:  
> (The dead appeared dead, and the living alive:)
>
> 68 non vide mei di me chi vide il vero,  
> (one who saw the reality saw no better than I,)
>
> 69 quant' io calcai, fin che chinato givi.  
> (all that I trod upon while I went bent down.)

The divine carvings exceed the achievements of any human painter or draftsman. Dante experiences them with the immediacy of eyewitness testimony.

Their perfection is not merely decorative. The images make moral truth unavoidable. Bodies, gestures, defeat, and grief communicate what an abstract prohibition against pride might leave untouched.

There is also a productive tension in Dante's own artistry. He uses extraordinary poetic skill to describe an art surpassing every human maker. The poet must acknowledge the limits of the very excellence he is demonstrating.

> 70 Or superbite, e via col viso altero,  
> (Now be proud, and onward with haughty faces,)
>
> 71 figliuoli d'Eva, e non chinate il volto  
> (children of Eve, and do not lower your faces)
>
> 72 sì che veggiate il vostro mal sentero!  
> (so that you may see your evil path!)

The command is bitterly ironic. Dante does not encourage pride; he exposes the folly of maintaining it after such evidence.

“Children of Eve” replaces the catalogue's individual names with the whole human family. The proud person's elevated face prevents him from seeing the path beneath his feet. Physical posture becomes an image of moral blindness: someone absorbed in his own greatness cannot recognize where he is going.

## 7. The Sixth Hour: Contemplation Must Become Action

> 79 Vedi colà un angel che s'appresta  
> (See there an angel who makes ready)
>
> 80 per venir verso noi; vedi che torna  
> (to come toward us; see that there returns)
>
> 81 dal servigio del dì l'ancella sesta.  
> (from the service of the day the sixth handmaid.)

Dante's concentration on the pavement has absorbed his sense of time. Virgil redirects his attention toward an approaching angel and toward the passing day.

The “sixth handmaid” personifies the sixth daylight hour, now completing its service: it is approximately noon. The graceful mythological language describes a practical necessity. The pilgrim must not allow even valuable contemplation to become delay.

> 84 pensa che questo dì mai non raggiorna!».  
> (Think that this day never dawns again!”)

Virgil's warning makes time itself part of the moral education. Each day offers opportunities that cannot simply be recovered later.

In Purgatory, salvation is assured for the souls undergoing purification, but effort remains meaningful. For the living Dante, the urgency is sharper still. Understanding the danger of pride must lead to a changed manner of living, not merely to prolonged fascination with its consequences.

## 8. The Angel: Humanity Was Made for Flight

> 94 A questo invito vegnon molto radi:  
> (To this invitation very few come:)
>
> 95 o gente umana, per volar sù nata,  
> (O human race, born to fly upward,)
>
> 96 perché a poco vento così cadi?».  
> (why do you fall so at a little wind?”)

The angel's invitation states the positive truth behind the canto's many falls. Human beings are made for ascent toward God.

This is not the same ascent attempted by Lucifer, the Giants, or the builders of Babel. Pride seeks elevation through self-assertion; spiritual ascent occurs through the acceptance of one's created nature and proper end.

The “little wind” suggests the disproportion between humanity's high vocation and the comparatively slight temptations that divert it. Fame, power, wealth, and superiority can feel overwhelming, yet they are insubstantial beside the good for which human beings were made.

> 97 Menocci ove la roccia era tagliata;  
> (He led us where the rock was cut;)
>
> 98 quivi mi batté l'ali per la fronte;  
> (there he struck his wings against my forehead;)
>
> 99 poi mi promise sicura l'andata.  
> (then he promised me a safe passage.)

At the entrance to Purgatory, an angel inscribed seven letters **P** on Dante's forehead, representing the seven capital sins: *P* for *peccato*, “sin.” Here the angel's wings remove the first letter, marking the purification of pride.

Dante does not immediately understand the action. The spiritual effect precedes his conscious recognition of it.

The sequence joins personal effort to grace. Dante has listened, looked, reflected, and walked; nevertheless, the mark is erased by an angelic gesture, not by his own hand.

## 9. Florence, the Stairway, and the Poor in Spirit

> 100 Come a man destra, per salire al monte  
> (As on the right, to climb the hill)
>
> 101 dove siede la chiesa che soggioga  
> (where stands the church that overlooks)
>
> 102 la ben guidata sopra Rubaconte,  
> (the well-governed city above Rubaconte,)

Dante compares the ascent to the steps leading toward the church of San Miniato al Monte above Florence. Rubaconte was the bridge later known as the Ponte alle Grazie.

The comparison anchors the otherworldly journey in a familiar earthly landscape. A Florentine reader can imagine the steepness, the stairs, and the bodily experience of climbing.

But “well-governed” is sarcastic. Dante's native city is not presented as a secure model of civic order. In the continuation of the comparison, he contrasts an earlier age of trustworthy public records and measures with later corruption. Even a practical description of stairs becomes an occasion to judge earthly society.

> 110 ‘Beati pauperes spiritu!’ voci  
> (“Blessed are the poor in spirit!” voices)
>
> 111 cantaron sì, che nol diria sermone.  
> (sang so that speech could not express it.)

The voices sing the first Beatitude from Matthew 5:3. “Poor in spirit” means humble: those who do not regard themselves as spiritually self-sufficient.

This blessing supplies the affirmative counterpart to the pavement's warnings. The goal is not simply to avoid the catastrophes of Lucifer or Troy, but to acquire the inward poverty that makes room for God.

Dante immediately contrasts this musical passage with the entrances of Hell, marked by fierce lamentation. Purgatory contains suffering, but its governing direction is restoration. The climb is accompanied by blessing because its hardships lead toward freedom.

## 10. The Vanishing Weight of Sin

> 121 Rispuose: «Quando i P che son rimasi  
> (He answered: “When the P's that have remained)
>
> 122 ancor nel volto tuo presso che stinti,  
> (still upon your face, almost faded,)
>
> 123 saranno, com' è l'un, del tutto rasi,  
> (are, as one is, entirely erased,)
>
> 124 fier li tuoi piè dal buon voler sì vinti,  
> (your feet will be so mastered by good will,)
>
> 125 che non pur non fatica sentiranno,  
> (that not only will they feel no weariness,)
>
> 126 ma fia diletto loro esser sù pinti».  
> (but it will be their delight to be urged upward.”)

Dante notices that climbing now requires less effort than walking on level ground did before. Virgil explains that the removal of the first **P** has made him lighter.

This reverses ordinary physical expectations. In the moral geography of Purgatory, difficulty depends not only on the slope but on the condition of the will. Sin weighs the person down; purification makes movement toward the good more natural.

The remaining letters are already nearly faded. The detail suggests the foundational importance of humility: overcoming pride weakens the resistance that sustains other sins.

Virgil's promise also defines freedom positively. The perfected person does not merely obey reluctantly or withstand temptation through endless strain. The will comes to delight in the good. Ascent ceases to feel like an alien demand.

> 136 a che guardando, il mio duca sorrise.  
> (and watching this, my guide smiled.)

Dante raises his hand and discovers that only six letters remain. He resembles someone who learns from other people's gestures that he has something on his head and then feels for it.

After the monumental catastrophes of the pavement, the canto ends with an ordinary, almost comic action. Dante cannot even inspect his own forehead without assistance. His dependence is gentle rather than humiliating, and Virgil's smile closes the lesson with affection.

## Epilogue — Humility Makes Ascent Possible

Canto XII moves from bowed heads and shattered greatness to song, lightness, and a teacher's smile. Its biblical and classical examples reveal pride as a refusal of rightful relationship—with God, with other people, and with the truth about oneself.

Humility is the remedy not because human beings were made to remain low, but because they were made to rise rightly. By surrendering the demand to exalt himself, Dante becomes more capable of the ascent for which humanity was created.
