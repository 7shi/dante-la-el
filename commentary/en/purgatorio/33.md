# From a Wounded Church to the Stars: Allegory in *Purgatorio* XXXIII

The final canto of *Purgatorio* takes place in the Earthly Paradise, the garden of Eden at the summit of Mount Purgatory. Dante has completed his ascent, confessed his failures before Beatrice, and witnessed a disturbing pageant: a chariot representing the Church has been attacked, corrupted, and finally dragged away by a giant accompanying a prostitute.

Canto XXXIII turns from that spectacle of historical corruption toward prophecy, understanding, and spiritual renewal. Its movement is double: Beatrice announces that the world's disorder will be judged, while Dante undergoes the final preparation necessary to enter Heaven.

## 1. A Psalm for the Desecrated Church

> 1 ‘Deus, venerunt gentes’, alternando  
> (‘God, the nations have come’, alternating)
>
> 2 or tre or quattro dolce salmodia,  
> (now three, now four, sweet psalmody,)
>
> 3 le donne incominciaro, e lagrimando;  
> (the women began, and weeping;)

The opening words come from Psalm 78 in the Vulgate, Psalm 79 in most modern English Bibles. It laments the invasion of God's inheritance and the desecration of Jerusalem's Temple. Here it becomes a lament for the violated Church, whose degradation Dante has just seen enacted.

The women sing in alternating groups of three and four. They personify the three theological virtues—faith, hope, and charity—and the four cardinal virtues—prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance. Their sorrow gives the preceding spectacle its moral interpretation: the Church's corruption is not merely an institutional failure but an offense against the virtues that should govern Christian life.

> 4 e Bëatrice, sospirosa e pia,  
> (and Beatrice, sighing and compassionate,)
>
> 5 quelle ascoltava sì fatta, che poco  
> (listened to them in such a state that little)
>
> 6 più a la croce si cambiò Maria.  
> (more was Mary changed at the cross.)

Beatrice's grief approaches that of Mary witnessing the Crucifixion. The comparison presents the Church's suffering as a renewed injury to Christ's body.

Yet the analogy also prepares a reversal. The Crucifixion was not the end of Christian history; likewise, the Church's apparent defeat will not have the final word.

## 2. Beatrice's Promise: Absence Will Give Way to Return

> 10 ‘Modicum, et non videbitis me;  
> (‘A little while, and you will not see me;)
>
> 11 et iterum, sorelle mie dilette,  
> (and again, my beloved sisters,)
>
> 12 modicum, et vos videbitis me’.  
> (a little while, and you will see me.’)

Beatrice repeats Christ's words from John 16:16, inserting an affectionate address to the virtues. In the Gospel, Christ prepares his disciples for an absence that will become renewed presence; the immediate Christian horizon is his death and Resurrection.

Within the canto, the quotation promises that the present eclipse of sacred order is temporary. Beatrice speaks with the authority of revealed truth, but she should not simply be identified with Christ. Her scriptural language makes her the bearer of his promise.

Dante thus places the coming political and ecclesiastical prophecy within a larger Christian pattern: grief, apparent abandonment, and eventual restoration.

## 3. Dante Must Become a Witness, Not Remain a Frightened Pupil

> 31 Ed ella a me: «Da tema e da vergogna  
> (And she to me: “From fear and shame)
>
> 32 voglio che tu omai ti disviluppe,  
> (I wish you now to disentangle yourself,)
>
> 33 sì che non parli più com' om che sogna.  
> (so that you no longer speak like someone dreaming.)

Dante's response to Beatrice is initially hesitant and almost inaudible. His reverence has become an obstacle to conversation. She does not abolish humility, but demands that it cease to be paralysis.

This marks a development in his education. Earlier, shame helped bring him to confession; now he must acquire the freedom and clarity needed to understand and communicate what he has witnessed.

> 52 Tu nota; e sì come da me son porte,  
> (Take note; and just as they are delivered by me,)
>
> 53 così queste parole segna a' vivi  
> (so record these words for the living)
>
> 54 del viver ch'è un correre a la morte.  
> (of the life that is a running toward death.)

Beatrice commissions Dante to write. His journey is not a private escape from history: he must return its lessons to people still living within time.

The description of earthly life as a race toward death reverses ordinary assumptions. Those whom Dante addresses are conventionally called alive, yet their existence continually approaches its end. His encounter with the eternal is meant to awaken them to that condition.

These lines also establish the poem's claimed authority. Dante presents himself not simply as an inventor of visions but as a witness charged with transmitting them faithfully.

## 4. The Broken Vessel and the Eagle Without an Heir

> 34 Sappi che 'l vaso che 'l serpente ruppe,  
> (Know that the vessel which the serpent broke,)
>
> 35 fu e non è; ma chi n'ha colpa, creda  
> (was and is not; but let whoever bears the blame believe)
>
> 36 che vendetta di Dio non teme suppe.  
> (that God's vengeance does not fear sops.)

The vessel is the chariot of the preceding canto, an image of the Church. The serpent recalls the dragon that tore part of it away. Beatrice's language also echoes Revelation 17:8, with its description of a beast that was and is not.

The Church's condition is therefore presented as a catastrophic loss of its proper character, not necessarily as the literal extinction of the Church. Its corrupted historical form no longer adequately embodies what it ought to be.

The reference to sops is deliberately earthy and obscure. Traditional commentators connect it with a supposed custom by which a murderer could evade vengeance by eating bread soaked in wine at the victim's grave. Whatever the precise background, the force is clear: no trick, ritual loophole, or worldly protection can defeat divine judgment.

> 37 Non sarà tutto tempo sanza reda  
> (Not forever will it be without an heir,)
>
> 38 l'aguglia che lasciò le penne al carro,  
> (the eagle that left its feathers on the chariot,)
>
> 39 per che divenne mostro e poscia preda;  
> (through which it became a monster and then prey;)

The eagle represents the Roman Empire. Its feathers recall the wealth and temporal privileges bestowed on the Church, traditionally associated with the Donation of Constantine. Dante accepted that supposed donation as historical; it was later exposed as a forgery.

For Dante, imperial enrichment helped deform the Church by drawing it into the pursuit of possessions and political power. The chariot consequently became monstrous and vulnerable to seizure.

Yet the eagle is not simply evil. Beatrice predicts the restoration of an effective imperial heir. Dante's political ideal requires both Church and Empire to fulfill their distinct responsibilities, rather than allowing spiritual authority and temporal ambition to become destructively entangled.

## 5. The Enigma of the Five Hundred, Ten, and Five

> 43 nel quale un cinquecento diece e cinque,  
> (in which a five hundred, ten, and five,)
>
> 44 messo di Dio, anciderà la fuia  
> (a messenger of God, will slay the thieving woman)
>
> 45 con quel gigante che con lei delinque.  
> (together with that giant who commits crimes with her.)

The numerical prophecy is among the poem's most famous unresolved riddles. Five hundred, ten, and five gives 515, written **DXV** in Roman numerals. Rearranging those letters produces **DVX**, the Latin word *dux*, meaning leader.

This supports the expectation of a divinely commissioned ruler who will restore justice. Proposed identifications include Henry VII, whose imperial expedition into Italy inspired Dante's hopes, and other potential political reformers. No identification commands universal agreement.

The thieving woman is the prostitute of the preceding pageant, commonly understood as the corrupted papal institution or curia. The giant represents the French monarchy, especially its coercive relationship with the papacy under Philip IV. Their destruction signifies an end to the criminal partnership of corrupted spiritual authority and predatory secular power.

> 49 ma tosto fier li fatti le Naiade,  
> (but soon the events will be the Naiads,)
>
> 50 che solveranno questo enigma forte  
> (who will solve this difficult enigma)
>
> 51 sanza danno di pecore o di biade.  
> (without harm to sheep or crops.)

Beatrice says that events themselves will interpret her prophecy. This is an important qualification: the prediction is intentionally obscure within the dramatic present.

The Naiads refer to a medieval textual tradition of Ovid's account of the Sphinx. A reading involving Naiads circulated where modern editions identify the solver as the son of Laius, Oedipus. The reference to sheep and crops recalls the devastation associated with the aftermath of the ancient riddle's solution.

Beatrice promises a different outcome. Her enigma will be resolved not through further ruin, but through the establishment of justice. Classical myth supplies the comparison; providential history will surpass it.

## 6. The Sacred Tree: Divine Justice and the Limits of Human Power

> 58 Qualunque ruba quella o quella schianta,  
> (Whoever robs that tree or breaks it,)
>
> 59 con bestemmia di fatto offende a Dio,  
> (with blasphemy in deed offends God,)
>
> 60 che solo a l'uso suo la creò santa.  
> (who created it holy for his use alone.)

The tree belongs first to the story of Eden: it recalls the tree whose fruit God forbade Adam and Eve to eat. Beatrice makes it an image of divine justice and of the limits God places upon created freedom.

To injure it is blasphemy enacted rather than spoken. The offense consists in appropriating something reserved to divine authority.

Within the surrounding political allegory, the tree also bears imperial associations. These meanings need not cancel one another. Dante can use the same tree to connect the original violation of God's command with later violations of the providential order governing Church and Empire.

> 61 Per morder quella, in pena e in disio  
> (For biting that tree, in punishment and longing,)
>
> 62 cinquemilia anni e più l'anima prima  
> (five thousand years and more the first soul)
>
> 63 bramò colui che 'l morso in sé punio.  
> (yearned for him who punished the bite in himself.)

The first soul is Adam; the awaited redeemer is Christ. According to the sacred chronology Dante follows, more than five thousand years separated Adam's sin from the redemption accomplished by Christ.

The final line concentrates the logic of the Atonement: Christ bears in himself the punishment incurred through humanity's disobedience. The tree therefore links the Fall to the Cross.

This immense interval gives weight to Beatrice's warning. Violations of sacred order cannot be dismissed as expedient politics when the first such violation required so costly a remedy.

## 7. A Petrified Mind and an Image Carried Home

> 67 E se stati non fossero acqua d'Elsa  
> (And if they had not been water of the Elsa,)
>
> 68 li pensier vani intorno a la tua mente,  
> (the vain thoughts surrounding your mind,)
>
> 69 e 'l piacer loro un Piramo a la gelsa,  
> (and pleasure in them a Pyramus to the mulberry,)

Beatrice explains Dante's difficulty through two images of lasting alteration.

The Elsa, a Tuscan river, was known for waters that encrusted objects with mineral deposits. Dante's vain thoughts have similarly hardened his understanding. The allusion to Pyramus comes from Ovid: his blood stains the mulberry's fruit, changing its color. Pleasure in mistaken pursuits has not merely distracted Dante; it has colored his perception.

The paired images distinguish intellectual rigidity from the desires that reinforce it. Error becomes especially difficult to escape when people enjoy what prevents them from seeing clearly.

> 76 voglio anco, e se non scritto, almen dipinto,  
> (I wish further, if not written, at least pictured,)
>
> 77 che 'l te ne porti dentro a te per quello  
> (that you carry it away within yourself for the reason)
>
> 78 che si reca il bordon di palma cinto».  
> (that one carries the pilgrim's staff encircled with palm.”)

Since Dante cannot yet fully understand, he must at least retain the image. Beatrice compares this remembrance to a pilgrim's palm-adorned staff, a sign of a completed sacred journey.

The distinction between something written and something pictured suggests different levels of possession: articulated understanding on the one hand, a faithfully preserved impression on the other.

It also illuminates the *Comedy*'s method. Images can convey truths that readers do not immediately exhaust. Like Dante, they may carry the vision in memory before fully grasping it.

## 8. Human Learning and the Paradox of Forgotten Sin

> 85 «Perché conoschi», disse, «quella scuola  
> (“So that you may recognize,” she said, “that school)
>
> 86 c'hai seguitata, e veggi sua dottrina  
> (which you have followed, and see its teaching)
>
> 87 come può seguitar la mia parola;  
> (how it can follow my word;)

Beatrice's difficult speech is intended partly to reveal the limitations of Dante's previous intellectual formation.

The precise school is disputed. It may indicate a philosophical outlook treated as self-sufficient, or more broadly the worldly habits of thought that drew him away from Beatrice. It is unsafe to reduce the passage to a rejection of one named philosopher.

Nor does Dante simply condemn reason: the poem depends deeply upon philosophical reasoning. The criticism concerns reason or learning that mistakes itself for the complete measure of divine truth.

> 97 e se dal fummo foco s'argomenta,  
> (and if fire is inferred from smoke,)
>
> 98 cotesta oblivïon chiaro conchiude  
> (that forgetfulness clearly proves)
>
> 99 colpa ne la tua voglia altrove attenta.  
> (fault in your will directed elsewhere.)

Dante protests that he does not remember abandoning Beatrice. She answers that he has drunk from Lethe, the river that removes the memory of sin.

His forgetfulness is therefore not proof of innocence. Within the spiritual logic of the garden, it is evidence that there was guilt for Lethe to erase. Beatrice's argument turns his defense inside out.

The distinction is subtle: forgiveness and purification do not make past wrongdoing retrospectively innocent. They transform the soul's relation to it, freeing the person from its burden.

## 9. The Two Rivers and the Restoration of Good Memory

> 112 Dinanzi ad esse Ëufratès e Tigri  
> (Before them Euphrates and Tigris)
>
> 113 veder mi parve uscir d'una fontana,  
> (I seemed to see issuing from one spring,)
>
> 114 e, quasi amici, dipartirsi pigri.  
> (and, like friends, parting slowly.)

The divided waters remind Dante of the rivers of Eden in Genesis. They are actually Lethe and Eunoë, whose common source gives Dante's garden both biblical resonance and its own distinctive spiritual geography.

Their gentle separation suggests complementary functions rather than opposition. Lethe removes the memory of sin; Eunoë restores and strengthens the memory of good.

Dante adapts Lethe from classical tradition and gives it a Christian purgatorial role. Eunoë, whose Greek-derived name evokes good-mindedness or good memory, supplies the positive counterpart. Purification requires more than forgetting evil.

> 127 Ma vedi Eünoè che là diriva:  
> (But see Eunoë flowing away there:)
>
> 128 menalo ad esso, e come tu se' usa,  
> (lead him to it, and as you are accustomed,)
>
> 129 la tramortita sua virtù ravviva».  
> (revive his fainting power.”)

Beatrice directs Matelda to complete Dante's preparation. Matelda, the gracious guide of the Earthly Paradise, conducts him to the water and invites Statius to accompany them.

The river restores an enfeebled capacity for good. Redemption is thus not merely subtraction—the removal of guilt, disorder, and painful recollection—but renewal of the soul's affirmative powers.

Dante must be able to recognize and rejoice in goodness before he can ascend into the fuller life of Heaven.

## 10. The Poem's Limit and the Soul's New Beginning

> 139 ma perché piene son tutte le carte  
> (but because all the pages are full)
>
> 140 ordite a questa cantica seconda,  
> (planned for this second canticle,)
>
> 141 non mi lascia più ir lo fren de l'arte.  
> (the bridle of art does not let me go farther.)

Dante says that he would gladly describe the sweetness of drinking from Eunoë, but the formal design of the canticle restrains him.

The bridle is not simply a practical limit on space. It presents poetry as an art of proportion and discipline. The experience exceeds what this portion of the poem can contain, and the poet must respect the boundary of its appointed form.

The ending therefore holds abundance and restraint together: inexhaustible spiritual sweetness is expressed through a carefully measured work.

> 142 Io ritornai da la santissima onda  
> (I returned from the most holy water)
>
> 143 rifatto sì come piante novelle  
> (remade like new plants)
>
> 144 rinovellate di novella fronda,  
> (renewed with new foliage,)
>
> 145 puro e disposto a salire a le stelle.  
> (pure and ready to ascend to the stars.)

The repeated language of newness makes renewal audible. Dante is not merely washed clean; he is remade like vegetation putting forth fresh leaves.

This final plant image answers the canto's wounded sacred tree. Historical humanity continues to violate divine order, but an individual soul can already be restored to fruitful life through repentance and grace.

The last word, *stelle*, closes all three canticles of the *Comedy*. Here the stars mark a threshold. Dante has recovered the innocence and freedom represented by Eden, but his journey is not complete: he is now prepared for the ascent beyond earthly happiness into the vision of God.

## Epilogue — Renewal Before the Ascent

*Purgatorio* XXXIII joins a prophecy of public restoration to the completion of a personal conversion. The Church's corruption will not escape judgment, and Dante's purified soul must carry that warning back to the world.

Yet the canto ends not with punishment but with renewed life. Fear yields to witness, hardened thought begins to receive truth, and the removal of sin is completed by the revival of goodness. Dante reaches the summit of Purgatory not as someone who has finished learning, but as someone finally ready to rise.
