# Hunger Transformed: Allegory and Background in *Purgatorio*, Canto XXIII

On the sixth terrace of Purgatory, Dante encounters souls being purified of gluttony. Their wasted bodies reveal the disorder of their former appetites, but their suffering is neither hopeless nor merely punitive: it teaches desire to seek its proper end.

The canto's emotional center is Dante's meeting with his friend Forese Donati. Their conversation connects bodily hunger with spiritual renewal, private affection with intercessory prayer, and personal repentance with the moral condition of Florence.

## 1. Learning to Use Time

> 4 lo più che padre mi dicea: «Figliuole,  
> (he who was more than a father was saying to me: “My son,)
>
> 5 vienne oramai, ché 'l tempo che n'è imposto  
> (come away now, for the time allotted to us)
>
> 6 più utilmente compartir si vuole».  
> (must be apportioned more usefully.”)

Dante has been staring into the foliage of the strange tree introduced at the end of the preceding canto. Virgil interrupts this absorbed attention and recalls him to the journey.

The description of Virgil as “more than a father” makes moral guidance an expression of affection. He does not merely explain the afterlife: he trains Dante's attention, habits, and use of time.

That last concern is fundamental to Purgatory. Unlike Hell, where punishment never produces improvement, this realm is organized around change. Time offers an opportunity for purification and must therefore be used well. Even an apparently innocent fascination can become a distraction from the pilgrim's larger purpose.

## 2. The Mouth Learns a Different Song

> 10 Ed ecco piangere e cantar s'udìe  
> (And suddenly weeping and singing were heard)
>
> 11 ‘Labïa mëa, Domine’ per modo  
> (‘My lips, Lord’ in a manner)
>
> 12 tal, che diletto e doglia parturìe.  
> (such that it brought forth delight and sorrow.)

The souls sing words from the *Miserere*, Psalm 50 in the Vulgate numbering, Psalm 51 in most modern Bibles. The fuller petition asks God to open the speaker's lips so that the mouth may proclaim divine praise.

This prayer is especially appropriate to gluttony. The mouth formerly devoted to excessive consumption becomes an instrument of confession and worship. Purification does not abolish bodily faculties; it redirects their use.

The combination of delight and sorrow also captures the emotional structure of Purgatory. These souls grieve over sin and experience real pain, yet they know that their suffering leads toward God. Their song is therefore neither simple lamentation nor uncomplicated celebration.

## 3. Wasted Bodies and the Legibility of Sin

> 22 Ne li occhi era ciascuna oscura e cava,  
> (Each was dark and hollow about the eyes,)
>
> 23 palida ne la faccia, e tanto scema  
> (pale in the face, and so wasted)
>
> 24 che da l'ossa la pelle s'informava.  
> (that the skin took its shape from the bones.)

The emaciated souls display a reversal of their earthly excess. Those who pursued food and drink beyond measure now endure hunger and thirst.

This is *contrapasso*: a condition that corresponds meaningfully to the sin being corrected. In Purgatory, however, correspondence serves a cure. Their deprivation retrains appetite rather than simply retaliating against indulgence.

Dante intensifies the description through classical and historical comparisons. Erysichthon, in Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, is punished with insatiable hunger after violating a sacred grove. Dante also recalls the horrific account, transmitted by Josephus, of a mother named Mary who ate her child during the Roman siege of Jerusalem. These examples push hunger beyond ordinary discomfort into the destruction of bodily and social order.

> 31 Parean l'occhiaie anella sanza gemme:  
> (The eye sockets seemed rings without gems:)
>
> 32 chi nel viso de li uomini legge ‘omo’  
> (whoever reads ‘omo’ in human faces)
>
> 33 ben avria quivi conosciuta l'emme.  
> (would clearly have recognized the M here.)

The face becomes something that can be read. According to a medieval visual conceit, the word *omo*, meaning “man” or “human being,” could be discerned in the human face: the eyes supplied the two O's, while the intervening facial structure suggested an M.

Extreme thinness makes that imagined lettering especially visible. The result is unsettling: the souls look scarcely human, yet the sign of their humanity becomes more legible. Their outward beauty has disappeared, but their human identity has not.

Dante's astonishment also raises a literal question: how can souls without ordinary living bodies become thin? The poem postpones the fuller explanation until Canto XXV. Here, emotional recognition comes before physiological understanding.

## 4. Forese Recognized: Friendship Beyond Appearance

> 46 Questa favilla tutta mi raccese  
> (This spark fully rekindled in me)
>
> 47 mia conoscenza a la cangiata labbia,  
> (my recognition of the altered countenance,)
>
> 48 e ravvisai la faccia di Forese.  
> (and I recognized Forese's face.)

Dante cannot identify the wasted figure by sight. Forese's voice provides the “spark” that revives recognition.

Forese Donati was a Florentine poet and a friend of Dante's, who died in 1296. He belonged to the prominent Donati family: his brother Corso became a leading political figure, while his sister Piccarda will appear in *Paradiso*.

The encounter also recalls the *tenzone*, or exchange of insulting sonnets, traditionally attributed to Dante and Forese. Those poems trade accusations involving appetite, poverty, sexual inadequacy, and disreputable behavior. Their precise biographical implications remain uncertain, but their coarse language forms an important background to this tender reunion.

Here, speech restores rather than degrades another person. The old intimacy survives, but its moral direction changes. Dante's recognition of Forese becomes an image of friendship purified of its former corruptions.

## 5. Appetite Under Divine Discipline

> 61 Ed elli a me: «De l'etterno consiglio  
> (And he to me: “From the eternal counsel)
>
> 62 cade vertù ne l'acqua e ne la pianta  
> (power descends into the water and into the tree)
>
> 63 rimasa dietro ond' io sì m'assottiglio.  
> (left behind, through which I grow so thin.)

Forese explains that the tree and water operate through divine power. Their fragrance awakens hunger and thirst, but the souls cannot satisfy those desires by eating and drinking.

The punishment is therefore not merely a natural consequence of going without food. It is a providentially ordered exercise of desire. Things that are good in themselves—fruit, water, bodily nourishment—become instruments through which attachment to excessive pleasure is corrected.

The distinction matters. Dante does not treat eating as sinful. The problem is appetite's refusal of measure.

> 64 Tutta esta gente che piangendo canta  
> (All these people who sing while weeping)
>
> 65 per seguitar la gola oltra misura,  
> (for indulging the appetite beyond measure,)
>
> 66 in fame e 'n sete qui si rifà santa.  
> (in hunger and thirst here become holy again.)

Forese describes the terrace through its result: the souls become holy again. Their identity is not permanently fixed by the vice they once practiced.

The phrase “beyond measure” identifies gluttony as disordered desire rather than ordinary bodily need. Purgatory restores the proper relationship between appetite, reason, and love of God. Hunger becomes educational because it breaks the expectation that every desire must immediately be satisfied.

## 6. Why Pain Can Be Called Joy

> 72 io dico pena, e dovria dir sollazzo,  
> (I say pain, and I ought to say solace,)
>
> 73 ché quella voglia a li alberi ci mena  
> (for that desire leads us to the trees)
>
> 74 che menò Cristo lieto a dire ‘Elì’,  
> (which led Christ willingly to say ‘Eli’,)
>
> 75 quando ne liberò con la sua vena».  
> (when he freed us with his blood.”)

Forese corrects his own vocabulary. What he has called pain should also be called solace or joy, because the souls willingly embrace the means of their purification.

The comparison with Christ supplies the deepest explanation. “Eli” recalls Christ's cry from the cross, reported in the Gospels of Matthew and Mark. The reference to his blood identifies the Crucifixion as an act of liberation.

Dante is not denying the agony of the Passion or suggesting that these souls enjoy suffering simply as suffering. Their joy lies in the end toward which pain leads. Their wills increasingly agree with divine love.

The comparison does not make their purification equivalent to Christ's unique redemptive sacrifice. Rather, their willing endurance follows its pattern: suffering can be accepted lovingly when it serves salvation. Purgatory transforms not only what the souls desire but also how they understand what happens to them.

## 7. Repentance, Delay, and Dante's Surprise

> 82 come se' tu qua sù venuto ancora?  
> (how have you already come up here?)
>
> 83 Io ti credea trovar là giù di sotto,  
> (I thought I would find you down below,)
>
> 84 dove tempo per tempo si ristora».  
> (where time is made good by time.”)

Within the poem's setting in 1300, Forese has been dead for less than five years. Dante understands that his friend repented only near the end of his life, and therefore expects to find him among the late repentant in Ante-Purgatory.

There, delayed conversion entails a corresponding delay before active purification can begin. “Time is made good by time” gives that correspondence its compact formulation: time neglected in earthly life must be answered by time spent waiting.

Dante's question distinguishes repentance from the completion of purification. A final sincere turning toward God can save a person without instantly removing every consequence of a disordered life.

Yet Forese has already reached a high terrace. His progress reveals that the spiritual order is not a closed accounting system in which each person acts entirely alone.

## 8. Nella's Prayer and the Power of Love

> 85 Ond' elli a me: «Sì tosto m'ha condotto  
> (Then he to me: “So swiftly has brought me)
>
> 86 a ber lo dolce assenzo d'i martìri  
> (to drink the sweet wormwood of these torments)
>
> 87 la Nella mia con suo pianger dirotto.  
> (my Nella with her abundant weeping.)

The explanation is Forese's widow, Nella. Her grief, joined to prayer, has helped him advance.

The “sweet wormwood” condenses the canto's understanding of purification. Wormwood is bitter; these torments are sweet because they heal. Their value depends not on immediate sensation but on their saving purpose.

Nella's loving presence also revises the atmosphere of the earlier comic sonnets, in which Forese's married life had supplied material for mockery. The wife who could become an object of coarse joking is now honored as an agent of spiritual rescue.

> 88 Con suoi prieghi devoti e con sospiri  
> (With her devout prayers and with sighs)
>
> 89 tratto m'ha de la costa ove s'aspetta,  
> (she has drawn me from the slope where one waits,)
>
> 90 e liberato m'ha de li altri giri.  
> (and has freed me from the other circles.)

These lines express the medieval Christian doctrine of prayers offered for the dead. The living can assist souls undergoing purification through intercession.

Nella does not overturn divine justice or purchase an exception to it. Her charity operates within a spiritual community whose members remain connected across death. Forese's advance depends partly on another person's faithful love.

The contrast with gluttony is significant. Excessive appetite encloses the self within its own demands; prayer reaches outward for another's good.

## 9. Florence as a Moral Wilderness

> 94 ché la Barbagia di Sardigna assai  
> (for the Barbagia of Sardinia, by far,)
>
> 95 ne le femmine sue più è pudica  
> (is more modest in its women)
>
> 96 che la Barbagia dov' io la lasciai.  
> (than the Barbagia where I left her.)

Forese's praise of Nella turns into condemnation of Florence. Barbagia, a mountainous region of Sardinia, carried associations of roughness and supposedly uncivilized customs for Dante's audience. Forese provocatively applies its name to his own city.

The comparison reverses Florence's assumptions about cultural superiority. A wealthy, sophisticated city may be morally less civilized than the people it dismisses as backward.

This rhetoric depends on medieval regional and gender stereotypes, which should be recognized rather than adopted uncritically. Forese's immediate target is the immodest dress of Florentine women, set against Nella's fidelity and devotion. He anticipates public religious prohibitions against revealing clothing.

> 106 Ma se le svergognate fosser certe  
> (But if the shameless women were certain)
>
> 107 di quel che 'l ciel veloce loro ammanna,  
> (of what swift heaven prepares for them,)
>
> 108 già per urlare avrian le bocche aperte;  
> (they would already have their mouths open to howl;)

The speech becomes prophetic. Forese foresees suffering soon enough to overtake the present generation.

The passage does not securely identify one particular future disaster. Its force comes from the expectation of approaching judgment against civic corruption.

This shift from domestic affection to public denunciation is characteristic of the *Comedy*. A private virtue exposes a collective failure. Nella's quiet faithfulness becomes a standard by which Florence's conspicuous display is found wanting.

## 10. A Shared Past and a New Direction

> 115 Per ch'io a lui: «Se tu riduci a mente  
> (Therefore I to him: “If you bring back to mind)
>
> 116 qual fosti meco, e qual io teco fui,  
> (what you were with me, and what I was with you,)
>
> 117 ancor fia grave il memorar presente.  
> (remembering now will still be painful.)

When Dante finally explains his journey, he begins with their shared past. The balanced wording makes the admission mutual: each participated in the other's former way of life.

The lines do not specify every offense, and they should not be treated as a detailed autobiographical confession. They nevertheless establish a moral distance between past companionship and present understanding.

Memory itself has changed. What may once have seemed pleasurable or amusing now causes pain. This is one sign of repentance: the past is not erased, but its meaning is reassessed.

> 127 Tanto dice di farmi sua compagna  
> (He says he will keep me in his company so far)
>
> 128 che io sarò là dove fia Beatrice;  
> (that I shall be where Beatrice will be;)
>
> 129 quivi convien che sanza lui rimagna.  
> (there I must remain without him.)

Dante identifies Virgil as the guide who turned him away from that former life and led him through Hell. Yet Virgil's guidance has a limit: he will bring Dante to Beatrice, after which Dante must continue without him.

Allegorically, Virgil is associated with human reason and classical wisdom, while Beatrice is associated with revelation and the guidance of divine grace. Neither character is exhausted by that symbolic role, but the succession clarifies the journey's direction. Moral reasoning can diagnose disorder and train desire; it cannot by itself complete the ascent to God.

The canto ends by identifying the other companion as the soul whose release caused the mountain to tremble: Statius. In Dante's imaginative account, this Roman poet has completed his purification. His presence offers a living assurance that the process Forese is undergoing will eventually end in freedom.

## Epilogue — Desire Restored Through Love

Canto XXIII presents gluttony as desire that has lost its measure, and purification as the recovery of desire's proper direction. Hunger becomes healing, the consuming mouth becomes a praising mouth, and suffering becomes bearable through its promised end.

The same transformation governs the human relationships of the canto. Dante and Forese reconsider their friendship; Nella's prayers bridge the distance between life and death; Virgil guides Dante toward a wisdom beyond his own. Against Florence's public vanity, the canto places the quieter powers of repentance, faithful affection, and love that seeks another's good.
