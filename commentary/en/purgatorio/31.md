# Confession, Forgetting, and Renewed Vision in *Purgatorio* XXXI

At the summit of Mount Purgatory, Dante has reached the Earthly Paradise: the garden of Eden, where humanity's original innocence can be recovered. Yet reaching the garden does not mean that his purification is complete. Before he can ascend to heaven, he must acknowledge how he has misdirected his love.

Beatrice, who appeared in the preceding canto, now questions him across the sacred river Lethe. She is both the woman Dante loved, who died in 1290, and a figure of divine wisdom and revelation. Her severity joins personal reproach to spiritual instruction. The canto moves from confession and shame through cleansing to a renewed ability to see.

## 1. Confession: Giving Voice to What God Already Knows

> 7 Era la mia virtù tanto confusa,  
> (My power was so confounded,)
>
> 8 che la voce si mosse, e pria si spense  
> (that my voice stirred, and died away before)
>
> 9 che da li organi suoi fosse dischiusa.  
> (it could be released from its organs.)

Dante's first obstacle is not ignorance but the inability to admit what he knows. His faculties fail under Beatrice's accusation: his voice begins to move but never becomes audible speech.

The physical failure matters. Repentance is not presented as an abstract conclusion reached by the intellect. It disturbs the whole person—voice, breath, tears, and bodily strength. Shortly afterward, Dante compares himself to an overstrained crossbow that breaks when fired: emotional pressure destroys his ability to answer forcefully.

Beatrice nevertheless requires confession, even though his wrongdoing is already known to God.

> 40 Ma quando scoppia de la propria gota  
> (But when from one's own cheek bursts forth)
>
> 41 l'accusa del peccato, in nostra corte  
> (the accusation of sin, in our court)
>
> 42 rivolge sé contra 'l taglio la rota.  
> (the wheel turns itself against the cutting edge.)

The difficult image of the wheel is generally understood as a grindstone whose altered movement blunts rather than sharpens the blade. Voluntary confession diminishes the severity of judgment.

The scene draws on Christian penitential practice: contrition must become acknowledgment. Confession does not supply an omniscient judge with missing information. It changes the sinner's relationship to the truth, replacing concealment and self-defense with consent.

## 2. The False Pleasure of Present Things

> 34 Piangendo dissi: «Le presenti cose  
> (Weeping, I said: “Present things)
>
> 35 col falso lor piacer volser miei passi,  
> (with their false pleasure turned my steps,)
>
> 36 tosto che 'l vostro viso si nascose».  
> (as soon as your face was hidden”.)

Dante identifies his error as a turning of his steps. The language makes moral failure a deviation in a journey: he has not ceased to desire happiness, but has followed misleading guides toward it.

The disappearance of Beatrice's face refers to her death. While she lived, his love for her could direct him beyond immediate gratification. Afterward, he became absorbed in things whose pleasure promised more than it could deliver.

These “present things” need not be reduced to a single affair or a particular episode in Dante's biography. They encompass the attractions of earthly life when treated as ultimate goods. The problem is not that created things possess no goodness, but that their limited goodness cannot satisfy an unlimited desire.

Beatrice's preceding questions expose the irrationality of his choice: what obstacle actually prevented him from pursuing the highest good, and what superior advantage did competing objects offer?

## 3. Beatrice's Death Should Have Taught Ascent

> 55 Ben ti dovevi, per lo primo strale  
> (Indeed you ought, at the first arrow)
>
> 56 de le cose fallaci, levar suso  
> (of deceptive things, to have risen upward)
>
> 57 di retro a me che non era più tale.  
> (behind me, who was no longer such.)

The “first arrow” is the devastating lesson delivered by Beatrice's death. If even the greatest beauty Dante had known could disappear, he should have recognized the instability of every mortal attraction.

Beatrice reverses his interpretation of bereavement. He treated her absence as an occasion to seek substitutes; he should instead have followed her beyond the realm of perishable things. She is now “no longer such”—no longer subject to the mortality and instability that make earthly promises unreliable.

This is a central transformation of courtly love. Love for a beautiful woman is not simply rejected as a distraction from God. Properly understood, it becomes a beginning whose fulfillment lies beyond possession of her earthly body.

The upward movement also anticipates the *Paradiso*. Dante must learn to let beauty raise him rather than bind him to what passes away.

## 4. The Experienced Bird and the Visible Snare

> 61 Novo augelletto due o tre aspetta;  
> (A young little bird waits for two or three;)
>
> 62 ma dinanzi da li occhi d'i pennuti  
> (but before the eyes of feathered birds)
>
> 63 rete si spiega indarno o si saetta».  
> (a net is spread in vain, or an arrow is shot”.)

The young bird waits for repeated attacks before learning caution. An experienced bird, by contrast, should recognize a trap being set before its eyes. The image recalls Proverbs 1:17, where spreading a net in the sight of a bird exemplifies a futile act.

Beatrice's point is that Dante cannot claim perpetual innocence. Her death has already taught him that mortal pleasures fail. Further entanglement therefore reveals a refusal to apply what experience has shown him.

Immediately before this comparison, she mentions a *pargoletta*, a young girl, among the attractions that might have weighed down his wings. The word may recall figures in Dante's lyric poetry, but its precise biographical reference is uncertain. Its allegorical function is clear: a new fascination must not become an excuse to repeat an old error.

The bird imagery gives desire two possible outcomes. Love can supply wings for ascent, or attachment can make those wings useless.

## 5. The Beard: Shame Without the Excuse of Childhood

> 68 per udir se' dolente, alza la barba,  
> (through hearing you are sorrowful, lift your beard,)
>
> 69 e prenderai più doglia riguardando».  
> (and you will take on greater sorrow by looking”.)

Dante stands like a chastened child, silent and looking at the ground. Beatrice's command deliberately calls attention to his beard rather than merely his face.

The word exposes the inadequacy of his childlike posture. He is a grown man, responsible for choices that cannot be excused as youthful inexperience. Dante recognizes the sting of the expression when he finally raises his head.

There is also a progression from hearing to seeing. Her words have already caused pain, but the sight of her will make his loss and error more fully intelligible. This painful education of vision prepares for the canto's conclusion, when looking will become a source of joy rather than accusation.

Beatrice's severity is therefore corrective, not vindictive. She makes him confront what his lowered eyes would avoid.

## 6. The Griffin and the Collapse of Self-Deception

> 80 vider Beatrice volta in su la fiera  
> (saw Beatrice turned toward the beast)
>
> 81 ch'è sola una persona in due nature.  
> (that is a single person in two natures.)

When Dante looks up, Beatrice is looking toward the griffin. This fabulous creature, combining eagle and lion, appeared in the procession of the preceding cantos. Here its meaning becomes especially explicit: it represents Christ.

The formula “a single person in two natures” invokes Christian teaching about Christ as fully divine and fully human. The griffin's composite form supplies an image of distinction within unity, although the theological claim is not that Christ is a mixture of two partial beings.

Beatrice's gaze is significant. She is not the ultimate object of the spiritual journey; her own attention is directed toward Christ. Her beauty mediates a truth beyond herself.

> 88 Tanta riconoscenza il cor mi morse,  
> (Such recognition bit into my heart,)
>
> 89 ch'io caddi vinto; e quale allora femmi,  
> (that I fell overcome; and what I then became,)
>
> 90 salsi colei che la cagion mi porse.  
> (she knows who gave me the cause.)

Here *riconoscenza* means recognition or acknowledgment, especially the recognition of guilt. Seeing Beatrice's transfigured beauty makes Dante understand the poverty of the attractions that diverted him.

He collapses under that understanding. His fainting is not itself the cleansing, but the culmination of contrition: self-deception has become impossible. The woman whose reproach caused his anguish knows what his own interrupted consciousness cannot report.

## 7. Lethe: Purification Rather Than Escape

> 98 ‘Asperges me’ sì dolcemente udissi,  
> (‘You will sprinkle me’ was heard so sweetly,)
>
> 99 che nol so rimembrar, non ch'io lo scriva.  
> (that I cannot recall it, much less write it.)

When Dante recovers, Matelda—the woman he encountered earlier in the Earthly Paradise—is drawing him through Lethe. The singing of *Asperges me* identifies the action as purification.

The words come from the penitential Psalm 50 in the Vulgate, Psalm 51 in most modern numbering. Its prayer asks God to cleanse the sinner, making the soul whiter than snow. The phrase also has a liturgical association with the sprinkling of holy water.

Dante joins this biblical prayer to a river inherited from classical mythology. Classical Lethe brings forgetfulness; in Dante's Eden, its power is specifically ordered toward the removal of the memory of sin.

> 100 La bella donna ne le braccia aprissi;  
> (The beautiful woman opened her arms;)
>
> 101 abbracciommi la testa e mi sommerse  
> (she clasped my head and submerged me)
>
> 102 ove convenne ch'io l'acqua inghiottissi.  
> (where I had to swallow the water.)

The immersion carries baptismal resonance, though it is not a repetition of sacramental baptism. Dante must experience the water fully, including drinking it.

Crucially, Lethe comes after confession. Forgetting is not a way of evading responsibility. He has first remembered, admitted, and repented; only then can the burden of sinful memory be removed.

The canto thus distinguishes healing from denial. Innocence is restored through truth, not by pretending that the wrongdoing never mattered.

## 8. The Seven Virtues and the Education of Sight

> 106 «Noi siam qui ninfe e nel ciel siamo stelle;  
> (“We are nymphs here and stars in heaven;)
>
> 107 pria che Beatrice discendesse al mondo,  
> (before Beatrice descended into the world,)
>
> 108 fummo ordinate a lei per sue ancelle.  
> (we were appointed to her as her handmaids.)

Matelda delivers Dante into the dance of four women representing the cardinal virtues: prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance.

Their double identity as nymphs and stars brings classical imagery into a Christian moral order. Their stellar form recalls the four stars Dante saw near the beginning of the *Purgatorio*, associated with the virtues possessed by humanity in its original innocence.

Calling themselves Beatrice's handmaids places moral excellence in the service of divine wisdom. The virtues prepare a person for revelation; they do not compete with it.

> 109 Merrenti a li occhi suoi; ma nel giocondo  
> (We shall lead you to her eyes; but in the joyful)
>
> 110 lume ch'è dentro aguzzeranno i tuoi  
> (light that is within, yours will be sharpened by)
>
> 111 le tre di là, che miran più profondo».  
> (the three over there, who gaze more deeply”.)

The remaining three women are the theological virtues: faith, hope, and charity. The distinction marks two related levels of spiritual preparation.

The cardinal virtues guide conduct and bring Dante before Beatrice. The theological virtues enable deeper penetration into the truth her eyes disclose. Moral discipline is necessary, but the fullest vision also requires gifts that orient the soul directly toward God.

## 9. Beatrice's Eyes as a Mirror of Christ

> 121 Come in lo specchio il sol, non altrimenti  
> (As the sun in a mirror, in no other way)
>
> 122 la doppia fiera dentro vi raggiava,  
> (the double beast shone within them,)
>
> 123 or con altri, or con altri reggimenti.  
> (now with one, now with another aspect.)

The griffin is reflected in Beatrice's eyes. Its reflected appearance alternates, disclosing the distinction between the two natures symbolized by its animal forms.

The image makes her eyes instruments of understanding. They do not merely attract Dante emotionally; they reveal Christ. Her beauty has become inseparable from the truth toward which it directs him.

Dante emphasizes the mystery by contrasting the changing reflection with its unchanged source.

> 125 quando vedea la cosa in sé star queta,  
> (when I saw the thing itself remain still,)
>
> 126 e ne l'idolo suo si trasmutava.  
> (and in its image it was changing.)

The griffin itself does not alternate between being one creature and another. The variation occurs in its image. This protects the underlying unity while dramatizing the limits of Dante's perception: he apprehends distinct aspects successively rather than comprehending the mystery all at once.

The episode also refines the canto's treatment of desire.

> 128 l'anima mia gustava di quel cibo  
> (my soul was tasting that food)
>
> 129 che, saziando di sé, di sé asseta,  
> (which, satisfying with itself, makes one thirst for itself,)

Earthly false pleasures provoke desire without fulfilling their promises. Spiritual nourishment satisfies while awakening further desire.

This is not the frustration Dante confessed earlier. It is an expanding capacity for enjoyment: receiving truth makes the soul more able, and more eager, to receive it.

## 10. The Unveiled Smile and the Limit of Poetry

> 136 Per grazia fa noi grazia che disvele  
> (By grace grant us the grace that you unveil)
>
> 137 a lui la bocca tua, sì che discerna  
> (your mouth to him, so that he may discern)
>
> 138 la seconda bellezza che tu cele».  
> (the second beauty that you conceal”.)

The three theological virtues ask Beatrice to unveil her mouth. After the revelation through her eyes comes the revelation of her smile, the “second beauty.”

Eyes and smile are recurrent features of Beatrice's spiritual significance. Here the movement from one to the other suggests that understanding culminates not in bare knowledge but in radiant delight.

The repeated appeal to grace matters. Dante cannot simply claim this vision as a reward owed to his effort. The virtues themselves petition for it as a gift.

> 139 O isplendor di viva luce etterna,  
> (O splendor of living eternal light,)

Beatrice's unveiled beauty prompts an invocation rather than an adequate description. The language recalls biblical descriptions of divine Wisdom as the radiance of everlasting light.

In the remaining lines, Dante invokes Parnassus and its poetic spring, traditional emblems of classical inspiration. Yet even the most accomplished poet would be overwhelmed by the task of representing this revelation.

The canto ends by acknowledging a boundary of language. Poetry can guide the reader toward the encounter and communicate its transforming effects, but it cannot fully reproduce what Dante sees.

## Epilogue — Love Purified into Vision

*Purgatorio* XXXI transforms the reunion of Dante and Beatrice into a drama of spiritual restoration. Confession breaks self-deception; repentance corrects the direction of desire; Lethe removes the burden of sin after it has been acknowledged.

The virtues then prepare Dante for a different kind of looking. Beatrice is no longer simply the beloved whose loss he mourns: her eyes reveal Christ, and her smile communicates the joy of divine wisdom.

The canto's lesson is not that earthly love must be extinguished. It must be purified—so that beauty ceases to imprison desire and becomes a path toward its true fulfillment.
