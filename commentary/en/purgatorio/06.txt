When the game of zara breaks up,
the one who loses remains sorrowful,
repeating the throws, and sadly learns;
with the other all the people go away;
one goes ahead, and another catches him from behind,
and another at his side brings himself to his notice;
he does not stop, and listens to this one and that;
whoever he holds his hand out to presses him no more;
and thus he defends himself from the throng.
Such was I in that dense crowd,
turning toward them, here and there, my face,
and by promising I freed myself from it.
There was the Aretine who from the fierce arms
of Ghin di Tacco met his death,
and the other who drowned while running in pursuit.
There with outstretched hands was praying
Federigo Novello, and he of Pisa
who made good Marzucco show his strength.
I saw Count Orso and the soul divided
from its body through spite and envy,
as he said, not for any fault committed;
I mean Pierre de la Brosse; and let her take heed here,
while she is on this side, the lady of Brabant,
so that on this account she may not belong to a worse flock.
When I was free of all
those shades who prayed only that others pray,
so that their becoming holy might be hastened,
I began: “It seems that you deny to me,
O my light, expressly in some passage,
that prayer can bend a decree of heaven;
and these people pray for just this:
would their hope then be vain,
or is your saying not quite clear to me?”
And he to me: “My writing is plain;
and the hope of these does not fail,
if one considers well with a sound mind;
for the summit of judgment is not lowered
because a fire of love accomplishes in an instant
what whoever waits here must satisfy;
and where I established that point,
no deficiency was made good by praying,
because the prayer was separated from God.
Yet on so deep a doubt
do not settle, unless she tells you
who shall be a light between truth and intellect.
I do not know whether you understand: I mean Beatrice;
you will see her above, upon the summit
of this mountain, smiling and happy.”
And I: “Master, let us go with greater haste,
for I no longer grow weary as before,
and see now how the mountain casts its shadow.”
“We shall go forward with this daylight,”
he replied, “as far as we now can;
but the matter is otherwise than you suppose.
Before you are up there, you will see return
him who already hides behind the slope,
so that you no longer break his rays.
But see there a soul that, placed
all alone, looks toward us:
it will show us the quickest way.”
We came to it: O Lombard soul,
how you stood proud and disdainful
and in the movement of your eyes dignified and slow!
It said nothing to us,
but let us go on, only watching
in the manner of a lion when it rests.
Yet Virgil drew near to it, asking
that it show us the best ascent;
and it did not answer his question,
but about our country and our life
it asked us; and the gentle guide was beginning
“Mantua . . . ”, and the shade, all withdrawn into itself,
rose toward him from the place where it had been,
saying: “O Mantuan, I am Sordello
of your land!”; and each embraced the other.
Ah, enslaved Italy, lodging-place of sorrow,
ship without a helmsman in a great storm,
not mistress of provinces, but a brothel!
That noble soul was so ready,
merely at the sweet sound of its native land,
to welcome its fellow citizen there;
and now within you they do not remain without war,
your living people, and one gnaws at another
among those whom one wall and one moat enclose.
Search, wretched one, along the shores
of your seas, and then look within your breast,
whether any part of you enjoys peace.
What good is it that he repaired your bridle,
Justinian, if the saddle is empty?
Without it the shame would be less.
Ah, people who ought to be devout,
and let Caesar sit in the saddle,
if you rightly understand what God indicates to you,
see how this beast has grown fierce
through not being corrected by the spurs,
since you laid your hand upon the bridle.
O German Albert, who abandon
her who has become untamed and savage,
and ought to straddle her saddlebows,
may just judgment fall from the stars
upon your blood, and may it be strange and manifest,
such that your successor may fear it!
For you and your father have allowed,
held back by greed over there,
the garden of the empire to lie waste.
Come to see the Montecchi and Cappelletti,
the Monaldi and Filippeschi, heedless man:
those already in sorrow, and these in apprehension!
Come, cruel one, come, and see the oppression
of your nobles, and heal their afflictions;
and you will see how dark is Santafiora!
Come to see your Rome, which weeps
widowed and alone, and day and night calls:
“My Caesar, why do you not keep me company?”.
Come to see how much the people love one another!
and if no pity for us moves you,
come to feel shame for your reputation.
And if I am permitted, O highest Jove
who were crucified on earth for us,
are your just eyes turned elsewhere?
Or is it a preparation that in the abyss
of your counsel you make for some good
wholly cut off from our discernment?
For the cities of Italy are all full
of tyrants, and a Marcellus becomes
every peasant who comes forward taking sides.
My Florence, you may indeed be content
with this digression, which does not touch you,
thanks to your people, who are so resourceful.
Many have justice in their hearts, and slowly it flies
so as not to come to the bow without deliberation;
but your people have it on the tips of their tongues.
Many refuse the common burden;
but your people eagerly answer
without being called, and cry: “I take it upon myself!”
Now rejoice, for you have good reason:
you rich, you at peace, and you wise!
If I speak the truth, the outcome does not hide it.
Athens and Lacedaemon, which made
the ancient laws and were so civilized,
made but a small gesture toward living well
compared with you, who make such subtle
provisions that by the middle of November
what you spin in October does not last.
How many times, within the time you remember,
law, coinage, office, and custom
have you changed, and renewed your members!
And if you remember well and see clearly,
you will see yourself resembling that sick woman
who cannot find rest upon the feathers,
but by turning over seeks relief from her pain.
