After we were inside the threshold of the gate
which the souls' misguided love leaves unused,
because it makes the crooked way appear straight,
I heard it close again with a clang;
and if I had turned my eyes toward it,
what worthy excuse would there have been for the fault?
We climbed through a cleft in the rock,
which wound now to one side, now to the other,
just like the wave that retreats and draws near.
“Here one must use a little skill,”
my guide began, “in drawing close
now here, now there, to the side that draws away.”
And this made our steps slow,
so that the waning moon had first
returned to its bed to lie down again,
before we were outside that needle's eye;
but when we were free and in the open
up where the mountain draws back inward,
I weary and both of us uncertain
of our way, we stopped upon a level place
more solitary than roads through deserts.
From its edge, where it borders the void,
to the foot of the high cliff that rises still,
a human body would measure it three times;
and as far as my eye could spread its wings,
now on the left and now on the right,
this ledge appeared to me to be the same.
Our feet had not yet moved up there,
when I perceived that encircling cliff,
which offered no way of ascent,
to be of white marble and adorned
with carvings such that not only Polyclitus,
but nature herself would there be put to shame.
The angel who came to earth with the decree
of the peace wept for through many years,
which opened heaven from its long prohibition,
appeared before us so lifelike
carved there in a gentle gesture,
that he did not seem a silent image.
One would have sworn that he was saying ‘Hail!’;
for there was portrayed that lady
who turned the key to open the love on high;
and she had this utterance imprinted in her bearing,
‘Behold the handmaid of God’, precisely
as a figure is stamped into wax.
“Do not keep your mind on one place alone,”
said the gentle master, who had me
on the side where people have their hearts.
So I turned my gaze, and saw
behind Mary, on that side
where stood the one who urged me on,
another story set into the rock;
so I passed beyond Virgil, and drew near,
so that it might be clearly before my eyes.
There was carved there in the marble itself
the cart and the oxen, drawing the holy ark,
because of which an unassigned office is feared.
In front appeared a crowd; and all of it,
divided into seven choirs, made my two senses
say, the one ‘No’, the other ‘Yes, they sing’.
Likewise, at the smoke of the incense
that was portrayed there, my eyes and nose
disagreed in their yes and no.
There before the blessed vessel went,
dancing with his garments raised, the humble psalmist,
and more and less than a king was he in that circumstance.
Opposite, portrayed at a window
of a great palace, Michal looked on
like a woman scornful and sorrowful.
I moved my feet from the place where I stood,
to view from close at hand another story,
which gleamed white behind Michal.
There was depicted the lofty glory
of the Roman ruler, whose worth
moved Gregory to his great victory;
I speak of the emperor Trajan;
and a poor widow stood at his bridle,
her bearing full of tears and grief.
Around him the place appeared thronged and full
of horsemen, and the eagles upon the gold
above them seemed to move in the wind.
The poor woman amid all these
seemed to say: “Lord, grant me vengeance
for my son who is dead, for whom my heart grieves”;
and he to answer her: “Now wait
until I return”; and she: “My lord,”
like one in whom grief urges haste,
“if you do not return?”; and he: “Whoever takes my place
will grant it to you”; and she: “Another's good,
what will it be to you, if you neglect your own?”;
whereupon he: “Now take comfort; for it is fitting
that I discharge my duty before I move:
justice wills it, and compassion holds me back.”
He who never saw anything new
produced this visible speech,
new to us because it is not found here.
While I delighted in looking upon
the images of so many acts of humility,
dear to behold also for their maker's sake,
“Look, on this side, though their steps are slow,”
murmured the poet, “many people:
these will direct us to the lofty stairs.”
My eyes, which were content to gaze
to see new things for which they long,
were not slow in turning toward him.
Yet I do not wish, reader, that you lose heart
in your good resolve through hearing
how God wills that the debt be paid.
Do not attend to the form of the torment:
think of what follows; think that at worst
it cannot go beyond the great sentence.
I began: “Master, what I see
moving toward us does not seem to me to be people,
and I know not what it is, so confused is my sight.”
And he to me: “The grievous condition
of their torment curls them toward the ground,
so that my eyes at first struggled with it.
But look steadily there, and disentangle
with your sight what comes beneath those stones:
already you can discern how each beats his breast.”
O proud Christians, wretched and weary,
who, impaired in the sight of the mind,
place confidence in backward steps,
do you not perceive that we are worms
born to form the angelic butterfly,
which flies to justice without defenses?
Why does your spirit soar so high,
since you are but imperfect insects,
like a worm in which development fails?
As, to support a floor or roof,
as a corbel sometimes a figure
is seen drawing its knees to its chest,
which from the unreal makes real distress
arise in whoever sees it; thus fashioned
I saw them, when I paid close attention.
It is true that they were bent more and less
according as they had more and less upon their backs;
and the one who showed most patience in his bearing,
weeping seemed to say: ‘I can bear no more’.
