# Learning to Love Another's Good: Allegory and Background in *Purgatorio* XIII

In Canto XIII, Dante and Virgil enter the second terrace of Purgatory, where envy is purified. Here the souls who once suffered at another person's happiness must learn to share in it. Their education takes place through blindness, mutual dependence, prayer, and examples of generous love.

The canto also introduces Sapìa, a woman from Siena whose envy became so destructive that she rejoiced in her own city's defeat. Through her story, Dante connects a private moral failing with the rivalries of Italian civic life—and contrasts both with the fellowship of heaven.

## 1. A Landscape Without Images

> 7 Ombra non lì è né segno che si paia:  
> (No image is there, nor any mark that shows:)
>
> 8 parsi la ripa e parsi la via schietta  
> (the cliff appears bare, and bare appears the path)
>
> 9 col livido color de la petraia.  
> (with the livid colour of the rocky ground.)

The second terrace contrasts sharply with the first. On the terrace of pride, carved images taught humility and displayed the consequences of arrogance. Here there are no such visible lessons: the rock face and pathway are bare.

The “livid” colour suggests bruising and the discoloured pallor traditionally associated with envy. The landscape thus reflects the sickness being healed. Envy turns another person's prosperity into one's own distress; its world is correspondingly drained of warmth.

The absence of images also prepares for the terrace's distinctive method of instruction. Its inhabitants cannot see, so their moral education must come through hearing. Dante changes not merely the scenery but the sense through which truth is received.

## 2. Following the Sun: Reason Seeking Light

> 19 Tu scaldi il mondo, tu sovr' esso luci;  
> (You warm the world, you shine upon it;)
>
> 20 s'altra ragione in contrario non ponta,  
> (unless another reason urges otherwise,)
>
> 21 esser dien sempre li tuoi raggi duci».  
> (your rays must always be our guides.”)

Finding no one immediately available to give directions, Virgil turns toward the sun. On the practical level, its position helps him choose the way around the mountain. Allegorically, the sun suggests the divine illumination that makes right judgment possible.

Virgil, the poem's principal representative of human reason, does not claim complete knowledge. His qualification—unless another reason directs otherwise—makes his guidance provisional and attentive rather than absolute.

The sun also provides an implicit contrast with envy. It warms and illuminates the world without becoming poorer through giving. Envy imagines good as something diminished when another possesses it; sunlight offers an image of goodness freely shared.

## 3. Unseen Voices: Charity Against Envy

> 29 ‘Vinum non habent’ altamente disse,  
> (‘They have no wine’ it said aloud,)

The first flying voice repeats Mary's words at the wedding at Cana in John 2:3. Seeing that the wine has run out, she brings the hosts' difficulty to Jesus, whose first public miracle follows.

Her words exemplify attentive charity. Mary notices another person's need without being asked and without seeking advantage for herself. Where envy watches others for an opportunity to resent or diminish them, charity watches for an opportunity to help.

> 32 per allungarsi, un'altra ‘I' sono Oreste’  
> (through drawing farther away, another ‘I am Orestes’)
>
> 33 passò gridando, e anco non s'affisse.  
> (passed crying out, and likewise did not stop.)

The second example comes from classical friendship. In the story of Orestes and Pylades, Pylades claims to be Orestes in order to die in his friend's place, while Orestes insists on his own identity. Their competing declarations express a willingness to surrender life rather than preserve it at a friend's expense.

Dante places this pagan example beside Mary's biblical one. Both overturn envy's possessiveness: the good of another becomes something to protect, even at a cost to oneself.

> 36 dicendo: ‘Amate da cui male aveste’.  
> (saying: ‘Love those from whom you received harm’.)

The third voice recalls Christ's command to love one's enemies. The examples progressively widen the reach of love: concern for people in need, self-sacrifice for a friend, and charity toward those who have caused injury.

Virgil explains that these loving examples are the cords of the “whip” that corrects envy. Their force is not simply punitive: they drive the will toward its neglected good. He also anticipates a “bridle” of contrary examples, warnings that will restrain the soul from returning to its sin.

## 4. Learning Fellowship Through Dependence

> 58 Di vil ciliccio mi parean coperti,  
> (They seemed to me covered in coarse haircloth,)
>
> 59 e l'un sofferia l'altro con la spalla,  
> (and one supported another with the shoulder,)
>
> 60 e tutti da la ripa eran sofferti.  
> (and all were supported by the cliff.)

The envious wear penitential haircloth and sit leaning against one another. Their posture reverses the isolation of envy. Those who once treated a neighbour's good as a threat now receive support from their neighbours and provide it in return.

Their common dependence is crucial. Purgatory does not merely make each soul suffer separately until a debt is paid; it retrains relationships. These penitents are learning to exist as members of a community.

Their prayers reinforce the same lesson. They invoke Mary, Michael, Peter, and all the saints, placing themselves within a fellowship larger than any earthly faction. Dante compares their appearance to blind beggars gathered at places of religious pardon. The comparison brings the spiritual lesson into ordinary experience: vulnerability calls others into compassion.

Dante himself weeps at the sight. His response is already an answer to envy, because he participates in another's suffering rather than enjoying it.

## 5. Sewn Eyes: Correcting the Envious Gaze

> 70 ché a tutti un fil di ferro i cigli fóra  
> (for an iron wire pierces the eyelids of them all)
>
> 71 e cusce sì, come a sparvier selvaggio  
> (and sews them together, as with a wild sparrowhawk)
>
> 72 si fa però che queto non dimora.  
> (is done because it will not remain still.)

The punishment is a striking example of *contrapasso*: an outward condition reveals and corrects the inward character of the sin. Envy is associated with corrupted sight—with looking at another person's success resentfully. Its Latin name, *invidia*, is linked to *invidere*, to look upon with hostility or envy.

The eyes that once misjudged another's good are temporarily closed. Yet this is not final darkness. The penitents are being prepared for the vision of God.

Dante compares the stitching to the practice of temporarily sewing shut a captured hawk's eyelids during training, often called seeling. The image is harsh, but its purpose is disciplinary: restless vision must become ordered attention.

The encounter also examines Dante's own use of sight. He feels that observing people who cannot see him may be an affront to them. The canto therefore asks more than whether one looks enviously. It asks whether looking itself is respectful, compassionate, and accountable to the person observed.

## 6. Heaven as the True City

> 94 «O frate mio, ciascuna è cittadina  
> (“O my brother, each is a citizen)
>
> 95 d'una vera città; ma tu vuo' dire  
> (of one true city; but you mean)
>
> 96 che vivesse in Italia peregrina».  
> (that she lived in Italy as a pilgrim.”)

Dante asks whether any soul is Italian. The answering spirit gently corrects the framework of his question: all these souls belong to the same true city, while their lives in Italy were a pilgrimage.

The passage draws on the Christian understanding of earthly life as a journey toward the heavenly homeland. It also recalls the distinction between earthly political communities and the city of God.

The correction matters especially in Dante's Italy, where civic loyalties and factional hatred could dominate moral life. Florence, Siena, and their rivals claimed intense allegiance; here those identities become secondary to a common citizenship.

Even the address “my brother” enacts the change. Before the speaker identifies her birthplace, she identifies Dante as kin. Charity establishes a relationship that envy and faction tend to deny.

## 7. Sapìa: Rejoicing in Her Own City's Ruin

> 109 Savia non fui, avvegna che Sapìa  
> (Wise I was not, although Sapìa)
>
> 110 fossi chiamata, e fui de li altrui danni  
> (I was called, and in the misfortunes of others I was)
>
> 111 più lieta assai che di ventura mia.  
> (far happier than in my own good fortune.)

The speaker is Sapìa, a Sienese noblewoman traditionally identified as Sapìa Salvani. She begins with a bitter play on her name: although “Sapìa” suggests wisdom, her conduct was anything but wise.

Her confession gives envy a precise psychological shape. It is not simply wanting what another possesses. It can become delight in another's deprivation—even when that loss brings no benefit to the envious person.

She illustrates this through the battle of Colle di Val d'Elsa in 1269, where Siena's Ghibelline forces were defeated by their Guelf opponents. Sapìa had prayed for the defeat of her own fellow citizens. Their flight brought her extraordinary joy.

> 121 tanto ch'io volsi in sù l'ardita faccia,  
> (so much that I turned my defiant face upward,)
>
> 122 gridando a Dio: “Omai più non ti temo!”,  
> (crying to God: “Now I fear you no more!”,)
>
> 123 come fé 'l merlo per poca bonaccia.  
> (as the blackbird did for a little fair weather.)

Her happiness becomes a challenge to God. Having obtained the disaster she desired, she acts as though she has nothing left to fear.

The blackbird comparison invokes a proverbial story about a bird that mistakes a brief improvement in the weather for the end of winter and boasts prematurely. Sapìa's defiance is similarly foolish: a passing satisfaction makes her imagine that she has escaped the conditions governing human life.

Her sin combines envy with pride. The destruction of others appears to confirm her own importance, and she treats the result as permission to dismiss divine authority.

## 8. Repentance and the Charity of Pier Pettinaio

> 124 Pace volli con Dio in su lo stremo  
> (I desired peace with God at the very end)
>
> 125 de la mia vita; e ancor non sarebbe  
> (of my life; and still would not be)
>
> 126 lo mio dover per penitenza scemo,  
> (my debt diminished through penance,)

Sapìa repented at the end of her life. Her salvation shows that even a deeply disordered life can turn toward God; but repentance does not mean that all its consequences have vanished.

Purgatory distinguishes forgiveness from completed moral healing. Sapìa is reconciled to God, yet the habits that made another's suffering pleasurable must still be corrected.

> 127 se ciò non fosse, ch'a memoria m'ebbe  
> (were it not that he held me in remembrance,)
>
> 128 Pier Pettinaio in sue sante orazioni,  
> (Pier Pettinaio, in his holy prayers,)
>
> 129 a cui di me per caritate increbbe.  
> (who, through charity, felt compassion for me.)

Pier Pettinaio was a Sienese layman remembered for his holiness; his name associates him with the trade in combs. His prayers assisted Sapìa after death. Given her late repentance, they are generally understood to have hastened her passage through the waiting of Ante-Purgatory into active purification.

The contrast is exact. Sapìa had taken pleasure in the harm suffered by others; Pier was moved by her need. She is helped by the very virtue she had lacked.

Intercessory prayer also expresses the canto's central challenge to envy. Another person's goodness does not impoverish Sapìa. It becomes a gift that advances her toward blessedness.

## 9. Dante's Confession: Envy and the Heavier Burden of Pride

> 133 «Li occhi», diss' io, «mi fieno ancor qui tolti,  
> (“My eyes,” I said, “will yet be taken from me here,)
>
> 134 ma picciol tempo, ché poca è l'offesa  
> (but for a short time, for slight is the offence)
>
> 135 fatta per esser con invidia vòlti.  
> (committed through their having been turned with envy.)

Dante does not place himself outside the moral world he describes. He expects that his own sight will require purification, although he judges envy to be a relatively minor fault in himself.

This admission prevents the encounter from becoming merely an inspection of another person's wrongdoing. The pilgrim must apply the terrace's lesson to his own life.

> 136 Troppa è più la paura ond' è sospesa  
> (Far greater is the fear in which is held)
>
> 137 l'anima mia del tormento di sotto,  
> (my soul concerning the torment below,)
>
> 138 che già lo 'ncarco di là giù mi pesa».  
> (for already the burden down there weighs upon me.”)

He fears the terrace of pride much more. The stones carried there already seem to press on him.

The statement is a significant act of self-recognition by a poet intensely conscious of his abilities, vocation, and public standing. He does not retract those gifts; he acknowledges the danger of making them grounds for self-exaltation.

Pride and envy are closely related. Pride insists upon one's superiority; envy resents whatever seems to challenge it. Dante's imagined burden connects the terraces and reminds the reader that their sins are not sealed into separate compartments.

## 10. Prayer, Reputation, and Siena's Vain Hopes

> 148 E cheggioti, per quel che tu più brami,  
> (And I ask you, by what you most desire,)
>
> 149 se mai calchi la terra di Toscana,  
> (if ever you tread the land of Tuscany,)
>
> 150 che a' miei propinqui tu ben mi rinfami.  
> (that you restore my good name among my kinsfolk.)

On learning that Dante is alive, Sapìa asks for his prayers and for a truthful report to her relatives. They should know that she repented and is among the saved.

Her concern for reputation need not mean a return to worldly vanity. A corrected account of her spiritual condition can also encourage remembrance and prayer. The living and the dead remain capable of helping one another.

Yet her final words show that purification does not erase personality or civic memory.

> 151 Tu li vedrai tra quella gente vana  
> (You will find them among those foolish people)
>
> 152 che spera in Talamone, e perderagli  
> (who place their hopes in Talamone, and will lose there)
>
> 153 più di speranza ch'a trovar la Diana;  
> (more hope than in searching for the Diana;)
>
> 154 ma più vi perderanno li ammiragli».  
> (but the admirals will lose still more there.”)

Sapìa ends by mocking Siena's ambitions. Talamone was the coastal port Siena acquired in 1303 in hopes of developing maritime commerce and power. Because the poem's journey is set in 1300, the reference takes the form of prophecy.

The Diana was a supposed underground watercourse that the Sienese sought through costly excavation. Dante places the hoped-for port beside the elusive river as examples of civic aspiration becoming expensive illusion.

The “admirals” add a final satirical sting. The precise reference is debated, but the title ridicules grand maritime expectations and those who stand to lose most through them.

Sapìa's sharpness is not entirely dissolved. Her closing mockery leaves a productive tension between truthful criticism and the old pleasure of belittling her fellow citizens. She is saved, but she is still undergoing purification.

## Epilogue — From Rivalry to Shared Good

Canto XIII presents envy as a failure of relationship: the soul experiences another person's good as its own loss. Its remedy is therefore communal. The penitents lean on one another, hear examples of self-giving love, invoke the saints, and benefit from the prayers of the living.

Their closed eyes are preparing for a truer vision. Against the resentful gaze that divides people into competitors, Dante sets the possibility of a heavenly city where another's happiness increases rather than diminishes one's own.
