# The Pageant of Revelation: Allegory in *Purgatorio* XXIX

At the summit of Mount Purgatory, Dante enters the Earthly Paradise: the garden humanity lost through the Fall. There, beside the river Lethe, the woman later identified as Matelda guides him toward an extraordinary procession. Its figures draw chiefly on biblical visions, especially Ezekiel and Revelation, but also on classical mythology and the ceremonial language of Roman triumphs.

The procession presents sacred history as a visible order: Scripture surrounds the Church, Christ draws it forward, and the virtues accompany it. Yet Dante does not encounter this order as an explanation already understood. He must first learn to see and listen.

## 1. Matelda's Song: Forgiveness in the Earthly Paradise

> 1 Cantando come donna innamorata,  
> (Singing like a woman in love,)
>
> 2 continüò col fin di sue parole:  
> (she continued upon the ending of her words:)
>
> 3 ‘Beati quorum tecta sunt peccata!’.  
> (‘Blessed are those whose sins are covered!’.)

Matelda's song echoes Psalm 32:1, numbered 31 in the Vulgate. Its blessing concerns forgiven sin, making it especially appropriate at the summit of the mountain where souls are purified.

Her manner is equally important: she sings “like a woman in love.” Earthly Paradise does not abolish delight, beauty, or affection; it restores their proper direction. Love becomes harmonious with God rather than distorted by possessiveness or appetite.

Matelda is often interpreted as an embodiment of innocent earthly happiness or the perfected active life. Neither identification exhausts her significance. Here she is principally Dante's guide to a condition in which natural pleasure and spiritual obedience no longer conflict.

## 2. The Memory of Eve: Freedom and Its Rightful Limit

> 24 mi fé riprender l'ardimento d'Eva,  
> (made me rebuke Eve's audacity,)
>
> 25 che là dove ubidia la terra e 'l cielo,  
> (who, where earth and heaven obeyed,)
>
> 26 femmina, sola e pur testé formata,  
> (a woman, alone and only just formed,)
>
> 27 non sofferse di star sotto alcun velo;  
> (could not endure remaining beneath any veil;)

The beautiful light and music awaken Dante's indignation at Eve. Surrounded by the delights of Eden, he feels the cost of humanity's expulsion more immediately than before.

The “veil” signifies the boundary imposed by God: above all, the prohibition concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. Eve would not accept a limit on what she might possess or know. Her action therefore contrasts with the obedience of the entire created world.

The phrasing reflects the gendered assumptions of Dante's medieval culture, particularly in its emphasis on Eve as newly created and alone. Within the canto's larger argument, however, the essential opposition is between creaturely obedience and self-assertion. Dante's own education requires him to receive revelation rather than demand unrestricted mastery over it.

In the following lines, he imagines that without the Fall he would have enjoyed these delights earlier and longer. Sacred history thus becomes personal: humanity's ancient loss has shaped his own life.

## 3. Poetry and Reason Before Revelation

> 40 Or convien che Elicona per me versi,  
> (Now Helicon must pour forth for me,)
>
> 41 e Uranìe m'aiuti col suo coro  
> (and Urania help me with her choir)
>
> 42 forti cose a pensar mettere in versi.  
> (to put into verse things difficult to conceive.)

Dante calls upon the Muses because the approaching vision strains both thought and language. Helicon is their mountain; Urania, traditionally associated with astronomy, is especially appropriate to a poem approaching heavenly realities.

The invocation brings classical poetic resources into the service of Christian revelation. Dante does not discard ancient culture: he redirects it toward a subject beyond its original religious horizon. The difficulty is not merely decorative description. He must translate a dense spiritual order into intelligible poetry.

> 55 Io mi rivolsi d'ammirazion pieno  
> (I turned, full of wonder,)
>
> 56 al buon Virgilio, ed esso mi rispuose  
> (toward good Virgil, and he answered me)
>
> 57 con vista carca di stupor non meno.  
> (with a look laden with no less amazement.)

Virgil's answering astonishment marks a limit. The guide who has explained so much can now offer no authoritative interpretation.

As a representative of classical wisdom and, broadly, natural reason, Virgil has brought Dante to the threshold of restored innocence. But the procession belongs to revelation. Reason is not disgraced here; it recognizes something it cannot independently supply. Virgil's silent wonder also prepares for his approaching disappearance from the poem.

## 4. The Seven Candlesticks: Illumination Before Understanding

> 49 la virtù ch'a ragion discorso ammanna,  
> (the faculty that prepares material for reason's judgment,)
>
> 50 sì com' elli eran candelabri apprese,  
> (apprehended them as the candlesticks they were,)
>
> 51 e ne le voci del cantare ‘Osanna’.  
> (and in the voices of the singing, ‘Hosanna’.)

At first Dante mistakes the distant lights for golden trees. As he approaches, perception becomes more accurate: he recognizes seven candlesticks and distinguishes the sung word “Hosanna.”

This correction makes the act of interpretation part of the narrative. Spiritual understanding does not arrive as an instantaneous possession. Dante must move nearer, attend carefully, and distinguish appearance from reality.

The candlesticks recall Revelation's seven golden lampstands and its seven lamps associated with the spirits of God. Within the procession, they are commonly understood as the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit, traditionally enumerated from Isaiah 11. Their position at the front makes illumination the condition under which the sacred pageant becomes visible.

> 76 sì che lì sopra rimanea distinto  
> (so that overhead there remained marked out)
>
> 77 di sette liste, tutte in quei colori  
> (in seven bands, all in those colors)
>
> 78 onde fa l'arco il Sole e Delia il cinto.  
> (with which the Sun makes the rainbow and Delia her girdle.)

The flames leave colored trails, forming a canopy above the procession. “Delia” is Diana as the moon; her “girdle” is the lunar halo.

These enduring bands extend the lights' significance beyond their immediate position. Divine illumination covers the entire community advancing beneath it. The rainbow also invites comparison with the sign of God's covenant after the Flood, although Dante explicitly describes its colors rather than explaining it as a separate emblem.

## 5. The Twenty-Four Elders: The Old Testament Comes First

> 83 ventiquattro seniori, a due a due,  
> (twenty-four elders, two by two,)
>
> 84 coronati venien di fiordaliso.  
> (came crowned with lilies.)

The twenty-four elders recall the heavenly elders of Revelation 4. In Dante's procession, they are generally understood to represent the books of the Old Testament according to a twenty-four-book reckoning known through the tradition discussed by Jerome.

The number therefore does not match the arrangement familiar from many modern Bibles. It belongs to a historical system for grouping the scriptural books.

Their white garments and lilies emphasize purity, faith, and the holiness of the revelation they carry. Their place before the chariot expresses the Christian understanding of the Old Testament as preparing for Christ and the Church.

> 85 Tutti cantavan: «Benedicta tue  
> (All were singing: “Blessed are you)
>
> 86 ne le figlie d'Adamo, e benedette  
> (among the daughters of Adam, and blessed)
>
> 87 sieno in etterno le bellezze tue!».  
> (may your beauties be forever!”)

The blessing uses biblical language associated especially with holy women and, in Christian devotion, with Mary. In the immediate dramatic setting, it anticipates Beatrice, whose arrival will occur in the next canto.

The overlapping associations are deliberate. Beatrice enters a ceremonial world shaped by Marian language, but she is not simply identical with Mary. As Dante's coming guide, she bears the authority of revealed wisdom. The elders' song makes her appearance part of a scriptural pattern rather than merely a reunion between two individuals.

## 6. The Four Living Creatures: The Gospels as Watchful Witnesses

> 94 Ognuno era pennuto di sei ali;  
> (Each was feathered with six wings;)
>
> 95 le penne piene d'occhi; e li occhi d'Argo,  
> (the feathers full of eyes; and the eyes of Argus,)
>
> 96 se fosser vivi, sarebber cotali.  
> (if they were alive, would be like these.)

The four living creatures represent the four Gospels. Their visionary forms combine Ezekiel's opening vision with the creatures surrounding God's throne in Revelation 4.

Dante explicitly notes later in the passage that, on the number of wings, he follows John's Revelation rather than Ezekiel: six wings, not four. This detail demonstrates that the procession is a carefully composed biblical synthesis, not a reproduction of one source.

The eyes suggest comprehensive spiritual perception and vigilance. Dante also invokes Argus, the many-eyed watchman of classical mythology. Pagan narrative supplies a visual comparison, while Scripture supplies the creatures' authority and place in the sacred order.

Their position around the chariot indicates the Gospels' close relation to the Church: they surround and bear witness to its central mystery.

## 7. The Chariot and the Griffin: The Church Drawn by Christ

> 106 Lo spazio dentro a lor quattro contenne  
> (The space within those four contained)
>
> 107 un carro, in su due rote, trïunfale,  
> (a triumphal chariot upon two wheels,)
>
> 108 ch'al collo d'un grifon tirato venne.  
> (which came drawn by a griffin's neck.)

The chariot is conventionally understood as the Church. Its triumphal form borrows from Roman public ceremony, but its glory exceeds the victories of emperors and generals.

Dante's comparisons with the triumphs of Scipio Africanus and Augustus establish that Christian history surpasses imperial magnificence. His subsequent reference to the disastrous chariot of the Sun also supplies a contrast: this sacred vehicle is drawn by its rightful guide, not driven into catastrophe by an inadequate charioteer.

The two wheels have received several interpretations, including the two Testaments. Unlike the main identification of the chariot with the Church, their precise significance is not universally settled.

> 113 le membra d'oro avea quant' era uccello,  
> (it had limbs of gold insofar as it was bird,)
>
> 114 e bianche l'altre, di vermiglio miste.  
> (and the others white, mingled with crimson.)

The griffin combines eagle and lion and represents Christ's divine and human natures united in one person. Its golden avian parts signify divinity; the white and crimson of its animal parts evoke humanity, bodily flesh, and blood.

The image is not intended to suggest that Christ is two separate beings. Its allegorical force lies in one creature possessing two distinguishable natures.

The griffin draws the Church rather than being carried by it. This establishes a theological hierarchy: the Church's movement and authority depend upon Christ. The later cantos will test the historical Church against the ideal displayed here.

## 8. The Dancing Women: Theological and Cardinal Virtues

> 121 Tre donne in giro da la destra rota  
> (Three women in a circle beside the right wheel)
>
> 122 venian danzando; l'una tanto rossa  
> (came dancing; one so red)
>
> 123 ch'a pena fora dentro al foco nota;  
> (that she would scarcely have been noticed within fire;)

The three women beside the right wheel are the theological virtues: Faith, Hope, and Charity. The fiery red dancer is Charity; the emerald-green figure described immediately afterward is Hope; the snow-white figure is Faith.

Their dance embodies virtues as active and mutually ordered powers, not abstract labels. Faith provides access to revealed truth, while Charity gives Christian life its fullest animating force. Dante accordingly describes the dancers as led now by Faith, now by Charity, with Charity's song regulating their pace.

> 130 Da la sinistra quattro facean festa,  
> (On the left four made festival,)
>
> 131 in porpore vestite, dietro al modo  
> (clothed in purple, following the measure)
>
> 132 d'una di lor ch'avea tre occhi in testa.  
> (of one of them who had three eyes in her head.)

The four women on the left are the cardinal virtues: Prudence, Justice, Fortitude, and Temperance. Their leader is Prudence, whose three eyes conventionally signify attention to past, present, and future.

Prudence directs moral action by understanding circumstances and consequences. The other cardinal virtues require that practical judgment if they are to act rightly.

These virtues were recognized in classical philosophy as well as Christian ethics. Their inclusion shows natural moral excellence incorporated into the Christian order. The theological virtues do not render them unnecessary; the two groups dance beside the same chariot.

## 9. The Final Seven Figures: Completing the New Testament

> 136 L'un si mostrava alcun de' famigliari  
> (One appeared to be among the followers)
>
> 137 di quel sommo Ipocràte che natura  
> (of that supreme Hippocrates whom nature)
>
> 138 a li animali fé ch'ell' ha più cari;  
> (made for the creatures she holds dearest;)

After the chariot and virtues come figures representing the remaining New Testament writings. The first appears as a physician, a follower of Hippocrates.

He represents the Acts of the Apostles through its traditional author, Luke, whom Christian tradition identifies as a physician. Since Luke's Gospel has already been represented among the four creatures, this new appearance concerns his other scriptural work.

His companion carries a shining sword and represents the Pauline Epistles. The sword is an attribute of Paul and also recalls the “sword of the Spirit,” identified with the word of God in Ephesians 6:17. The physician and swordsman thus offer contrasting images of spiritual ministry: healing and penetrating judgment.

> 142 Poi vidi quattro in umile paruta;  
> (Then I saw four of humble appearance;)
>
> 143 e di retro da tutti un vecchio solo  
> (and behind them all, a solitary old man)
>
> 144 venir, dormendo, con la faccia arguta.  
> (coming, asleep, with an alert face.)

The four humble figures represent the Catholic, or General, Epistles through their traditional authors: James, Peter, John, and Jude. These are four authorial figures, not merely four individual letters.

The final old man represents Revelation through John, traditionally identified in Dante's religious culture with the apostle and evangelist. His sleeping yet alert appearance is a purposeful paradox: outward sensation rests while inward visionary perception remains keen.

Together, these seven figures complete the procession's representation of Scripture. Their red floral crowns distinguish them from the earlier elders, suggesting the ardor of charity and, potentially, the blood of Christian witness.

## 10. The Thunder: Revelation Becomes an Encounter

> 151 E quando il carro a me fu a rimpetto,  
> (And when the chariot was opposite me,)
>
> 152 un tuon s'udì, e quelle genti degne  
> (a thunderclap was heard, and those worthy people)
>
> 153 parvero aver l'andar più interdetto,  
> (seemed forbidden to go any farther,)
>
> 154 fermandosi ivi con le prime insegne.  
> (stopping there with the foremost standards.)

The procession stops precisely opposite Dante. What began as a distant spectacle has become an event addressed to him.

Thunder belongs to the biblical language of divine manifestation, from Sinai to Revelation. Here it functions as an authoritative signal: the entire company halts in coordinated obedience.

The final “standards” are the luminous banners at the procession's head. Their military and ceremonial vocabulary reinforces the pageant's unity, but no ordinary commander issues its orders. The halt prepares for Beatrice's appearance and for Dante's personal confrontation with his own past.

This movement is crucial. Knowledge of sacred history is not sufficient by itself. The pilgrim who sees the Church, Scripture, and the virtues must also submit his own life to judgment and renewal.

## Epilogue — Sacred History Becomes Personal

*Canto* XXIX turns Christian revelation into a moving, luminous order. The Spirit illuminates it; the Old Testament precedes it; the Gospels surround the Church; Christ draws its chariot; the virtues accompany it; and the remaining New Testament writings complete its witness.

But the canto also dramatizes how understanding develops: mistaken sight becomes recognition, classical wisdom yields to wonder, and a public pageant stops before one particular soul. At the threshold of Beatrice's return, Dante discovers that revelation is not merely something to contemplate. It demands a changed life.
