# The Weight of Pride: Allegory and Background in *Purgatorio* XI

In Canto XI, Dante walks along the first terrace of Purgatory, where the proud are purified beneath enormous stones. These souls once elevated themselves above others; now they must bend toward the ground. But their punishment is not merely a reversal of their earthly behavior. It teaches them to recognize their dependence on God, their kinship with other people, and the transience of everything that once made them feel superior.

The canto moves from collective prayer to three examples of pride: aristocratic lineage, artistic achievement, and political power. Throughout, Dante himself is implicated. He does not simply observe the correction of pride; he begins to undergo it.

## 1. The Lord's Prayer: Learning Dependence

> 1 «O Padre nostro, che ne' cieli stai,  
> (“O our Father, who dwell in the heavens,)
>
> 2 non circunscritto, ma per più amore  
> (not circumscribed, but through the greater love)
>
> 3 ch'ai primi effetti di là sù tu hai,  
> (that you have for your first creations up there,)

The canto opens with a paraphrase of the Lord's Prayer. Its first words establish the community that pride denies: God is *our* Father, not the exclusive possession of any individual, family, or class.

Dante immediately qualifies the statement that God dwells in heaven. God is not spatially confined there. Rather, heaven expresses his special relationship with his “first creations,” generally understood as the angels. Divine presence cannot be measured by the geographical distinctions that organize human life.

For the proud, this is a fundamental reeducation. They must abandon the assumption that their own position determines the order of the universe. Their prayer begins by acknowledging a reality that exceeds every human boundary.

> 13 Dà oggi a noi la cotidiana manna,  
> (Give us today the daily manna,)
>
> 14 sanza la qual per questo aspro diserto  
> (without which through this harsh wilderness)
>
> 15 a retro va chi più di gir s'affanna.  
> (backward goes whoever strives hardest to advance.)

The prayer's daily bread becomes “manna,” the food God supplied to the Israelites in the wilderness. Purgatory is thereby connected to the biblical Exodus: the souls are traveling out of slavery toward freedom, but cannot sustain that journey by their own resources.

The paradox in line 15 directly challenges pride. Strenuous effort without divine assistance produces regression rather than progress. Dante does not dismiss human effort; the mountain demands it. He denies that effort is self-sufficient. The manna signifies the spiritual nourishment and grace without which the journey cannot succeed.

## 2. Prayer Beyond the Self: The Communion of Living and Dead

> 19 Nostra virtù che di legger s'adona,  
> (Our strength, which so easily yields,)
>
> 20 non spermentar con l'antico avversaro,  
> (do not put to the test against the ancient adversary,)
>
> 21 ma libera da lui che sì la sprona.  
> (but deliver it from him who so assails it.)

The “ancient adversary” is Satan. The speakers acknowledge that human moral strength is vulnerable, an admission particularly appropriate to souls being cured of exaggerated confidence in themselves.

Their version of the petition against temptation emphasizes the inequality of the contest. Humanity cannot safely rely on its own powers against evil. The language replaces proud independence with a request for protection.

> 22 Quest' ultima preghiera, segnor caro,  
> (This final prayer, dear Lord,)
>
> 23 già non si fa per noi, ché non bisogna,  
> (indeed is not made for ourselves, since there is no need,)
>
> 24 ma per color che dietro a noi restaro».  
> (but for those who remained behind us.”)

The souls explain that this final petition is for the living. Those in Purgatory are assured of salvation: they must still be purified, but they cannot lose their ultimate destination through renewed surrender to Satan.

Their concern has therefore moved beyond themselves. Pride isolates; charity makes another person's danger one's own concern. Even while carrying their burdens, these souls pray for people still exposed to temptation.

> 34 Ben si de' loro atar lavar le note  
> (Surely we should help them wash away the stains)
>
> 35 che portar quinci, sì che, mondi e lievi,  
> (that they carried from here, so that, clean and light,)
>
> 36 possano uscire a le stellate ruote.  
> (they may rise to the starry spheres.)

Dante turns that charity into an obligation for his readers. If the dead pray for the living, the living should assist the dead through intercessory prayer.

This reciprocal assistance belongs to the medieval Christian understanding of the communion of saints: death does not dissolve the bonds connecting members of the Church. The words “clean and light” also unite spiritual and physical meanings. Purification removes the stain of sin and the weight that prevents ascent.

## 3. Bent Bodies and the Healing of Pride

> 28 disparmente angosciate tutte a tondo  
> (unequally tormented, all around the circle)
>
> 29 e lasse su per la prima cornice,  
> (and weary along the first terrace,)
>
> 30 purgando la caligine del mondo.  
> (purging away the darkness of the world.)

The souls circle the terrace beneath stones whose differing burdens suggest punishment proportioned to their individual need. Their bodily condition reverses the posture of pride: those who once held themselves above others now struggle to raise their heads.

This is *contrapasso*, a correspondence between sin and punishment. In Purgatory, however, that correspondence is medicinal. The stones do not eternally fix the souls in their sin; they help undo its habits.

The “darkness of the world” describes pride as a failure of vision. Proud people mistake temporary distinctions for ultimate truths. Their bowed posture becomes a means of learning to see correctly.

Dante's own living body introduces another kind of weight. Virgil asks for an accessible route because his companion still wears the flesh inherited from Adam. The contrast is instructive: embodiment makes Dante physically slow, while the souls' moral burdens make them spiritually slow. Neither condition is overcome by simply insisting on one's own strength.

## 4. Omberto Aldobrandeschi: The Illusion of Noble Blood

> 61 L'antico sangue e l'opere leggiadre  
> (The ancient bloodline and the noble deeds)
>
> 62 d'i miei maggior mi fer sì arrogante,  
> (of my ancestors made me so arrogant,)
>
> 63 che, non pensando a la comune madre,  
> (that, giving no thought to our common mother,)
>
> 64 ogn' uomo ebbi in despetto tanto avante,  
> (I held every person in contempt to such an extent,)

The first named penitent is Omberto Aldobrandeschi, a member of a powerful Tuscan noble family. He attributes his arrogance to inherited distinction: an ancient lineage and the achievements of his ancestors.

His mistake was not recognizing admirable deeds, but treating them as evidence that he belonged to a higher order of humanity. The “common mother,” generally understood as the earth, recalls an origin shared by noble and commoner alike. Aristocratic genealogy cannot erase universal human kinship or mortality.

Omberto's confession also exposes the borrowed nature of hereditary pride. The achievements were his ancestors', yet he converted them into his own license to despise others.

He connects this arrogance with his violent death at Campagnatico in 1259, during conflict involving Siena. The precise historical circumstances are uncertain, but the canto's moral emphasis is clear: the habits celebrated as family greatness became destructive both to him and to his kin. In Purgatory, inherited prestige no longer exempts him from the common human condition.

## 5. Oderisi of Gubbio: Art Without Possessiveness

> 82 «Frate», diss' elli, «più ridon le carte  
> (“Brother,” he said, “more brightly smile the pages)
>
> 83 che pennelleggia Franco Bolognese;  
> (that Franco Bolognese paints;)
>
> 84 l'onore è tutto or suo, e mio in parte.  
> (the honor is now wholly his, and mine only in part.)

Dante recognizes Oderisi of Gubbio, an illuminator of manuscripts, and greets him as an honor to his hometown and profession. Oderisi answers by praising a rival, Franco Bolognese.

The pages that “smile” suggest radiance, color, and artistic vitality. Oderisi has not ceased to appreciate beauty. His purification consists instead in learning to acknowledge excellence without insisting that it belong to him.

His first word, “Brother,” is equally important. It substitutes fellowship for competition. The artist who once sought superiority can now recognize both a rival's achievement and another person's equal standing.

> 88 Di tal superbia qui si paga il fio;  
> (For such pride the penalty is paid here;)
>
> 89 e ancor non sarei qui, se non fosse  
> (and I would not yet be here, were it not)
>
> 90 che, possendo peccar, mi volsi a Dio.  
> (that, while still able to sin, I turned to God.)

Oderisi identifies the fault precisely: the consuming desire for preeminence. Artistic skill itself is not condemned; the attempt to make distinction the foundation of one's identity is.

He also explains that his repentance during life enabled his present progress. Purgatory is not a mechanism that saves an unrepentant person automatically. Its inhabitants have already turned toward God; their remaining task is to have their disordered attachments healed.

## 6. Cimabue, Giotto, and the Two Guidos: Fame Changes Hands

> 94 Credette Cimabue ne la pittura  
> (Cimabue believed that in painting)
>
> 95 tener lo campo, e ora ha Giotto il grido,  
> (he held the field, and now Giotto has the acclaim,)
>
> 96 sì che la fama di colui è scura.  
> (so that the former's fame is obscured.)

Oderisi expands his personal confession into a history of artistic succession. Cimabue once seemed supreme in painting; now Giotto commands public admiration.

The point is not that Cimabue's paintings have suddenly become worthless. It is that reputation is comparative and unstable. A newer achievement changes the public's distribution of attention.

Giotto, Dante's contemporary, was associated with major developments in pictorial naturalism and expressive power. By invoking living artistic culture, Dante makes the moral lesson immediate: the apparent permanence of fame can dissolve within a single lifetime.

> 97 Così ha tolto l'uno a l'altro Guido  
> (Thus one Guido has taken from the other)
>
> 98 la gloria de la lingua; e forse è nato  
> (the glory of the language; and perhaps one is born)
>
> 99 chi l'uno e l'altro caccerà del nido.  
> (who will drive both of them from the nest.)

The two Guidos are generally identified as Guido Cavalcanti and Guido Guinizzelli: Cavalcanti has displaced Guinizzelli in poetic renown. The unnamed successor who may surpass both is commonly understood as Dante himself.

That possibility makes the passage deliberately uncomfortable. A poem condemning artistic pride also announces its author's potential greatness.

The tension should not be explained away. Dante distinguishes real poetic achievement from the fantasy that achievement can provide lasting self-sufficiency. Even if he surpasses both Guidos, the same law of transience will apply to his reputation. The warning includes the poet who delivers it.

## 7. A Breath of Wind Against Eternity

> 100 Non è il mondan romore altro ch'un fiato  
> (Worldly renown is nothing but a breath)
>
> 101 di vento, ch'or vien quinci e or vien quindi,  
> (of wind, which comes now from here and now from there,)
>
> 102 e muta nome perché muta lato.  
> (and changes its name because it changes direction.)

Reputation becomes an atmospheric disturbance: audible and noticeable, yet impossible to possess. Its changing direction determines which name people acclaim.

The image dismantles the idea that fame is a stable substance belonging to its recipient. It depends on movement outside the individual—public attention, historical circumstance, and shifting taste.

“Worldly renown” is also literally worldly noise. Against the collective prayer opening the canto, this noise offers a rival use of the human voice: one joins people in dependence and charity, while the other circulates temporary distinctions.

> 106 pria che passin mill' anni? ch'è più corto  
> (before a thousand years pass? which is a shorter)
>
> 107 spazio a l'etterno, ch'un muover di ciglia  
> (span beside eternity than a movement of the eyelids)
>
> 108 al cerchio che più tardi in cielo è torto.  
> (beside the sphere that turns most slowly in heaven.)

Oderisi asks whether dying old rather than in infancy will make any meaningful difference to one's fame after a thousand years. He then makes even that millennium negligible beside eternity.

The comparison draws on medieval astronomy, with its heavenly spheres and immensely long celestial cycles. A blink is vanishingly brief beside the slowest celestial revolution; a thousand years is still less significant when measured against eternity.

The argument does not make earthly actions meaningless. Their moral consequences matter profoundly. What it discredits is the attempt to measure a life's ultimate value by the duration of public remembrance.

## 8. Dante's Own Swelling Begins to Subside

> 118 E io a lui: «Tuo vero dir m'incora  
> (And I to him: “Your truthful speech instills in me)
>
> 119 bona umiltà, e gran tumor m'appiani;  
> (good humility, and you flatten a great swelling in me;)
>
> 120 ma chi è quei di cui tu parlavi ora?».  
> (but who is that man of whom you were speaking just now?”)

Dante acknowledges that Oderisi's lesson concerns him personally. Pride becomes a “swelling,” a pathological enlargement of the self. Humility restores proportion rather than destroying worth.

This bodily metaphor complements the terrace's physical punishment. The stones press down upon inflated self-importance, while truthful speech works inwardly toward the same cure.

Earlier, Dante had lowered his face to hear Omberto and walked bent beside Oderisi. His posture thus begins to resemble that of the penitents before he explicitly admits his need for humility. The encounter makes him more than an observer: the proud help diagnose and treat the pride of their visitor.

His continuing question also matters. Recognition is not the end of instruction. He must still confront another example, this time involving political power and an extraordinary act of self-abasement.

## 9. Provenzano Salvani: Political Mastery and Delayed Repentance

> 122 ed è qui perché fu presuntüoso  
> (and he is here because he was presumptuous)
>
> 123 a recar Siena tutta a le sue mani.  
> (in bringing all Siena into his hands.)

The third example is Provenzano Salvani, a leading Sienese Ghibelline associated with Siena's victory over Florence at Montaperti in 1260. He later died after the defeat of the Sienese at Colle in 1269.

His pride was political: the desire to gather an entire city into his own control. Where Omberto absolutized lineage and Oderisi artistic distinction, Provenzano absolutized power.

Oderisi has already contrasted his former Tuscan renown with the faint memory of him remaining even in Siena. Political supremacy, like artistic fame, cannot secure permanence.

> 130 se buona orazïon lui non aita,  
> (unless good prayer assists him,)
>
> 131 prima che passi tempo quanto visse,  
> (before as much time has passed as he lived,)
>
> 132 come fu la venuta lui largita?».  
> (how was his coming granted to him?”)

Dante raises a procedural difficulty. Souls who postpone repentance until the end of life ordinarily wait in Ante-Purgatory for a period corresponding to their earthly lifetime, unless effective prayer assists them. How, then, has Provenzano already reached this terrace?

The question distinguishes reconciliation with God from immediate readiness for purification on the mountain. Late repentance can save, but it does not make the moral history of a life irrelevant.

The answer will reveal that Provenzano had already performed a remarkable act contrary to his ruling vice. His progress rests not on the survival of political influence after death, but on a moment when he willingly surrendered its dignity.

## 10. Begging for a Friend: Humility Before Exile

> 134 «liberamente nel Campo di Siena,  
> (“freely, in the Campo of Siena,)
>
> 135 ogne vergogna diposta, s'affisse;  
> (putting aside all shame, he stationed himself;)
>
> 136 e lì, per trar l'amico suo di pena,  
> (and there, to draw his friend out of suffering,)
>
> 137 ch'e' sostenea ne la prigion di Carlo,  
> (which he endured in Charles's prison,)
>
> 138 si condusse a tremar per ogne vena.  
> (he brought himself to tremble in every vein.)

At the height of his prestige, Provenzano publicly begged in Siena's principal square to ransom a friend held captive by Charles of Anjou.

Every circumstance reverses his characteristic pride. The man who wanted Siena in his hands placed himself before its inhabitants as a petitioner. The powerful man accepted dependence; the seeker of supremacy acted for someone else.

The act was voluntary, as “freely” emphasizes, but not emotionally easy. Trembling in every vein conveys the cost of exposing himself to possible refusal and humiliation. Humility is not presented as effortless indifference to reputation. It is the decision to put another person's need above one's own standing.

Because that decision directly opposed his deepest vice, it anticipated the work of Purgatory.

> 140 ma poco tempo andrà, che ' tuoi vicini  
> (but little time will pass before your neighbors)
>
> 141 faranno sì che tu potrai chiosarlo.  
> (will bring it about that you can explain it.)
>
> 142 Quest' opera li tolse quei confini».  
> (This deed removed those bounds for him.”)

Oderisi's explanation becomes a prophecy of Dante's exile. The poem's journey occurs in 1300; Dante's expulsion from Florence followed in 1302. His “neighbors” will make him understand through experience the humiliation of needing other people's assistance.

To “explain” or gloss the episode will therefore mean more than interpreting its words. Dante will acquire the knowledge of dependence bodily and socially.

Yet the situations are not identical. Provenzano voluntarily humbled himself for a friend; Dante will have dependence forced upon him. The implied challenge is whether involuntary suffering can also become an education in humility rather than an occasion for deeper resentment.

The final line returns to Provenzano. His charitable self-abasement removed the boundary that would otherwise have delayed him in Ante-Purgatory. Power did not advance him; relinquishing its pretensions did.

## Epilogue — Greatness Relearned as Dependence

*Purgatorio* XI does not condemn ancestry, artistic excellence, or public achievement in themselves. It condemns turning them into grounds for self-sufficiency and contempt.

Its movement is from elevation to relationship: the nobleman remembers a common origin, the artist recognizes a rival's excellence, and the political leader begs for a friend. Prayer joins the living and the dead in mutual assistance.

The stones make visible the burden of an inflated self. As that inflation subsides, the soul becomes capable of rising. Dante's own poetic ambition remains, but it must be transformed by the same lesson: genuine greatness is compatible with humility only when it acknowledges grace, transience, and the claims of other people.
