# Shadows, Tears, and Mercy: Dante's *Purgatorio*, Canto V

Canto V takes place in **Ante-Purgatory**, on the lower slopes of the mountain, where souls await admission to the terraces of purification. Dante and Virgil encounter people who died violently and repented only at the end of their lives.

Their stories turn on a contrast: human violence can destroy the body, but it cannot determine the soul's final destiny. Alongside this theological argument, Dante explores the responsibilities of the living toward the dead—and learns how to listen compassionately without abandoning his own journey.

## 1. The Living Body Among the Shadows

> 8 e vidile guardar per maraviglia  
> (and I saw them looking in wonder)
>
> 9 pur me, pur me, e 'l lume ch'era rotto.  
> (only at me, only at me, and at the light that was broken.)

The souls notice that Dante interrupts the sunlight. Unlike their insubstantial forms, his living body casts a shadow. The repeated “pur me” captures both their astonishment and Dante's sudden awareness of being the center of attention.

This physical distinction establishes his exceptional position. He is traveling through the afterlife before death, still possessing the body with which he must eventually return to earthly responsibilities. What appears to the souls as a marvel will soon become a practical opportunity: he can carry messages and requests for prayer to the living.

The shadow also complicates ordinary symbolism. Here, blocking the light does not indicate spiritual darkness. It reveals embodied life—and therefore Dante's capacity to act on behalf of others.

## 2. The Unmoving Tower and the Wandering Mind

> 13 Vien dietro a me, e lascia dir le genti:  
> (Come behind me, and let the people talk:)
>
> 14 sta come torre ferma, che non crolla  
> (stand like a firm tower, which never shakes)
>
> 15 già mai la cima per soffiar di venti;  
> (its summit at the blowing of winds;)

Virgil rebukes Dante for slowing down to attend to the souls' remarks. The tower represents constancy of purpose: the pilgrim must not let external attention govern his progress.

There is a deliberate paradox in being told to “stand” firm while continuing to walk. Moral steadfastness does not mean physical immobility. Dante must remain inwardly stable precisely so that he can keep moving toward his goal.

Nor does Virgil demand indifference to other people. Later, he instructs Dante to listen while walking. The lesson concerns disciplined compassion, not withdrawal from human concerns.

> 16 ché sempre l'omo in cui pensier rampolla  
> (for always the man in whom thought springs up)
>
> 17 sovra pensier, da sé dilunga il segno,  
> (upon thought, distances the goal from himself,)
>
> 18 perché la foga l'un de l'altro insolla».  
> (because the force of one weakens the other.”)

The image shifts from architecture to uncontrolled growth. Thoughts keep sprouting, and competing impulses dissipate the energy needed for purposeful action.

Virgil, who frequently embodies the guidance of reason, teaches Dante to order his attention. Spiritual progress requires more than good intentions: the will must resist distraction and sustain its direction.

## 3. The *Miserere*: A Community Learning Repentance

> 24 cantando ‘Miserere’ a verso a verso.  
> (singing ‘Have mercy’ verse by verse.)

The approaching souls sing Psalm 50 in the traditional Latin numbering, Psalm 51 in most modern English Bibles. Its opening, *Miserere mei, Deus*, means “Have mercy on me, O God.” Traditionally associated with David's repentance, it asks for cleansing and a renewed heart.

Singing “verse by verse” suggests an ordered, responsive performance. These souls may have ended their earthly lives amid disorder and bloodshed, but they now share the language and rhythm of repentance.

Unlike the self-justifying speeches often heard in *Inferno*, their song acknowledges dependence on mercy.

> 25 Quando s'accorser ch'i' non dava loco  
> (When they realized that I gave no passage)
>
> 26 per lo mio corpo al trapassar d'i raggi,  
> (through my body to the passing rays,)
>
> 27 mutar lor canto in un «oh!» lungo e roco;  
> (they changed their song into a long and hoarse “oh!”;)

Their communal worship breaks into communal astonishment. Dante's body interrupts both sunlight and song.

The interruption does not cancel the penitential setting. Instead, it creates a new possibility within it: the living traveler may help those whose earthly opportunities have ended. Wonder becomes hope, and the souls hurry toward him.

## 4. Violent Death and the Last Opening to Grace

> 52 Noi fummo tutti già per forza morti,  
> (We were all once put to death by violence,)
>
> 53 e peccatori infino a l'ultima ora;  
> (and sinners until the final hour;)
>
> 54 quivi lume del ciel ne fece accorti,  
> (then the light of heaven made us aware,)
>
> 55 sì che, pentendo e perdonando, fora  
> (so that, repenting and forgiving, out)
>
> 56 di vita uscimmo a Dio pacificati,  
> (of life we went, reconciled to God,)
>
> 57 che del disio di sé veder n'accora».  
> (who pains our hearts with the desire to see him.”)

These lines define the group. Violent death alone has not saved them, nor does being murdered establish innocence. They explicitly acknowledge having remained sinners until their last hour.

What changes their destiny is heavenly illumination answered by repentance and forgiveness. The pairing matters: they turn away from their own sins while relinquishing hatred toward those who injured them.

Their stories therefore reject the assumption that an apparently disastrous death must signify divine rejection. Human observers see wounds and lost bodies; God sees the final movement of the will.

Their present suffering is also unlike damnation. They are reconciled to God, but not yet able to see him. Their pain comes from an assured love still awaiting fulfillment.

## 5. Prayer as a Bond Between the Living and the Dead

> 70 che tu mi sie di tuoi prieghi cortese  
> (that you be generous to me with your entreaties)
>
> 71 in Fano, sì che ben per me s'adori  
> (in Fano, so that prayer may duly be offered for me)
>
> 72 pur ch'i' possa purgar le gravi offese.  
> (so that I may purge my grave offenses.)

The first individual speaker, traditionally identified as **Jacopo del Cassero**, asks Dante to obtain prayers for him in Fano, his native town.

The request reflects the medieval Christian understanding of the communion of saints: death does not dissolve the spiritual relationships joining believers. Prayers offered by the living can assist the saved dead, including those awaiting purification.

These prayers do not purchase salvation for someone who has rejected God. Jacopo is already saved. What he seeks is help in advancing toward the purification and vision of God that he desires.

Dante's journey consequently acquires a social purpose. Remembering and reporting what he hears are forms of service, not merely exercises in literary commemoration.

## 6. Jacopo del Cassero: Political Hatred and the Vulnerable Body

> 77 quel da Esti il fé far, che m'avea in ira  
> (the man of Este had it done, who held me in hatred)
>
> 78 assai più là che dritto non volea.  
> (far beyond what justice allowed.)

Jacopo was a political and military figure from Fano. While serving as chief magistrate of Bologna, he opposed the ambitions of **Azzo VIII d'Este**, lord of Ferrara. Dante's account attributes his assassination to Azzo's hostility.

In 1298, while traveling to take office in Milan, Jacopo was attacked in the territory of Padua. The political background matters because the death was not a random misfortune: it emerged from the rivalries and retaliations of Italian civic life.

Jacopo names the injustice without making vengeance his final concern. His request remains prayer, not retaliation.

> 82 Corsi al palude, e le cannucce e 'l braco  
> (I ran to the marsh, and the reeds and mud)
>
> 83 m'impigliar sì ch'i' caddi; e lì vid' io  
> (entangled me so that I fell; and there I saw)
>
> 84 de le mie vene farsi in terra laco».  
> (from my veins a lake forming on the ground.”)

Political explanation gives way to bodily immediacy. Reeds, mud, a fall, and spreading blood reduce public conflict to one person's helpless final moments.

The marsh is first a concrete place, not simply an allegorical landscape. Yet its entanglement also resonates with the canto's earlier concern about being impeded. Dante can still learn to move forward; Jacopo remembers the fatal obstruction of his earthly escape.

His lost blood forms a terrible counterpart to the living blood still circulating in Dante's body. The pilgrim retains the power to act that Jacopo no longer possesses.

## 7. Bonconte da Montefeltro: Forgotten on Earth, Remembered by Heaven

> 88 Io fui di Montefeltro, io son Bonconte;  
> (I was of Montefeltro; I am Bonconte;)
>
> 89 Giovanna o altri non ha di me cura;  
> (Giovanna or anyone else takes no care of me;)
>
> 90 per ch'io vo tra costor con bassa fronte».  
> (therefore I go among these with lowered brow.”)

**Bonconte da Montefeltro** died fighting for the Ghibellines at the Battle of Campaldino in 1289. Dante himself fought there on the opposing, Florentine Guelf side. Their encounter places former enemies within a spiritual order that transcends political allegiance.

Bonconte's wife, Giovanna, and others have neglected him, especially by failing to offer the prayers he needs. His lowered head expresses the sorrow of being forgotten.

The movement from “I was of Montefeltro” to “I am Bonconte” also invites reflection on identity. Earthly lineage belongs to his former condition; the person persists beyond it.

> 100 Quivi perdei la vista e la parola;  
> (There I lost sight and speech;)
>
> 101 nel nome di Maria fini', e quivi  
> (in the name of Mary I ended, and there)
>
> 102 caddi, e rimase la mia carne sola.  
> (I fell, and my flesh remained alone.)

Dante asks why Bonconte's body was never found after the battle. The answer supplies an imagined spiritual history behind that earthly uncertainty.

Mortally wounded, Bonconte reaches the meeting of the Archiano and the Arno. His last invocation is Mary's name. Speech fails, but its final use becomes an appeal for mercy.

The flesh left “alone” marks the separation of body and soul. What happens to the corpse will explain its disappearance; it will not determine what has happened to the person.

## 8. One Little Tear Against the Logic of Damnation

> 106 Tu te ne porti di costui l'etterno  
> (You carry away this man's eternal part)
>
> 107 per una lagrimetta che 'l mi toglie;  
> (for one little tear that takes him from me;)
>
> 108 ma io farò de l'altro altro governo!”.  
> (but I shall deal otherwise with the other!”)

An angel receives Bonconte's soul, while a devil protests that a single tear has deprived him of his expected victim. The “eternal part” is the soul; “the other” is the body, against which the devil threatens revenge.

The diminutive “lagrimetta” expresses the devil's contempt. To him, the outward sign seems absurdly small beside a lifetime of sin. But divine mercy is not measured by the physical quantity of tears. The tear manifests a genuine final conversion.

This episode reverses the fate of Bonconte's father, Guido da Montefeltro, in *Inferno* XXVII. Guido tries to rely on absolution while consenting to a fraudulent act; Bonconte truly repents. The contrast is not between a clever and an inadequate formula, but between attempted spiritual manipulation and an actual change of heart.

## 9. The Storm: Power Over the Body, Not the Soul

> 112 Giunse quel mal voler che pur mal chiede  
> (He joined that evil will which seeks only evil)
>
> 113 con lo 'ntelletto, e mosse il fummo e 'l vento  
> (with his intellect, and stirred the mist and the wind)
>
> 114 per la virtù che sua natura diede.  
> (through the power that his nature gave.)

The frustrated devil raises a storm. Dante presents this through a combination of medieval natural philosophy and Christian angelology: atmospheric moisture becomes rain, while a fallen angel uses the capacities of its created nature to manipulate physical conditions.

Evil here is not a rival creative power equal to God. It is an intelligent will directing existing powers toward destruction. The devil can produce havoc, but he cannot overturn Bonconte's reconciliation with God.

> 124 Lo corpo mio gelato in su la foce  
> (My ice-cold body at the river's mouth)
>
> 125 trovò l'Archian rubesto; e quel sospinse  
> (the raging Archiano found; and it thrust that)
>
> 126 ne l'Arno, e sciolse al mio petto la croce  
> (into the Arno, and loosened upon my breast the cross)
>
> 127 ch'i' fe' di me quando 'l dolor mi vinse;  
> (that I made of myself when pain overcame me;)

The swollen river carries Bonconte into the Arno and uncrosses the arms he had folded over his chest.

That physical undoing has a precise theological limit. The flood can dismantle the bodily sign of the cross, but cannot erase the repentance it expressed. The devil's apparent victory over the corpse exposes his decisive failure to possess the soul.

The missing body, which might suggest abandonment, thus becomes evidence of the distance between earthly appearances and divine judgment.

## 10. Pia: A Whole Tragedy in a Few Words

> 130 «Deh, quando tu sarai tornato al mondo  
> (“Ah, when you have returned to the world)
>
> 131 e riposato de la lunga via»,  
> (and rested from the long journey,”)

The final speaker begins by considering Dante's fatigue. Before asking anything of him, she allows him time to return and rest.

After the urgency of the other souls and the violence of Bonconte's storm, this courtesy transforms the canto's emotional register. Pia's concern for the traveler quietly exemplifies the charity toward which purgatorial life is directed.

> 133 «ricorditi di me, che son la Pia;  
> (“remember me, who am Pia;)
>
> 134 Siena mi fé, disfecemi Maremma:  
> (Siena made me; Maremma unmade me:)
>
> 135 salsi colui che 'nnanellata pria  
> (he knows it who, having first ringed me,)
>
> 136 disposando m'avea con la sua gemma».  
> (had married me with his jewel.”)

Pia is traditionally identified as **Pia de' Tolomei**, a Sienese woman whose death was attributed to her husband, commonly identified as Nello dei Pannocchieschi. The historical details remain uncertain, and Dante's text does not establish the elaborate motives supplied by later retellings.

“Siena made me; Maremma unmade me” compresses an entire life into birthplace and place of destruction. The balanced wording makes that destruction devastatingly concise.

The wedding jewel then recalls a pledge of fidelity beside an implied betrayal. Pia does not narrate the murder or explicitly name its agent; her husband knows what happened. Her restraint need not be mistaken for denying the wrong. Instead, the canto allows a victim to be remembered without requiring her to rehearse every detail of her suffering.

Her request, “remember me,” gathers together the canto's concerns with prayer, personal identity, and the obligations of survivors.

## Epilogue — Mercy Beyond the Last Violence

Canto V separates the fate of the soul from the visible circumstances of death. Political assassination, battlefield slaughter, and intimate betrayal can destroy bodies without defeating a genuine final turn toward God.

Yet salvation does not erase relationship or responsibility. The dead still seek the prayers of the living, and Dante must learn to give them attention without losing his direction. From Virgil's firm tower to Bonconte's little tear and Pia's quiet appeal, the canto joins steadfast purpose with compassionate remembrance.
