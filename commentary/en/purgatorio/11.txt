“O our Father, who dwell in the heavens,
not circumscribed, but through the greater love
that you have for your first creations up there,
praised be your name and your power
by every creature, as is fitting
to render thanks to your sweet breath.
May the peace of your kingdom come toward us,
for we cannot reach it by ourselves,
unless it comes, with all our striving.
As your angels of their will
make sacrifice to you, singing hosanna,
so may men do with their own.
Give us today the daily manna,
without which through this harsh wilderness
backward goes whoever strives hardest to advance.
And as we the wrong we have suffered
forgive in everyone, so do you forgive
graciously, and do not regard our merit.
Our strength, which so easily yields,
do not put to the test against the ancient adversary,
but deliver it from him who so assails it.
This final prayer, dear Lord,
indeed is not made for ourselves, since there is no need,
but for those who remained behind us.”
Thus, for good fortune for themselves and us,
those shades were praying as they went beneath the burden,
like that which is sometimes dreamed of,
unequally tormented, all around the circle
and weary along the first terrace,
purging away the darkness of the world.
If over there good is always spoken for us,
what can be said and done for them here
by those whose will has a good root?
Surely we should help them wash away the stains
that they carried from here, so that, clean and light,
they may rise to the starry spheres.
“Ah, may justice and mercy relieve your burden
soon, so that you may spread your wings,
which may lift you according to your desire,
show on which hand toward the stairway
the shortest way lies; and if there is more than one passage,
point out to us the one that slopes down least steeply;
for this man who comes with me, because of the burden
of Adam's flesh in which he is clothed,
is slow to climb, against his will.”
Their words, which they returned to these
spoken by the one whom I followed,
did not make clear from whom they came;
but it was said: “To the right along the bank
come with us, and you will find the passage
possible for a living person to climb.
And were I not hindered by the stone
that subdues my proud neck,
so that I must keep my face lowered,
this man, who still lives and is not named,
I would look at, to see whether I know him,
and to move him to pity for this burden.
I was Italian and born of a great Tuscan:
Guiglielmo Aldobrandesco was my father;
I do not know whether his name was ever known to you.
The ancient bloodline and the noble deeds
of my ancestors made me so arrogant,
that, giving no thought to our common mother,
I held every person in contempt to such an extent,
that I died of it, as the Sienese know,
and every child in Campagnatico knows it.
I am Omberto; and harm, not to me alone,
pride does, for all my kinsmen
has it dragged with it into misfortune.
And here I must carry this weight
because of it, until satisfaction is made to God,
since I did not make it among the living, here among the dead.”
Listening, I bent my face downward;
and one of them, not the one who was speaking,
twisted beneath the weight that hampered him,
and saw me and recognized me and called out,
keeping his eyes with difficulty fixed
on me, who walked all bent down with them.
“Oh!” I said to him, “are you not Oderisi,
the honor of Gubbio and the honor of that art
which in Paris is called illumination?”
“Brother,” he said, “more brightly smile the pages
that Franco Bolognese paints;
the honor is now wholly his, and mine only in part.
Surely I would not have been so generous
while I lived, because of the great desire
for excellence on which my heart was set.
For such pride the penalty is paid here;
and I would not yet be here, were it not
that, while still able to sin, I turned to God.
Oh vain glory of human powers!
how briefly the green upon the summit lasts,
unless an age of ignorance follows it!
Cimabue believed that in painting
he held the field, and now Giotto has the acclaim,
so that the former's fame is obscured.
Thus one Guido has taken from the other
the glory of the language; and perhaps there is born
one who will drive both of them from the nest.
Worldly renown is nothing but a breath
of wind, which comes now from here and now from there,
and changes its name because it changes direction.
What greater fame will you have if you shed in old age
your flesh than if you had died
before you left behind your ‘num-nums’ and your ‘pennies’,
before a thousand years pass? which is a shorter
span beside eternity than a movement of the eyelids
beside the sphere that turns most slowly in heaven.
He who covers so little of the road
ahead of me resounded through all Tuscany;
and now he is scarcely whispered of in Siena,
whose lord he was when it was destroyed,
the Florentine fury, which was proud
at that time as it is now a whore.
Your renown is the color of grass,
which comes and goes, and he discolors it
through whom it springs fresh from the earth.”
And I to him: “Your truthful speech instills in me
good humility, and you flatten a great swelling in me;
but who is that man of whom you were speaking just now?”
“That man is,” he replied, “Provenzan Salvani;
and he is here because he was presumptuous
in bringing all Siena into his hands.
Thus he has gone and goes, without rest,
since he died; such is the coin paid
in atonement by whoever is too bold over there.”
And I: “If that spirit who awaits,
before repenting, the edge of life,
remains down here and does not ascend up here,
unless good prayer assists him,
before as much time has passed as he lived,
how was his coming granted to him?”
“When he lived in greatest glory,” he said,
“freely, in the Campo of Siena,
putting aside all shame, he stationed himself;
and there, to draw his friend out of suffering,
which he endured in Charles's prison,
he brought himself to tremble in every vein.
I will say no more, and I know I speak obscurely;
but little time will pass before your neighbors
will bring it about that you can explain it.
This deed removed those bounds for him.”
