# Love, Anger, and the Ascent of the Soul: Dante's *Purgatorio*, Canto XVII

Canto XVII takes Dante from the smoke of the wrathful to the terrace of the slothful. Its movement is both physical and intellectual: emerging from the darkness of anger, the pilgrim begins to understand the principle underlying every terrace of Purgatory. That principle is love—not simply love as a virtue, but love as the motive that can become either virtuous or sinful according to its object, intensity, and direction.

## 1. Emerging from the Smoke of Anger

> 4 come, quando i vapori umidi e spessi  
> (how, when the damp and dense vapors)
>
> 5 a diradar cominciansi, la spera  
> (begin to thin, the sphere)
>
> 6 del sol debilemente entra per essi;  
> (of the sun enters faintly through them;)

Dante asks the reader to remember being caught in mountain fog and then seeing sunlight gradually penetrate it. This familiar experience supplies an image for his emergence from the smoke covering the third terrace, where wrath is purified.

The smoke is an instance of *contrapasso*: a punishment or corrective condition that reveals the nature of the sin. Anger clouds judgment, obscures other people, and prevents the angry person from seeing clearly. Here that inward blindness becomes an outward environment.

The returning sunlight therefore signifies more than improved visibility. Dante is recovering the clarity that anger destroys. Yet the sun is setting: his progress takes place within limits, and he must use the remaining light while he can.

## 2. Imagination and the Source of Vision

> 16 chi move te, se 'l senso non ti porge?  
> (who moves you, if sense supplies you nothing?)
>
> 17 Moveti lume che nel ciel s'informa,  
> (A light that takes form in heaven moves you,)
>
> 18 per sé o per voler che giù lo scorge.  
> (of itself, or through a will that guides it downward.)

Dante addresses the imagination as a power capable of absorbing attention so completely that the external world disappears. His question is philosophical: if a mental image does not come from present sensory experience, what produces it?

His answer reflects medieval accounts of the relationship between the human mind and the heavens. Imagination may be moved through celestial influence or through a will directing heavenly illumination toward the individual. In this setting, the visions function as providential instruction rather than arbitrary daydreams.

The distinction matters because Dante is about to encounter images of destructive anger. They arrive within his mind, but their moral authority does not depend merely on his private feelings. Imagination becomes an instrument through which otherwise distant histories can be experienced as immediate warnings.

## 3. Three Visions of Wrath: Procne, Haman, and Amata

> 19 De l'empiezza di lei che mutò forma  
> (Of the impiety of her who changed form)
>
> 20 ne l'uccel ch'a cantar più si diletta,  
> (into the bird that delights most in singing,)
>
> 21 ne l'imagine mia apparve l'orma;  
> (the imprint appeared in my imagination;)

The first vision concerns Procne. After her husband Tereus raped her sister Philomela, Procne took revenge by killing their son Itys and serving his flesh to his father.

Dante follows the tradition in which Procne becomes a nightingale; other versions assign that transformation to Philomela. The beautiful singing bird thus conceals a history of appalling violence.

This example shows how anger can turn a response to genuine wrongdoing into a new atrocity. Procne has suffered an outrage against her family, but revenge makes her destroy that family herself. The horror lies in the transformation of maternal love into murderous retaliation.

> 25 Poi piovve dentro a l'alta fantasia  
> (Then there rained into the lofty fantasy)
>
> 26 un crucifisso, dispettoso e fero  
> (one crucified, scornful and fierce)
>
> 27 ne la sua vista, e cotal si moria;  
> (in his appearance, and thus he was dying;)

The second figure is Haman from the biblical Book of Esther. Angered by Mordecai's refusal to honor him, Haman planned not only Mordecai's death but the destruction of the Jewish people. Esther's intervention exposed the plot, and Haman suffered the execution he had prepared for his enemy.

Dante's image of crucifixion reflects the Latin biblical tradition's language for Haman's execution; translations also describe hanging or impalement. Around him stand King Ahasuerus, Esther, and Mordecai.

Haman remains fierce and contemptuous even as he dies. Unlike the penitents on the mountain, he is presented at the moment when anger has hardened into a settled disposition. His story also reveals the disproportion characteristic of wrath: a personal grievance expands into a project of collective destruction.

> 36 perché per ira hai voluto esser nulla?  
> (why in anger have you wished to be nothing?)
>
> 37 Ancisa t'hai per non perder Lavina;  
> (You have slain yourself so as not to lose Lavinia;)
>
> 38 or m'hai perduta! Io son essa che lutto,  
> (now you have lost me! I am she who mourns,)

The third vision comes from Virgil's *Aeneid*. Queen Amata opposed the marriage of her daughter Lavinia to Aeneas and favored Turnus instead. Believing Turnus defeated and dead, she killed herself.

Dante gives the emotional center of the scene to Lavinia, who confronts her mother's self-destructive action. Amata's determination not to lose her daughter has brought about precisely that separation.

Together, the three visions show anger attacking the bonds it claims to defend: Procne destroys her child, Haman turns injured pride into murderous persecution, and Amata abandons her daughter through suicide. Drawn from classical myth, Scripture, and Roman epic, they form a universal moral history of wrath.

## 4. The Angel: Light and Unprompted Help

> 57 e col suo lume sé medesmo cela.  
> (and with his light conceals his very self.)

A dazzling light interrupts Dante's visions. An angel directs the travelers toward the stairway, but its brightness overwhelms Dante's sight.

The paradox is important: the angel becomes invisible not through darkness but through excessive light. Divine reality exceeds the capacity of ordinary human perception. Dante's inability to see is therefore no longer the blindness produced by anger; it is the limitation of a finite creature encountering a higher order of being.

> 59 ché quale aspetta prego e l'uopo vede,  
> (for whoever waits for a request and sees the need,)
>
> 60 malignamente già si mette al nego.  
> (is already maliciously inclining toward refusal.)

Virgil explains that the angel guides them without waiting to be asked. He then turns this action into an ethical lesson: someone who recognizes another person's need but insists upon a request has already begun to withhold help.

The angel's conduct is the opposite of wrathful self-absorption. Instead of concentrating on injury, status, or retaliation, it attends immediately to another's good. Dante thus moves from examples of distorted relationships to an enactment of generous love.

## 5. Peace at the Threshold of Night

> 67 senti'mi presso quasi un muover d'ala  
> (I felt near me something like a movement of a wing)
>
> 68 e ventarmi nel viso e dir: ‘Beati  
> (and a fanning of my face, and the saying: ‘Blessed)
>
> 69 pacifici, che son sanz' ira mala!’.  
> (the peacemakers, who are without evil wrath!’.)

As Dante begins climbing, the angel's wing and voice mark his departure from the terrace of anger. The proclamation adapts the Beatitude from Matthew 5:9: blessed are the peacemakers.

Dante's qualification, “evil wrath,” is significant. Medieval Christian ethics did not necessarily condemn every movement of anger. Anger directed against injustice could be legitimate if governed by reason and charity. What must be purified is anger that becomes malicious, excessive, or vengeful.

Peace therefore means more than emotional quietness. It is the restoration of rightly ordered relationships.

Night then falls, and Dante's legs lose their power to climb. The mountain's prohibition on upward movement after sunset dramatizes the pilgrim's dependence on enabling illumination. Yet physical stillness need not become spiritual idleness: unable to ascend, Dante asks Virgil to teach him.

## 6. Sloth: Love That Fails to Act

> 85 Ed elli a me: «L'amor del bene, scemo  
> (And he to me: “Love of the good, falling short)
>
> 86 del suo dover, quiritta si ristora;  
> (of its duty, is restored here;)
>
> 87 qui si ribatte il mal tardato remo.  
> (here the sinfully slackened oar is plied anew.)

The travelers have reached the fourth terrace, where sloth is purified. Virgil defines its fault as an insufficiency of love for the good.

The traditional term is *acedia*. It means more than physical laziness or a preference for rest. It is spiritual sluggishness: a failure to pursue the good with the energy and commitment it deserves. A person may recognize what matters and still resist the effort required to attain it.

The image of the delayed oar makes this failure concrete. Life requires purposeful movement, but the slothful person neglects the labor of advancing. Purgatory restores that motion. Its corrective purpose is not simply to punish inactivity but to strengthen deficient love.

## 7. Natural Love and Chosen Love

> 94 Lo naturale è sempre sanza errore,  
> (Natural love is always without error,)
>
> 95 ma l'altro puote errar per malo obietto  
> (but the other can err through an evil object)
>
> 96 o per troppo o per poco di vigore.  
> (or through too much or too little vigor.)

Virgil's explanation begins from a sweeping premise: neither creator nor creature is without love. He then distinguishes natural love from love involving the mind's apprehension and choice.

Natural love is the inclination belonging to a being's created nature. Considered as that fundamental inclination, it does not err. Love involving judgment and choice, however, can become misdirected.

Its errors fall into three categories:

- It can pursue an evil object.
- It can pursue a good with insufficient strength.
- It can pursue a lesser good with excessive strength.

This account draws on medieval Aristotelian and Christian thought, especially the idea that desire seeks something apprehended as good. Sin does not require a separate faculty opposed to love. It arises when the power of loving loses its proper order.

## 8. The Same Root Produces Virtue and Sin

> 103 Quinci comprender puoi ch'esser convene  
> (From this you can understand that it must be)
>
> 104 amor sementa in voi d'ogne virtute  
> (love that is the seed in you of every virtue)
>
> 105 e d'ogne operazion che merta pene.  
> (and of every action that deserves punishment.)

These lines state the canto's central proposition. Love is the seed of both morally excellent action and punishable action.

Dante does not mean that every action is therefore equally acceptable. The common source makes right direction more important, not less. Love directed toward God as the highest good, and toward lesser goods in due measure, produces virtue. Love that reverses or neglects that order produces sin.

Purgatory consequently does not aim to extinguish desire. Its work is restorative: to retrain desire until the soul loves the right things in the right way.

Placed at the midpoint of *Purgatorio*, this teaching explains the mountain's architecture. Its terraces are not merely a catalogue of prohibitions. They constitute an ordered education of love.

## 9. Loving Another's Harm: Pride, Envy, and Wrath

> 115 È chi, per esser suo vicin soppresso,  
> (There is one who, through his neighbor's being brought low,)
>
> 116 spera eccellenza, e sol per questo brama  
> (hopes for eminence, and for this alone desires)
>
> 117 ch'el sia di sua grandezza in basso messo;  
> (that he be cast down from his greatness;)

The first distortion is pride. Its object is not simply personal excellence but superiority achieved through another person's humiliation.

The proud person treats the neighbor's standing as an obstacle. Instead of pursuing genuine goodness, this person imagines that becoming greater requires someone else to become smaller.

> 118 è chi podere, grazia, onore e fama  
> (there is one who power, favor, honor, and fame)
>
> 119 teme di perder perch' altri sormonti,  
> (fears to lose because another rises,)
>
> 120 onde s'attrista sì che 'l contrario ama;  
> (and therefore grieves so much that he loves the opposite;)

The second distortion is envy. Whereas pride seeks elevation through another's abasement, envy fears that another's advancement will diminish one's own possessions or standing.

Another person's flourishing becomes a source of sorrow. The envious person therefore desires its opposite: that person's decline.

Dante exposes a competitive conception of happiness in which honor and success seem valuable only when others possess less of them.

> 121 ed è chi per ingiuria par ch'aonti,  
> (and there is one who seems to take offense at an injury,)
>
> 122 sì che si fa de la vendetta ghiotto,  
> (so that he becomes greedy for revenge,)
>
> 123 e tal convien che 'l male altrui impronti.  
> (and such a person must press for another's harm.)

The third distortion is wrath. Injury, whether rightly understood or magnified by wounded pride, becomes an appetite for retaliation.

The description of the angry person as greedy for revenge makes vengeance resemble a craving. It no longer serves measured justice; it seeks satisfaction through someone else's suffering.

These three forms of love explain the terraces below Dante: pride, envy, and wrath all make another person's harm into something desirable. The preceding visions have supplied the dramatic examples; Virgil now reveals their common moral structure.

## 10. Excessive Love and the Terraces Still Ahead

> 136 L'amor ch'ad esso troppo s'abbandona,  
> (The love that abandons itself too much to it,)
>
> 137 di sovr' a noi si piange per tre cerchi;  
> (is lamented above us in three circles;)
>
> 138 ma come tripartito si ragiona,  
> (but how its division into three is explained,)
>
> 139 tacciolo, acciò che tu per te ne cerchi».  
> (I leave unsaid, so that you may seek it out for yourself”.)

The object referred to here is a lesser good: something desirable that cannot provide ultimate happiness. Above the terrace of sloth lie the three terraces associated with avarice and prodigality, gluttony, and lust.

Their objects—possessions, food, and sexual love—are not simply evil in themselves. The fault lies in excessive attachment, through which a limited good takes the place of the highest good.

The mountain's governing arrangement is therefore:

- **Pride, envy, and wrath:** love directed toward another's harm.
- **Sloth:** insufficient love for the true good.
- **The upper three terraces:** excessive love for lesser goods.

Virgil ends by withholding part of the explanation. His purpose is educational: Dante must become capable of reasoning, not merely of receiving answers. That invitation prepares for the next canto's further examination of love, freedom, and moral responsibility.

## Epilogue — Learning to Love in the Right Order

Canto XVII moves from obscured vision to moral understanding. The smoke reveals anger's blindness; the visions expose its violence; the angel demonstrates generous and peaceful action. Virgil then gathers these experiences into a single account of human motivation.

The canto's answer to sin is not the suppression of love but its reordering. Spiritual ascent requires learning what deserves love, how strongly it should be loved, and how that love should become action. Even when darkness halts Dante's feet, understanding can prepare his next step.
