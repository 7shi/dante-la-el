# Beatrice's Return: Allegory and Renewal in *Purgatorio* XXX

At the summit of Mount Purgatory, in the Earthly Paradise, Dante encounters Beatrice for the first time in the *Divine Comedy*. Her arrival fulfills the promise of the poem's opening: she was the heavenly figure who sent Virgil to rescue him. Yet their reunion brings accusation before consolation.

The canto joins several kinds of meaning. It recalls Dante's youthful love for the historical Beatrice, develops her role as a guide toward divine truth, and stages the painful self-recognition necessary for spiritual renewal. Its movement is from expectation to recognition, from bereavement to shame, and finally toward repentance.

## 1. The Heavenly Compass

> 1 Quando il settentrïon del primo cielo,  
> (When the seven stars of the highest heaven,)
>
> 2 che né occaso mai seppe né orto  
> (which never knew either setting or rising)
>
> 3 né d'altra nebbia che di colpa velo,  
> (nor a veil of any mist other than guilt,)
>
> 4 e che faceva lì ciascun accorto  
> (and which there made each one mindful)
>
> 5 di suo dover, come 'l più basso face  
> (of his duty, as the lower one does)
>
> 6 qual temon gira per venire a porto,  
> (for whoever turns the helm to come to port,)

The canto opens by continuing the sacred procession described in Canto XXIX. Its seven candlesticks become a heavenly counterpart to the seven stars by which sailors orient themselves. Just as the earthly constellation guides a helmsman toward harbor, these lights guide the procession toward its proper end.

The “highest heaven” evokes the Empyrean, the divine realm beyond the revolving physical heavens. Its light does not rise or set. Only guilt can obscure it: the obstruction is moral, not astronomical.

The candlesticks are commonly associated with the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit, within a procession drawing heavily on biblical imagery, especially Revelation. Their function here is unmistakable even where particular symbolic correspondences remain open: they embody divine illumination directing human action. Dante's journey needs not merely movement but a trustworthy orientation.

## 2. A Bride Welcomed with Biblical and Classical Words

> 10 e un di loro, quasi da ciel messo,  
> (and one of them, as though sent from heaven,)
>
> 11 ‘Veni, sponsa, de Libano’ cantando  
> (singing ‘Come, bride, from Lebanon’)
>
> 12 gridò tre volte, e tutti li altri appresso.  
> (cried out three times, and all the others after him.)

One of the twenty-four elders calls for the bride. These elders represent the books of the Old Testament according to a traditional medieval reckoning; the speaker is commonly understood as the figure associated with the Song of Songs.

His words come from Song of Songs 4:8. Medieval readers interpreted that biblical love poem as expressing, among other meanings, the union of Christ and the Church, or of God and the soul. Its use makes Beatrice's arrival more than the return of a beloved woman. Personal love becomes the vehicle of a sacred encounter.

The threefold repetition gives the summons a ceremonial force and suggests the Trinitarian patterning that permeates the procession.

> 19 Tutti dicean: ‘Benedictus qui venis!’,  
> (All were saying: ‘Blessed are you who come!’,)
>
> 20 e fior gittando e di sopra e dintorno,  
> (and throwing flowers above and all around,)
>
> 21 ‘Manibus, oh, date lilïa plenis!’.  
> (‘Oh, give lilies with full hands!’.)

The angels' greeting combines Scripture with Virgil. “Blessed are you who come” adapts the biblical acclamation associated with Christ's entry into Jerusalem. Beatrice receives a Christ-like welcome because she acts as an instrument of salvation, not because she is simply identical with Christ.

The lilies come from *Aeneid* VI, where Anchises laments the early death of the young Marcellus. A classical expression of mourning is transformed into a greeting for someone who has passed through death into eternal life.

This mingling matters especially because Virgil is about to disappear. His poetry remains present within the Christian ceremony even as his personal guidance reaches its limit.

## 3. Beatrice's Colors and the Recognition beyond Sight

> 31 sovra candido vel cinta d'uliva  
> (crowned with olive over a white veil,)
>
> 32 donna m'apparve, sotto verde manto  
> (a lady appeared to me, beneath a green mantle)
>
> 33 vestita di color di fiamma viva.  
> (clothed in the color of living flame.)

Beatrice appears in white, green, and flame-red, conventionally associated with the theological virtues: faith, hope, and charity. Her clothing presents her as a bearer of the virtues that lead human beings toward God.

The olive wreath carries associations of peace and wisdom. Later Dante explicitly connects its leaves with Minerva, the classical goddess of wisdom. As elsewhere in the canto, classical symbolism is incorporated into a Christian order.

Beatrice is often described as an allegory of theology or revelation. That is useful provided it does not erase her individuality. She is not merely an abstract subject in disguise: her theological authority works through Dante's memory of an actual beloved person.

> 37 sanza de li occhi aver più conoscenza,  
> (without having further recognition through the eyes,)
>
> 38 per occulta virtù che da lei mosse,  
> (through a hidden power that came from her,)
>
> 39 d'antico amor sentì la gran potenza.  
> (felt the great power of ancient love.)

Dante recognizes Beatrice before obtaining a clear view of her face. Her presence reaches him through an “occult” or hidden power, awakening the love of his youth.

This recalls the *Vita nuova*, where the sight and greeting of Beatrice possess extraordinary spiritual force. Yet recognition here is not simply nostalgic. The same love that once unsettled the young Dante must now expose the distance between his original promise and his subsequent conduct.

The concealed face also suits the canto's larger movement: Dante has encountered the authority of divine truth before he is ready to look upon it fully.

## 4. The Ancient Flame and Virgil's Disappearance

> 46 per dicere a Virgilio: ‘Men che dramma  
> (to say to Virgil: ‘Less than a dram)
>
> 47 di sangue m'è rimaso che non tremi:  
> (of blood remains in me that does not tremble:)
>
> 48 conosco i segni de l'antica fiamma’.  
> (I recognize the signs of the ancient flame’.)

Overwhelmed, Dante turns toward Virgil as a frightened child turns toward its mother. His intended words culminate in a translation of Dido's declaration in *Aeneid* IV: she recognizes the signs of an old passion as she falls in love with Aeneas.

The borrowing establishes a delicate contrast. Dido's renewed passion leads toward catastrophe; Dante's renewed love can lead toward salvation, but only through moral correction. Powerful feeling is not, by itself, proof of rightly ordered love.

Dante also reaches instinctively for Virgil's language at the very moment when he discovers that Virgil is gone.

> 49 Ma Virgilio n'avea lasciati scemi  
> (But Virgil had left us bereft)
>
> 50 di sé, Virgilio dolcissimo patre,  
> (of himself, Virgil, sweetest father,)
>
> 51 Virgilio a cui per mia salute die'mi;  
> (Virgil, to whom I entrusted myself for my salvation;)

The repeated name makes the loss intensely personal. Virgil is not dismissed as an inadequate doctrine: he is mourned as a beloved father.

Allegorically, his departure marks the limit of unaided human reason and classical wisdom. He has helped Dante understand sin, undergo purification, and recover moral freedom. He cannot lead him into the direct knowledge of heavenly realities.

Yet reason is not simply rejected. Beatrice's guidance builds upon the journey Virgil made possible. The transition is from one necessary form of guidance to another, more encompassing one.

## 5. A Reunion That Begins with Rebuke

> 55 «Dante, perché Virgilio se ne vada,  
> (“Dante, because Virgil goes away,)
>
> 56 non pianger anco, non piangere ancora;  
> (do not weep yet, do not weep yet;)
>
> 57 ché pianger ti conven per altra spada».  
> (for you must weep for another sword”.)

Beatrice's first word is Dante's name. This is the only occurrence of the poet's own name in the *Comedy*, and the surrounding narrative explicitly acknowledges the exceptional necessity of recording it.

The naming makes judgment personal. The traveler who has examined other souls must now hear his own life examined.

The “other sword” is the pain of remorse. Beatrice does not deny the reality of his grief for Virgil; she redirects him toward a deeper cause for tears. Loss hurts, but recognition of one's own wrongdoing must hurt as well.

> 73 «Guardaci ben! Ben son, ben son Beatrice.  
> (“Look well here! Indeed I am, indeed I am Beatrice.)
>
> 74 Come degnasti d'accedere al monte?  
> (How did you deign to ascend the mountain?)
>
> 75 non sapei tu che qui è l'uom felice?».  
> (Did you not know that here humankind is happy?”.)

Her questions are sharply ironic. How could Dante, who had pursued happiness elsewhere, finally have condescended to climb toward its true dwelling?

The Earthly Paradise represents the innocence and right ordering lost through Adam and Eve. Dante has reached the place of human happiness, but arrival does not automatically mean that his inward condition matches his surroundings.

Beatrice's severity is later compared to a mother's sternness toward her child. Its purpose is corrective rather than vindictive. Love appears bitter because it refuses to leave the beloved's self-deception untouched.

## 6. The Angels' Compassion and the Thawing Heart

> 82 Ella si tacque; e li angeli cantaro  
> (She fell silent; and the angels sang)
>
> 83 di sùbito ‘In te, Domine, speravi’;  
> (at once ‘In you, Lord, I have hoped’;)
>
> 84 ma oltre ‘pedes meos’ non passaro.  
> (but beyond ‘my feet’ they did not go.)

The angels sing from Psalm 30 in the Vulgate numbering, Psalm 31 in many modern Bibles. Its opening expresses trust in God and appeals against shame and abandonment.

They stop at the words “my feet,” within the passage about God setting the speaker's feet in a spacious place. The selected verses emphasize refuge, deliverance, and divine mercy—an appropriate counterpoint to Dante's humiliation.

Their song does not overturn Beatrice's judgment. Rather, it makes mercy audible within that judgment. Dante hears their harmony as compassion for his distress.

> 97 lo gel che m'era intorno al cor ristretto,  
> (the ice that had gathered tightly around my heart,)
>
> 98 spirito e acqua fessi, e con angoscia  
> (became breath and water, and with anguish)
>
> 99 de la bocca e de li occhi uscì del petto.  
> (through mouth and eyes came out from my breast.)

Before the song, Dante's shame had frozen him into silence. Compassion melts that rigidity into sighs and tears.

The preceding extended comparison describes mountain snow thawing under a warm wind. Here the natural process becomes an image of spiritual change: what was locked and unresponsive begins to move.

These tears differ from the tears shed over Virgil. Bereavement is giving way to contrition. Mercy makes it possible for Dante to acknowledge what severity has exposed.

## 7. Great Gifts, Greater Responsibility

> 115 questi fu tal ne la sua vita nova  
> (this man was such in his early life)
>
> 116 virtüalmente, ch'ogne abito destro  
> (in potential, that every good disposition)
>
> 117 fatto averebbe in lui mirabil prova.  
> (would have achieved a wonderful result in him.)

Addressing the angels, Beatrice describes Dante's exceptional early promise. The phrase “vita nova” means his youthful life while also inviting readers to remember the book in which he had narrated his transforming love for her.

“Virtually” here means potentially, not merely outwardly or approximately. Dante possessed capacities that could have developed into remarkable excellence.

In the preceding lines, Beatrice attributes those capacities both to celestial influences and, more importantly, to divine grace. This reflects a medieval distinction: the heavens may shape natural inclinations, but they do not remove freedom or determine moral choices.

> 118 Ma tanto più maligno e più silvestro  
> (But so much more harmful and more wild)
>
> 119 si fa 'l terren col mal seme e non cólto,  
> (does the soil become with bad seed and left untilled,)
>
> 120 quant' elli ha più di buon vigor terrestro.  
> (the more it possesses good earthly vigor.)

The agricultural metaphor explains why gifts increase responsibility. Fertile ground can produce a richer harvest, but neglected fertility can also nourish more vigorous weeds.

Dante's failure is therefore not a lack of ability. It is the misuse or neglect of abundance. Natural intelligence, emotional intensity, and poetic talent do not guarantee goodness; they require cultivation.

The image also resists an easy equation between artistic greatness and moral achievement. The poet places himself under precisely the judgment that his exceptional powers might otherwise tempt him to evade.

## 8. Beatrice's Death and the Pursuit of False Goods

> 124 Sì tosto come in su la soglia fui  
> (As soon as I was upon the threshold)
>
> 125 di mia seconda etade e mutai vita,  
> (of my second age and changed life,)
>
> 126 questi si tolse a me, e diessi altrui.  
> (this man withdrew from me and gave himself to another.)

Beatrice died in 1290, at about twenty-four—the threshold of the “second age” in Dante's scheme of human life. “Changed life” presents death as passage into another condition, not annihilation.

Her accusation is paradoxical: when she became spiritually more perfect, Dante valued her less. Her earthly presence had directed him toward goodness; her death should have deepened that orientation toward the eternal.

Instead, he turned elsewhere. The precise biographical meaning of “another” remains disputed. It can suggest other loves, but the accusation develops into something broader than romantic infidelity.

> 130 e volse i passi suoi per via non vera,  
> (and he turned his steps along an untrue way,)
>
> 131 imagini di ben seguendo false,  
> (following false images of good,)
>
> 132 che nulla promession rendono intera.  
> (which fulfill no promise completely.)

The central problem is misdirected desire. Dante pursued things that looked like the good but could not provide the fulfillment they promised.

This does not require the conclusion that all earthly goods are inherently evil. The danger lies in mistaking limited goods for ultimate happiness: treating pleasure, prestige, worldly achievement, or intellectual satisfaction as though they could answer the soul's entire need.

The “untrue way” also recalls the dark wood at the poem's beginning. Beatrice now supplies the moral history behind that opening crisis.

## 9. Why the Journey Had to Begin in Hell

> 136 Tanto giù cadde, che tutti argomenti  
> (So low he fell that all means)
>
> 137 a la salute sua eran già corti,  
> (for his salvation were already insufficient,)
>
> 138 fuor che mostrarli le perdute genti.  
> (except showing him the lost people.)

Gentler interventions had failed. Beatrice explains that she had sought to recall Dante through inspirations, dreams, and other promptings, but he paid them little heed.

The encounter with the damned therefore became an emergency remedy. Dante needed to see what disordered choices become when fixed permanently: the ruined forms of love and freedom displayed in *Inferno*.

Hell is not merely spectacle or punishment witnessed at a safe distance. Within Dante's conversion, it is a revelation of consequences he had refused to recognize in his own life.

> 139 Per questo visitai l'uscio d'i morti,  
> (For this I visited the doorway of the dead,)
>
> 140 e a colui che l'ha qua sù condotto,  
> (and to him who has led him up here,)
>
> 141 li prieghi miei, piangendo, furon porti.  
> (my prayers, weeping, were offered.)

These lines return to *Inferno* II, where Beatrice descended to seek Virgil's help. The canto now closes the narrative circle: the woman who commissioned the journey explains why it was necessary.

Her remembered tears qualify her present sternness. She has already wept for the person she now rebukes. Judgment and intercession belong to the same saving love.

Virgil's work also receives renewed dignity. Though absent, he is acknowledged as the guide who successfully carried out her request.

## 10. Lethe and the Necessary Cost of Repentance

> 142 Alto fato di Dio sarebbe rotto,  
> (The high decree of God would be broken,)
>
> 143 se Letè si passasse e tal vivanda  
> (if Lethe were crossed and such nourishment)
>
> 144 fosse gustata sanza alcuno scotto  
> (were tasted without any payment)
>
> 145 di pentimento che lagrime spanda».  
> (of repentance that sheds tears”.)

Lethe comes from classical mythology, where its waters bring forgetfulness. In Dante's Earthly Paradise, it has a specifically moral function: it removes the memory of sin after repentance and purification.

Beatrice insists that this release cannot precede acknowledgment of guilt. Otherwise forgetfulness would become evasion rather than healing. Dante must understand and own the past before being freed from its burden.

The metaphor of a meal and its “payment” does not mean that salvation can be purchased. It expresses a necessary moral relation: forgiveness cannot be treated as permission never to confront what one has done.

Earlier, Beatrice says that Dante's sorrow must match his fault. Her purpose is therefore not endless humiliation but truthful proportion. At the canto's close, repentance has begun, but the process is not complete; confession and immersion in Lethe still lie ahead.

## Epilogue — Love That Leads through Truth

*Purgatorio* XXX transforms a long-awaited reunion into an examination of conscience. Beatrice's beauty recalls Dante's original vocation; her rebuke reveals how he abandoned it. Virgil's disappearance marks both the achievement and the limit of the guidance that brought him this far.

The canto's deepest claim is that saving love does not merely comfort. It illuminates, judges, and restores. Before Dante can forget his sin, he must recognize it; before he can rise toward heavenly vision, his grief must become repentance.
