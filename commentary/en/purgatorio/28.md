# The Forest of Restored Innocence: Allegory and Background in *Purgatorio* XXVIII

At the summit of Mount Purgatory, Dante enters the Earthly Paradise: the garden humanity lost through the Fall. He has completed the mountain's penitential ascent, and Virgil has declared his will free, upright, and sound. Yet moral freedom is not the end of his journey. Before ascending to Heaven, he must understand the innocence represented by Eden and undergo a final transformation of memory.

Canto XXVIII therefore occupies a threshold. Its forest, flowers, birds, and streams are sensuous realities within the poem, but they also disclose a world in which nature and human desire have regained their proper relationship with God.

## 1. A Forest That Reverses the Dark Wood

> 1 Vago già di cercar dentro e dintorno  
> (Already eager to explore within and all around)
>
> 2 la divina foresta spessa e viva,  
> (the divine forest, dense and living,)
>
> 3 ch'a li occhi temperava il novo giorno,  
> (which tempered the new daylight for my eyes,)

The opening recalls the dark wood in which the *Inferno* began, but reverses its meaning. There Dante was lost, frightened, and unable to find the right way. Here he enters willingly, moved by an awakened desire to explore.

Both forests are dense, but density no longer signifies moral confusion. This forest is “divine” and “living”; its shade moderates the light rather than concealing the path. Nature shelters perception instead of threatening it.

The contrast also marks a change in Dante's desire. His eagerness is not the uncontrolled appetite punished below. It is the curiosity of a will newly capable of enjoying created things rightly. Eden does not abolish the senses: it restores their innocence.

## 2. The Breeze and the Music of Creation

> 7 Un'aura dolce, sanza mutamento  
> (A gentle breeze, without alteration)
>
> 8 avere in sé, mi feria per la fronte  
> (having any within itself, struck me upon the forehead)
>
> 9 non di più colpo che soave vento;  
> (with no stronger stroke than a mild wind;)

The deliberately gentle movement distinguishes Eden from the unstable weather of the inhabited world. The breeze touches Dante without assaulting him; movement exists without disturbance.

Its constancy anticipates the explanation given later in the canto. This is not an ordinary wind produced by changing atmospheric conditions. It belongs to an ordered environment protected from the disturbances associated with earthly weather.

Allegorically, such motion suggests freedom without disorder. The garden is alive, not static, but its activity does not break its peace.

> 19 tal qual di ramo in ramo si raccoglie  
> (such as from branch to branch gathers)
>
> 20 per la pineta in su 'l lito di Chiassi,  
> (through the pinewood upon the shore of Classe,)
>
> 21 quand' Ëolo scilocco fuor discioglie.  
> (when Aeolus releases the sirocco.)

The rustling leaves accompany the birds' song, and Dante compares their sound to the coastal pinewoods at Classe, near Ravenna. The comparison gives his otherworldly landscape a recognizable earthly texture.

Aeolus, the classical keeper of the winds, introduces pagan mythology into the description of biblical Eden. This is not a competing explanation of creation. Classical poetry supplies a language through which earthly beauty can suggest something of Paradise.

The birds and leaves together form a natural music: distinct voices participate in one harmony. The image anticipates the canto's larger reconciliation of nature, poetry, and divine order.

## 3. Clear Water Beneath Deep Shade

> 28 Tutte l'acque che son di qua più monde,  
> (All the waters that here are purest,)
>
> 29 parrieno avere in sé mistura alcuna  
> (would seem to contain some admixture)
>
> 30 verso di quella, che nulla nasconde,  
> (beside that one, which conceals nothing,)

A stream interrupts Dante's walk. Its water is purer than any water available in the ordinary human world: even the clearest earthly stream would seem contaminated beside it.

The comparison makes purity something Dante can almost see. The water's transparency anticipates its spiritual function, though he does not yet know that this is Lethe, the river that removes the memory of sin.

> 31 avvegna che si mova bruna bruna  
> (although it flows dark, dark)
>
> 32 sotto l'ombra perpetüa, che mai  
> (beneath the perpetual shade, which never)
>
> 33 raggiar non lascia sole ivi né luna.  
> (allows sun or moon to cast their rays there.)

The apparent paradox matters: the stream is shaded but not impure. Darkness here is an effect of sheltering foliage, not a sign of evil or deception.

Dante thus separates physical appearance from moral meaning. The garden's shade cannot simply be read through the symbolism of the *Inferno*. In this restored world, obscurity may protect, and a boundary may prepare rather than punish. The stream stops Dante because another stage of his transformation must precede his advance.

## 4. The Flower-Gathering Woman and the Memory of Proserpina

> 40 una donna soletta che si gia  
> (a woman all alone who went)
>
> 41 e cantando e scegliendo fior da fiore  
> (both singing and choosing flower from flower)
>
> 42 ond' era pinta tutta la sua via.  
> (with which her whole path was painted.)

Across the stream appears the woman later identified as Matelda. Her name is not supplied in this canto, and attempts to identify her conclusively with a historical woman remain uncertain.

Her immediate significance lies in what she does. Singing and gathering flowers, she participates joyfully in the garden's life. She recalls Leah in Dante's dream in the preceding canto, where flower-gathering is associated with the active life. Matelda is therefore often understood as an image of innocent, rightly ordered activity: human action no longer divided from happiness or divine purpose.

She is more than a decorative personification, however. She will interpret Eden and guide Dante through its waters. Beauty here becomes an invitation to understanding.

> 49 Tu mi fai rimembrar dove e qual era  
> (You make me remember where and what she was,)
>
> 50 Proserpina nel tempo che perdette  
> (Proserpina at the time when she lost)
>
> 51 la madre lei, ed ella primavera».  
> (her mother her, and she springtime.”)

The syntax of the last line is compressed: Proserpina's mother lost her daughter, while Proserpina lost springtime—evoking both the flowers she had gathered and the innocent world from which she was taken.

In classical myth, Proserpina was gathering flowers when Pluto abducted her into the underworld. Her mother Ceres's grief, and the eventual arrangement for her daughter's return, became associated with the cycle of the seasons.

The comparison brings the possibility of loss into a scene of beauty. Matelda resembles Proserpina before the abduction, but Eden is the place of perpetual spring. The pagan story remembers a threatened or interrupted happiness; Dante's Christian garden reveals the original innocence whose loss gives such stories their poignancy.

## 5. Love, Myth, and the Barrier of the Stream

> 64 Non credo che splendesse tanto lume  
> (I do not believe that so much light shone)
>
> 65 sotto le ciglia a Venere, trafitta  
> (beneath the eyelashes of Venus, pierced)
>
> 66 dal figlio fuor di tutto suo costume.  
> (by her son quite contrary to his usual practice.)

Matelda's eyes surpass even those of Venus when Cupid accidentally wounded her, causing her love for Adonis in Ovid's *Metamorphoses*.

Dante does not strip the Earthly Paradise of physical attraction. Instead, he uses the language of erotic poetry to suggest beauty released from possessiveness and corruption. Matelda's radiance is attractive, but her role is to lead desire toward knowledge and purification.

The comparison also demonstrates the limits of classical precedent. Venus supplies a recognizable measure of love's brightness, yet Matelda exceeds it.

> 73 più odio da Leandro non sofferse  
> (did not suffer more hatred from Leander)
>
> 74 per mareggiare intra Sesto e Abido,  
> (for surging between Sestos and Abydos,)
>
> 75 che quel da me perch' allor non s'aperse.  
> (than that stream from me because it did not then open.)

The implied subject of the first quoted line is the Hellespont, named immediately beforehand. Leander swam across that strait to visit Hero and eventually drowned in its waters. Dante compares his own frustration at the little river to the lover's hostility toward the sea separating him from his beloved.

The disproportion is revealing: a stream only a few steps wide can feel as formidable as a famous strait. Dante's attraction is genuine, but he cannot simply cross by an act of impatient desire. The boundary must be negotiated through the spiritual process that Matelda oversees.

Classical love supplies the emotional language; the Christian journey gives that emotion a new direction.

## 6. Edenic Joy and Humanity's Fall

> 80 ma luce rende il salmo Delectasti,  
> (but the psalm Delectasti gives light,)
>
> 81 che puote disnebbiar vostro intelletto.  
> (which can clear the mist from your understanding.)

Matelda explains her happiness by referring to Psalm 91 in the Vulgate numbering, Psalm 92 in many modern Bibles. Its “Delectasti” passage celebrates the joy God gives through his works.

This reference corrects any suspicion that her smile is inappropriate to a sacred place. Joy in creation is not necessarily distraction from God. Properly ordered, it is a response to the Creator.

Matelda's delight therefore joins perception, affection, and praise. She enjoys the garden without treating it as something independent of its divine source.

> 91 Lo sommo Ben, che solo esso a sé piace,  
> (The supreme Good, which alone is pleasing to itself,)
>
> 92 fé l'uom buono e a bene, e questo loco  
> (made humankind good and for good, and this place)
>
> 93 diede per arr' a lui d'etterna pace.  
> (gave to it as a pledge of eternal peace.)

God creates humanity both good in its original condition and directed toward good as its end. Eden is a pledge of eternal peace, not the final fulfillment itself. Even unfallen earthly happiness points beyond itself toward God.

Matelda then attributes humanity's expulsion to its own fault. The distinction is fundamental: suffering is not evidence that creation was originally evil. Human disobedience disrupted an order intended for happiness.

The canto's allegory rests on that possibility of restoration. Purgatory does not destroy human nature; it heals the will so that human beings can again move toward the good for which they were created.

## 7. A Garden That Nourishes the World

> 103 Or perché in circuito tutto quanto  
> (Now because in a circuit all)
>
> 104 l'aere si volge con la prima volta,  
> (the air revolves with the first revolution,)
>
> 105 se non li è rotto il cerchio d'alcun canto,  
> (unless its circle is interrupted somewhere,)

Dante has been puzzled by the wind and water because he previously learned that ordinary atmospheric disturbances do not reach the upper mountain.

Matelda answers within medieval cosmology. Eden rises above the region troubled by exhalations from earth and water. Its breeze instead results from the air's participation in the heavens' great daily revolution, unobstructed at this height.

The distinction makes the scene both marvelous and intelligible within the poem's account of nature. Paradise is not chaotic exception: its phenomena have an orderly cause.

> 109 e la percossa pianta tanto puote,  
> (and the plant thus struck has such power,)
>
> 110 che de la sua virtute l'aura impregna  
> (that it impregnates the breeze with its virtue)
>
> 111 e quella poi, girando, intorno scuote;  
> (and that breeze then, revolving, scatters it around;)

Here “virtue” means an effective natural power, not merely moral goodness. The breeze carries the plants' generative influence outward, helping explain vegetation that appears elsewhere without any visible sowing.

Eden thus becomes a source of fertility for the inhabited world. Although humanity has lost access to its original home, creation has not become wholly disconnected from that source.

Allegorically, the image suggests a goodness that continues to communicate itself. The fallen world retains traces and gifts of an order it no longer possesses intact.

## 8. Lethe and Eunoë: The Healing of Memory

> 124 ma esce di fontana salda e certa,  
> (but issues from a fountain constant and sure,)
>
> 125 che tanto dal voler di Dio riprende,  
> (which receives again from the will of God as much)
>
> 126 quant' ella versa da due parti aperta.  
> (as it pours out, open on two sides.)

The garden's water does not depend on the ordinary cycle of evaporation, rainfall, and replenished springs. Its source is sustained directly by God's will and divides into two streams.

The constancy of the source parallels the constancy of the breeze. Eden contains motion and abundance without depletion or instability.

> 127 Da questa parte con virtù discende  
> (On this side it descends with a power)
>
> 128 che toglie altrui memoria del peccato;  
> (that takes away one's memory of sin;)
>
> 129 da l'altra d'ogne ben fatto la rende.  
> (on the other it restores it of every good deed.)

The rivers perform complementary operations upon memory. Lethe removes the memory of sin; Eunoë restores the memory of good actions.

Dante adapts Lethe from classical accounts of the afterlife, where its waters bring forgetfulness. Eunoë, generally understood as Dante's own naming, draws on Greek elements suggesting a good mind or good disposition.

The distinction prevents purification from becoming mere erasure. The soul must not only be released from the burden of evil; it must also recover its relation to the good it has done.

These waters do not offer an escape from repentance. Dante reaches them only after the penitential ascent, and his confrontation with his past is not yet complete. Their full efficacy requires drinking from both streams: restored innocence involves a transformed memory, not an unexamined one.

## 9. The Golden Age as a Dream of Eden

> 139 Quelli ch'anticamente poetaro  
> (Those who in ancient times sang in poetry)
>
> 140 l'età de l'oro e suo stato felice,  
> (the Golden Age and its happy condition,)
>
> 141 forse in Parnaso esto loco sognaro.  
> (perhaps dreamed of this place upon Parnassus.)

Matelda proposes that the ancient poets' Golden Age was a dream of Eden. Parnassus, sacred to Apollo and the Muses, stands for poetic inspiration.

The suggestion is generous but carefully qualified by “perhaps.” Pagan poetry did not possess the full truth of Christian revelation, yet it could imagine features of humanity's original happiness.

Dante thus presents classical myth neither as simple error nor as equivalent to Scripture. It can be a partial, dreamlike apprehension of a truth more fully disclosed elsewhere.

> 142 Qui fu innocente l'umana radice;  
> (Here the human root was innocent;)
>
> 143 qui primavera sempre e ogne frutto;  
> (here spring is everlasting and every fruit;)
>
> 144 nettare è questo di che ciascun dice».  
> (this is the nectar of which each one speaks.”)

The “human root” is humanity in its first parents, Adam and Eve. Their innocence is the historical and theological reality that the Golden Age resembles.

Perpetual spring, spontaneous abundance, and divine nectar are familiar classical images. Matelda relocates them within the biblical history of creation and the Fall. Their deepest reference is not merely to a pleasant imaginary landscape, but to a condition in which nature, human desire, and divine purpose were undivided.

The garden consequently fulfills both a religious promise and a poetic longing.

## 10. The Poets' Smile and the Turn Toward a New Guide

> 145 Io mi rivolsi 'n dietro allora tutto  
> (I then turned completely around)
>
> 146 a' miei poeti, e vidi che con riso  
> (toward my poets, and saw that with a smile)
>
> 147 udito avëan l'ultimo costrutto;  
> (they had heard the final statement;)
>
> 148 poi a la bella donna torna' il viso.  
> (then toward the beautiful woman I turned my face again.)

Dante turns toward Virgil and Statius, who have heard Matelda's interpretation of the Golden Age. Their smiles suggest pleasure in this recognition of ancient poetry's capacity to anticipate truth.

The moment does not erase their differences: Virgil remains a pagan excluded from heavenly beatitude, while Statius is a saved Christian. Yet both belong to the poetic inheritance that has helped bring Dante this far.

The canto ends with a small but decisive movement. Dante honors his poets by looking back, then returns his attention to Matelda. Poetry and reason have guided him toward restored earthly happiness; further preparation is now necessary before he can ascend beyond it.

## Epilogue — Innocence Restored, Fulfillment Still Ahead

Canto XXVIII presents Eden as the recovery of right relationship: desire with goodness, the senses with understanding, human activity with joy, and creation with its Creator. Its beauty is neither a temptation to reject nor a possession to seize, but something to receive and comprehend.

Yet the Earthly Paradise remains a threshold. Dante's will has been healed, but memory still requires transformation, and earthly innocence is not the same as the vision of God. The canto gathers the beauty of nature and the insights of classical poetry into a restored world, then directs Dante beyond them toward their ultimate source.
