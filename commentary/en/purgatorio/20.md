# The Hunger That Cannot Be Filled: Allegory and History in *Purgatorio* XX

Canto XX takes place on the fifth terrace of Purgatory, where avarice and prodigality are purified. The souls lie face downward: their posture makes visible the attachment to earthly things that once prevented them from looking toward God. Their tears, prayers, and recitation of moral examples gradually redirect their desires.

Against this penitential background, Dante develops a fierce criticism of the French monarchy. Private greed becomes dynastic ambition, political betrayal, and violence against the Church. Yet the canto ends with a sign that desire can finally be freed from its earthly captivity.

## 1. The Unsatisfied Sponge: Learning to Yield

> 1 Contra miglior voler voler mal pugna;  
> (Against a better will, a will struggles in vain;)
>
> 2 onde contra 'l piacer mio, per piacerli,  
> (therefore against my own pleasure, to please him,)
>
> 3 trassi de l'acqua non sazia la spugna.  
> (I drew the sponge from the water unsatisfied.)

The opening continues the preceding canto, in which Dante spoke with Pope Adrian V. Dante wishes to remain and learn more, but submits to the wish that he move on.

The sponge represents his capacity and appetite for knowledge: he withdraws it before it has absorbed everything it wants. This desire is not condemned as avarice. Nevertheless, its placement on this terrace invites a comparison between different kinds of hunger. An appetite becomes morally disordered when it refuses to recognize any higher claim.

Dante therefore begins by practicing something the penitents must also learn: surrendering immediate satisfaction to a better-directed will. His unsatisfied curiosity will return at the canto's close.

## 2. The Ancient She-Wolf: Avarice as a Universal Power

> 10 Maladetta sie tu, antica lupa,  
> (Accursed be you, ancient she-wolf,)
>
> 11 che più che tutte l'altre bestie hai preda  
> (who have more prey than all the other beasts)
>
> 12 per la tua fame sanza fine cupa!  
> (because of your endlessly deep hunger!)

The she-wolf recalls the animal that blocked Dante's ascent in *Inferno* I. There she embodied ravenous acquisitiveness; here the identification with avarice becomes explicit through the terrace's setting.

Her defining characteristic is not possession but insatiability. She cannot be satisfied because acquisition itself has become the object of desire. The more she consumes, the more she wants.

Calling her ancient also places the problem beyond any single ruler or generation. The political crimes described later are historical manifestations of a persistent human disorder.

> 13 O ciel, nel cui girar par che si creda  
> (O heaven, in whose turning it seems people believe)
>
> 14 le condizion di qua giù trasmutarsi,  
> (the conditions down here to be transformed,)
>
> 15 quando verrà per cui questa disceda?  
> (when will the one come through whom she departs?)

Dante's invocation reflects the medieval belief that celestial movements influence earthly conditions. But the essential question is political and providential: when will an agent arrive who can drive avarice from the world?

This recalls the promised hound that will defeat the she-wolf in *Inferno* I. Neither passage securely identifies a particular historical deliverer. The uncertainty matters: Dante can diagnose the corruption with confidence, but the means and timing of its defeat remain hidden.

## 3. Mary, Fabricius, and Nicholas: Three Alternatives to Possession

> 22 e seguitar: «Povera fosti tanto,  
> (and continue: “You were so poor,)
>
> 23 quanto veder si può per quello ospizio  
> (as can be seen from that lodging)
>
> 24 dove sponesti il tuo portato santo».  
> (where you laid down your holy child”.)

Dante hears a soul praising Mary's poverty at Christ's birth. The humble lodging becomes evidence that spiritual worth does not depend on material abundance. Indeed, the Incarnation places supreme value within circumstances of outward deprivation.

The soul's preceding cry to Mary resembles a woman's cry in childbirth. The comparison gives penitence an almost bodily intensity: the formation of a new moral life is painful, not merely intellectual.

> 25 Seguentemente intesi: «O buon Fabrizio,  
> (Next I heard: “O good Fabricius,)
>
> 26 con povertà volesti anzi virtute  
> (with poverty you preferred virtue)
>
> 27 che gran ricchezza posseder con vizio».  
> (rather than to possess great wealth with vice”.)

Gaius Fabricius, the Roman statesman celebrated for resisting corruption, supplies a pagan counterpart to Mary. Classical accounts associated him with voluntary poverty and the rejection of rich inducements from King Pyrrhus.

The contrast is precise: poverty is not automatically holy, nor wealth automatically vicious. Fabricius chooses virtue when wealth would require surrendering it. Dante places Roman civic integrity beside Christian humility because both expose the false equation of riches with genuine good.

> 31 Esso parlava ancor de la larghezza  
> (He was still speaking of the generosity)
>
> 32 che fece Niccolò a le pulcelle,  
> (that Nicholas showed to the maidens,)
>
> 33 per condurre ad onor lor giovinezza.  
> (to bring their youth to an honorable life.)

Saint Nicholas completes the sequence. According to the familiar legend, he secretly provided dowries for three impoverished young women whose father could not support honorable marriages and whose circumstances threatened to force them into prostitution.

Here money is neither worshipped nor simply rejected: it is used to protect others. Mary exemplifies holy poverty, Fabricius incorruptibility, and Nicholas generosity. Together they show how material possessions can be subordinated to spiritual and human goods.

## 4. Hugh Capet and the Tree of Dynastic Corruption

> 43 Io fui radice de la mala pianta  
> (I was the root of the evil plant)
>
> 44 che la terra cristiana tutta aduggia,  
> (that overshadows all the Christian land,)
>
> 45 sì che buon frutto rado se ne schianta.  
> (so that good fruit is seldom gathered from it.)

The speaker identifies himself as Hugh Capet, founder of the Capetian dynasty. His family tree becomes an evil plant whose shade prevents the flourishing of Christian society.

The metaphor attacks more than individual misconduct. Dynastic institutions allow wrongdoing to reproduce itself: inheritance passes on power, ambition, and opportunities for predation. The expected association between a noble lineage and good fruit is reversed.

Importantly, Hugh speaks as a penitent. He condemns his own descendants and asks for divine justice against them. Purification requires him to place justice above family solidarity.

> 52 Figliuol fu' io d'un beccaio di Parigi:  
> (I was the son of a butcher of Paris:)
>
> 53 quando li regi antichi venner meno  
> (when the ancient kings failed)
>
> 54 tutti, fuor ch'un renduto in panni bigi,  
> (all except one who had become a monk in gray garments,)

This genealogy is not reliable history. Hugh Capet was the son of Hugh the Great, a powerful aristocrat, not a Parisian butcher. Dante draws on a hostile medieval tradition and appears to combine elements associated with Hugh Capet and his father.

The account of the old royal line withdrawing into monastic obscurity is likewise historically confused. Its purpose is polemical: it strips the dynasty of its claims to exalted origins and exposes the acquisition of royal authority as a worldly process.

Dante's underlying ethical point is not that humble birth produces wickedness. It is that hereditary prestige cannot turn wicked actions into noble ones.

## 5. Conquest and the Bitter Repetition of Amends

> 61 Mentre che la gran dota provenzale  
> (As long as the great Provençal dowry)
>
> 62 al sangue mio non tolse la vergogna,  
> (had not taken shame away from my bloodline,)
>
> 63 poco valea, ma pur non facea male.  
> (it was of little power, yet did no harm.)

The Provençal dowry refers chiefly to the wealth and territory brought to Charles of Anjou through his marriage to Beatrice of Provence in 1246. Hugh presents this acquisition as a turning point: the family's power grows, and its restraints disappear.

The shame removed here can suggest the modesty or inhibiting sense of low status that previously checked ambition. Enrichment does not improve the dynasty morally; it enables its appetite.

The subsequent references to Ponthieu, Normandy, and Gascony form a compressed indictment rather than a dependable chronological history. Some of the acquisitions preceded the Provençal marriage. Dante arranges the material to reveal a pattern of accumulation by force and deception.

> 67 Carlo venne in Italia e, per ammenda,  
> (Charles came into Italy and, to make amends,)
>
> 68 vittima fé di Curradino; e poi  
> (made a victim of Conradin; and then)
>
> 69 ripinse al ciel Tommaso, per ammenda.  
> (sent Thomas back to heaven, to make amends.)

Charles of Anjou defeated the young Hohenstaufen claimant Conradin and had him executed in Naples in 1268. Dante presents the execution as the sacrifice of a victim to political ambition.

Thomas is Thomas Aquinas, who died in 1274 while traveling to the Council of Lyon. Dante adopts a contemporary accusation that Charles was responsible for his death, traditionally understood as poisoning. This allegation is not established historical fact.

The repeated phrase about making amends is savage irony. Instead of correcting earlier wrongdoing, each new action adds another crime. The dynasty practices a grotesque reversal of penitence: its supposed remedies aggravate its guilt.

## 6. Charles of Valois: The Weapon of Judas

> 73 Sanz' arme n'esce e solo con la lancia  
> (He comes out without arms and only with the lance)
>
> 74 con la qual giostrò Giuda, e quella ponta  
> (with which Judas jousted, and he thrusts that)
>
> 75 sì, ch'a Fiorenza fa scoppiar la pancia.  
> (so that he makes Florence's belly burst.)

The second Charles is Charles of Valois, who entered Florence in 1301 under the authority of Pope Boniface VIII, ostensibly as a peacemaker. His intervention enabled the Black Guelfs to seize power and helped bring about the exile of Dante, a White Guelf.

Because the poem's journey takes place in 1300, Hugh describes these events as future. For Dante's first readers, however, the prophecy concerned an already recognizable political catastrophe.

The lance of Judas is an intentionally paradoxical weapon. Judas conquered through betrayal, not honorable combat; Charles likewise turns an apparent mission of peace into an instrument of factional violence. Florence's bursting belly makes the city a wounded body, exposing the human damage behind diplomatic language.

## 7. A Daughter for Sale: Avarice Against Natural Affection

> 79 L'altro, che già uscì preso di nave,  
> (The other, who once came from a ship as a captive,)
>
> 80 veggio vender sua figlia e patteggiarne  
> (I see selling his daughter and bargaining over her)
>
> 81 come fanno i corsar de l'altre schiave.  
> (as corsairs do over other female slaves.)

This Charles is Charles II of Anjou, captured in a naval battle in 1284. The sale concerns his daughter Beatrice's marriage to Azzo VIII d'Este in 1305, which Dante presents as a financially motivated transaction.

The comparison with corsairs strips a dynastic marriage of its ceremonial dignity. What rulers may describe as alliance or policy becomes, in Hugh's language, the sale of a human being.

The episode advances the canto's argument about greed. Avarice does not merely injure strangers or political enemies; it can destroy the natural affection owed to one's own children. Its victims include the very family whose greatness it supposedly serves.

## 8. Anagni and the Templars: Christ Assaulted Again

> 86 veggio in Alagna intrar lo fiordaliso,  
> (I see the fleur-de-lis enter Anagni,)
>
> 87 e nel vicario suo Cristo esser catto.  
> (and Christ be taken captive in his vicar.)
>
> 88 Veggiolo un'altra volta esser deriso;  
> (I see him mocked once again;)
>
> 89 veggio rinovellar l'aceto e 'l fiele,  
> (I see the vinegar and gall renewed,)
>
> 90 e tra vivi ladroni esser anciso.  
> (and him slain among living robbers.)

The fleur-de-lis represents the French monarchy. In 1303, agents of Philip IV, acting with Sciarra Colonna, seized and humiliated Pope Boniface VIII at Anagni.

Dante's treatment is striking because he was intensely hostile to Boniface. Nevertheless, the distinction between a corrupt officeholder and the sacred office remains decisive. An assault on the pope as Christ's vicar becomes symbolically an assault on Christ.

The vinegar, gall, mockery, and robbers recast Anagni as a renewed Passion. This is not a literal account of Boniface's death: he survived the immediate attack and died shortly afterward. The language interprets the event's spiritual significance, presenting political violence as sacrilege.

> 91 Veggio il novo Pilato sì crudele,  
> (I see the new Pilate so cruel,)
>
> 92 che ciò nol sazia, ma sanza decreto  
> (that this does not satisfy him, but without decree)
>
> 93 portar nel Tempio le cupide vele.  
> (he carries his covetous sails into the Temple.)

The new Pilate is Philip IV. The Temple refers to the Knights Templar, against whom Philip launched arrests and confiscations in 1307, before the order's eventual formal suppression.

The nautical metaphor makes royal greed resemble a predatory expedition. The king's appetite sets sail toward another source of wealth.

Dante thus links the humiliation of the papacy with the attack on a religious order. Whatever arguments royal policy might offer in its defense, Hugh identifies the driving force as covetousness. The she-wolf's hunger has entered the institutions entrusted with protecting Christian life.

## 9. Day and Night: The Reeducation of Desire

> 100 tanto è risposto a tutte nostre prece  
> (for so long it is a response to all our prayers)
>
> 101 quanto 'l dì dura; ma com' el s'annotta,  
> (as the day lasts; but when night falls,)
>
> 102 contrario suon prendemo in quella vece.  
> (we take up a contrary sound in its place.)

Hugh now answers Dante's earlier question about the recitation of virtuous examples. During daylight the souls proclaim models of poverty and generosity; at night they recall the destructive consequences of avarice.

This alternating discipline educates both attraction and aversion. The penitents learn to love rightly, but also to recognize the ugliness of what they once desired.

Hugh later explains that he was not the only soul praising virtue: he was simply the only nearby speaker raising his voice. The terrace's instruction is communal, although its intensity varies with each soul's inward impulse.

> 106 e la miseria de l'avaro Mida,  
> (and the misery of avaricious Midas,)
>
> 107 che seguì a la sua dimanda gorda,  
> (which followed his greedy request,)
>
> 108 per la qual sempre convien che si rida.  
> (at which one must always laugh.)

Midas's wish that everything he touched become gold makes avarice expose its own absurdity. The fulfillment of his desire deprives him of nourishment: wealth becomes incompatible with life.

The other nighttime examples extend this diagnosis across classical and biblical history. Pygmalion murders for treasure; Achan steals forbidden spoils; Ananias and Sapphira lie about money supposedly dedicated to the community; Heliodorus attempts to seize sacred treasure; Polymestor murders the young Polydorus for his gold.

The sequence culminates in the traditional story of molten gold poured into the mouth of the dead Crassus. Its horrible joke literalizes his appetite. These examples show wealth displacing loyalty to kin, guests, community, and God—the same relationships violated by the royal crimes Hugh has just described.

## 10. The Trembling Mountain: Liberation Beyond Possession

> 127 quand' io senti', come cosa che cada,  
> (when I felt, like something falling,)
>
> 128 tremar lo monte; onde mi prese un gelo  
> (the mountain tremble; whereupon a chill seized me)
>
> 129 qual prender suol colui ch'a morte vada.  
> (such as commonly seizes one going to death.)

The earthquake abruptly interrupts political history and moral instruction. Dante initially experiences it as a threat: the mountain, which should provide stable ground for ascent, seems to become unstable.

Its meaning will be explained in the next canto. The mountain shakes when a purified soul becomes ready to rise toward heaven; on this occasion, that soul is Statius. What frightens Dante is therefore a sign of liberation.

The reversal is characteristic of Purgatory. Pain, bodily abasement, and apparent disturbance belong to a process whose end is freedom.

> 136 ‘Glorïa in excelsis’ tutti ‘Deo’  
> (‘Glory in the highest’ all ‘to God’)
>
> 137 dicean, per quel ch'io da' vicin compresi,  
> (were saying, from what I understood from those nearby,)
>
> 138 onde intender lo grido si poteo.  
> (so that the cry could be understood.)

The souls sing the angels' proclamation at Christ's birth. Dante compares himself and Virgil to the shepherds who first heard it.

This returns the canto to the Nativity recalled in Mary's poverty. The humble birth that opened the terrace's moral examples now supplies the language for celebrating spiritual renewal.

The collective rejoicing also offers a direct opposite to avarice. One soul's gain diminishes no one else. Instead of competing to possess a limited good, the entire mountain celebrates a freedom that others will eventually share.

> 149 né per la fretta dimandare er' oso,  
> (nor, because of the haste, did I dare to ask,)
>
> 150 né per me lì potea cosa vedere:  
> (nor could I discern anything there by myself:)
>
> 151 così m'andava timido e pensoso.  
> (thus I went on, timid and thoughtful.)

Dante ends as he began: eager to know more, yet unable to satisfy his desire immediately. The canto does not condemn this hunger for understanding. It teaches him that knowledge must sometimes be received patiently rather than seized.

His uncertainty also distinguishes the pilgrim from the poem's reader, who will eventually understand the earthquake. Spiritual progress can precede full comprehension of what is happening.

## Epilogue — From Insatiable Hunger to Shared Freedom

Canto XX traces avarice from the individual appetite to the corruption of families, kingdoms, and religious institutions. Its governing images—the hungry wolf, the poisonous family tree, the daughter treated as merchandise—show possession becoming a power that consumes human relationships.

Against this stand poverty without humiliation, integrity without purchase, and generosity without calculation. The final earthquake reveals their ultimate direction: liberation from a will imprisoned by lesser goods. Where avarice insists on having more for oneself, Purgatory teaches every soul to rejoice in another's freedom.
