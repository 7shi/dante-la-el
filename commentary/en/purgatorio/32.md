# Purgatorio XXXII: The Tree, the Chariot, and the Corruption of the Church

Canto XXXII turns the earthly paradise into a stage for sacred history. Dante has reached Eden, undergone Beatrice's rebuke, and been cleansed in Lethe. Now his personal restoration opens onto a larger question: how has the Christian world departed from the order that Christ restored?

The canto answers through a procession, a miraculously renewed tree, and a series of assaults upon a chariot. Its symbolism draws on Genesis, the Gospels, Revelation, classical mythology, and medieval political history. Some identifications are clear; others remain disputed. The governing contrast, however, is unmistakable: divine justice renews what human disobedience and institutional greed destroy.

## 1. Looking at Beatrice: Desire Must Become Understanding

> 1 Tant' eran li occhi miei fissi e attenti  
> (So fixed and intent were my eyes)
>
> 2 a disbramarsi la decenne sete,  
> (on satisfying their ten-year thirst,)
>
> 3 che li altri sensi m'eran tutti spenti.  
> (that all my other senses were extinguished.)

The “ten-year thirst” recalls the interval between Beatrice's death in 1290 and the poem's fictional present in 1300. Dante gazes at the woman whose earthly beauty first awakened his love and whose heavenly authority has now brought him to repentance.

Yet even this apparently sacred absorption requires correction. The attendant virtues warn that he is looking too fixedly. Beatrice is not simply a beautiful object in which his private longing may rest: she also guides him toward truths that exceed personal desire.

His temporary inability to see after turning away resembles the aftereffect of looking at the sun. The comparison establishes a hierarchy of brightness: what he now sees in Eden is comparatively dim beside Beatrice's radiance. But he must nevertheless attend to it. The canto redirects his eyes from the satisfaction of love toward the responsibilities of understanding and testimony.

## 2. The Procession Turns: Christ Leads the Church

> 25 Indi a le rote si tornar le donne,  
> (Then the women returned to the wheels,)
>
> 26 e 'l grifon mosse il benedetto carco  
> (and the griffin moved the blessed burden)
>
> 27 sì, che però nulla penna crollonne.  
> (so that not a feather of his stirred thereby.)

The procession introduced earlier in the earthly paradise now turns and advances. Its ordered movement, compared just before this passage to a military formation following its standard, presents the heavenly community as disciplined and harmonious.

The chariot represents the Church. Its attendants include seven women, conventionally identified as the four cardinal virtues and the three theological virtues. The griffin, combining an eagle's and a lion's forms, represents Christ's divine and human natures united in one person.

The griffin moves the chariot without disturbing a feather. The detail suggests effortless power and an integrity unaffected by action: Christ sustains the Church without suffering alteration in his own nature.

Dante, Statius, and Matelda follow. Their movement through the otherwise deserted garden also recalls humanity's original exclusion from Eden. This is the rightful human dwelling made empty by the Fall, not an untouched landscape without a history.

## 3. The Barren Tree: Adam's Disobedience and Christ's Obedience

> 37 Io senti' mormorare a tutti «Adamo»;  
> (I heard everyone murmur “Adam”;)
>
> 38 poi cerchiaro una pianta dispogliata  
> (then they encircled a tree stripped)
>
> 39 di foglie e d'altra fronda in ciascun ramo.  
> (of leaves and other foliage on every branch.)

The name “Adam” identifies the tree's primary biblical background: the forbidden tree of Genesis and humanity's first disobedience. Its barrenness makes the consequences of that act visible.

Yet Dante's tree carries more than one meaning. It also represents divine justice and the order under which human beings should live. In political readings of the canto, it is associated with the Empire as a providential guardian of earthly justice. These meanings overlap rather than exclude one another: the central issue is obedience to a legitimate order that human appetite must not violate.

The tree broadens as it rises, making its upper growth increasingly inaccessible. Its extraordinary shape and height suggest a standard above ordinary human possession or control.

> 48 «Sì si conserva il seme d'ogne giusto».  
> (“Thus is preserved the seed of every righteous person.”)

The griffin speaks after the company praises him for not biting the tree. Christ's abstention reverses Adam's grasping.

The background includes the Christian understanding of Christ as the new Adam, especially in Paul's letters: where the first Adam disobeyed, Christ obeyed. The preservation of righteousness depends upon accepting the divine limit rather than treating everything desirable as something available for appropriation.

For the later political allegory, this matters profoundly. Christ does not seize what is not his to seize; the institutional Church will fail to maintain that restraint.

## 4. The Tree Flowers Again: The Restoration Accomplished by Christ

> 49 E vòlto al temo ch'elli avea tirato,  
> (And turning to the pole that he had drawn,)
>
> 50 trasselo al piè de la vedova frasca,  
> (he drew it to the foot of the widowed tree,)
>
> 51 e quel di lei a lei lasciò legato.  
> (and left that which came from her bound to her.)

The griffin binds the chariot's pole to the tree. The compressed final line indicates that the pole derives from the tree to which it is attached.

This action joins the Church to the order established by God. It also invites an association with the Cross: medieval traditions linked the wood of the Cross with the tree involved in Adam's Fall. What had been implicated in humanity's ruin becomes, through Christ, an instrument of restoration.

The political resonance remains important. The Church flourishes when properly related to divine justice, not when it takes possession of every form of authority.

> 58 men che di rose e più che di vïole  
> (less than that of roses and more than that of violets)
>
> 59 colore aprendo, s'innovò la pianta,  
> (unfolding its color, the tree renewed itself,)
>
> 60 che prima avea le ramora sì sole.  
> (which before had its branches so bare.)

The tree's renewal follows the griffin's obedient action. Dante compares it to the flowering of earthly plants in spring, but here the renewal is a miracle within a sacred drama.

The color lies between that of roses and violets. Its reddish-purple character can suggest Christ's blood and Passion, although the precise symbolism should not be reduced to a single fixed equivalence.

The essential point is the reversal of barrenness. Adam's disobedience deprived humanity of its original flourishing; Christ's obedience makes renewed life possible.

## 5. Sleep and Awakening: A Vision Patterned on the Transfiguration

> 70 Però trascorro a quando mi svegliai,  
> (Therefore I pass on to when I awoke,)
>
> 71 e dico ch'un splendor mi squarciò 'l velo  
> (and say that a radiance tore apart for me the veil)
>
> 72 del sonno, e un chiamar: «Surgi: che fai?».  
> (of sleep, and a call: “Rise: what are you doing?”.)

Dante falls asleep while the company sings a hymn he cannot adequately understand or reproduce. His account acknowledges a boundary: some experiences exceed the pilgrim's present powers and the poet's ability to represent them.

Before describing his awakening, he invokes the classical story of Argus, whose many eyes Mercury lulled to sleep before killing him. That myth supplies an image of irresistible sleep, but Dante then turns to a Christian model for the significance of waking.

> 76 Pietro e Giovanni e Iacopo condotti  
> (Peter and John and James, led)
>
> 77 e vinti, ritornaro a la parola  
> (and overcome, came back to themselves at the word)
>
> 78 da la qual furon maggior sonni rotti,  
> (by which greater sleeps had been broken,)
>
> 79 e videro scemata loro scuola  
> (and saw their company diminished)
>
> 80 così di Moïsè come d'Elia,  
> (by Moses as well as by Elijah,)
>
> 81 e al maestro suo cangiata stola;  
> (and their master's robe changed;)

The comparison is to the Transfiguration. Peter, James, and John witness Christ's glory in the company of Moses and Elijah; afterward, the extraordinary manifestation passes, and they return to ordinary awareness.

The “greater sleeps” broken by Christ's word are deaths: his voice can awaken not merely the sleeping but the dead.

Dante does not claim that his experience is identical to the apostles' revelation. Rather, their experience supplies a pattern for his own: overwhelming brightness, diminished consciousness, awakening, and the discovery that the visible company has changed.

Matelda stands over him, while Beatrice remains beside the renewed tree. The griffin and much of the procession have departed heavenward. Dante must now learn what happens to the Church within history after Christ's earthly presence.

## 6. Beatrice's Commission: A Vision to Be Written for the World

> 100 «Qui sarai tu poco tempo silvano;  
> (“Here you will be a forest-dweller a little while;)
>
> 101 e sarai meco sanza fine cive  
> (and you will be with me forever a citizen)
>
> 102 di quella Roma onde Cristo è romano.  
> (of that Rome of which Christ is Roman.)
>
> 103 Però, in pro del mondo che mal vive,  
> (Therefore, for the benefit of the world that lives badly,)
>
> 104 al carro tieni or li occhi, e quel che vedi,  
> (keep your eyes now on the chariot, and what you see,)
>
> 105 ritornato di là, fa che tu scrive».  
> (having returned to the world beyond, make sure you write.”)

The “Rome” of which Christ is a citizen is the heavenly community, not the corrupt political or ecclesiastical city of Dante's present. Dante applies the language of citizenship and imperial order to salvation itself.

Beatrice contrasts his brief stay in Eden with his eventual eternal dwelling. But between those conditions lies a task: he must return to the living world and write.

This command defines the purpose of the coming spectacle. Dante is not merely a privileged spectator of mysterious images. He is commissioned to expose the historical corruption of an institution whose divine origin he continues to affirm.

The earlier correction of his gaze now reaches its fulfillment. He must look where Beatrice directs him, not simply where his own desire draws him.

## 7. The Eagle and the Fox: Persecution and Heresy

> 112 com' io vidi calar l'uccel di Giove  
> (as I saw the bird of Jove descend)
>
> 113 per l'alber giù, rompendo de la scorza,  
> (down through the tree, breaking its bark,)
>
> 114 non che d'i fiori e de le foglie nove;  
> (as well as its flowers and its new leaves;)
>
> 115 e ferì 'l carro di tutta sua forza;  
> (and it struck the chariot with all its strength;)

The eagle, Jupiter's bird and Rome's imperial emblem, attacks both tree and chariot. This first assault conventionally represents the Roman Empire's persecution of early Christianity.

Its violence against the tree indicates that persecution violates justice itself. Dante's criticism is therefore not a rejection of imperial authority as such. An empire can possess a legitimate providential role and still act against that role.

The chariot reels like a storm-tossed ship but does not collapse. The Church survives direct coercion.

> 118 Poscia vidi avventarsi ne la cuna  
> (Then I saw leap into the body)
>
> 119 del trïunfal veiculo una volpe  
> (of the triumphal vehicle a fox)
>
> 120 che d'ogne pasto buon parea digiuna;  
> (that seemed starved of every good food;)

The fox represents heresy: cunning, intrusive, and spiritually undernourished. Its hunger is not a sign of holy asceticism but of deprivation of wholesome truth.

Beatrice drives it away by exposing its shameful errors. As the bearer of revealed wisdom, she defeats doctrinal corruption through correction rather than force.

The two attacks distinguish external violence from internal falsehood. Both threaten the Church, but neither yet produces the monstrous transformation that follows.

## 8. Imperial Feathers and the Dragon: Wealth and Rupture

> 124 Poscia per indi ond' era pria venuta,  
> (Then, by the way along which it had first come,)
>
> 125 l'aguglia vidi scender giù ne l'arca  
> (I saw the eagle descend into the body)
>
> 126 del carro e lasciar lei di sé pennuta;  
> (of the chariot and leave it feathered with itself;)

The eagle returns, but this time its apparent gift proves more damaging than its earlier attack. Its feathers commonly represent the temporal wealth and authority associated with the Donation of Constantine.

According to the medieval account, Constantine granted extensive possessions and powers to the papacy. The document supporting those claims was later demonstrated to be a forgery, but Dante treats the donation as a historical event and condemns its consequences.

His objection is not that Constantine necessarily intended evil. The canto later allows that the gift may have been offered with good intentions. The danger lies in the institutional result: spiritual government becomes entangled with property, sovereignty, and worldly ambition.

The heavenly lament immediately following this passage identifies the apparent enrichment as a disastrous burden.

> 130 Poi parve a me che la terra s'aprisse  
> (Then it seemed to me that the earth opened)
>
> 131 tr'ambo le ruote, e vidi uscirne un drago  
> (between the two wheels, and I saw a dragon emerge)
>
> 132 che per lo carro sù la coda fisse;  
> (that drove its tail upward through the chariot;)
>
> 133 e come vespa che ritragge l'ago,  
> (and like a wasp that withdraws its sting,)
>
> 134 a sé traendo la coda maligna,  
> (drawing its malignant tail toward itself,)
>
> 135 trasse del fondo, e gissen vago vago.  
> (it tore away part of the floor, and went wandering off.)

The dragon tears away part of the chariot's foundation. Unlike the fox, which enters and is expelled, it produces an enduring loss.

Its exact historical identity is disputed. Traditional interpretations often connect it with the rise of Islam and the loss of formerly Christian territories; others emphasize schism or a more general satanic assault. The dragon's biblical background, especially Revelation's imagery of Satan, makes destructive spiritual opposition its clearest meaning.

The sequence is not simply a list of hostile outsiders. It alternates persecution, doctrinal error, compromising patronage, and violent rupture. The Church's historical wounds have different causes, and apparently benevolent gifts may be especially dangerous.

## 9. The Seven-Headed Monster: Corruption Becomes Structural

> 142 Trasformato così 'l dificio santo  
> (The holy structure, transformed in this way,)
>
> 143 mise fuor teste per le parti sue,  
> (put forth heads from its different parts,)
>
> 144 tre sovra 'l temo e una in ciascun canto.  
> (three above the pole and one at each corner.)
>
> 145 Le prime eran cornute come bue,  
> (The first were horned like an ox,)
>
> 146 ma le quattro un sol corno avean per fronte:  
> (but the four had only one horn upon each forehead:)

Before this transformation, the imperial feathers spread over the surviving chariot like invasive grass. Wheels and pole alike become covered: worldly privilege no longer affects only one part of the institution but permeates it.

The resulting creature has seven heads and ten horns. These numbers recall the beasts of Revelation, especially the beast associated with the woman of Babylon in Revelation 17.

The heads are commonly read as the seven capital sins. Yet the larger apocalyptic resemblance matters more than assigning every anatomical feature a single exclusive meaning. An institution established to guide humanity toward salvation has acquired the appearance of the powers that oppose God.

Dante still calls it the “holy structure” at the moment of transformation. The phrase preserves the distinction between sacred foundation and corrupted historical condition. His denunciation depends upon the Church's vocation; it does not erase that vocation.

## 10. The Prostitute and the Giant: Papal Power Under French Domination

> 148 Sicura, quasi rocca in alto monte,  
> (Secure, like a fortress on a high mountain,)
>
> 149 seder sovresso una puttana sciolta  
> (sitting upon it, an unrestrained prostitute)
>
> 150 m'apparve con le ciglia intorno pronte;  
> (appeared to me, with her eyes alert on every side;)

The woman seated upon the beast develops the imagery of Revelation's prostitute of Babylon. Here she represents the corrupted papal establishment: spiritual authority prostituted to wealth and worldly power.

Her apparent security is deceptive. A giant stands beside her, and their occasional kisses signify an alliance between ecclesiastical and secular ambition. The giant is generally identified with the French monarchy, particularly Philip IV.

The relationship is not an equal partnership. When the woman turns her wandering gaze toward Dante, the giant beats her. The precise political meaning of that glance is uncertain, but the structural meaning is clear: an authority that has traded spiritual independence for powerful protection becomes vulnerable to its protector's violence.

> 157 poi, di sospetto pieno e d'ira crudo,  
> (then, full of suspicion and fierce with anger,)
>
> 158 disciolse il mostro, e trassel per la selva,  
> (he untied the monster and dragged it through the forest,)
>
> 159 tanto che sol di lei mi fece scudo  
> (so far that the forest alone became a screen for me)
>
> 160 a la puttana e a la nova belva.  
> (against the prostitute and the new beast.)

The giant removes the transformed chariot from its place beside the tree and drags it out of Dante's sight. The Church's corrupted leadership is forcibly detached from the setting of rightful order.

The scene is usually connected with French domination of the papacy, including the conflict that culminated in the assault on Boniface VIII at Anagni in 1303 and the establishment of the papal residence at Avignon in 1309 under Clement V. These events were future to the pilgrim's journey in 1300 but belonged to the political world in which Dante wrote.

The canto ends not with the beast's defeat but with its disappearance. Dante leaves the historical crisis unresolved, preparing for the prophetic response of the following canto.

## Epilogue — From Restored Life to Captive Power

Canto XXXII sets two transformations against one another. Under Christ's obedient action, the barren tree flowers. Under the pressure of worldly wealth and political ambition, the sacred chariot becomes a monster.

The contrast explains the canto's governing allegory: divine order is life-giving, while the appropriation of power corrupts even institutions founded for holy purposes. External persecution can wound the Church, but compromised prosperity can deform it from within.

Dante's role is to witness that deformation without confusing it with the defeat of divine justice. Beatrice turns his restored sight into a public obligation: what he has learned to see, he must write for a world that has lost its way.
