# Hunger, Betrayal, and Frozen Love in Dante's *Inferno*, Canto 33

Canto 33 takes place near the bottom of Hell, in the frozen lake of Cocytus. It begins in **Antenora**, where traitors to their homeland or political party are punished, and moves into **Tolomea**, the region assigned to those who betray their guests.

The canto's horrors are intensely physical: gnawing teeth, starvation, frozen tears, and bodies inhabited by demons. Yet their meaning is also moral. Betrayal destroys the relationships that sustain human life—political trust, family affection, hospitality—and Dante makes that destruction visible in damaged bodies and corrupted meals.

## 1. A Meal of Hatred: Ugolino's Savage Introduction

> 1 La bocca sollevò dal fiero pasto  
> (His mouth he raised from the savage meal,)
>
> 2 quel peccator, forbendola a' capelli  
> (that sinner, wiping it on the hair)
>
> 3 del capo ch'elli avea di retro guasto.  
> (of the head that he had ravaged at the back.)

The sinner is Count Ugolino della Gherardesca; the head belongs to Archbishop Ruggieri degli Ubaldini. Dante encountered them at the end of the preceding canto, with Ugolino gnawing Ruggieri's skull.

The gesture of wiping his mouth on his victim's hair makes this a grotesque parody of a meal. It also establishes the canto's governing imagery: mouths will speak, demand bread, offer flesh, and consume enemies.

Ugolino's punishment is inseparable from his hatred. Even when he becomes an eloquent narrator of suffering, he remains the figure introduced here—a human being reduced to animal violence.

> 7 Ma se le mie parole esser dien seme  
> (But if my words are to be seed)
>
> 8 che frutti infamia al traditor ch'i' rodo,  
> (that bears infamy for the traitor whom I gnaw,)
>
> 9 parlar e lagrimar vedrai insieme.  
> (you will see speaking and weeping together.)

Ugolino does not speak to confess or repent. He speaks to damage Ruggieri's reputation among the living. The metaphor of words as seed promises a harvest of infamy rather than nourishment.

His grief may be genuine, but his narrative also serves revenge. Dante therefore asks readers to hear a suffering father without forgetting that the father is also a damned and interested witness.

## 2. Political Betrayal Behind the Prison Walls

> 16 Che per l'effetto de' suo' mai pensieri,  
> (That through the effect of his evil designs,)
>
> 17 fidandomi di lui, io fossi preso  
> (trusting in him, I was taken prisoner)
>
> 18 e poscia morto, dir non è mestieri;  
> (and afterward killed, there is no need to tell;)

Ugolino was a powerful Pisan nobleman involved in the unstable factional politics of thirteenth-century Tuscany. His alliances crossed the divisions conventionally associated with the papal Guelfs and imperial Ghibellines. Ruggieri, Pisa's archbishop, helped bring about his overthrow in 1288.

Ugolino was imprisoned with two sons and two grandsons. In 1289, they were left to starve in the tower subsequently remembered as the Tower of Hunger.

His account emphasizes violated trust: he was captured while relying on Ruggieri. But he passes over his own political conduct. Dante places him among traitors without providing a complete judicial explanation of his guilt.

This distinction matters. The canto does not require Ugolino to have been politically innocent for his death—and especially the deaths of his descendants—to have been atrocious.

## 3. The Prophetic Hunt: Wolves, Hounds, and Political Power

> 28 Questi pareva a me maestro e donno,  
> (This man appeared to me as master and lord,)
>
> 29 cacciando il lupo e ' lupicini al monte  
> (hunting the wolf and the wolf cubs toward the mountain)
>
> 30 per che i Pisan veder Lucca non ponno.  
> (because of which the Pisans cannot see Lucca.)

Before the prisoners lose their food supply, Ugolino dreams that Ruggieri leads a hunt against a wolf and its cubs. The mountain is Monte Pisano, which stands between Pisa and Lucca.

The dream translates political events into animal allegory. Ruggieri becomes the commanding huntsman; Ugolino and his descendants become hunted creatures. The prominent Pisan families named in the surrounding lines—Gualandi, Sismondi, and Lanfranchi—participate in the pursuit.

The wolf is not an uncomplicated emblem of innocence. Its predatory associations preserve something of Ugolino's own dangerous political identity. But the cubs turn the hunt into an image of collective punishment: the offspring are destroyed along with the father.

> 34 In picciol corso mi parieno stanchi  
> (After a short run they seemed to me exhausted,)
>
> 35 lo padre e ' figli, e con l'agute scane  
> (the father and the sons, and with sharp fangs)
>
> 36 mi parea lor veder fender li fianchi.  
> (I seemed to see their flanks being torn open.)

The dream foretells the family's destruction, though not its exact physical form. The tearing of bodies anticipates the violence of starvation and recalls Ugolino's own biting of Ruggieri.

Predator and victim thus exchange positions across the episode. Political society has become a hunt, and infernal revenge perpetuates the same bestial logic.

## 4. The Locked Door and the Father Turned to Stone

> 46 e io senti' chiavar l'uscio di sotto  
> (and I heard the door below being nailed shut)
>
> 47 a l'orribile torre; ond' io guardai  
> (in the horrible tower; therefore I looked)
>
> 48 nel viso a' mie' figliuoi sanza far motto.  
> (into the faces of my children without saying a word.)
>
> 49 Io non piangëa, sì dentro impetrai:  
> (I did not weep, so completely had I turned to stone within:)

The sound of the door being secured converts suspicion into certainty. A small auditory detail communicates a death sentence more effectively than an explicit announcement would.

Ugolino's response is not an outburst but petrification. His inward transformation into stone anticipates the frozen emotional landscape of Cocytus. Yet his silence here need not mean lack of love: it expresses the paralysis of someone who understands what the children cannot fully comprehend.

Throughout the prison narrative, the children speak while the father repeatedly fails to answer. His political authority and paternal power have become useless. He can neither provide food nor explain their suffering in a way that helps them.

## 5. The Children's Offer: Love in the Language of Consumption

> 61 e disser: “Padre, assai ci fia men doglia  
> (and they said: “Father, it will be much less pain for us)
>
> 62 se tu mangi di noi: tu ne vestisti  
> (if you eat of us: you clothed us)
>
> 63 queste misere carni, e tu le spoglia”.  
> (in this miserable flesh, and you strip it away.”)

After Ugolino bites his hands in anguish, the children mistake the gesture for hunger. They offer him their own bodies as food.

Their words reverse the ordinary relationship between parent and child. The father who gave them flesh and should sustain it is invited to take it back. Clothing becomes a metaphor for embodiment, and stripping becomes a metaphor for destroying the life he helped create.

The offer can also suggest Christian sacrificial and Eucharistic language: flesh is offered so that another may live. But here there is no assured redemption, only a horrifying attempt to express filial love under impossible conditions.

Dante's presentation intensifies the children's dependence and tenderness. Historically, the prisoners were not all small children, and two were Ugolino's grandsons. The poem gathers them into the emotionally unified category of his children.

## 6. What Finally Defeats Ugolino?

> 73 già cieco, a brancolar sovra ciascuno,  
> (already blind, to grope over each one,)
>
> 74 e due dì li chiamai, poi che fur morti.  
> (and for two days I called them, after they were dead.)
>
> 75 Poscia, più che 'l dolor, poté 'l digiuno».  
> (Then fasting had more power than grief.”)

The children die one after another. Ugolino, blinded by his physical collapse, searches over their bodies and continues calling to them after death. His earlier silence gives way to speech only when no answer is possible.

The final line is famously ambiguous. Its straightforward meaning is that starvation killed him when grief had not: hunger proved stronger than sorrow.

Yet the canto's repeated imagery of eating—his gnawing of Ruggieri, his bitten hands, and the children's offer—also permits the darker suspicion that hunger overcame his resistance to consuming their bodies. The poem never explicitly says that this happened.

That uncertainty is part of the line's force. It leaves readers suspended between a father's death from starvation and the possibility of a final, unspeakable degradation. When Ugolino resumes gnawing Ruggieri, the return to the opening image offers no release from that unease.

## 7. Pisa as a New Thebes

> 82 muovasi la Capraia e la Gorgona,  
> (let Capraia and Gorgona move,)
>
> 83 e faccian siepe ad Arno in su la foce,  
> (and make a barrier across the Arno at its mouth,)
>
> 84 sì ch'elli annieghi in te ogne persona!  
> (so that it may drown every person within you!)

After Ugolino's account, Dante the poet interrupts with an invective against Pisa. He imagines the islands of Capraia and Gorgona moving to block the Arno and drown the city.

The fantasy enlarges the crime from an individual betrayal into a civic catastrophe. Pisa's political community is held responsible for permitting such cruelty.

There is also a disturbing excess in the response. The denunciation of collective punishment takes the form of a wish for collective destruction. Dante's indignation is morally powerful without being emotionally moderate.

> 87 non dovei tu i figliuoi porre a tal croce.  
> (you should not have put the children to such torment.)
>
> 88 Innocenti facea l'età novella,  
> (Their youthful age made innocent,)
>
> 89 novella Tebe, Uguiccione e 'l Brigata  
> (new Thebes, Uguiccione and Brigata)
>
> 90 e li altri due che 'l canto suso appella.  
> (and the other two whom the canto names above.)

Even if Ugolino betrayed Pisa by surrendering castles, Dante insists, his descendants should not have shared his punishment.

The phrase *tal croce*, literally such a cross, gives their suffering a Christian resonance. Their innocence concerns their freedom from responsibility for the alleged political offense, not a claim that they were without every possible sin.

Calling Pisa a new Thebes invokes the classical city notorious for dynastic violence and destructive conflict among kin. Christian and classical references converge: Pisa has transformed civic life into a place where the innocent suffer for the crimes of their elders.

## 8. Frozen Tears: Suffering That Cannot Escape

> 94 Lo pianto stesso lì pianger non lascia,  
> (Weeping itself there does not allow weeping,)
>
> 95 e 'l duol che truova in su li occhi rintoppo,  
> (and the grief that finds an obstruction at the eyes,)
>
> 96 si volge in entro a far crescer l'ambascia;  
> (turns inward to make the anguish grow;)

Dante and Virgil now enter Tolomea. Unlike the souls whose faces incline downward, these sinners lie face upward in the ice.

Their punishment is a precise example of *contrapasso*: a bodily condition that reflects the spiritual character of the offense. Those who betrayed the openness and trust of hospitality are sealed within themselves. Even grief cannot move outward freely.

> 97 ché le lagrime prime fanno groppo,  
> (for the first tears form a knot,)
>
> 98 e sì come visiere di cristallo,  
> (and like visors of crystal,)
>
> 99 rïempion sotto 'l ciglio tutto il coppo.  
> (they fill the whole hollow beneath the eyebrow.)

The first tears freeze over the eye sockets, preventing further tears from escaping. What ordinarily expresses and partly relieves suffering becomes an additional instrument of torment.

The crystal visor also suggests a barrier between persons. The eye, normally an organ of recognition and communication, is imprisoned behind its own frozen discharge.

Nearby, Dante notices a mysterious wind. Its source will be revealed in the next canto: Lucifer's beating wings. The deepest evil produces not warmth or creative motion, but the cold that immobilizes its own kingdom.

## 9. Fra Alberigo and the Hospitality of Death

> 118 Rispuose adunque: «I' son frate Alberigo;  
> (Then he answered: “I am Friar Alberigo;)
>
> 119 i' son quel da le frutta del mal orto,  
> (I am the one of the fruit from the evil orchard,)
>
> 120 che qui riprendo dattero per figo».  
> (who here receives a date for a fig.”)

Fra Alberigo, a member of the religious order known as the Jovial Friars, arranged the murder of relatives during a banquet in 1285. The command to bring fruit served as the signal for the killers.

Fruit, ordinarily the pleasant conclusion of a meal, became an announcement of death. This completes another stage in the canto's corruption of nourishment: food is denied in Ugolino's prison, human flesh is offered in desperation, and hospitality becomes a weapon at Alberigo's table.

Receiving a date for a fig is an ironic expression of repayment with interest. Alberigo's infernal reward exceeds the evil fruit he served.

> 124 Cotal vantaggio ha questa Tolomea,  
> (Such a privilege has this Tolomea,)
>
> 125 che spesse volte l'anima ci cade  
> (that often the soul falls here)
>
> 126 innanzi ch'Atropòs mossa le dea.  
> (before Atropos gives it movement.)

The supposed privilege is a terrible exception: a traitor's soul may fall into Hell before the body dies. Atropos, the Fate who cuts the thread of life, represents the normal moment of death.

Tolomea is generally associated with the Ptolemy of First Maccabees who murdered Simon and his sons after receiving them at a feast. Its name thus reinforces the betrayal of hospitality.

> 129 sappie che, tosto che l'anima trade  
> (know that, as soon as the soul betrays)
>
> 130 come fec' ïo, il corpo suo l'è tolto  
> (as I did, its body is taken from it)
>
> 131 da un demonio, che poscia il governa  
> (by a demon, which afterward governs it)
>
> 132 mentre che 'l tempo suo tutto sia vòlto.  
> (until its allotted time has entirely run its course.)

This extraordinary arrangement belongs to Dante's poetic vision rather than to an ordinary account of Christian doctrine.

Its allegorical meaning is stark: betrayal can make someone spiritually dead while still biologically alive. The outward routines of existence continue, but the person's capacity for faithful human relationship has been surrendered to evil.

## 10. Branca Doria and the Refusal of Mercy

> 140 ché Branca Doria non morì unquanche,  
> (for Branca Doria has not yet died,)
>
> 141 e mangia e bee e dorme e veste panni».  
> (and eats and drinks and sleeps and wears clothes.”)

Dante objects when Alberigo identifies another soul as the Genoese nobleman Branca Doria. According to the account underlying the episode, Doria murdered his father-in-law, Michele Zanche, at a banquet.

The deliberately ordinary list of activities makes the distinction between bodily and spiritual life unforgettable. Eating, drinking, sleeping, and dressing prove that a body functions; they do not prove that its inhabitant is morally alive.

By placing a contemporary who was still living in Hell, Dante also turns his imagined afterlife into an audacious judgment on the present world.

> 149 aprimi li occhi». E io non gliel' apersi;  
> (open my eyes.” And I did not open them for him;)
>
> 150 e cortesia fu lui esser villano.  
> (and it was courtesy to be rude to him.)

Alberigo has asked Dante to remove the ice from his eyes. Dante appeared to promise assistance, but now refuses.

Earlier, Dante made his promise conditional on going to the bottom of the ice if he failed—a destination toward which he was already traveling. The offer therefore contains an evasive verbal trap.

The paradox of courteous cruelty expresses the infernal logic that kindness to obstinate evil may be morally misplaced. Yet the scene remains unsettling: Dante responds to a betrayer with something resembling a deceptive promise. The poem does not make the relationship between justice, pity, and hardness emotionally simple.

> 151 Ahi Genovesi, uomini diversi  
> (Ah, Genoese, men estranged)
>
> 152 d'ogne costume e pien d'ogne magagna,  
> (from every civilized custom and full of every vice,)
>
> 153 perché non siete voi del mondo spersi?  
> (why are you not scattered from the world?)

The canto ends with another civic denunciation, this time against Genoa. As with Pisa, the crime of particular men becomes an accusation against a wider political culture.

The reference to civilized custom is especially pointed after an episode about hospitality. Betrayal is not simply a private moral failure: it attacks the shared practices that allow people to eat, meet, and live together without fear.

## Epilogue — When Trust Becomes Hunger and Ice

Canto 33 presents betrayal as the destruction of life-sustaining bonds. Political trust leads to imprisonment; fatherhood cannot protect the children; a banquet becomes a murder scene; tears cease to offer relief.

Its central images—hunger, devouring, and ice—show different forms of the same spiritual collapse. Other people become objects to exploit, consume, punish, or exclude.

Yet the canto also tests the reader's judgment. Ugolino is both a condemned traitor and a suffering father; Dante's compassion for the children coexists with violent civic anger and severity toward Alberigo. At the bottom of Hell, recognizing evil does not make the demands of pity and justice easy to reconcile.
