# Fraud in the Boiling Pitch: Allegory and Background in Dante's *Inferno*, Canto 22

Canto 22 takes place in the fifth ditch of Malebolge, the eighth circle of Hell, where fraud is punished. Here the **barrators**—people who turned public responsibilities into opportunities for private profit—lie submerged in boiling pitch. The Malebranche, a troop of armed devils, hook and torment anyone who emerges.

Dante and Virgil are travelling under the devils' escort. What follows resembles a grotesque comedy: a prisoner outwits his captors, the guards quarrel, and discipline collapses. Yet the comedy exposes something serious. A world governed by corruption cannot sustain trust, even among those supposedly enforcing its rules.

## 1. A Military Procession with a Grotesque Signal

> 10 né già con sì diversa cennamella  
> (Nor ever with so strange a shawm)
>
> 11 cavalier vidi muover né pedoni,  
> (did I see horsemen move, or foot soldiers,)
>
> 12 né nave a segno di terra o di stella.  
> (or a ship by a signal from land or star.)

The canto opens with Dante recalling cavalry movements, raids, tournaments, and the various signals used to organize collective action. His reference to Aretine territory evokes the warfare of his youth, including the campaign associated with the Florentine victory at Campaldino in 1289.

But this impressive military catalogue ends in deliberate indignity. The strange “shawm”—a wind instrument—is Barbariccia's fart, which concluded the preceding canto and now serves as the devils' marching signal.

The contrast establishes the canto's method: recognizable institutions are reproduced in degraded forms. The devils have a commander, weapons, and something like military organization, but their ceremonial signal reduces authority to bodily noise. Their later disorder will reveal how little substance lies beneath this appearance of discipline.

## 2. Travelling with Evil without Belonging to It

> 13 Noi andavam con li diece demoni.  
> (We were going with the ten demons.)
>
> 14 Ahi fiera compagnia! ma ne la chiesa  
> (Ah, savage company! But in church)
>
> 15 coi santi, e in taverna coi ghiottoni.  
> (with saints, and in the tavern with gluttons.)

Dante's exclamation gives way to a proverbial observation: one encounters saints in church and gluttons in a tavern. In Hell, the available company is infernal.

The saying is comic and practical, not an endorsement of moral conformity. Dante must pass through this environment to understand it; he does not thereby share its guilt. The distinction matters throughout the *Inferno*: witnessing evil is necessary to the pilgrim's education, but understanding it requires resisting its standards.

There is also a warning beneath the joke. Virgil and Dante are temporarily dependent on creatures whose nature makes them unreliable. Accommodation may permit a journey to continue, but it does not make dangerous companions trustworthy.

## 3. Dolphins and Frogs: The Human Form Reduced to Survival

> 22 talor così, ad alleggiar la pena,  
> (so at times, to ease the pain,)
>
> 23 mostrav' alcun de' peccatori 'l dosso  
> (one of the sinners would show his back)
>
> 24 e nascondea in men che non balena.  
> (and hide it in less time than lightning flashes.)

These lines complete a comparison with dolphins arching their backs above the sea. According to the maritime belief Dante invokes, dolphins could warn sailors of approaching danger. The sinners likewise break the surface, but only to obtain a moment's relief.

The resemblance contains an inversion. The dolphins' movements help others; the barrators' movements serve their own immediate survival. People who misused responsibilities toward a community now concentrate entirely on concealing and protecting themselves.

The pitch makes their predicament especially apt. Dark, adhesive, and concealing, it suggests the secrecy and entanglement of corrupt dealings. This is a **contrapasso**: punishment that expresses the character of the sin, rather than merely inflicting an unrelated pain.

> 28 sì stavan d'ogne parte i peccatori;  
> (so on every side stood the sinners;)
>
> 29 ma come s'appressava Barbariccia,  
> (but as Barbariccia drew near,)
>
> 30 così si ritraén sotto i bollori.  
> (so they withdrew beneath the bubbling surface.)

The intervening comparison presents frogs keeping only their snouts above the water. Like them, the sinners remain poised between concealment and exposure.

The successive animal images reduce the apparent dignity of these former political operators. Their intelligence has not disappeared, but it now serves the elementary calculations of hunted creatures: when to rise, when to hide, and how long to risk remaining visible.

Their relationship with the devils is therefore not one of moral correction. It is predation.

## 4. The Captured Sinner: A Person Turned into Prey

> 34 e Graffiacan, che li era più di contra,  
> (and Graffiacan, who was most directly opposite him,)
>
> 35 li arruncigliò le 'mpegolate chiome  
> (hooked him by his pitch-coated hair)
>
> 36 e trassel sù, che mi parve una lontra.  
> (and hauled him up, so that he seemed to me an otter.)

One sinner fails to dive quickly enough. Graffiacan catches him by the hair and pulls him from the pitch.

The otter comparison continues the canto's animal imagery, but now the emphasis falls on the captured body: wet, dark, and dragged into view. The sinner first appears not as a named individual but as something caught.

The devils' names reinforce this world of bodily aggression. Graffiacan suggests scratching or clawing; other names evoke dogs, dragons, tangled beards, and disorderly movement. These are not solemn ministers of justice. They take visible pleasure in their victim's helplessness.

Dante's request to discover who the prisoner is interrupts that spectacle. Speech briefly restores a human history to the creature the devils have hooked.

## 5. The Navarrese: Service Converted into Corruption

> 48 «I' fui del regno di Navarra nato.  
> (“I was born of the kingdom of Navarre.)
>
> 49 Mia madre a servo d'un segnor mi puose,  
> (My mother placed me as a servant to a lord,)
>
> 50 che m'avea generato d'un ribaldo,  
> (who had borne me by a scoundrel,)
>
> 51 distruggitor di sé e di sue cose.  
> (a destroyer of himself and of his possessions.)

The prisoner is traditionally called **Ciampolo**, although Dante does not name him in the canto and his historical identity remains uncertain.

The mother mentioned in line 49 is also the subject of line 50: she bore him to a dissolute father and subsequently placed him in service. His account begins with family instability and dependence rather than inherited power.

This background explains the circumstances of his entry into service without excusing his later choices. Dante gives corruption a personal history, but does not make deprivation an automatic cause of guilt.

> 52 Poi fui famiglia del buon re Tebaldo;  
> (Then I was a member of good King Theobald's household;)
>
> 53 quivi mi misi a far baratteria,  
> (there I took to practising barratry,)
>
> 54 di ch'io rendo ragione in questo caldo».  
> (for which I render account in this heat.”)

The king is generally identified as Theobald II of Navarre, who ruled from 1253 to 1270. “Famiglia” means the royal household or service, not biological kinship.

The contrast between the “good” king and his corrupt servant is significant. Barratry betrays entrusted responsibility: access to authority becomes a marketable personal advantage. The language of rendering an account turns the pitch into a terrible audit of that betrayal.

The subject also had a sharp personal relevance for Dante. Florentine proceedings against him in 1302 included accusations of barratry and contributed to his exile. Those politically charged accusations should not be treated as established proof of guilt. Nevertheless, they made the corruption of public office an especially consequential subject for his poem.

## 6. Infernal Government: Protection as Possession

> 58 Tra male gatte era venuto 'l sorco;  
> (Among wicked cats the mouse had come;)
>
> 59 ma Barbariccia il chiuse con le braccia  
> (but Barbariccia enclosed him with his arms)
>
> 60 e disse: «State in là, mentr' io lo 'nforco».  
> (and said: “Stand back, while I hold him fast.”)

The mouse-and-cats proverb makes the imbalance unmistakable. The prisoner has fallen among creatures eager to play with him before destroying him.

Barbariccia restrains the other devils so that Virgil can question the captive, but this is not mercy. His apparent protection is an assertion of control over the victim and over his subordinates.

The scene resembles an institution whose officers observe procedure only when it suits their power. Authority can briefly regulate violence, but it shares the appetites it attempts to restrain.

> 70 E Libicocco «Troppo avem sofferto»,  
> (And Libicocco: “We have endured too much,”)
>
> 71 disse; e preseli 'l braccio col runciglio,  
> (he said; and caught his arm with the hook,)
>
> 72 sì che, stracciando, ne portò un lacerto.  
> (so that, tearing, he carried off a piece of flesh.)

Libicocco's complaint grotesquely reverses the situation: the torturer speaks as though waiting to inflict pain were itself an intolerable suffering.

His attack also shows the weakness of Barbariccia's command. Private appetite repeatedly overrides collective discipline. The devils punish corruption, but their behaviour mirrors a central feature of corrupt government: office becomes an opportunity to satisfy personal desires.

This does not mean that Dante presents divine judgment itself as corrupt. Rather, the agents encountered within Hell reveal their own viciousness even while operating inside its larger punitive order.

## 7. Sardinian Politics and the Scale of Graft

> 85 Danar si tolse e lasciolli di piano,  
> (He took money and let them go outright,)
>
> 86 sì com' e' dice; e ne li altri offici anche  
> (as he says; and in his other offices too)
>
> 87 barattier fu non picciol, ma sovrano.  
> (he was no petty barrator, but a sovereign one.)

The Navarrese is describing **Friar Gomita**, associated with the government of Gallura in Sardinia. Medieval Sardinia was divided into territories known as *giudicati*, each ruled by a *giudice*.

According to the account reflected here, Gomita held authority under Nino Visconti and accepted money to release his lord's enemies. An official entrusted with custody converted that trust into a private transaction.

“Sovereign” is mock praise. Gomita excelled not in governing justly but in corruption. The elevated word makes him a master of the very activity that destroys legitimate authority.

> 88 Usa con esso donno Michel Zanche  
> (With him keeps company Don Michel Zanche)
>
> 89 di Logodoro; e a dir di Sardigna  
> (of Logudoro; and in speaking of Sardinia)
>
> 90 le lingue lor non si sentono stanche.  
> (their tongues do not feel weary.)

Michel Zanche is another figure associated with Sardinian political intrigue, although the historical details surrounding him are uncertain.

The two men endlessly discuss Sardinia. Their punishment has not erased their regional attachments or political interests. Such persistence is characteristic of Dante's damned: they remain recognizably themselves, often absorbed in the same narrow concerns that shaped their earthly lives.

The passage also widens the canto's political geography. Navarre, Sardinia, and the Italian regions mentioned later place corrupt service within an international world of courts and offices.

## 8. A Fraudulent Offer That Exploits Greed

> 103 per un ch'io son, ne farò venir sette  
> (for the one that I am, I will make seven come)
>
> 104 quand' io suffolerò, com' è nostro uso  
> (when I whistle, as is our custom)
>
> 105 di fare allor che fori alcun si mette».  
> (to do whenever one of us comes out.”)

The prisoner offers to summon more sinners if the devils withdraw far enough not to frighten them. He claims that a whistle is the customary signal for emerging.

His proposal turns captivity into negotiation. One prisoner promises seven: the devils are invited to imagine a profitable increase in victims. Their appetite for cruelty becomes the weakness through which he can manipulate them.

The scheme also repeats the moral pattern of barratry. Apparently useful service conceals a private purpose. The captive offers cooperation while preparing his own escape.

> 107 crollando 'l capo, e disse: «Odi malizia  
> (shaking his head, and said: “Hear the trickery)
>
> 108 ch'elli ha pensata per gittarsi giuso!».  
> (he has devised to throw himself down!”)

Cagnazzo correctly recognizes the plan. The devils therefore fail not because deception is impossible to detect, but because sound suspicion cannot overcome their competing desires.

Alichino's confidence in his own flying speed proves decisive. He believes that even an attempted escape will let him demonstrate superiority. Vanity makes him willing to accept a risk that another devil has already understood.

Fraud succeeds here by using the victim's character against him.

## 9. The Escape: Fear Outruns Boastful Power

> 121 Lo Navarrese ben suo tempo colse;  
> (The Navarrese chose his moment well;)
>
> 122 fermò le piante a terra, e in un punto  
> (he planted his feet on the ground, and in an instant)
>
> 123 saltò e dal proposto lor si sciolse.  
> (jumped and freed himself from their commander.)

The narrator temporarily gives the action the rhythm of an adventure story. The prisoner calculates the moment, braces himself, and springs free.

Readers may feel satisfaction at his success, especially after the devils' cruelty. But escape from their hooks is not escape from damnation. He returns to the boiling pitch.

His cunning therefore remains morally limited. It wins a tactical victory without changing the condition of his soul.

> 130 non altrimenti l'anitra di botto,  
> (just so the duck suddenly,)
>
> 131 quando 'l falcon s'appressa, giù s'attuffa,  
> (when the falcon approaches, dives down,)
>
> 132 ed ei ritorna sù crucciato e rotto.  
> (and it returns upward angry and defeated.)

The duck escapes the falcon by entering an element the pursuer cannot safely follow. Likewise, the sinner defeats Alichino by diving into the pitch.

This is the canto's most pointed reversal: the substance of punishment becomes temporary shelter from an additional torment. Hell offers no genuine refuge, only the possibility of avoiding something worse.

The comparison also punctures the devil's boast. Wings do not guarantee mastery; fear and precise timing prove more effective than confident aggression.

## 10. The Punishers Caught in Their Own Trap

> 139 Ma l'altro fu bene sparvier grifagno  
> (But the other was indeed a fierce sparrowhawk)
>
> 140 ad artigliar ben lui, e amendue  
> (in clawing him soundly, and both)
>
> 141 cadder nel mezzo del bogliente stagno.  
> (fell into the middle of the boiling pool.)

Calcabrina attacks Alichino after the prisoner escapes, and Alichino fights back. The predator imagery now applies to the devils' treatment of one another.

Their common task cannot hold them together. Humiliation and anger transform cooperation into mutual assault. The scene makes visible a broader consequence of fraud: those who recognize no binding obligation beyond advantage cannot sustain a durable community.

Both devils fall into the pitch they were appointed to patrol.

> 142 Lo caldo sghermitor sùbito fue;  
> (The heat immediately made them release their grip;)
>
> 143 ma però di levarsi era neente,  
> (but even so there was no getting up,)
>
> 144 sì avieno inviscate l'ali sue.  
> (so thoroughly had their wings become mired.)

The heat separates the combatants, accomplishing what discipline could not. Yet their wings are clogged, and other devils must attempt a rescue.

Their predicament mirrors that of the sinners without making their punishments identical. Creatures who wield hooks against the trapped must now depend on hooks themselves. Their apparent superiority proves precarious.

> 151 E noi lasciammo lor così 'mpacciati.  
> (And we left them thus entangled.)

The closing line is deliberately understated. Dante and Virgil leave while the supposedly formidable escort is occupied with its own mess.

“Entangled” describes both a physical condition and the result of the devils' behaviour. Their pride, cruelty, and rivalry have immobilized them.

## Epilogue — Corruption Entangles Its Own Agents

Canto 22 presents fraud as a social order that continually defeats itself. Officials sell their responsibilities, prisoners deceive their guards, and guards abandon discipline for cruelty and rivalry. The pitch gives this world a physical form: opaque, adhesive, and difficult to escape.

The comedy does not cancel the horror. Instead, it strips corrupt power of dignity. The Navarrese's escape shows that intelligence without goodness can win a momentary contest; the devils' collapse shows that force without trustworthy cooperation cannot maintain order. Neither cunning nor domination offers the freedom toward which Dante's journey is directed.
