# Strength Without Wisdom: Allegory and Background in Inferno XXXI

Canto XXXI brings Dante and Virgil to the boundary between the eighth circle, where fraud is punished, and the ninth, where traitors lie imprisoned in ice. Around the central pit stand the giants: beings whose enormous bodies once seemed capable of challenging heaven, but whose strength now serves only to display their defeat.

The canto joins biblical history, classical mythology, medieval legend, and recognizable Italian architecture. Its governing question is not simply what makes a creature powerful, but what happens when power becomes separated from wisdom, moral restraint, and meaningful speech.

## 1. The Tongue That Wounds and Heals

> 1 Una medesma lingua pria mi morse,  
> (One and the same tongue first bit me,)
>
> 2 sì che mi tinse l'una e l'altra guancia,  
> (so that it colored both my cheeks,)
>
> 3 e poi la medicina mi riporse;  
> (and then offered me the medicine;)

The opening looks back to the previous canto. Virgil rebuked Dante for becoming absorbed in the degrading quarrel between Master Adam and Sinon. Dante blushed with shame; Virgil then reassured him when he recognized the sincerity of his repentance.

The tongue is therefore both an instrument of correction and a source of healing. Virgil's severity is not destructive anger: it restores his pupil's moral judgment. This establishes a standard of responsible speech against which the giants' shouting and incomprehension will later be measured.

> 4 così od' io che solea far la lancia  
> (so I hear that the spear was accustomed to act,)
>
> 5 d'Achille e del suo padre esser cagione  
> (of Achilles and his father, being the cause)
>
> 6 prima di trista e poi di buona mancia.  
> (first of a grievous and then of a good gift.)

The comparison invokes the spear of Achilles, inherited from his father Peleus. In the myth of Telephus, the weapon that caused a wound also supplied its cure, through scrapings from its point.

The image makes correction medicinal. Something painful can become beneficial when directed toward recovery. It also introduces a contrast central to the canto: a weapon can wound or heal, just as strength and language can serve either rightful order or destructive pride.

## 2. The Horn in the Darkness

> 12 ma io senti' sonare un alto corno,  
> (but I heard a loud horn sound,)
>
> 13 tanto ch'avrebbe ogne tuon fatto fioco,  
> (so loudly that it would have made every thunderclap faint,)

Leaving the last ditch of Malebolge, the travelers enter a dim region where sight reaches only a short distance. Hearing takes over: the overwhelming horn directs Dante's attention before he can identify its source.

The horn belongs to Nimrod, though Dante does not yet know this. Its tremendous volume anticipates the giants' disproportionate physical power. Yet volume is not meaning. The sound commands attention without communicating intelligible thought.

> 16 Dopo la dolorosa rotta, quando  
> (After the grievous rout, when)
>
> 17 Carlo Magno perdé la santa gesta,  
> (Charlemagne lost his holy company,)
>
> 18 non sonò sì terribilmente Orlando.  
> (Roland did not sound so terribly.)

Dante compares the blast with Roland's horn at Roncevaux. In medieval epic, Roland sounds his horn to summon Charlemagne as the rear guard faces destruction. The episode belongs to the Christian heroic tradition of the *Song of Roland*, rather than to a strictly historical account of the battle.

The comparison establishes an immense scale: even the most famous horn blast in medieval literature was less terrible. It also places heroic history beside infernal monstrosity. Roland's signal belongs to a shared cause; Nimrod's sound will prove to be the frustrated expression of an isolated being.

## 3. Towers That Become Giants

> 31 sappi che non son torri, ma giganti,  
> (know that they are not towers, but giants,)
>
> 32 e son nel pozzo intorno da la ripa  
> (and they are in the pit around its rim)
>
> 33 da l'umbilico in giuso tutti quanti».  
> (from the navel downward, every one of them.”)

Dante initially mistakes the giants for the towers of a city. Virgil explains that their lower bodies are inside the pit, while their upper bodies rise above its edge.

This is a lesson in the limits of perception. Darkness and distance allow Dante's imagination to turn incomplete evidence into a false conclusion. Reason corrects the error, but correction brings greater fear: the truth is more threatening than the mistaken appearance.

The episode therefore complicates any simple association between knowledge and comfort. Understanding is necessary, but what it reveals may be terrifying.

> 40 però che, come su la cerchia tonda  
> (for, just as upon its circular enclosure)
>
> 41 Montereggion di torri si corona,  
> (Monteriggioni crowns itself with towers,)
>
> 42 così la proda che 'l pozzo circonda  
> (so the bank that surrounds the pit)
>
> 43 torreggiavan di mezza la persona  
> (they towered above with half their bodies,)
>
> 44 li orribili giganti, cui minaccia  
> (the horrible giants, whom threatens)
>
> 45 Giove del cielo ancora quando tuona.  
> (Jupiter from heaven still whenever he thunders.)

Monteriggioni was a fortified town near Siena, famous for the towers rising around its enclosing walls. Dante uses a recognizable Tuscan landmark to give visible form to an otherwise unimaginable scene.

The giants become a grotesque fortification around the deepest region of Hell. Their bodies resemble military architecture, but they no longer defend an independent power: they stand within the order they cannot overthrow.

Jupiter's thunder recalls the classical war between the giants and the Olympian gods. Within Dante's Christian poem, that defeated rebellion becomes an analogue of resistance to divine authority. The giants' enduring terror exposes the impotence beneath their threatening appearance.

## 4. When Intelligence Serves Malice

> 55 ché dove l'argomento de la mente  
> (for where the instrument of the mind)
>
> 56 s'aggiugne al mal volere e a la possa,  
> (is joined to evil will and to power,)
>
> 57 nessun riparo vi può far la gente.  
> (human beings can make no defense against it.)

These lines explain Dante's preceding praise of Nature for ceasing to produce giants. He does not condemn enormous size by itself: elephants and whales remain legitimate parts of creation. The greater danger is the union of intelligence, malicious intention, and overwhelming strength.

The giants are therefore more than embodiments of brute force. They represent the terrifying potential of human-like intelligence when it becomes an instrument of domination.

Their position near the entrance to the circle of treachery is significant. Dante's descent increasingly reveals wrongdoing that corrupts distinctively human capacities. Reason should guide the will toward good; when enlisted by malice, it makes evil more effective.

The passage also prevents us from treating all the giants as merely stupid. Their present degradation does not erase the danger represented by the capacities they once possessed.

## 5. Nimrod and the Destruction of Shared Language

> 67 «Raphèl maì amècche zabì almi»,  
> (“Raphèl maì amècche zabì almi”,)

Nimrod's cry cannot be securely translated. Proposals have been made to identify elements from various languages, but within the poem its essential function is to remain unintelligible.

Dante follows a medieval tradition associating Nimrod with the building of the Tower of Babel. Genesis identifies Nimrod as a mighty ruler and places Babel within his kingdom; later interpretation closely connected him with the tower and its presumptuous challenge to heaven.

The earlier confusion between towers and giants now acquires another resonance. Nimrod is both a tower-like body and a figure associated with the most famous tower of human pride.

> 79 Lasciànlo stare e non parliamo a vòto;  
> (Let us leave him alone and not speak in vain;)
>
> 80 ché così è a lui ciascun linguaggio  
> (for every language is so to him)
>
> 81 come 'l suo ad altrui, ch'a nullo è noto».  
> (as his is to others, which is known to no one.”)

Virgil explains that the linguistic separation works in both directions. Others cannot understand Nimrod, and Nimrod cannot understand them.

This is a punishment by correspondence: the figure associated with the destruction of linguistic unity is denied participation in any community of speech. His huge body and immense voice cannot secure the elementary human good of mutual understanding.

The contrast with the canto's opening is sharp. Virgil's words can wound, explain, console, and guide because they remain meaningful. Nimrod can produce only forceful sound.

## 6. Ephialtes: Rebellion Bound in Chains

> 94 Fïalte ha nome, e fece le gran prove  
> (Ephialtes is his name, and he performed his great feats)
>
> 95 quando i giganti fer paura a' dèi;  
> (when the giants frightened the gods;)
>
> 96 le braccia ch'el menò, già mai non move».  
> (the arms that he wielded, he never moves again.”)

Ephialtes embodies rebellion made permanently powerless. The arms once raised against the gods are now restrained by a chain that winds repeatedly around his exposed body.

The punishment answers the offense with exact physical irony. What formerly enacted his pride now displays his captivity. His size remains, but the freedom to use it has disappeared.

Dante also asks to see Briareus, the monstrous figure famous in classical tradition for his extraordinary bodily form. Virgil tells him that Briareus is farther away and similarly bound. The encounter Dante actually needs is not with the most spectacular monster, but with Antaeus, who can carry the travelers downward.

> 109 Allor temett' io più che mai la morte,  
> (Then I feared death more than ever,)
>
> 110 e non v'era mestier più che la dotta,  
> (and nothing more than the fright was needed,)
>
> 111 s'io non avessi viste le ritorte.  
> (had I not seen the bonds.)

Ephialtes shakes himself with a violence Dante compares to an earthquake. Dante suggests that fear alone might have killed him had the visible chains not reassured him.

The moment distinguishes threatening appearance from actual power. The giant can still terrify, but he cannot escape the limits imposed upon him. His agitation is not a renewed battle against heaven; it is the futile movement of a defeated rebel.

## 7. Antaeus and the Persuasive Power of Fame

> 119 e che, se fossi stato a l'alta guerra  
> (and who, had you been at the great war)
>
> 120 de' tuoi fratelli, ancor par che si creda  
> (of your brothers, it still seems to be believed)
>
> 121 ch'avrebber vinto i figli de la terra:  
> (that the sons of earth would have won:)
>
> 122 mettine giù, e non ten vegna schifo,  
> (set us down below, and let no disdain for it come over you,)
>
> 123 dove Cocito la freddura serra.  
> (where the cold locks Cocytus.)

Antaeus differs from the bound rebels because he did not participate in their war against the gods. In classical mythology he was a son of Earth who renewed his strength through contact with the ground. Hercules defeated him by lifting him away from that source of power.

Dante's account draws especially on the classical literary tradition represented by Lucan. Virgil begins his appeal by recalling Antaeus's exploits in North Africa, in the region associated with Scipio's victory over Hannibal.

His suggestion that the giants might have won with Antaeus's help is calculated flattery, not a statement of Dante's theology. Virgil appeals to the giant's pride to obtain practical assistance. The strength that once threatened others will now serve the travelers' authorized descent.

> 125 questi può dar di quel che qui si brama;  
> (this man can give some of what is desired here;)
>
> 126 però ti china e non torcer lo grifo.  
> (therefore bend down and do not turn aside your snout.)
>
> 127 Ancor ti può nel mondo render fama,  
> (He can yet restore your fame in the world,)
>
> 128 ch'el vive, e lunga vita ancor aspetta  
> (for he lives, and still expects a long life)
>
> 129 se 'nnanzi tempo grazia a sé nol chiama».  
> (if grace does not call him to itself before his time.”)

The desired reward is earthly fame. Dante, as a living traveler and future poet, can renew Antaeus's reputation among the living.

This appeal exposes a persistent infernal desire: the dead still care about how the world remembers them. Fame cannot save them, but it remains something they covet.

Virgil's rhetoric mixes praise, bargaining, and brusque command. Unlike Nimrod, Antaeus can understand and respond. Yet the exchange is not spiritual conversion: his vanity is redirected toward a useful action.

## 8. The Human Embrace and the Falling Tower

> 133 Virgilio, quando prender si sentio,  
> (Virgil, when he felt himself being taken,)
>
> 134 disse a me: «Fatti qua, sì ch'io ti prenda»;  
> (said to me: “Come here, so that I may take you”;)
>
> 135 poi fece sì ch'un fascio era elli e io.  
> (then made it so that he and I were one bundle.)

Antaeus takes Virgil in the hands that once grappled with Hercules. Virgil then gathers Dante into his own embrace.

Against the giants' overwhelming size, the poem places a small but effective human bond. Dante and Virgil become one bundle: vulnerable, but held together through trust and care.

Virgil's guidance is not merely intellectual. He explains mistaken perceptions, negotiates with dangerous beings, and physically supports his pupil. Reason appears here as an attentive relationship rather than an impersonal abstraction.

> 136 Qual pare a riguardar la Carisenda  
> (As the Garisenda appears when one looks at it)
>
> 137 sotto 'l chinato, quando un nuvol vada  
> (from beneath its leaning side, when a cloud passes)
>
> 138 sovr' essa sì, ched ella incontro penda:  
> (above it so that it seems to lean against it:)
>
> 139 tal parve Antëo a me che stava a bada  
> (so Antaeus appeared to me as I watched intently)
>
> 140 di vederlo chinare, e fu tal ora  
> (to see him bend, and there was such a moment)
>
> 141 ch'i' avrei voluto ir per altra strada.  
> (that I would have wished to go by another road.)

The Garisenda is one of Bologna's celebrated leaning towers. Seen from beneath its overhang, with clouds moving above it, the stationary tower can appear to be falling.

Dante uses this optical illusion to recreate the sensation of Antaeus bending over him. Once again, a giant becomes a tower; once again, perception generates fear.

The architectural comparisons frame the encounter. At first Dante mistakes bodies for buildings. Now he knowingly uses a building to explain the terrifying movement of a body. Experience has corrected his knowledge without abolishing his vulnerability.

## 9. The Gentle Descent into the Worst Evil

> 142 Ma lievemente al fondo che divora  
> (But gently, to the bottom that devours)
>
> 143 Lucifero con Giuda, ci sposò;  
> (Lucifer together with Judas, he set us down;)
>
> 144 né, sì chinato, lì fece dimora,  
> (nor, thus bent, did he remain there,)
>
> 145 e come albero in nave si levò.  
> (and like a mast in a ship he rose.)

The feared catastrophe does not occur. Antaeus sets the travelers down gently. His immense strength becomes, briefly, an instrument of careful service.

Yet the destination is the place containing Lucifer and Judas. The movement downward brings Dante toward the fullest revelation of treachery: rebellion against the ultimate source of good and betrayal of the closest bonds of trust.

The final comparison turns Antaeus from a tower into a ship's mast. He becomes an almost mechanical means of passage, useful within a journey whose purpose he does not control.

The contrast between the gentle landing and the terrible destination is deliberate. The deepest evil will not be defined by the loudest sound or the most violent movement. Beyond these roaring giants lies the frozen immobility of betrayal.

## Epilogue — Power Reduced, Guidance Preserved

Canto XXXI strips enormous power of its claim to greatness. The giants possess imposing bodies, formidable histories, and memories of rebellion, but they are isolated, chained, or susceptible to flattery. Their strength cannot overcome the order they opposed.

Against them stand quieter powers: intelligible speech, correction, judgment, persuasion, and the trust between guide and traveler. The descent prepares Dante to see that evil reaches its deepest form not in sheer physical violence, but in the corruption of intelligence and the betrayal of relationship.
