# Inferno XVIII: The Architecture of Deception

Canto XVIII brings Dante and Virgil into **Malebolge**, the eighth circle of Hell, where fraud is punished. They have arrived on the back of Geryon, the monster whose trustworthy-looking face conceals a dangerous body: an embodiment of deception.

The canto moves through the first two of Malebolge's ten ditches. In the first, pimps and seducers are driven onward by demons with whips; in the second, flatterers are submerged in excrement. The connection is the exploitation of other people through calculated persuasion. Sexual manipulation and flattering speech become visible as forms of moral degradation.

## 1. Malebolge: A Landscape Built for Fraud

> 1 Luogo è in inferno detto Malebolge,  
> (There is a place in Hell called Malebolge,)
>
> 2 tutto di pietra di color ferrigno,  
> (entirely of stone of an iron color,)
>
> 3 come la cerchia che dintorno il volge.  
> (like the encircling wall that surrounds it.)

The name **Malebolge** means approximately “evil pouches” or “evil ditches.” A *bolgia* is a bag or pocket; Dante turns the word into a name for a compartment of punishment. The landscape appears hard, enclosed, and stripped of life.

Its iron-colored stone contributes to an atmosphere of imprisonment. Fraud may disguise itself through pleasing appearances, but its final dwelling offers no softness or ornament.

> 7 Quel cinghio che rimane adunque è tondo  
> (That belt which therefore remains is circular)
>
> 8 tra 'l pozzo e 'l piè de l'alta ripa dura,  
> (between the well and the foot of the high, hard cliff,)
>
> 9 e ha distinto in dieci valli il fondo.  
> (and has its floor divided into ten valleys.)

Malebolge is an annular space surrounding a central well. Ten concentric ditches divide its floor, while rocky bridges allow Dante and Virgil to cross them.

This exact geography expresses the discriminating character of divine judgment: fraud is not treated as one undifferentiated offense. Its varieties receive distinct places and punishments. The well at the center eventually leads toward the ninth circle, where betrayal—the violation of a special bond of trust—is punished.

The canto's opening therefore functions as both a map and a moral classification.

## 2. The Jubilee Bridge: Sacred Movement Reflected in Hell

> 28 come i Roman per l'essercito molto,  
> (as the Romans, because of the great multitude,)
>
> 29 l'anno del giubileo, su per lo ponte  
> (in the year of the Jubilee, upon the bridge,)
>
> 30 hanno a passar la gente modo colto,  
> (have adopted a way of getting the people across,)
>
> 31 che da l'un lato tutti hanno la fronte  
> (so that on one side all have their faces)
>
> 32 verso 'l castello e vanno a Santo Pietro,  
> (toward the castle and go to Saint Peter's,)
>
> 33 da l'altra sponda vanno verso 'l monte.  
> (on the other side they go toward the hill.)

The first ditch contains two streams of naked sinners moving in opposite directions: pimps and procurers in one stream, seducers in the other. Dante explains their arrangement through a contemporary Roman scene.

Pope Boniface VIII proclaimed the first Jubilee in 1300, the year in which Dante sets his journey. Large numbers of pilgrims visited Rome, and traffic across the bridge beside Castel Sant'Angelo required organization. Dante compares the infernal procession to this managed movement toward and away from Saint Peter's.

The comparison does not simply equate pilgrims with sinners. Rather, it makes an unfamiliar infernal spectacle intelligible through a familiar human arrangement. It also creates a disturbing contrast: pilgrims travel in hope of forgiveness, while these sinners move endlessly without approaching release.

## 3. The Whips: Manipulators Become the Driven

> 34 Di qua, di là, su per lo sasso tetro  
> (On this side, on that side, upon the dark rock)
>
> 35 vidi demon cornuti con gran ferze,  
> (I saw horned demons with great whips,)
>
> 36 che li battien crudelmente di retro.  
> (who beat them cruelly from behind.)

The horned, whip-bearing demons belong to a recognizable medieval imagery of Hell. Their work turns the first ditch into a brutal mechanism: the sinners must keep moving because punishment presses upon them from behind.

The **contrapasso**, the correspondence between wrongdoing and punishment, reverses the sinners' former position. Those who manipulated other people's desires and bodies are now themselves handled as bodies to be driven.

Pimps and seducers had exercised different kinds of control, but the punishment exposes their shared offense. They treated another person as an instrument of gratification or advantage; now their own agency is reduced to a desperate response to force.

## 4. Venedico Caccianemico: A Public Name and a Private Betrayal

> 49 se le fazion che porti non son false,  
> (if the features you bear are not misleading,)
>
> 50 Venedico se' tu Caccianemico.  
> (you are Venedico Caccianemico.)
>
> 51 Ma che ti mena a sì pungenti salse?».  
> (But what brings you to such stinging sauces?”.)

One sinner tries to hide by lowering his face, but Dante recognizes him. Venedico Caccianemico was a prominent Bolognese political figure. His appearance here shows how little public standing protects anyone from the exposure of Hell.

The word *salse*, literally “sauces,” makes the torment sound like a bitter seasoning. It is also commonly understood as an allusion to a notorious place near Bologna associated with the disposal of bodies. The question combines recognition, local knowledge, and savage irony.

There is a historical complication: Venedico is now generally dated as having died in 1302, after the poem's fictional journey in 1300. Dante may have believed him already dead.

> 55 I' fui colui che la Ghisolabella  
> (I was the one who Ghisolabella)
>
> 56 condussi a far la voglia del marchese,  
> (led to fulfill the marquis's desire,)
>
> 57 come che suoni la sconcia novella.  
> (however the disgraceful story may be told.)

Venedico confesses that he procured his sister Ghisolabella for a marquis, usually identified as Obizzo II d'Este, though the identification has been disputed.

The offense joins sexual exploitation to family betrayal and political ambition. A sister becomes a means of serving a powerful man. Venedico's reference to competing versions of the scandal cannot alter the essential fact he admits: he facilitated her submission to another person's desire.

Dante gives the wrongdoing a specific name and social setting. Fraud is not merely an abstract defect; it operates through families, political connections, and unequal power.

## 5. Bologna: When Avarice Becomes a Civic Habit

> 58 E non pur io qui piango bolognese;  
> (And I am not the only Bolognese who weeps here;)
>
> 59 anzi n'è questo loco tanto pieno,  
> (indeed, this place is so full of them,)
>
> 60 che tante lingue non son ora apprese  
> (that so many tongues are not now trained)
>
> 61 a dicer ‘sipa’ tra Sàvena e Reno;  
> (to say ‘sipa’ between the Savena and the Reno;)
>
> 62 e se di ciò vuoi fede o testimonio,  
> (and if you want assurance or testimony of this,)
>
> 63 rècati a mente il nostro avaro seno».  
> (call to mind our avaricious hearts”.)

Venedico expands his confession into an indictment of Bologna. He claims that more Bolognese inhabit this ditch than remain alive in the territory between the Savena and Reno rivers.

The dialect word *sipa*, meaning “yes,” identifies the city through its inhabitants' speech. Such local detail makes Hell feel continuous with the world Dante knows: civic identities and recognizable accents survive death.

Yet this is also a sinner's attempt to distribute his shame. Venedico admits his guilt while insisting that his fellow citizens share it. His testimony combines confession with deflection.

The explanatory word is *avaro*: avaricious. Dante links the exploitation of sexuality to the appetite for gain. The individual scandal reveals a wider culture in which people can become objects of exchange.

## 6. Jason: Heroic Grandeur Does Not Cancel Guilt

> 83 mi disse: «Guarda quel grande che vene,  
> (he said to me: “Look at that great one who comes,)
>
> 84 e per dolor non par lagrime spanda:  
> (and despite his pain does not seem to shed tears:)
>
> 85 quanto aspetto reale ancor ritene!  
> (how much royal bearing he still retains!)
>
> 86 Quelli è Iasón, che per cuore e per senno  
> (That is Jason, who through courage and intelligence)
>
> 87 li Colchi del monton privati féne.  
> (deprived the Colchians of the ram.)

Virgil identifies Jason, leader of the Argonauts, who obtained the Golden Fleece from Colchis. The “ram” refers to the creature whose golden fleece was the object of the expedition.

Jason's appearance contrasts with Venedico's furtive embarrassment. He retains a royal bearing and seems to endure punishment without tears. Dante does not erase his courage, intelligence, or grandeur.

But admirable qualities do not amount to moral innocence. The same intelligence that enables great achievements can serve deception. Jason's presence challenges the assumption that heroic reputation should outweigh the suffering inflicted on others.

Hell judges the use made of exceptional gifts, not merely their possession.

## 7. Hypsipyle and Medea: The Human Cost of Polished Speech

> 91 Ivi con segni e con parole ornate  
> (There, with gestures and polished words)
>
> 92 Isifile ingannò, la giovinetta  
> (he deceived Hypsipyle, the young woman)
>
> 93 che prima avea tutte l'altre ingannate.  
> (who earlier had deceived all the others.)

On Lemnos, Jason seduced Hypsipyle after the island's women had killed their men. She had secretly saved her father, Thoas, while allowing the other women to believe that he too was dead.

The repeated idea of deception invites comparison, not equivalence. Hypsipyle's concealment preserved a life; Jason's deception exploited her trust. Dante distinguishes actions by their purposes rather than treating every concealment as morally identical.

Jason's weapons are “gestures” and “polished words.” Eloquence, ordinarily a mark of civilization, becomes an instrument of injury when separated from truth.

> 94 Lasciolla quivi, gravida, soletta;  
> (He left her there, pregnant, all alone;)
>
> 95 tal colpa a tal martiro lui condanna;  
> (such guilt condemns him to such torment;)
>
> 96 e anche di Medea si fa vendetta.  
> (and vengeance is also exacted for Medea.)

The short sequence “there, pregnant, all alone” strips away heroic glamour. Jason's adventure leaves another person with abandonment and its consequences.

Medea, who helped him obtain the Golden Fleece and was later abandoned by him, supplies a second case. Dante does not retell her whole violent story; he concentrates on Jason's responsibility toward her.

The mention of vengeance means that Jason's treatment of Medea is also being punished. It does not require approval of her own acts. The moral focus falls on the injuries concealed beneath the celebrated hero's career.

## 8. The Second Ditch: Corruption Becomes an Environment

> 106 Le ripe eran grommate d'una muffa,  
> (The banks were crusted with a mold,)
>
> 107 per l'alito di giù che vi s'appasta,  
> (from the breath below that cakes upon them,)
>
> 108 che con li occhi e col naso facea zuffa.  
> (which fought with the eyes and with the nose.)

Before Dante sees the second ditch clearly, he encounters its assault on sight and smell. Foul vapor rises from below and deposits a crust on the banks.

The language makes corruption almost active: it “fights” with the senses. Moral disorder is no longer hidden within an intention or concealed beneath persuasive speech. It has become the atmosphere of the place.

> 112 Quivi venimmo; e quindi giù nel fosso  
> (We came there; and from there, down in the ditch,)
>
> 113 vidi gente attuffata in uno sterco  
> (I saw people plunged into excrement)
>
> 114 che da li uman privadi parea mosso.  
> (that seemed to have come from human privies.)

These are the flatterers. Their punishment turns the pleasant surface of their speech inside out. Words offered as praise are revealed as waste when they are produced dishonestly to win favor.

The contrapasso is an exposure of moral value rather than a literal reproduction of the offense. What seemed agreeable was inwardly foul. The sinners are now submerged in the substance that makes that concealed foulness visible.

Dante's deliberately disgusting imagery prevents the reader from treating flattery as a harmless social ornament.

## 9. Alessio Interminei: The Tongue That Never Had Enough

> 124 Ed elli allor, battendosi la zucca:  
> (And he then, beating his pate:)
>
> 125 «Qua giù m'hanno sommerso le lusinghe  
> (“Down here the flatteries have submerged me)
>
> 126 ond' io non ebbi mai la lingua stucca».  
> (with which I never had my tongue sated”.)

Dante recognizes Alessio Interminei, generally known as Alessio Interminelli, a man from Lucca about whose life relatively little is securely known.

Alessio explains his own punishment with striking directness. He was never tired of flattering, and now the accumulated consequence of that habitual speech engulfs him. His offense is presented not as one isolated lie but as a persistent way of dealing with others.

The colloquial *zucca*, literally “gourd” and here meaning his head or pate, belongs to the passage's deliberately low register. Flattering language had manufactured dignity and approval; Dante's language punctures such pretensions.

The contrast with Jason is revealing. One sinner retains royal bearing, while another beats his filthy head, but neither social appearance nor literary prestige alters the judgment.

## 10. Thais: Exaggerated Praise Reduced to Its Truth

> 133 Taïde è, la puttana che rispuose  
> (She is Thais, the prostitute who replied)
>
> 134 al drudo suo quando disse “Ho io grazie  
> (to her lover when he said “Have I thanks)
>
> 135 grandi apo te?”: “Anzi maravigliose!”.  
> (great with you?”: “Rather, marvelous ones!”.)

Thais embodies flattery through a tiny inflation of language. “Great” thanks are not enough: they must become “marvelous.” The answer exceeds the question in order to satisfy the questioner's vanity.

The literary background is unusually intricate. Thais is a courtesan in Terence's comedy *Eunuchus*. In the original exchange, however, the boastful soldier Thraso asks the parasite Gnatho whether Thais gave him great thanks, and Gnatho supplies the exaggerated answer.

Cicero quotes this exchange in *De amicitia* as an example of flattery. Dante's version is commonly explained through his reading of that discussion, though why he assigns the reply directly to Thais remains debated.

Within the canto, her offense is specifically flattering speech, not simply prostitution. This distinction matters: she is placed in the second ditch, where corrupt language is punished.

> 136 E quinci sian le nostre viste sazie».  
> (And with this let our eyes be satisfied”.)

Virgil closes the spectacle. No further inspection is necessary: its moral meaning is clear.

The ending also limits the reader's curiosity. Dante's journey is an education in recognizing evil, not an invitation to enjoy degradation indefinitely.

## Epilogue — The False Surface and the Truth Beneath

Canto XVIII exposes the connection between manipulation, appetite, and language. Pimps commodify other people; seducers use persuasive appearances to betray trust; flatterers make praise an instrument of advantage.

Their punishments strip away these disguises. Those who directed others are driven by whips. Those whose speech seemed pleasing are immersed in filth.

Across contemporary Italian scandals and classical legend, Dante insists on the same distinction: reputation, elegance, and eloquence are not moral goodness. Their worth depends on whether they serve truth and respect other people—or conceal the desire to use them.
