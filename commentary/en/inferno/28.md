# The Wounds of Division: Allegory and History in Inferno XXVIII

In the ninth bolgia of the eighth circle of Hell, Dante encounters those who sowed religious schism, political discord, and conflict within families. Their bodies are cut apart because they divided communities that should have remained united.

The canto turns history into a procession of wounds. Its figures come from ancient Rome, medieval religious controversy, Italian factional politics, and aristocratic rebellion. What unites them is the destructive power of counsel and speech: words that once separated people now return as divisions in the speakers' own bodies.

## 1. Language Before the Unspeakable

> 4 Ogne lingua per certo verria meno  
> (Every tongue would certainly fall short)
>
> 5 per lo nostro sermone e per la mente  
> (because of our speech and because of the mind)
>
> 6 c'hanno a tanto comprender poco seno.  
> (which have little capacity to encompass so much.)

Dante begins by declaring that neither language nor thought can adequately contain what he has seen. The limitation is double: human speech cannot express the spectacle fully, and the human mind cannot fully comprehend it.

This is a traditional declaration of poetic inadequacy, but it has a particular force here. A canto about the destructive use of language begins by exposing language's weakness. The condemned once spoke with terrible effectiveness; Dante now struggles to speak adequately about the consequences.

The opening also prepares the reader for an unusually physical style. Rather than retreating into abstraction, Dante answers the failure of language with graphic anatomical detail. His difficulty is not finding a decorous way to describe Hell, but making words confront its horror.

## 2. The Battlefields of History Cannot Equal This Ditch

> 19 e qual forato suo membro e qual mozzo  
> (and one his pierced limb and another his severed one)
>
> 20 mostrasse, d'aequar sarebbe nulla  
> (were to show, it would be nothing toward equalling)
>
> 21 il modo de la nona bolgia sozzo.  
> (the foul condition of the ninth bolgia.)

These lines complete an enormous hypothetical comparison: even if the wounded and dead from successive wars in southern Italy assembled and displayed their injuries, they could not equal this single infernal ditch.

The preceding catalogue compresses centuries of violence. The “Trojans” evoke the Romans through their legendary Trojan ancestry. The war that produced a great heap of rings recalls Hannibal's victory at Cannae in 216 BCE: according to Livy, rings taken from fallen Roman knights were collected as evidence of the slaughter. Dante then moves to the Norman conquests of Robert Guiscard.

The later references concern the destruction of Hohenstaufen power in Italy. Ceprano evokes the betrayal that, in Dante's account, helped expose Manfred's kingdom to Charles of Anjou; the decisive battle was fought at Benevento in 1266. Tagliacozzo recalls Conradin's defeat in 1268, attributed here to the strategic advice of the elderly Alardo.

The comparison does more than establish the quantity of suffering. It brings the consequences of political division into view before Dante meets its instigators. History supplies armies of broken bodies; Hell reveals the moral structure that Dante believes lies behind such destruction.

## 3. Muhammad and Ali: Division Written on the Body

> 31 vedi come storpiato è Mäometto!  
> (see how mutilated Muhammad is!)
>
> 32 Dinanzi a me sen va piangendo Alì,  
> (Before me Ali goes weeping,)
>
> 33 fesso nel volto dal mento al ciuffetto.  
> (split in the face from the chin to the forelock.)

Muhammad's torso has been opened from chin to lower body; Ali's face is split vertically. These wounds make religious division visible as bodily division.

The historical framing requires care. Dante places Muhammad among the sowers of schism because he interprets Islam through a medieval Latin Christian and polemical understanding of religious history. This is not an accurate account of Islam's origins, nor a neutral description of Muhammad's role. It is part of the poem's Christian theological construction of the world.

Ali was Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, and a central figure in the history of Muslim succession disputes. His appearance can evoke further division within Islam, although Dante does not explicitly explain his particular offense here. It would be too precise to treat every detail of his wound as an exact diagram of Sunni–Shiʿa history.

> 34 E tutti li altri che tu vedi qui,  
> (And all the others whom you see here,)
>
> 35 seminator di scandalo e di scisma  
> (sowers of discord and of schism)
>
> 36 fuor vivi, e però son fessi così.  
> (were while alive, and therefore are split like this.)

Muhammad now states the governing principle of the bolgia. “Scandal” here means more than public embarrassment: it signifies discord, disruption, or an offense that causes others to stumble. “Schism” names a rupture in religious unity.

The agricultural metaphor is important. These sinners were “sowers”: they planted words and advice whose consequences spread beyond themselves. Their punishment concentrates those dispersed consequences within their own bodies.

> 40 quand' avem volta la dolente strada;  
> (when we have gone around the sorrowful road;)
>
> 41 però che le ferite son richiuse  
> (because the wounds have closed again)
>
> 42 prima ch'altri dinanzi li rivada.  
> (before anyone goes before him again.)

A devil waits with a sword, cutting each sinner as the procession passes. During the circuit, the wounds heal; when the souls return, the cutting begins again.

Healing therefore offers no escape. It merely restores the body's capacity to suffer renewed division. The circular movement turns the consequences of a historical act into an eternal condition: the divisiveness these souls practiced is now the world they inhabit.

## 4. A Living Witness and the Warning to Fra Dolcino

> 52 Più fuor di cento che, quando l'udiro,  
> (There were more than a hundred who, when they heard it,)
>
> 53 s'arrestaron nel fosso a riguardarmi  
> (stopped in the ditch to look at me)
>
> 54 per maraviglia, oblïando il martiro.  
> (in wonder, forgetting their torment.)

Virgil has explained that Dante is alive and that his journey is intended to give him knowledge of Hell. The announcement briefly interrupts the procession.

The moment reverses the direction of attention. Dante has been staring at the wounded; now the wounded stare at him. The living body, intact and still capable of change, becomes more astonishing than the mutilated bodies around it.

Dante's presence also creates a connection with the historical world. He can return to daylight, carry messages, and influence how the dead are remembered. Muhammad uses that opportunity to warn Fra Dolcino.

> 58 sì di vivanda, che stretta di neve  
> (so well with provisions, that a blockade of snow)
>
> 59 non rechi la vittoria al Noarese,  
> (may not bring victory to the Novarese,)

The suspended syntax continues Muhammad's advice that Dolcino should arm himself—not chiefly with weapons, but with food.

Dolcino led the Apostolic Brethren, a dissident religious movement condemned by the Church. His followers were besieged in the mountains near the territories of Novara and Vercelli, and their resistance ended in 1307 after severe deprivation. Dolcino was captured and executed.

Because the poem's journey takes place in 1300, the warning appears as prophecy. For Dante's later readers, however, it points toward an already completed catastrophe. Muhammad's advice is practical rather than theological: winter and hunger, not simply military strength, will decide the struggle. At the same time, his expectation that Dolcino will join him places the religious dissident within the canto's category of schism.

## 5. Pier da Medicina: Remembering the Divided Homeland

> 73 rimembriti di Pier da Medicina,  
> (remember Pier da Medicina,)
>
> 74 se mai torni a veder lo dolce piano  
> (if ever you return to see the sweet plain)
>
> 75 che da Vercelli a Marcabò dichina.  
> (that slopes down from Vercelli to Marcabò.)

Pier da Medicina asks Dante to remember him when he returns to northern Italy. The broad plain stretching toward the Adriatic appears as a single geographical landscape, even though its political life is fractured.

The adjective “sweet” matters. Affection for the earthly homeland survives inside Hell. The landscape's remembered beauty contrasts with the disfigured speaker: Pier has a pierced throat, a mutilated nose, and only one ear.

Early commentators portray him as a political intriguer who fostered hostility among rulers, although secure historical information about him is limited. His damaged organs suggest a world of corrupted communication—hearing, speaking, carrying reports—but the text does not assign a separate, explicit meaning to every injury.

His request also shows how much the damned still depend on human memory. They cannot repair their earthly actions, but they continue to seek recognition in the world those actions helped damage.

## 6. The Prophecy of Murder at Sea

> 79 gittati saran fuor di lor vasello  
> (they will be thrown out of their vessel)
>
> 80 e mazzerati presso a la Cattolica  
> (and drowned with weights near Cattolica)
>
> 81 per tradimento d'un tiranno fello.  
> (through the treachery of a wicked tyrant.)

Pier predicts the murder of two leading men from Fano, Guido del Cassero and Angiolello da Carignano. They will be lured into danger and drowned near Cattolica.

The tyrant is generally identified as Malatestino Malatesta, ruler of Rimini. The precise crime described here is not securely corroborated by independent surviving evidence, so the passage should not be treated as an uncomplicated historical record. It is also Dante's political indictment.

The method of killing intensifies that indictment. The victims are not to fall in open combat; they will disappear beneath the water through calculated betrayal. Political conversation itself becomes a trap, since the invitation to a meeting provides the opportunity for murder.

> 85 Quel traditor che vede pur con l'uno,  
> (That traitor who sees with only one,)
>
> 86 e tien la terra che tale qui meco  
> (and holds the city that someone here with me)
>
> 87 vorrebbe di vedere esser digiuno,  
> (would wish never to have seen,)

The reference to seeing with only one eye identifies the one-eyed Malatestino. His bodily characteristic is not itself his sin; it is a recognizable feature attached to an accusation of treachery.

The city he controls is Rimini. Pier's unnamed companion wishes he had never seen it, and that remark links medieval tyranny to an ancient political catastrophe. Dante asks who the companion is, and the canto turns from the Malatesta world to Julius Caesar's civil war.

## 7. Curio: The Tongue That Ended Hesitation

> 100 Oh quanto mi pareva sbigottito  
> (Oh how dismayed he seemed to me)
>
> 101 con la lingua tagliata ne la strozza  
> (with his tongue cut away in his throat,)
>
> 102 Curïo, ch'a dir fu così ardito!  
> (Curio, who had been so bold in speaking!)

The companion is Gaius Scribonius Curio. Following the literary tradition represented by Lucan's *Pharsalia*, Dante associates him with urging Caesar to abandon hesitation and proceed toward civil war.

Pier summarizes Curio's advice as the claim that delay harms a person who is already prepared for action. Its danger lies partly in its apparent practicality. A brief maxim makes an immense political and moral decision seem like a simple question of efficiency.

Curio's missing tongue offers a concentrated form of retributive symbolism. The organ that delivered destructive counsel has been cut away. The once-confident speaker now depends on another sinner to open his mouth and reveal who he is.

Dante's imperial sympathies make the episode more complex than a simple condemnation of Caesar. Whatever larger providential significance Caesar may possess elsewhere in the poem, the incitement to civil conflict remains subject to judgment.

## 8. Mosca: A Proverb Becomes a City's Disaster

> 107 che disse, lasso!, “Capo ha cosa fatta”,  
> (who said, alas!, “A thing done has an end”,)
>
> 108 che fu mal seme per la gente tosca».  
> (which was an evil seed for the Tuscan people.”)

Mosca dei Lamberti recalls the maxim associated with his advice to kill Buondelmonte de' Buondelmonti after a broken marriage agreement. The proverb means, approximately, that decisive action settles a matter: once the deed is done, uncertainty is over.

In Florentine historical tradition, Buondelmonte's murder in 1215 became a foundational episode in the hostility between Guelf and Ghibelline factions. Those divisions had broader causes, but Dante presents the killing as a disastrous seed from which further conflict grew.

The resemblance to Curio is deliberate. Both men turn destructive action into a persuasive formula. Their concise advice suppresses consideration of consequences, yet the consequences become enormous.

Mosca has lost both hands. The punishment evokes agency and the violent deed, while his own words expose the inadequacy of his maxim: the murder did not end the affair. It multiplied it.

> 110 per ch'elli, accumulando duol con duolo,  
> (because of which he, piling grief upon grief,)
>
> 111 sen gio come persona trista e matta.  
> (went away like a sorrowful and maddened person.)

Dante has added that Mosca's counsel also brought death to his own lineage. This intervention makes the scene especially harsh.

The pilgrim is not merely recording punishment; his reply increases the sinner's anguish. The exchange shows how deeply the factional history of Florence touches him. It also complicates the canto's moral authority: the poet condemning civic hatred remains emotionally implicated in the history that produced it.

## 9. Conscience and the Impossible Spectacle

> 115 se non che coscïenza m'assicura,  
> (except that conscience reassures me,)
>
> 116 la buona compagnia che l'uom francheggia  
> (the good companion that gives a person courage)
>
> 117 sotto l'asbergo del sentirsi pura.  
> (under the armor of feeling itself pure.)

Before describing the canto's final figure, Dante pauses to defend the truthfulness of his testimony. What he saw was so extraordinary that he would fear reporting it without the reassurance of a clear conscience.

The military metaphor is striking. Conscience becomes armor, protecting the narrator as he describes a landscape of bodies that have no protection from violence. His defense rests not on the scene's plausibility but on his conviction that he is reporting faithfully.

This is also an assertion of poetic authority. Dante asks the reader to accept an impossible spectacle as meaningful within the divinely ordered reality of the poem.

> 124 Di sé facea a sé stesso lucerna,  
> (Of himself he made a lamp for himself,)
>
> 125 ed eran due in uno e uno in due;  
> (and they were two in one and one in two;)
>
> 126 com' esser può, quei sa che sì governa.  
> (how this can be, he knows who governs thus.)

A headless body carries its severed head by the hair like a lantern. The head looks and speaks, while the body walks and lifts it toward Dante.

The paradox of “two in one and one in two” makes a divided person remain horribly unified. The sinner cannot escape himself by being dismembered: body and head still form one suffering identity.

Dante declines to explain the mechanism. God, who orders this punishment, knows how it is possible. The spectacle is therefore both bodily and metaphysical—a visible violation of ordinary human wholeness sustained by infernal justice.

## 10. Bertran de Born and the Explicit Law of Contrapasso

> 134 sappi ch'i' son Bertram dal Bornio, quelli  
> (know that I am Bertran de Born, the one)
>
> 135 che diedi al re giovane i ma' conforti.  
> (who gave the young king the evil counsels.)
>
> 136 Io feci il padre e 'l figlio in sé ribelli;  
> (I made the father and the son rebels against each other;)

The speaker is Bertran de Born, the twelfth-century Occitan troubadour and lord associated with aristocratic warfare. The “young king” is Henry the Young King, son of Henry II of England.

Medieval tradition portrayed Bertran as encouraging the younger Henry's hostility toward his father. Dante turns that attributed role into the defining fact of his damnation. The historical degree of Bertran's responsibility is less certain than the poem's absolute judgment suggests.

His presence also makes the canto reflect on poetry itself. A distinguished poet can use verbal brilliance to encourage division. Artistic achievement does not exempt its possessor from responsibility for the effects of his words.

Bertran compares himself to the biblical counselor Ahithophel, who supported Absalom's rebellion against his father David. The comparison places medieval courtly intrigue within a longer history of counsel that corrupts family bonds.

> 139 Perch' io parti' così giunte persone,  
> (Because I divided persons so closely joined,)
>
> 140 partito porto il mio cerebro, lasso!,  
> (divided I carry my brain, alas!,)
>
> 141 dal suo principio ch'è in questo troncone.  
> (from its source, which is in this trunk.)

Bertran explains his own punishment. Because he separated father and son, he now carries his brain separated from its bodily source. The verbal repetition of “divided” makes the correspondence unmistakable.

The canto ends by naming this relationship *contrapasso*: punishment that answers the sin through a meaningful correspondence. Here the principle works through resemblance. An act of separation returns as an enduring state of separation.

Bertran's injury is the culmination of the entire bolgia. Religious communities, cities, political orders, and families have all been treated as forms of connected life. His severed head makes the destruction of those connections immediately visible.

## Epilogue — The Broken Body of Community

Inferno XXVIII imagines human community as a body capable of being wounded by words. Advice, slogans, religious conflict, and political manipulation cease to be abstract forces: they appear as split faces, severed tongues, mutilated limbs, and a head detached from its trunk.

The canto's judgments belong to Dante's medieval Christian and political worldview, and some of its historical accusations require qualification. Its larger warning nevertheless remains clear: speech becomes dangerous when it makes rupture seem righteous, profitable, or decisively simple.

The final horror is not division alone, but division without release. Those who broke human bonds must forever carry the brokenness within themselves.
