# Blood, Beasts, and Broken Stone: Allegory in Dante's *Inferno*, Canto XII

Canto XII brings Dante and Virgil into the seventh circle of Hell, where violence is punished. Its first ring holds those who harmed other people or their possessions: tyrants, murderers, and violent robbers. Further rings will punish violence against oneself and against God, nature, and human industry.

The canto gives violence a landscape and a bodily form. Broken rocks, hybrid monsters, and boiling blood reveal what happens when appetite overthrows reason. Yet this seemingly savage world remains subject to divine justice—and its shattered terrain also bears witness to Christ's power over Hell.

## 1. The Broken Threshold: A Landscape of Violence

> 4 Qual è quella ruina che nel fianco  
> (Like that landslide which, on the flank,)
>
> 5 di qua da Trento l'Adice percosse,  
> (on this side of Trent, struck the Adige,)
>
> 6 o per tremoto o per sostegno manco,  
> (either through earthquake or through failed support,)

Dante introduces the descent through a comparison with an actual Italian landscape. The traditional identification is the great rockfall known as the Slavini di Marco, near Rovereto, south of Trent. Whether caused by an earthquake or the failure of the mountain's supporting structure, the fallen rock provides a recognizable model for the infernal slope.

The comparison makes Hell physically imaginable: Dante does not enter an abstract diagram of evil but scrambles through something resembling a dangerous mountain pass.

The landscape also suits the circle's moral character. Violence breaks the integrity of bodies, communities, and possessions; here the ground itself has been torn apart. That correspondence is suggestive rather than a complete explanation, however. Virgil will reveal that this particular ruin arose from an event whose meaning extends far beyond violence: Christ's death.

## 2. The Minotaur: Humanity Deformed by Appetite

> 11 e 'n su la punta de la rotta lacca  
> (and on the edge of the shattered hollow)
>
> 12 l'infamïa di Creti era distesa  
> (the infamy of Crete lay stretched out,)
>
> 13 che fu concetta ne la falsa vacca;  
> (which was conceived in the counterfeit cow;)
>
> 14 e quando vide noi, sé stesso morse,  
> (and when it saw us, it bit itself,)
>
> 15 sì come quei cui l'ira dentro fiacca.  
> (like one whom anger consumes within.)

The “infamy of Crete” is the Minotaur, born from Pasiphaë's union with a bull. The “counterfeit cow” was the artificial animal constructed by Daedalus so that Pasiphaë could satisfy her desire. The resulting monster was confined in the labyrinth and eventually killed by Theseus.

Its position at the entrance to the circle is morally appropriate. A creature combining human and animal characteristics represents humanity degraded when irrational appetite governs conduct. Its origin also joins disordered desire to ingenuity: human skill can serve appetite rather than restrain it.

The Minotaur's first response to the travelers is to bite itself. Anger does not merely threaten an external victim; it turns destructively upon its possessor. Before Dante reaches the river of blood, the guardian's body already demonstrates the self-defeating character of violent passion.

## 3. Virgil's Strategy: Reason Exploits Blind Rage

> 19 Pàrtiti, bestia, ché questi non vene  
> (Depart, beast, for this man does not come)
>
> 20 ammaestrato da la tua sorella,  
> (instructed by your sister,)
>
> 21 ma vassi per veder le vostre pene».  
> (but goes to see your punishments.”)

Virgil provokes the Minotaur by recalling its defeat. The “sister” is Ariadne, who helped Theseus navigate the labyrinth. Dante, Virgil insists, is not another Theseus arriving with her assistance: his purpose is to witness punishment, not to repeat the classical exploit.

This distinction establishes the special character of Dante's journey. Although it resembles ancient descents into the underworld, it is a Christian pilgrimage toward moral understanding, not an adventure undertaken for heroic glory.

> 26 e quello accorto gridò: «Corri al varco;  
> (and the wary one cried: “Run to the passage;)
>
> 27 mentre ch'e' 'nfuria, è buon che tu ti cale».  
> (while it rages, it is well that you descend.”)

Virgil does not defeat the monster by superior force. He uses judgment, timing, and knowledge of its passions. The Minotaur becomes so enraged that it cannot effectively guard the passage.

Virgil, often associated with human reason, thus shows reason's practical superiority to uncontrolled anger. But he does not cure the beast's irrationality: he turns it into an opportunity to escape. The episode presents violence as both terrifying and incompetent, full of energy but incapable of directing that energy intelligently.

## 4. The Earthquake: Classical Cosmology Meets Christian History

> 37 Ma certo poco pria, se ben discerno,  
> (But certainly shortly before, if I judge rightly,)
>
> 38 che venisse colui che la gran preda  
> (there came the one who the great spoil)
>
> 39 levò a Dite del cerchio superno,  
> (took from Dis out of the uppermost circle,)

Virgil explains that the rock had not yet fallen when he previously descended into the lower regions of Hell. That earlier journey, mentioned in Canto IX, took place under the compulsion of the sorceress Erichtho.

The later visitor is Christ. His “great spoil” consists of the souls liberated from Limbo in the Harrowing of Hell: the righteous of the Old Testament whom medieval Christian tradition understood Christ to have rescued after his death.

The language turns a military image upside down. Christ takes captives away from the ruler of the underworld, so that conquest becomes liberation. The earthquake shortly preceding that descent is associated with the earthquake at the Crucifixion described in Matthew's Gospel.

> 40 da tutte parti l'alta valle feda  
> (on every side the deep, foul valley)
>
> 41 tremò sì, ch'i' pensai che l'universo  
> (shook so that I thought the universe)
>
> 42 sentisse amor, per lo qual è chi creda  
> (felt love, through which some believe)
>
> 43 più volte il mondo in caòsso converso;  
> (the world repeatedly turned to chaos;)

Virgil initially interprets the earthquake through ancient natural philosophy. His explanation recalls Empedocles, whose cosmos is governed by the opposing powers of Love and Strife. Love draws the elements together; Strife separates them. The passage evokes the possibility that their changing dominance might undo the world's differentiated order.

Dante places this classical account beside a Christian explanation that exceeds it. Virgil recognized a cosmic disturbance but could not fully understand its cause.

His reference to love is nevertheless suggestive. From the poem's Christian perspective, divine love truly has shaken Hell through Christ's redemptive death. Virgil's theory is inadequate, but its vocabulary approaches a truth he cannot completely possess.

## 5. Boiling Blood: The Logic of the Punishment

> 47 la riviera del sangue in la qual bolle  
> (the river of blood in which boils)
>
> 48 qual che per vïolenza in altrui noccia».  
> (whoever harms another through violence.”)

The river is the Phlegethon, adapted from classical accounts of the underworld. Its defining substance here is boiling blood.

This is a clear example of *contrapasso*: punishment corresponds to the character of the sin. Those who shed blood are now immersed in it. What they inflicted upon others has become the medium of their own existence.

The boiling also gives physical expression to the heat of violent passion. Yet the punishment is not simply a picture of anger: it encompasses calculated oppression and predatory acquisition as well.

> 49 Oh cieca cupidigia e ira folle,  
> (O blind greed and mad anger,)
>
> 50 che sì ci sproni ne la vita corta,  
> (which so spur us in our brief life,)
>
> 51 e ne l'etterna poi sì mal c'immolle!  
> (and then in the eternal steep us in such misery!)

Dante names two impulses behind violence: greed and anger. One seeks possession without regard for others; the other abandons self-command in the desire to strike or destroy. Their adjectives emphasize damaged judgment: greed is “blind,” anger “mad.”

The contrast between the “brief life” and eternal consequences exposes the terrible disproportion involved. Temporary gain or momentary satisfaction produces an enduring moral condition.

The pronoun “us” matters too. Dante's exclamation does not isolate violence as the incomprehensible behavior of distant monsters. It identifies temptations belonging to human life, including the life of the speaker and reader.

## 6. The Centaurs: Armed Force Under Judgment

> 73 Dintorno al fosso vanno a mille a mille,  
> (Around the ditch they go by thousands,)
>
> 74 saettando qual anima si svelle  
> (shooting any soul that pulls itself)
>
> 75 del sangue più che sua colpa sortille».  
> (farther out of the blood than its guilt allotted it.”)

The centaurs patrol the river with bows, preventing the condemned from rising above their assigned depth. Their task makes an important distinction: the river's violence is measured rather than indiscriminate. Each soul's immersion corresponds to its guilt.

Like the Minotaur, the centaurs combine human and animal forms. Unlike it, they display organization, intelligence, and an ability to cooperate. Their traditional associations with hunting and violence make them fitting guards, but their disciplined patrol contrasts with the Minotaur's helpless frenzy.

Virgil identifies three: Nessus, Chiron, and Pholus. Their backgrounds complicate any simple equation between hybrid bodies and identical moral characters. Pholus is associated here with anger, while Chiron is the distinguished teacher of Achilles.

Nessus has an especially violent history. After attempting to assault Deianira, he was shot by Hercules. Before dying, he persuaded her to preserve his blood as a supposed means of securing her husband's love. Its poison later caused Hercules' death. The centaur who achieved revenge through poisoned blood now guides Dante beside a river of blood: an especially fitting conjunction of myth and punishment.

## 7. Dante's Living Body and the Authority of Heaven

> 81 che quel di retro move ciò ch'el tocca?  
> (that the one behind moves what he touches?)
>
> 82 Così non soglion far li piè d'i morti».  
> (The feet of the dead do not usually do so.”)

Chiron notices that Dante dislodges stones as he walks. This observation distinguishes the living traveler from the shades around him.

The detail anchors the journey in bodily experience. The rocks respond to Dante's weight; the descent requires physical effort; the river presents an obstacle he cannot simply pass through as a spirit. His presence is exceptional because he has entered the realm of final judgment before his own earthly life has ended.

Chiron's perceptiveness also contrasts with the Minotaur's rage. He looks, reasons from evidence, and asks a question before acting.

> 88 Tal si partì da cantare alleluia  
> (One departed from singing alleluia)
>
> 89 che mi commise quest' officio novo:  
> (who entrusted me with this new task:)
>
> 90 non è ladron, né io anima fuia.  
> (he is no robber, nor am I a thieving soul.)

Virgil invokes the heavenly origin of his mission. The unnamed figure is Beatrice, who left her blessed place to ask him to rescue Dante, as recounted in Canto II.

His denial that they are thieves separates their journey from the predatory conduct punished here. They are neither legitimate prisoners nor unauthorized intruders pursuing plunder.

More importantly, Virgil's authority is delegated. Human reason can guide Dante, but its work belongs within a larger action of grace. Chiron consequently assigns Nessus to escort them and carry the living man across the river. The infernal guardians cannot finally obstruct a journey authorized from Heaven.

## 8. Tyrants: Public Power Turned Predatory

> 104 e 'l gran centauro disse: «E' son tiranni  
> (and the great centaur said: “These are tyrants)
>
> 105 che dier nel sangue e ne l'aver di piglio.  
> (who laid their hands on blood and possessions.)

The deeply submerged sinners are rulers who attacked both lives and property. Dante defines tyranny through predation: political power becomes criminal when it treats human beings and their possessions as material for seizure.

This places public oppression beside other forms of violent wrongdoing. Political rank does not transform slaughter or robbery into legitimate action.

Nessus names Alexander and Dionysius. The identification of “Alexander” is disputed: Alexander the Great and Alexander of Pherae have both been proposed. Dionysius is generally understood as Dionysius I of Syracuse, whose rule exemplified tyranny in classical and medieval traditions.

> 109 E quella fronte c'ha 'l pel così nero,  
> (And that brow with hair so black)
>
> 110 è Azzolino; e quell' altro ch'è biondo,  
> (is Ezzelino; and that other who is blond)
>
> 111 è Opizzo da Esti, il qual per vero  
> (is Obizzo of Este, who in truth)
>
> 112 fu spento dal figliastro sù nel mondo».  
> (was slain by his stepson in the world above.”)

The movement from antiquity to Italian rulers makes the judgment immediate. Ezzelino III da Romano was remembered for the cruelty of his rule in northeastern Italy. Obizzo II d'Este was a powerful lord of Ferrara and neighboring territories.

The assertion that Obizzo was killed by his “stepson” reflects a hostile account of his death. The figure usually identified is Azzo VIII, officially his son; Dante's wording is deliberately derogatory, though its precise implication has been debated. The poem's accusation should not be confused with an uncontested historical finding.

The black and blond hair are not themselves moral symbols. They are identifying details that make these once-powerful rulers visible as individual bodies under judgment. Their titles offer no protection from the blood in which they stand.

## 9. Murder “in God's Lap”: Violence Against Sanctuary

> 118 Mostrocci un'ombra da l'un canto sola,  
> (He showed us a shade alone on one side,)
>
> 119 dicendo: «Colui fesse in grembo a Dio  
> (saying: “That one cleft, in God's lap,)
>
> 120 lo cor che 'n su Tamisi ancor si cola».  
> (the heart that beside the Thames is still honored.”)

The isolated murderer is Guy de Montfort. In 1271, at Viterbo, he killed Henry of Almain, a nephew of England's King Henry III, in revenge for the destruction of his own family during the English baronial conflicts.

The murder took place during Mass. “In God's lap” therefore emphasizes the violated sanctuary: an act of private vengeance was carried into a place dedicated to divine worship and peace.

The heart “still honored” beside the Thames refers to the English commemoration of the victim's heart, traditionally associated with Westminster. The expression turns attention from the murderer's action to the continuing remembrance of the dead.

This episode makes violence personal again after the catalogue of rulers. It also reveals a collision of jurisdictions: family vengeance presumes to override both public justice and the sacred protection of worship.

## 10. Degrees of Guilt and the End of the Escort

> 124 Così a più a più si facea basso  
> (Thus little by little grew shallow)
>
> 125 quel sangue, sì che cocea pur li piedi;  
> (that blood, so that it scorched only the feet;)
>
> 126 e quindi fu del fosso il nostro passo.  
> (and there was our passage across the ditch.)

The river's changing depth makes moral distinction visible. Some sinners are immersed almost completely; others stand in blood reaching their throats or chests; at the crossing, it burns only their feet.

Dante does not present every act of violence as equally grave. The punishment varies with culpability, although the canto does not provide a complete classification of every soul at every depth.

Nessus explains that the river deepens again around the other side until it returns to the region of tyranny. Its circular form contains differences of guilt within one comprehensive order of judgment.

> 133 La divina giustizia di qua punge  
> (Divine justice on this side torments)
>
> 134 quell' Attila che fu flagello in terra,  
> (that Attila who was a scourge on earth,)
>
> 135 e Pirro e Sesto; e in etterno munge  
> (and Pyrrhus and Sextus; and forever draws out)
>
> 136 le lagrime, che col bollor diserra,  
> (the tears, which with the boiling it releases,)
>
> 137 a Rinier da Corneto, a Rinier Pazzo,  
> (from Rinier da Corneto, from Rinier Pazzo,)
>
> 138 che fecero a le strade tanta guerra».  
> (who waged such warfare on the roads.”)

The final catalogue brings together notorious destroyers and more local predators. Attila appears in his traditional role as a scourge of humanity. The identity of Pyrrhus is debated, while Sextus is generally understood as Sextus Pompey, associated with maritime depredation. The two Riniers were remembered for violent highway robbery.

Their juxtaposition is morally pointed. Large-scale devastation and attacks on travelers differ in scope, but both may express the same predatory treatment of other people.

The language also reverses earthly power. Those who once made others suffer now have tears continually extracted from them. Crucially, the governing subject is “divine justice”: however savage the imagery, the poem presents the punishment as judgment, not another outbreak of uncontrolled revenge.

> 139 Poi si rivolse e ripassossi 'l guazzo.  
> (Then he turned and crossed the ford again.)

Nessus returns to his appointed place. His assistance was real but limited: he enabled the crossing without becoming a permanent companion or escaping his infernal role.

The quiet ending contrasts with the canto's opening frenzy. Dante and Virgil have passed through a realm of violent disorder, but their progress remains governed by an authority that its inhabitants cannot overturn.

## Epilogue — Violence Contained, Judgment Made Visible

Canto XII shows violence as the destruction of rightful order: appetite masters reason, rulers prey upon their subjects, revenge violates sanctuary, and bloodshed becomes habitual power. Its monsters and punishments make these distortions physically visible.

Yet violence does not have the final authority. Reason finds a passage past rage; divine judgment distinguishes degrees of guilt; heavenly assistance protects Dante's journey. Even Hell's broken rocks testify to a power greater than its own—the redemptive event that shook its foundations and released its captives.
