At the end of his words the thief
he raised his hands with both making the figs,
shouting: “Take that, God, for I aim them at you!”.
From then onward the serpents were my friends,
because one then wound itself around his neck,
as if saying, ‘I do not want you to speak any more’;
and another around his arms, and bound him fast,
knotting itself so tightly in front,
that he could not give them a shake.
Ah Pistoia, Pistoia, why do you not resolve
to burn yourself to ashes so that you endure no more,
since in wrongdoing you surpass your seed?
Through all the dark circles of hell
I saw no spirit so proud against God,
not even the one who fell at Thebes down from the walls.
He fled without speaking another word;
and I saw a centaur full of rage
come calling: “Where is, where is the bitter one?”.
I do not believe Maremma has so many,
as the snakes he had upon his haunches
up to where our human form begins.
Upon his shoulders, behind the nape,
lay a dragon with its wings spread;
and it sets fire to whoever it encounters.
My master said: “This is Cacus,
who, beneath the rock of Mount Aventine,
often made a lake of blood.
He does not go along one path with his brothers,
because of the theft that he committed fraudulently
from the great herd that he had nearby;
for which his wicked deeds came to an end
beneath the club of Hercules, who perhaps
gave him a hundred blows, and he did not feel the tenth.”
While he was speaking thus, the centaur ran past,
and three spirits came beneath us,
whom neither I nor my guide noticed,
until they cried: “Who are you?”;
at which our conversation stopped,
and we then attended only to them.
I did not know them; but it happened,
as it often happens by some chance,
that one had occasion to name another,
saying: “Where can Cianfa have remained?”;
at which I, so that my guide would pay attention,
laid my finger from my chin to my nose.
If you are now, reader, slow to believe
what I shall say, it will be no wonder,
for I who saw it scarcely allow myself to believe it.
As I kept my eyes raised toward them,
suddenly a serpent with six feet darts
in front of one, and fastens itself all upon him.
With its middle feet it clasped his belly
and with its front ones seized his arms;
then it bit both one cheek and the other;
it stretched its hind feet along his thighs,
and put its tail between the two
and stretched it upward behind along his loins.
Never was ivy rooted
to a tree so firmly as the horrible beast
entwined its limbs around the other's.
Then they stuck together, as if of warm wax
they had been made, and mingled their color,
and neither one nor the other now seemed what it had been:
as there spreads ahead of the heat,
upward along the paper, a brown color
that is not yet black, and the white dies away.
The other two looked at him, and each
cried: “Alas, Agnel, how you change!
See that already you are neither two nor one.”
Already the two heads had become one,
when there appeared to us two forms mingled
in one face, where both were lost.
The arms became two out of four limbs;
the thighs with the legs and the belly and the chest
became members never seen before.
Every former aspect was erased there:
both two and neither the distorted image
seemed; and thus it went away with slow steps.
As the green lizard beneath the great lash
of the dog days, changing hedges,
seems a lightning flash if it crosses the road,
so appeared, coming toward the bellies
of the other two, a fiery little serpent,
livid and black as a peppercorn;
and that part through which first is taken
our nourishment, it pierced in one of them;
then fell down stretched out before him.
The pierced one looked at it, but said nothing;
instead, with his feet fixed, he yawned
just as if sleep or fever were assailing him.
He gazed at the serpent, and it at him;
the one through the wound and the other through the mouth
smoked heavily, and the smoke met.
Let Lucan now be silent where he tells
of wretched Sabellus and of Nasidius,
and let him attend to hear what now shoots forth.
Let Ovid be silent about Cadmus and Arethusa,
for if the former into a serpent and the latter into a spring
he transforms through poetry, I do not envy him;
for two natures never face to face
did he transmute so that both the forms
were ready to exchange their matter.
Together they responded to such rules,
that the serpent split its tail into a fork,
and the wounded one drew his feet together.
The legs with the thighs to each other
clung so closely that soon the joining
left no mark that could be seen.
The split tail was taking on the shape
that was being lost there, and its skin
was becoming soft, and the skin over there hard.
I saw the arms withdraw through the armpits,
and the beast's two feet, which were short,
lengthen as much as those arms shortened.
Then the hind feet, twisted together,
became the member that a man conceals,
and the wretched one had two thrust out from his.
While the smoke veils both one and the other
in a new color, and brings forth hair
on the one and strips it from the other,
the one rose and the other fell down,
yet without turning away their wicked lamps,
beneath which each was changing his muzzle.
The one who stood upright drew his toward his temples,
and from the excess matter that went there
ears emerged from the bare cheeks;
what did not run backward and remained
of that excess made a nose for the face
and thickened the lips as much as was fitting.
The one who lay thrusts his muzzle forward,
and draws his ears back into his head
as the snail does its horns;
and the tongue, which he had whole and ready
before for speech, splits, and the forked tongue
in the other closes together; and the smoke ceases.
The soul that had become a beast
flees hissing through the valley,
and the other spits after him as he speaks.
Then he turned his new shoulders toward him,
and said to the other: “I want Buoso to run,
as I have done, on all fours along this path.”
Thus I saw the seventh ballast
change and transmute; and here may it excuse me,
the novelty, if the pen goes somewhat astray.
And although my eyes were somewhat confused
and my spirit bewildered,
those could not escape so well concealed,
that I did not clearly recognize Puccio Sciancato;
and he was the one who alone, of three companions
who had come before, had not been changed;
the other was the one whom you, Gaville, mourn.
