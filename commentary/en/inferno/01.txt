In the middle of the journey of our life
I found myself within a dark wood,
for the straight way was lost.
Ah, how hard a thing it is to tell what it was like,
this wood so wild and rough and dense
that in thought it renews my fear!
So bitter is it that death is little more;
but to tell of the good that I found there,
I shall speak of the other things I saw there.
I cannot well recount how I entered it,
so full of sleep was I at that moment
when I abandoned the true way.
But when I had reached the foot of a hill,
there where that valley ended
which had pierced my heart with fear,
I looked upward and saw its shoulders
already clothed in the rays of the planet
that leads others straight along every path.
Then the fear was quieted a little,
which in the lake of my heart had endured
the night that I passed in such distress.
And like one who, with labored breathing,
having come out of the open sea onto the shore,
turns toward the perilous water and gazes,
so my spirit, which was still fleeing,
turned back to look again at the passage
that never yet let anyone pass alive.
After I had rested my weary body a little,
I resumed my way along the deserted slope,
so that the firm foot was always the lower.
And behold, almost at the beginning of the ascent,
a leopard, light and very swift,
which was covered with a spotted coat;
and it did not move away from before my face,
but so impeded my path
that I turned several times to go back.
It was the beginning of the morning,
and the sun was rising with those stars
that were with it when divine love
first set those beautiful things in motion;
so that I had cause to hope well
concerning that beast with the dappled coat
from the hour of the day and the sweet season;
but not so much that fear was not brought upon me
by the sight of a lion that appeared to me.
He seemed to be coming against me
with head held high and with ravenous hunger,
so that the air seemed to tremble at it.
And a she-wolf, who with every craving
seemed laden in her leanness,
and had already made many people live in misery,
she brought such heaviness upon me
with the fear that came from the sight of her,
that I lost hope of reaching the height.
And like one who willingly acquires,
and the time comes that makes him lose,
who weeps and grieves in all his thoughts;
so did the restless beast make me,
which, coming against me, little by little
was driving me back to where the sun is silent.
While I was falling toward the low place,
there appeared before my eyes
one who through long silence seemed faint.
When I saw him in the great wilderness,
“Have mercy on me,” I cried to him,
“whatever you may be, whether shade or real man!”
He answered me: “Not a man, though once I was,
and my parents were Lombards,
both Mantuan by birthplace.
I was born under Julius, although it was late,
and I lived in Rome under the good Augustus
in the time of the false and lying gods.
I was a poet, and I sang of that just
son of Anchises who came from Troy,
after proud Ilium was burned.
But why do you return to such distress?
why do you not climb the delightful mountain
that is the beginning and cause of all joy?”
“Now are you that Virgil and that fountain
that pours forth so broad a river of speech?”
I answered him with a shamefaced brow.
“O honor and light of the other poets,
may the long study and the great love avail me
that have made me search your volume.
You are my master and my author,
you alone are the one from whom I took
the beautiful style that has brought me honor.
See the beast because of which I turned back;
help me against her, famous sage,
for she makes my veins and pulses tremble.”
“You must take another journey,”
he answered, when he saw me weeping,
“if you wish to escape this savage place;
for this beast, because of which you cry out,
lets no one pass along her way,
but so hinders him that she kills him;
and her nature is so wicked and evil,
that she never fills her ravenous desire,
and after feeding she is hungrier than before.
Many are the animals with which she mates,
and still more there will be, until the greyhound
comes, who will make her die in pain.
He will feed on neither land nor pewter,
but wisdom, love, and virtue,
and his birth shall be between felt and felt.
He shall be the salvation of that lowly Italy
for which the virgin Camilla died,
Euryalus and Turnus and Nisus of wounds.
He shall hunt her through every town,
until he has driven her back into hell,
from where envy first sent her forth.
Therefore, for your good, I think and judge
that you should follow me, and I shall be your guide,
and I shall lead you from here through an eternal place;
where you will hear the desperate cries,
you will see the ancient sorrowing spirits,
each of whom cries out for the second death;
and you will see those who are content
in the fire, because they hope to come
whenever that may be, among the blessed people.
Then if you wish to ascend to them,
there will be a soul more worthy of that than I:
with her I shall leave you when I depart;
for that emperor who reigns up there,
because I was rebellious against his law,
he does not will that through me one should come into his city.
He rules in every part and there he reigns;
there are his city and his lofty throne:
oh, happy is the one he chooses to be there!”
And I to him: “Poet, I beseech you
by that God whom you did not know,
so that I may flee this evil and worse,
that you lead me where you have just said,
so that I may see the gate of Saint Peter
and those whom you describe as so sorrowful.”
Then he moved, and I followed behind him.
