# The Face of Honesty, the Tail of Fraud: Dante's *Inferno*, Canto XVII

Canto XVII is a threshold between two kinds of evil. Dante and Virgil are leaving the seventh circle, where violence is punished, and preparing to descend into the eighth, the realm of fraud. Before they depart, Dante encounters the usurers; then the travelers cross an enormous abyss on the back of Geryon, the monster who embodies deception.

The canto brings together moral allegory, contemporary Italian history, classical mythology, and a remarkably physical account of fear.

## 1. Geryon: The Power That Defeats Every Defense

> 1 «Ecco la fiera con la coda aguzza,  
> (“Behold the beast with the pointed tail,)
>
> 2 che passa i monti e rompe i muri e l'armi!  
> (that crosses mountains and breaks through walls and weapons!)
>
> 3 Ecco colei che tutto 'l mondo appuzza!».  
> (Behold her who makes the whole world stink!”)

Virgil introduces fraud as a power against which ordinary defenses are useless. Mountains, fortifications, and weapons can resist an openly attacking enemy; they cannot reliably stop someone who gains entry through deception. Fraud crosses boundaries by making its victims cooperate with their own injury.

The final verb makes moral corruption almost physically perceptible: fraud contaminates the whole world like a spreading stench. This is not merely a description of one monster's ugliness. It is a claim about the damage deception does to human society.

In classical mythology, Geryon was a three-bodied figure whose cattle Hercules seized. Dante radically redesigns him. Instead of preserving the ancient creature's anatomy, he gives Geryon a body that can express the structure of fraud: reassuring appearances conceal a deadly purpose.

## 2. The Honest Face and the Decorated Body

> 10 La faccia sua era faccia d'uom giusto,  
> (His face was the face of a righteous man,)
>
> 11 tanto benigna avea di fuor la pelle,  
> (so kindly was the skin he had on the outside,)
>
> 12 e d'un serpente tutto l'altro fusto;  
> (and all the rest of his trunk was a serpent's;)

The decisive feature is not simply that Geryon combines human and animal parts. It is that the human part looks *righteous*. Fraud does not announce itself as wrongdoing: it borrows the appearance of honesty.

The phrase “on the outside” establishes the distinction between surface and reality. Geryon's kindly skin is a mask, while the serpent's body suggests concealed danger and recalls the biblical association between the serpent and deception. The symbolism works through that association without reducing Geryon to a single biblical figure.

> 14 lo dosso e 'l petto e ambedue le coste  
> (his back and chest and both his flanks)
>
> 15 dipinti avea di nodi e di rotelle.  
> (he had patterned with knots and little circles.)

The intricate decoration extends the allegory from false appearances to entanglement. The knots can suggest the complications and traps through which deception operates, while the elaborate patterns make the monster visually fascinating.

Dante compares this ornamentation to richly worked textiles associated with Tartars and Turks and to the weaving of Arachne. In classical myth, Arachne challenged Minerva's skill and became a spider. These comparisons connect Geryon with extraordinary craftsmanship and woven complexity.

The point is not that fine textiles are themselves fraudulent. Rather, fraud can possess the attractions of art: skill, intricacy, and a surface that captures attention while distracting it from danger.

## 3. The Hidden Weapon at the Edge of the Abyss

> 25 Nel vano tutta sua coda guizzava,  
> (In the void his whole tail was darting about,)
>
> 26 torcendo in sù la venenosa forca  
> (twisting upward the venomous fork)
>
> 27 ch'a guisa di scorpion la punta armava.  
> (that armed its tip like a scorpion's.)

Geryon rests his head and torso on the bank, but leaves his tail suspended over the abyss. His posture gives the moral allegory a spatial form: the apparently trustworthy part approaches the observer, while the weapon remains behind it.

The scorpion-like sting completes the contrast with the righteous face. What begins as an invitation to trust ends in injury. Fraud's true meaning lies not in its welcome but in its consequences.

The surrounding comparisons make this impossible creature strangely recognizable. Geryon resembles a boat partly drawn onto land and a beaver positioned at the water's edge. The beaver comparison draws on medieval animal lore that associated its posture with catching prey. Such familiar images make the monster's unfamiliar body easier to imagine—and show how deception can conceal itself within apparently ordinary behavior.

## 4. The Usurers: Violence Against the Order of Work

> 46 Per li occhi fora scoppiava lor duolo;  
> (Their anguish was bursting out through their eyes;)
>
> 47 di qua, di là soccorrien con le mani  
> (this way and that they sought relief with their hands)
>
> 48 quando a' vapori, e quando al caldo suolo:  
> (now against the fiery vapors, now against the hot ground:)

Before descending, Dante visits the usurers, who sit on burning sand under falling fire. Their hands move continually, but their activity achieves nothing: they cannot escape either the heat beneath them or the flames above.

Their placement depends on an argument Virgil explained earlier, in Canto XI. Human productive activity follows nature, and nature derives from divine intelligence. Usury, in Dante's theological framework, seeks gain through lending at interest rather than through the proper productivity of nature and human work. It therefore violates an order ultimately established by God.

This is the medieval Christian rationale of the passage, not a neutral description of every form of modern banking. It also explains a potentially puzzling detail: these sinners remain in the circle of violence, not the circle of fraud.

> 49 non altrimenti fan di state i cani  
> (No differently do dogs behave in summer)
>
> 50 or col ceffo or col piè, quando son morsi  
> (now with their muzzle, now with their paw, when they are bitten)
>
> 51 o da pulci o da mosche o da tafani.  
> (by fleas or flies or horseflies.)

The comparison strips the usurers' gestures of dignity. Their movements resemble the irritated, automatic responses of animals tormented by insects.

It also emphasizes the futility of their punishment. They are intensely active but accomplish nothing. Their restless hands can be read as a bitter inversion of productive labor: movement continues, but useful work and genuine relief are impossible.

## 5. The Purses: Wealth Becomes Identity

> 55 che dal collo a ciascun pendea una tasca  
> (that from each one's neck hung a pouch)
>
> 56 ch'avea certo colore e certo segno,  
> (that had a particular color and a particular emblem,)
>
> 57 e quindi par che 'l loro occhio si pasca.  
> (and on this their eyes seem to feed.)

Dante cannot recognize the sinners by their faces. Instead, he identifies their families through the heraldic emblems on their purses.

This substitution is central to the episode. A face ordinarily establishes personal identity; here, money and family insignia take its place. The sinners have become legible through the wealth with which they identified themselves.

Their eyes appear to “feed” on the purses. The metaphor suggests an appetite that survives bodily death but cannot provide nourishment. Even amid torment, their attention remains attached to the signs of money and status.

The heraldry also implicates prominent families rather than treating usury as an anonymous social vice. Dante describes a blue lion on yellow, associated with the Gianfigliazzi, and a white goose on red, associated with the Ubriachi: both Florentine families.

> 64 E un che d'una scrofa azzurra e grossa  
> (And one who with a large blue sow)
>
> 65 segnato avea lo suo sacchetto bianco,  
> (had his little white bag marked,)

The blue sow on a white purse identifies the Paduan Scrovegni family. The speaker is generally identified as Reginaldo, or Rinaldo, degli Scrovegni.

His son Enrico commissioned the famous Scrovegni Chapel in Padua, decorated by Giotto. That historical connection places Dante's episode beside a major monument of religious patronage, family prestige, and concern for salvation. It should not, however, be simplified into certainty that the chapel was built solely to atone for the father's usury.

The infernal purse makes the family emblem an accusation rather than an honor.

## 6. Infernal Prophecy and Civic Rivalry

> 68 sappi che 'l mio vicin Vitalïano  
> (know that my neighbor Vitaliano)
>
> 69 sederà qui dal mio sinistro fianco.  
> (will sit here at my left side.)
>
> 70 Con questi Fiorentin son padoano:  
> (Among these Florentines I am a Paduan:)

The speaker predicts the arrival of another usurer, Vitaliano, traditionally identified as Vitaliano del Dente, though the identification is not certain.

Such predictions exploit the poem's double chronology. Dante places his journey in 1300 but writes about it later. Events belonging to the poet's subsequent historical knowledge can therefore appear as prophecies within the journey.

The speaker also reports that the Florentines keep calling for a “supreme knight” whose purse will bear three goats. This is generally understood as Gianni Buiamonte de' Becchi, a Florentine financier. The grand title becomes sarcastic: worldly distinction will not prevent his admission to this degrading company.

Civic identity persists even in damnation. The Paduan distinguishes himself from the Florentines, and the sinners torment one another with anticipated arrivals. Hell preserves social rivalry while removing every possibility of reconciliation. The speaker's final gesture—sticking out his tongue—reduces heraldic pride and aristocratic pretension to contemptuous bodily display.

## 7. Virgil's Protection: Reason as Practical Care

> 83 monta dinanzi, ch'i' voglio esser mezzo,  
> (mount in front, for I wish to be between,)
>
> 84 sì che la coda non possa far male».  
> (so that the tail cannot do harm.”)

Virgil places himself between Dante and Geryon's tail. The arrangement turns the guide's protective role into a physical fact.

Virgil is often understood as representing human reason, but his function here is not merely intellectual. He recognizes danger, chooses a precaution, and takes responsibility for the frightened traveler. Reason acts through judgment and care.

Dante's fear is bodily: he trembles like someone approaching a fit of malarial fever. Shame before a respected master helps him obey, but courage does not mean that his terror disappears.

> 95 ad altro forse, tosto ch'i' montai  
> (in another peril, as soon as I mounted)
>
> 96 con le braccia m'avvinse e mi sostenne;  
> (with his arms he encircled me and supported me;)

Dante wants to ask Virgil to embrace him, but fear prevents the words from emerging. Virgil supplies the needed support without waiting for the request.

The scene sharply contrasts two kinds of relationship. Geryon offers a trustworthy appearance with danger concealed behind it; Virgil makes trust real through protective action. At the entrance to the realm of deception, the poem shows what genuine fidelity looks like.

## 8. Riding Fraud Without Surrendering to It

> 97 e disse: «Gerïon, moviti omai:  
> (and said: “Geryon, move now:)
>
> 98 le rote larghe, e lo scender sia poco;  
> (let the turns be wide, and the descent be slight;)
>
> 99 pensa la nova soma che tu hai».  
> (consider the unusual burden that you bear.”)

Virgil gives specific instructions: wide circles and a gradual descent. The journey through moral danger requires controlled movement rather than a reckless plunge.

The unusual burden is Dante's living body. Unlike the shades who populate Hell, he has physical weight and vulnerability. His exceptional condition repeatedly forces the infernal world to accommodate him.

There is an apparent paradox in using the embodiment of fraud as transport. Yet riding Geryon is not the same as accepting his values. Within the divinely permitted journey, the monster becomes an instrument by which Dante reaches the knowledge he must acquire. Evil is made to serve an end it does not define.

> 100 Come la navicella esce di loco  
> (As a little boat moves out from its place)
>
> 101 in dietro in dietro, sì quindi si tolse;  
> (backward, backward, so he withdrew from there;)

Geryon departs like a small vessel backing away from its berth. The simile makes a supernatural event mechanically intelligible.

Once clear of the bank, he turns and moves through the air with his tail and paws. Dante does not describe the beating of wings; the creature seems to swim. Familiar kinds of motion—boating, swimming, turning—combine into an unfamiliar and deeply unsettling voyage.

## 9. Phaethon and Icarus: The Fear of a Fatal Flight

> 106 Maggior paura non credo che fosse  
> (Greater fear I do not believe there was)
>
> 107 quando Fetonte abbandonò li freni,  
> (when Phaethon let go of the reins,)
>
> 108 per che 'l ciel, come pare ancor, si cosse;  
> (because of which the sky, as still appears, was scorched;)

Phaethon attempted to drive the chariot of the sun but could not control its horses. The resulting catastrophe threatened the world. Dante alludes to the ancient association between this disastrous journey and the Milky Way, imagined as a scorched trace across the heavens.

The comparison measures the intensity of Dante's fear: he feels as though he has entered a vehicle whose movement exceeds human control. The apparent stability of the world has vanished beneath him.

> 109 né quando Icaro misero le reni  
> (nor when wretched Icarus his back)
>
> 110 sentì spennar per la scaldata cera,  
> (felt losing its feathers because of the heated wax,)
>
> 111 gridando il padre a lui «Mala via tieni!»,  
> (his father crying to him, “You are taking the wrong course!”,)

Icarus escaped confinement on wings made by his father, Daedalus. Flying too near the sun, he melted their wax and fell.

Both myths concern disastrous aerial journeys, but they also establish a contrast. Phaethon cannot govern his borrowed power; Icarus disregards paternal instruction. Dante, by contrast, survives through obedience to a guide who understands his limitations.

The comparison therefore raises a question about Dante's own extraordinary undertaking: is this journey another presumptuous attempt to exceed human boundaries? The poem's answer lies in its authorization and guidance, not in Dante's independent strength.

Meanwhile, his account remains intensely sensory. With ordinary landmarks gone, he initially knows he is moving only because air strikes his face and blows from below. Later, the approaching fires and cries make the spiral descent visible. The moral journey is experienced through a vulnerable body, not from the security of an abstract viewpoint.

## 10. The Falcon and the Arrow: A Reluctant Instrument Released

> 131 per cento rote, e da lunge si pone  
> (through a hundred turns, and settles far away)
>
> 132 dal suo maestro, disdegnoso e fello;  
> (from its master, disdainful and sullen;)

Near the end of the descent, Dante compares Geryon to a falcon that has remained aloft for a long time and returns without either seeing prey or receiving the falconer's lure.

Falconry was an elite medieval practice, and the comparison gives Geryon a recognizable temperament. He behaves like a trained creature that complies without affection. His submission is not willing fellowship.

The falcon's distance from its master helps clarify the limits of Virgil's command. Geryon has performed a service, but he has not become benevolent. His obedient action does not transform his nature.

> 136 si dileguò come da corda cocca.  
> (he vanished like an arrow from a bowstring.)

After setting the travelers down at the foot of the sheer cliff, Geryon disappears with abrupt speed.

The ending contrasts with the slow, circling descent. While carrying Dante, the monster has moved under restraint; once the burden is removed, he shoots away. The arrow image leaves a final impression of concentrated force and potential danger.

The travelers have reached the eighth circle, but the disappearance of fraud's emblem does not mean that fraud has been left behind. They have arrived in its domain.

## Epilogue — Learning to See Beyond the Surface

Canto XVII teaches that evil cannot be judged by appearance alone. Geryon's righteous face conceals a poisonous tail; heraldic purses turn family prestige into evidence of spiritual degradation; honorific titles become objects of infernal ridicule.

Against these false appearances, the canto sets Virgil's practical fidelity. Genuine guidance protects rather than exploits vulnerability.

The descent is therefore both geographical and educational. Dante moves from violence toward fraud while learning how unreliable surfaces can be—and how much clear judgment depends on recognizing the difference between apparent goodness and trustworthy action.
