The deep sleep in my head was broken by
a heavy thunderclap, so that I started
like someone forcibly awakened;
and I turned my rested eyes around,
having risen upright, and gazed intently
to learn what place I was in.
The truth is that I found myself upon the brink
of the sorrowful valley of the abyss
that gathers the thunder of endless woes.
Dark and deep it was, and misty,
so much that, however deeply I pressed my gaze,
I could distinguish nothing there.
“Now let us descend down here into the blind world,”
began the poet, deathly pale.
“I shall be first, and you shall be second.”
And I, who had noticed his color,
said: “How shall I come, if you are afraid,
you who are always my comfort when I doubt?”
And he to me: “The anguish of the people
who are down here paints upon my face
that pity which you take for fear.
Let us go, for the long road urges us onward.”
So he set out, and so he had me enter
the first circle that girds the abyss.
Here, as far as I could tell by listening,
there was no weeping except sighs
that made the eternal air tremble;
this arose from grief without torments,
felt by the crowds, which were many and great,
of infants and of women and of men.
The good master said to me: “Do you not ask
what spirits these are that you see?
Now I want you to know, before you go farther,
that they did not sin; and if they have merits,
it is not enough, because they did not receive baptism,
which is the gate of the faith that you believe;
and if they lived before Christianity,
they did not worship God as they ought:
and of such as these am I myself.
For such failings, and for no other wrong,
we are lost, and afflicted only in this,
that without hope we live in longing.”
Great grief seized my heart when I heard him,
because people of great worth
I knew to be suspended in that limbo.
“Tell me, my master, tell me, my lord,”
I began, wishing to be certain
of that faith which conquers every error:
“did anyone ever leave here, through his own merit
or another's, who afterward was blessed?”
And he, who understood my veiled words,
replied: “I was new to this state
when I saw a mighty one come here,
crowned with a sign of victory.
He drew from here the shade of our first parent,
of Abel his son, and that of Noah,
of Moses, the lawgiver and obedient servant;
Abraham the patriarch and David the king,
Israel with his father and his children
and with Rachel, for whom he did so much,
and many others, and made them blessed.
And I want you to know that, before them,
no human spirits had been saved.”
We did not stop walking as he spoke,
but kept passing through the forest,
the forest, I mean, of thickly crowded spirits.
Our journey had not yet been long
since my sleep, when I saw a fire
that overcame a hemisphere of darkness.
We were still a little way from it,
but not so far that I could not partly discern
that honorable people occupied that place.
“O you who honor knowledge and art,
who are these who have such great honor,
which sets them apart from the condition of the others?”
And he to me: “The honored reputation
that resounds of them above in your living world,
wins favor in heaven that so exalts them.”
Meanwhile a voice was heard by me:
“Honor the most exalted poet;
his shade returns, which had departed.”
After the voice had ceased and fallen silent,
I saw four great shades coming toward us:
their looks were neither sad nor glad.
The good master began to say:
“Look at the one with that sword in his hand,
who comes before the three as their lord:
that is Homer, sovereign poet;
the other who comes is Horace the satirist;
Ovid is the third, and the last is Lucan.
Because each shares with me
the title that the single voice proclaimed,
they do me honor, and in this they do well.”
Thus I saw gathered the noble school
of that lord of the most exalted song
who soars above the others like an eagle.
After they had talked together for a while,
they turned to me with a gesture of greeting,
and my master smiled at this;
and they did me still far greater honor,
for they made me one of their company,
so that I was sixth among such wisdom.
Thus we went as far as the light,
speaking of things that it is fitting to leave unspoken,
just as it was fitting to speak of them where I was.
We came to the foot of a noble castle,
encircled seven times by high walls,
defended all around by a beautiful little stream.
This we crossed as though it were firm ground;
through seven gates I entered with these sages:
we came to a meadow of fresh greenery.
People were there with slow and grave eyes,
with great authority in their bearing:
they spoke seldom, with gentle voices.
Thus we drew aside to one corner,
into a place open, bright, and high,
so that all of them could be seen.
There, straight ahead, upon the green enamel,
the great spirits were shown to me,
and at having seen them I exult within myself.
I saw Electra with many companions,
among whom I recognized Hector and Aeneas,
Caesar armed, with falcon-like eyes.
I saw Camilla and Penthesilea;
on the other side I saw King Latinus,
who sat with Lavinia his daughter.
I saw that Brutus who drove out Tarquin,
Lucretia, Julia, Marcia, and Cornelia;
and alone, apart, I saw Saladin.
When I raised my eyes a little higher,
I saw the master of those who know
sitting among the philosophical family.
All gaze at him, all do him honor:
there I saw Socrates and Plato,
who stand closer to him than the others;
Democritus, who attributes the world to chance,
Diogenes, Anaxagoras, and Thales,
Empedocles, Heraclitus, and Zeno;
and I saw the good collector of qualities,
Dioscorides, I mean; and I saw Orpheus,
Cicero and Linus and Seneca the moralist;
Euclid the geometer and Ptolemy,
Hippocrates, Avicenna, and Galen,
Averroes, who made the great commentary.
I cannot portray them all in full,
because my long theme drives me on so,
that often my words fall short of the facts.
The company of six dwindles to two:
by another way my wise guide leads me,
out of the quiet air, into the air that trembles.
And I come to a place where nothing shines.
