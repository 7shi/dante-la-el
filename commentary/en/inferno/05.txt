Thus I descended from the first circle
down into the second, which encircles less space
and so much greater pain, which goads to wailing.
There stands Minos horribly, and growls:
he examines the sins at the entrance;
he judges and sends according to how he coils himself.
I mean that when the ill-fated soul
comes before him, it confesses all;
and that discerner of sins
sees what place in Hell is suited to it;
he girds himself with his tail as many times
as the degrees he wills that it be sent down.
Always before him many of them stand:
each goes in turn to judgment,
they speak and hear and then are hurled below.
“O you who come to this sorrowful dwelling,”
Minos said to me when he saw me,
ceasing the exercise of so great an office,
“take heed how you enter and whom you trust;
do not let the breadth of the entrance deceive you!”
And my guide to him: “Why do you keep shouting?
Do not obstruct his destined journey:
so it is willed there where there is power to do
whatever is willed, and ask no more.”
Now the sorrowful sounds begin
to make themselves heard by me; now I have come
where much weeping strikes upon me.
I came into a place mute of all light,
which bellows as the sea does in a tempest,
when it is battered by opposing winds.
The infernal storm, which never rests,
drives the spirits with its violent sweep;
turning and striking them, it torments them.
When they come before the ruin,
there are the shrieks, the grieving, the lamentation;
there they blaspheme the divine power.
I understood that to such torment
the carnal sinners are condemned,
who subject reason to appetite.
And as their wings carry the starlings
in the cold season, in a broad and crowded flock,
so that blast the wicked spirits
here, there, down, up drives them;
no hope ever comforts them,
not of rest, nor even of lesser pain.
And as the cranes go singing their lays,
making a long line of themselves in the air,
so I saw approaching, uttering cries,
shades borne by that same turmoil;
so I said: “Master, who are those
people whom the black air so chastises?”
“The first of those of whom news
you wish to know,” he said to me then,
“was empress of many tongues.
She was so abandoned to the vice of lust,
that she made desire lawful in her law,
to remove the disgrace into which she had fallen.
She is Semiramis, of whom we read
that she succeeded Ninus and was his wife:
she held the land that the Sultan rules.
The next is she who killed herself for love,
and broke faith with the ashes of Sychaeus;
then comes lustful Cleopatra.
You see Helen, for whose sake so grievous
a time passed, and you see the great Achilles,
who fought with love at the last.
You see Paris, Tristan”; and more than a thousand
shades he pointed out and named to me,
whom love had parted from our life.
After I had heard my teacher
name the ancient ladies and the knights,
pity came upon me, and I was almost bewildered.
I began: “Poet, gladly
would I speak to those two who go together,
and seem to be so light upon the wind.”
And he to me: “You will see when they are
nearer to us; and then entreat them
by that love which drives them, and they will come.”
As soon as the wind bends them toward us,
I raised my voice: “O troubled souls,
come speak to us, if no other forbids it!”
As doves called by desire
with wings raised and steady to their sweet nest
come through the air, borne by their will;
so they came out of the band where Dido is,
coming toward us through the malignant air,
so strong was the affectionate cry.
“O living creature, gracious and kind,
who go visiting through the dark air
us who stained the world blood-red,
if the King of the universe were our friend,
we would pray to him for your peace,
since you have pity on our cruel suffering.
Of whatever you please to hear and speak,
we will hear and speak with you,
while the wind, as now it does, is silent for us.
The town where I was born lies
upon the shore where the Po descends
to find peace with its followers.
Love, which swiftly kindles in the noble heart,
seized this man through the beautiful body
that was taken from me; and the manner still wounds me.
Love, which excuses no one loved from loving,
seized me so strongly with this man's beauty,
that, as you see, it still does not leave me.
Love led us to one death.
Caina awaits him who quenched our life.”
These words were borne to us from them.
When I heard those afflicted souls,
I bowed my face, and held it lowered so long,
until the poet said to me: “What are you thinking?”
When I answered, I began: “Alas,
how many sweet thoughts, how much desire
led these two to the sorrowful pass!”
Then I turned to them and spoke,
and began: “Francesca, your torments
make me weep with grief and pity.
But tell me: in the time of sweet sighs,
by what sign and how did love grant
that you should know your uncertain desires?”
And she to me: “There is no greater sorrow
than remembering the happy time
in misery; and this your teacher knows.
But if to know the first root
of our love you have so great a longing,
I will speak as one who weeps and speaks.
We were reading one day for pleasure
of Lancelot, how love constrained him;
we were alone and without any apprehension.
Many times our eyes were driven
by that reading, and our faces drained of color;
but one passage alone was what overcame us.
When we read of the desired smile
being kissed by so great a lover,
this one, who never shall be parted from me,
kissed my mouth, all trembling.
Galeotto was the book and he who wrote it:
that day we read no further in it.”
While the one spirit said this,
the other wept; so that from pity
I fainted as though I were dying.
And I fell as a dead body falls.
