# Wealth, Fortune, and Anger: The Allegory of *Inferno*, Canto VII

Canto VII takes Dante and Virgil through two regions of Hell: the fourth circle, where avarice and prodigality are punished, and the fifth, where anger erupts above the marsh of Styx or lies buried beneath it. Between these scenes, Virgil explains the nature of Fortune.

The canto's governing contrast is between divine order and human disorder. Wealth changes hands according to a providential arrangement that people cannot comprehend; meanwhile, they ruin themselves by hoarding it, wasting it, or resenting their circumstances.

## 1. Plutus: Wealth Given a Monstrous Voice

> 1 «Pape Satàn, pape Satàn aleppe!»,  
> (“Pape Satàn, pape Satàn aleppe!”,)
>
> 2 cominciò Pluto con la voce chioccia;  
> (Plutus began with his harsh, clucking voice;)

The opening sounds threatening before it means anything intelligible. Although many interpretations have been proposed, the precise meaning of Plutus's cry remains uncertain. “Satàn” is recognizable, but the whole utterance should not be treated as a securely deciphered sentence.

Plutus is usually identified with the classical god of wealth, though his Italian name also recalls Pluto, ruler of the underworld. Dante transforms this pagan figure into a demonic guardian of the circle devoted to the misuse of possessions.

His “clucking” or rasping voice strips him of dignity. Throughout the canto, corrupted desire produces corrupted speech: Plutus's obscure cry anticipates the damned's repetitive accusations and, eventually, the submerged souls' broken gurgling. Language becomes less an instrument of understanding than a symptom of spiritual disorder.

## 2. Heaven's Authority and the Collapse of Infernal Pride

> 10 Non è sanza cagion l'andare al cupo:  
> (Not without cause is this journey into the depths:)
>
> 11 vuolsi ne l'alto, là dove Michele  
> (it is willed on high, there where Michael)
>
> 12 fé la vendetta del superbo strupo».  
> (took vengeance for the proud rebellion”.)

Virgil answers Plutus by appealing to divine authorization. Dante's descent is not an intrusion that a local guardian can prevent: it forms part of a purpose established in Heaven.

Michael recalls the archangel's victory over the rebellious heavenly powers, especially the battle described in Revelation 12. The allusion places Plutus's resistance within the larger pattern of Satanic rebellion. Infernal power can threaten and rage, but it cannot overturn God's decree.

Virgil represents reason, yet his authority here does not rest on reason alone. He succeeds because he serves a divinely sanctioned journey. Dante's reason must operate within the larger assistance of grace.

The following comparison makes Plutus collapse like inflated sails when their mast breaks. His apparent force proves dependent and fragile. What looked formidable was, in part, mere inflation: the outward swelling of pride.

## 3. The Fourth Circle: Hoarding and Squandering Meet Head-On

> 27 voltando pesi per forza di poppa.  
> (rolling weights by force of the chest.)
>
> 28 Percotëansi 'ncontro; e poscia pur lì  
> (They struck against one another; and then, right there,)
>
> 29 si rivolgea ciascun, voltando a retro,  
> (each turned around, rolling backward,)
>
> 30 gridando: «Perché tieni?» e «Perché burli?».  
> (crying: “Why do you hoard?” and “Why do you squander?”.)

Two opposing groups push enormous weights around the circle. They collide, accuse one another, reverse direction, and repeat the same exhausting movement.

Their punishment is a *contrapasso*: an embodied revelation of their sin. The avaricious clung excessively to possessions; the prodigal disposed of them without proper judgment. Now both groups are bound to material burdens that demand immense effort but yield no benefit.

Their accusations remain narrowly partisan. Each recognizes the opposite fault without confronting its own. Even in punishment, they understand morality as an indictment of somebody else.

Dante introduces their movement through a comparison with the waves at Charybdis, the dangerous whirlpool of classical geography and myth. The image makes their collision resemble a violent natural cycle. Yet this is a cycle without development: endless activity replaces meaningful action.

## 4. Moral Blindness and the Wealth of the Church

> 40 Ed elli a me: «Tutti quanti fuor guerci  
> (And he to me: “All of them were squint-eyed)
>
> 41 sì de la mente in la vita primaia,  
> (so greatly in their minds in the first life,)
>
> 42 che con misura nullo spendio ferci.  
> (that none made any expenditure there with measure.)

Virgil identifies the common defect beneath the apparently opposite sins: distorted moral vision. Their minds were “squint-eyed” because they could not judge possessions according to their proper value.

The crucial word is “measure.” Dante draws on an ethical tradition, developed by Aristotle and absorbed into medieval Christian thought, in which virtue directs action between contrary excesses. In the use of wealth, generosity differs both from withholding what should be given and from wasting what should be preserved.

This does not make moderation a merely numerical compromise. Proper expenditure depends on purposes, circumstances, and responsibilities. Both groups failed because material goods displaced sound judgment.

> 46 Questi fuor cherci, che non han coperchio  
> (These were clerics, who have no covering)
>
> 47 piloso al capo, e papi e cardinali,  
> (of hair upon the head, and popes and cardinals,)
>
> 48 in cui usa avarizia il suo soperchio».  
> (in whom avarice practices its excess”.)

The bare crowns identify tonsured churchmen among the avaricious. Dante's criticism reaches the highest ecclesiastical offices: popes and cardinals appear beside other clergy.

The attack is especially severe because spiritual authority should teach the proper subordination of temporal goods. Those entrusted with directing others toward eternal life have instead become exemplary servants of wealth.

Dante is not saying that every cleric belongs here, nor that all the sinners in this circle were clerics. His point is the scandalous prominence of avarice within an institution whose vocation should oppose it.

## 5. Lost Identity and the Signs of Resurrection

> 53 la sconoscente vita che i fé sozzi,  
> (the undiscerning life that made them filthy,)
>
> 54 ad ogne conoscenza or li fa bruni.  
> (now makes them dark to every recognition.)

Dante expects to recognize some of these sinners, but Virgil explains why he cannot. Their failure to recognize the true worth of things has become an inability to be recognized themselves.

The verbal relationship between *sconoscente* and *conoscenza* joins moral ignorance to personal obscurity. Lives governed by possession or expenditure have become spiritually indistinct.

This is a striking departure from the memorable individual encounters elsewhere in the poem. Here Dante presents no extended personal confession. The crowd's anonymity helps express the flattening power of its obsession.

> 56 questi resurgeranno del sepulcro  
> (these will rise again from the tomb)
>
> 57 col pugno chiuso, e questi coi crin mozzi.  
> (with the fist closed, and these with their hair shorn.)

At the resurrection of the body, the two groups will bear different visible signs. The closed fist expresses the avaricious refusal to release possessions. The shorn hair marks the prodigal's condition of depletion or waste.

These bodily emblems show that the final judgment will not erase the truth of earthly choices. The resurrected body will disclose what the will has made of itself.

The passage also extends the punishment beyond the present scene. Their conflict is not a temporary corrective exercise: in Dante's Hell, the underlying moral fixation has become permanent.

## 6. All the World's Gold Cannot Buy Rest

> 64 ché tutto l'oro ch'è sotto la luna  
> (for all the gold that is beneath the moon)
>
> 65 e che già fu, di quest' anime stanche  
> (and that ever existed, of these weary souls)
>
> 66 non poterebbe farne posare una».  
> (could not give rest to a single one”.)

Virgil reduces the supposed power of wealth to nothing. Even all the gold that exists, together with all that has existed, could not purchase a moment of relief for one soul.

“Beneath the moon” draws on the medieval distinction between the changeable earthly realm and the heavens above it. Gold belongs to the temporal world. It cannot determine the soul's eternal condition.

The irony is exact: people exhausted themselves pursuing, retaining, or disposing of wealth, and now wealth cannot relieve their exhaustion. Their error was not simply wanting material things, but investing those things with a power they do not possess.

This prepares Dante's question about Fortune. If worldly goods are so unstable, what governs their distribution?

## 7. Fortune as a Minister of Providence

> 77 Similemente a li splendor mondani  
> (Similarly, for worldly splendors)
>
> 78 ordinò general ministra e duce  
> (he appointed a general minister and guide)
>
> 79 che permutasse a tempo li ben vani  
> (who would transfer, in due time, the empty goods)
>
> 80 di gente in gente e d'uno in altro sangue,  
> (from people to people and from one lineage to another,)
>
> 81 oltre la difension d'i senni umani;  
> (beyond the resistance of human understanding;)

The subject of “appointed” is God. Just as God established the ordering powers of the heavens, he appointed Fortune to administer the changing distribution of earthly advantages.

Dante thus reinterprets the classical goddess Fortuna within a Christian universe. Fortune is not a rival to providence or an independent source of chaos. She is its minister.

Her domain includes more than private money. The movement of goods between peoples and bloodlines encompasses political dominance, social prestige, and the rise and fall of dynasties. Human beings try to preserve their advantages, but their strategies cannot permanently arrest this circulation.

Calling these goods “empty” does not mean that they have no practical use. They are empty of the lasting fulfillment people demand from them. Their instability should teach detachment.

This account belongs to a tradition strongly associated with Boethius's *Consolation of Philosophy*, where Fortune's changes expose the insecurity of worldly happiness. Dante gives that instability a definite administrative role within divine order.

## 8. Why People Curse Fortune

> 91 Quest' è colei ch'è tanto posta in croce  
> (This is she who is so much crucified)
>
> 92 pur da color che le dovrien dar lode,  
> (even by those who ought to give her praise,)
>
> 93 dandole biasmo a torto e mala voce;  
> (giving her blame unjustly and an evil reputation;)
>
> 94 ma ella s'è beata e ciò non ode:  
> (but she is blessed and does not hear this:)

People condemn Fortune because they evaluate the universe through their immediate gains and losses. What deprives them of wealth appears unjust simply because it hurts.

Virgil reverses that judgment. Fortune deserves praise for carrying out her appointed function, not blame for failing to protect private interests. Her blessedness places her beyond the anger directed against her.

The violent metaphor of being “crucified” exposes the excess of human complaint. People turn an agent of divine order into the supposed villain of their disappointments.

This explanation does not remove human responsibility. Fortune governs external circumstances; individuals remain answerable for their responses. Losing wealth does not compel anger, just as possessing it does not compel avarice.

The discourse therefore links the two circles. A disordered attachment to worldly goods can readily become rage when those goods change hands.

## 9. Styx: Anger Turned into Bodily Violence

> 112 Queste si percotean non pur con mano,  
> (These struck one another not only with the hand,)
>
> 113 ma con la testa e col petto e coi piedi,  
> (but with the head and with the chest and with the feet,)
>
> 114 troncandosi co' denti a brano a brano.  
> (tearing one another with their teeth, piece by piece.)

Dante and Virgil descend beside a dark stream into the marsh of Styx, where the wrathful attack one another.

Styx comes from classical accounts of the underworld, where it is associated with dread and the binding oaths of the gods. Dante makes it a stagnant, filthy environment suited to the spiritual condition of its inhabitants.

The punishment externalizes wrath's destructive movement. Every part of the body becomes a weapon; even the mouth, which should make rational communication possible, serves as an instrument of tearing.

Unlike the measured judgment described in Virgil's account of Fortune, these souls respond through indiscriminate assault. They have surrendered self-government to anger.

The moral issue is not that every experience of anger is sinful. Christian ethics can recognize anger directed rightly against injustice. These souls are those whom anger “overcame”: passion ceased to serve judgment and became their master.

## 10. Beneath the Mud: Sullenness and Refused Joy

> 121 Fitti nel limo dicon: “Tristi fummo  
> (Embedded in the mud, they say: “Sullen were we)
>
> 122 ne l'aere dolce che dal sol s'allegra,  
> (in the sweet air that is gladdened by the sun,)
>
> 123 portando dentro accidïoso fummo:  
> (carrying within a smoke of acedia:)
>
> 124 or ci attristiam ne la belletta negra”.  
> (now we grieve in the black mire”.)

Other souls lie beneath the surface. Their sighs make the water bubble, and their words emerge only as obstructed sounds.

These submerged sinners are commonly understood as the sullen or those consumed by inward, suppressed anger. Dante's reference to *acedia* also connects them with spiritual torpor: a disabling refusal of the good, rather than simply physical laziness. The precise relationship between their sullenness, anger, and acedia has long invited interpretation.

The contrast with the wrathful above is powerful. One group projects its disorder outward through violence; the other encloses it inward as suffocating resentment. Both inhabit the same marsh.

Their recollection of the sunlit world sharpens the punishment. They lived in air capable of delight, but carried their own darkening “smoke” within. Now that inward atmosphere has become their external environment.

This is a moral and theological image, not a clinical description of depression. Its target is a culpable disposition toward life and goodness.

Their inability to speak whole words completes the canto's deterioration of language. Plutus began with an obscure outcry; the hoarders and squanderers exchanged mechanical accusations; the buried souls cannot even articulate their lament clearly. The canto ends with the travelers reaching a tower, leaving the marsh's conflict unresolved.

## Epilogue — The Disorder of Possession, the Order of Providence

Canto VII shows what happens when temporary goods become ultimate concerns. Hoarding and waste turn into futile labor; disappointed attachment becomes violence or inward bitterness; speech deteriorates into noise.

Against this disorder stands Fortune, calmly performing her place within providence. The canto's lesson is not that wealth or feeling must disappear, but that both require right judgment. Possessions must remain instruments rather than masters, and changing circumstances must not determine the soul's allegiance to the good.
