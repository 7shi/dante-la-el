Of a new punishment I must make verses
and give matter to the twentieth canto
of the first canticle, which is of the submerged.
I was already wholly prepared
to gaze into the uncovered depths,
which were bathed in anguished tears;
and I saw people through the circular valley
coming, silent and weeping, at the pace
that the litany processions keep in this world.
When my gaze descended lower upon them,
wondrously twisted appeared to be
each one between the chin and the beginning of the chest,
for the face was turned toward the loins,
and they had to move backward,
because seeing ahead was taken from them.
Perhaps once through the force of palsy
someone has been twisted so completely;
but I have not seen it, nor do I believe it happens.
So may God let you, reader, gather fruit
from your reading, now think for yourself
how I could keep my face dry,
when our human image, close at hand,
I saw so twisted that the weeping of the eyes
bathed the buttocks along the cleft.
Indeed I wept, leaning against one of the crags
of the hard rock, so that my guide
said to me: “Are you too among the other fools?
Here pity lives when it is wholly dead;
who is more wicked than the one
who brings compassion to the divine judgment?
Lift your head, lift it, and see him for whom
the earth opened before the eyes of the Thebans;
at which they all cried: ‘Where are you rushing,
Amphiaraus? Why do you leave the battle?’.
And he did not cease plunging downward
until he reached Minos, who seizes everyone.
Look how he has made a chest of his shoulders;
because he wished to see too far ahead,
he looks behind and makes his way backward.
See Tiresias, who changed his appearance
when from male he became female,
changing every one of his bodily members;
and afterward he had first to strike again
the two entwined serpents with his rod,
before he regained his male plumage.
Aruns is the one whose back is against his belly,
who in the mountains of Luni, where tills
the man of Carrara who dwells below,
had among the white marbles the cave
as his dwelling; from there, to observe the stars
and the sea, his view was not cut off.
And she who covers her breasts,
which you do not see, with her loosened tresses,
and has all her hairy skin on the other side,
was Manto, who wandered through many lands;
afterward she settled where I was born;
therefore I wish you to listen to me a little.
After her father departed this life
and the city of Bacchus became enslaved,
she wandered through the world a long time.
Up in beautiful Italy lies a lake,
at the foot of the Alps that shut in Germany
above Tyrol, which is named Benaco.
Through a thousand springs, I believe, and more, it is watered
between Garda and Val Camonica and Pennino
by the water that gathers in the said lake.
There is a place in the middle where the shepherd of Trent
and the one of Brescia and the Veronese
could give their blessing, if they went that way.
Peschiera stands, a fair and strong fortress
to confront the Brescians and Bergamasques,
where the surrounding shore slopes lowest.
There must fall away all
that cannot remain in Benaco's lap,
and it becomes a river through green pastures below.
As soon as the water begins to run,
it is called no longer Benaco, but Mincio
as far as Governolo, where it falls into the Po.
It has not run far when it finds a lowland,
in which it spreads and makes a marsh;
and in summer it is sometimes foul.
Passing that way, the cruel virgin
saw land in the middle of the marsh,
untilled and bare of inhabitants.
There, to flee all human fellowship,
she remained with her servants to practice her arts,
and lived, and there left her empty body.
Then the people who were scattered round about
gathered in that place, which was strong
because of the marsh it had on every side.
They built the city over those dead bones;
and after her who first chose the place,
they called it Mantua, without any further casting of lots.
Once its people were more numerous within,
before the foolishness of Casalodi
received deception from Pinamonte.
Therefore I warn you that, if you ever hear
my native city's origin told otherwise,
no falsehood should defraud the truth.”
And I: “Master, your accounts
are so certain to me and so command my faith,
that others would be spent coals to me.
But tell me, among the people passing,
if you see anyone worthy of note;
for to that alone my mind returns.”
Then he said to me: “That one who from his cheek
stretches his beard over his dusky shoulders,
was—when Greece was emptied of males,
so that scarcely enough remained for the cradles—
an augur, and with Calchas gave the signal
at Aulis to cut the first cable.
Eurypylus was his name, and thus sings of him
my lofty tragedy in a certain passage:
you know it well, you who know it all.
That other one who is so slight in the flanks,
was Michael Scot, who truly
knew the game of magical frauds.
See Guido Bonatti; see Asdente,
who to have attended to leather and thread
would now wish, but he repents too late.
See the wretched women who abandoned the needle,
the shuttle and the spindle, and made themselves diviners;
they made enchantments with herbs and with an image.
But come now, for already it occupies the boundary
of both hemispheres and touches the wave
below Seville, Cain and the thorns;
and already last night the moon was full:
you should well remember it, for it did not harm you
at a certain time through the deep wood.”
Thus he spoke to me, and meanwhile we walked.
