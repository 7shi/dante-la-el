# Inferno XXXII — The Frozen Heart of Treachery

In Canto XXXII, Dante and Virgil reach the frozen lake of Cocytus, in the ninth and deepest circle of Hell. Here treachery receives its punishment: the deliberate betrayal of people bound to the sinner by kinship, citizenship, hospitality, or special obligation.

This canto moves through **Caina**, where traitors to their relatives are punished, and **Antenora**, where the condemned betrayed their political communities. Its governing paradox is that the deepest evil is not fiery passion but frozen relationship: intelligence and will severed from love.

## 1. Harsh Rhymes for the Bottom of the Universe

> 1 S'ïo avessi le rime aspre e chiocce,  
> (If I had harsh and rasping rhymes,)
>
> 2 come si converrebbe al tristo buco  
> (as would be fitting for the dismal hole)
>
> 3 sovra 'l qual pontan tutte l'altre rocce,  
> (upon which all the other rocks press,)

Dante begins by questioning whether his poetry can adequately represent what he has reached. The imagined harshness of his rhymes would match the harshness of the place: sound itself must become an instrument of moral description.

The hole is also a structural foundation. All the other rocks of Hell bear down upon it. In Dante's moral architecture, treachery lies beneath other sins because it corrupts the trust that makes human relationships possible. Fraud already misuses the distinctively human power of reason; betrayal adds the destruction of a special bond.

> 7 ché non è impresa da pigliare a gabbo  
> (for it is no undertaking to be taken lightly)
>
> 8 discriver fondo a tutto l'universo,  
> (to describe the bottom of the whole universe,)
>
> 9 né da lingua che chiami mamma o babbo.  
> (nor one for a tongue that says mamma or babbo.)

In the medieval, Earth-centered cosmos, the center of the Earth is also the lowest point toward which material weight tends. Dante's geographical descent is therefore simultaneously a descent through degrees of moral corruption.

The language of infancy is inadequate to this undertaking. Yet the words for mother and father also anticipate Caina, where the bonds first learned within a family have been violated. The canto's difficulty is not merely that its subject is grotesque: it concerns the destruction of fundamental human attachments.

## 2. The Muses and the Lost Possibility of Community

> 10 Ma quelle donne aiutino il mio verso  
> (But may those ladies help my verse)
>
> 11 ch'aiutaro Anfïone a chiuder Tebe,  
> (who helped Amphion to enclose Thebes,)
>
> 12 sì che dal fatto il dir non sia diverso.  
> (so that the telling may not differ from the fact.)

The ladies are the Muses. According to classical mythology, Amphion's music moved stones into place to build the walls of Thebes.

The reference gives poetry a constructive power: harmonious art can gather scattered matter into a city. That power contrasts with the treachery encountered below, which dissolves households and political communities. Dante asks for language capable of representing a world where the human bonds that sustain civilization have collapsed.

Thebes also carries darker associations, including civil conflict and violence between brothers. Its appearance anticipates both the hostile brothers in Caina and the Theban atrocity recalled near the canto's end.

> 15 mei foste state qui pecore o zebe!  
> (better had you been sheep or goats here!)

Dante wishes that these souls had lived as animals rather than as human beings capable of such evil. The point is not that animals are morally guilty. Rather, rational creatures have abused capacities unavailable to beasts.

Animal comparisons subsequently crowd the canto. The damned resemble frogs, storks, fighting goats, and barking dogs. Their bodily degradation makes visible the betrayal of their human vocation.

## 3. Cocytus: Why the Deepest Hell Is Ice

> 23 e sotto i piedi un lago che per gelo  
> (and beneath my feet a lake that through freezing)
>
> 24 avea di vetro e non d'acqua sembiante.  
> (had the appearance of glass and not of water.)

The lake initially looks like glass: transparent, rigid, and lifeless. Dante intensifies its unnatural solidity by comparing it with frozen northern rivers and imagining mountains falling upon it without cracking its edge.

This is one of the *Inferno*'s most important reversals. The ultimate punishment is not consuming flame but immobilizing cold. Treachery extinguishes the warmth of affection and trust; the traitors now inhabit a world where movement and relationship have become almost impossible.

The punishment is a **contrapasso**, a correspondence between sin and its consequences. Those who destroyed bonds are held fast, but their enforced proximity produces no fellowship. Their bodies are joined to a common prison while their wills remain hostile.

The physical cause of Cocytus's freezing will be revealed later: the beating of Lucifer's wings. Here, Dante first makes the reader experience its effects.

## 4. Tears That Cannot Relieve Suffering

> 37 Ognuna in giù tenea volta la faccia;  
> (Each kept its face turned downward;)
>
> 38 da bocca il freddo, e da li occhi il cor tristo  
> (from the mouth the cold, and from the eyes the sorrowing heart)
>
> 39 tra lor testimonianza si procaccia.  
> (each obtains its testimony among them.)

The chattering mouth reveals the cold; the weeping eyes reveal inward suffering. Dante presents the body as an involuntary witness. The souls need not explain their condition for their misery to become legible.

Their lowered faces suggest withdrawal and confinement, but should not automatically be read as repentance. The encounters that follow reveal anger, accusation, and the determination to conceal disgrace rather than acknowledge guilt.

> 46 li occhi lor, ch'eran pria pur dentro molli,  
> (their eyes, which before were wet only within,)
>
> 47 gocciar su per le labbra, e 'l gelo strinse  
> (dripped over their lips, and the frost bound)
>
> 48 le lagrime tra essi e riserrolli.  
> (the tears between them and sealed them shut again.)

When two neighboring souls lift their faces, their tears freeze and close their eyes. Even the natural expression of grief turns into an additional punishment.

The image gives emotional meaning to the frozen landscape. Tears ordinarily promise release, invite sympathy, or establish communication. Here they harden into barriers. Suffering does not soften these souls or restore their capacity for relationship.

## 5. Caina: Brothers Joined by Hatred

> 50 forte così; ond' ei come due becchi  
> (so tightly; whereupon they, like two he-goats,)
>
> 51 cozzaro insieme, tanta ira li vinse.  
> (butted against each other, so greatly did anger overcome them.)

The two souls respond to their predicament by striking their heads together. Their closeness is a grotesque parody of familial intimacy: physically inseparable, they remain inwardly antagonistic.

They are generally identified as Napoleone and Alessandro degli Alberti, sons of Count Alberto and members of a powerful family in the Bisenzio valley near Florence. Their feud combined inheritance disputes with opposing political affiliations and reportedly ended in their killing one another.

> 58 D'un corpo usciro; e tutta la Caina  
> (From one body they came forth; and all Caina)
>
> 59 potrai cercare, e non troverai ombra  
> (you may search, and you will find no shade)
>
> 60 degna più d'esser fitta in gelatina:  
> (more deserving of being fixed in jelly:)

Their common origin makes their antagonism especially terrible. They came from one maternal body, yet repudiated the solidarity that kinship should have established.

Caina takes its name from Cain, the biblical murderer of his brother Abel. The place therefore translates a foundational story of fratricide into an entire moral category.

The contemptuous image of being set in jelly reduces the damned to inert material. It also exemplifies the deliberately coarse register promised at the canto's beginning: the deepest moral degradation resists elevated or consoling description.

## 6. Camiscion: Excusing Oneself Through Someone Worse

> 68 sappi ch'i' fu' il Camiscion de' Pazzi;  
> (know that I was Camiscion de' Pazzi;)
>
> 69 e aspetto Carlin che mi scagioni».  
> (and I await Carlin to exonerate me.”)

The speaker who identifies the brothers is Camiscion de' Pazzi, traditionally understood to have murdered a relative. His final remark exposes another feature of this moral world: guilt seeks relief through comparison rather than repentance.

Carlin is Carlino de' Pazzi. In 1302 he surrendered the castle of Piantravigne, where White Guelfs had taken refuge, to their Black Guelf enemies, reportedly in return for money. Because the poem's journey takes place in 1300, Camiscion's expectation is a prophecy within the narrative, though the event was already past when Dante wrote.

Carlino cannot literally acquit him before divine justice. Camiscion means that the later betrayal will make his own crime seem less atrocious. His language preserves the habits of factional accusation and self-defense even where no sentence can be overturned.

## 7. Antenora: A Political Crime Revealed by a Kick

> 76 se voler fu o destino o fortuna,  
> (whether it was will or destiny or chance,)
>
> 77 non so; ma, passeggiando tra le teste,  
> (I do not know; but, walking among the heads,)
>
> 78 forte percossi 'l piè nel viso ad una.  
> (I struck my foot hard against the face of one.)

The travelers move toward the center, entering Antenora, the region of political betrayal. Its name comes from Antenor, the Trojan whom medieval traditions accused of betraying Troy, although ancient accounts of him are more varied.

Dante leaves the kick's cause unresolved. Was it deliberate, providential, or accidental? The uncertainty matters because he is becoming more than an observer. His own actions now participate in the violence of the scene.

> 80 se tu non vieni a crescer la vendetta  
> (if you do not come to increase the vengeance)
>
> 81 di Montaperti, perché mi moleste?».  
> (for Montaperti, why do you torment me?”)

The injured soul gives away his historical connection before revealing his name. Montaperti was the battle of 1260 in which the Florentine Guelfs suffered a devastating defeat against Sienese forces and their Ghibelline allies.

The soul is Bocca degli Abati. According to the tradition Dante follows, Bocca helped bring about the defeat by cutting the hand of the Florentine standard-bearer, causing the banner to fall and spreading confusion.

The reference turns an anonymous frozen face into a focus of civic memory. For Dante, betrayal is not an abstraction: it persists in remembered disasters and the fractured history of his city.

## 8. Naming Bocca: Poetry as Exposure

> 97 Allor lo presi per la cuticagna  
> (Then I seized him by the hair at the nape)
>
> 98 e dissi: «El converrà che tu ti nomi,  
> (and said: “You will have to name yourself,)
>
> 99 o che capel qui sù non ti rimagna».  
> (or not a hair will remain on you up here.”)

Dante initially offers to preserve the sinner's name among his records of the journey. Bocca rejects the offer: unlike souls who desire remembrance, he wants concealment.

The pilgrim then resorts to coercion, pulling out his hair. The encounter is deliberately disturbing. Dante's hostility can be understood as indignation against treachery, but the scene also brings his behavior close to the surrounding brutality. He is no longer simply asking questions about punishment; he is inflicting pain to obtain knowledge.

> 109 «Omai», diss' io, «non vo' che più favelle,  
> (“Now,” I said, “I do not want you to speak further,)
>
> 110 malvagio traditor; ch'a la tua onta  
> (wicked traitor; for to your shame)
>
> 111 io porterò di te vere novelle».  
> (I shall carry true reports of you.”)

Another prisoner finally names Bocca. Once identification is secured, Dante needs no confession: he promises public exposure.

Poetry here becomes a tribunal of historical memory. The report carried back to the living will not redeem Bocca's reputation but perpetuate his disgrace. The insistence on truth also recalls the opening invocation, where Dante asked that his telling correspond to the facts.

Yet readers must distinguish that poetic claim to truth from modern historical verification. Dante incorporates partisan traditions and disputed accusations into the authority of his imagined afterlife.

## 9. Betrayal Continues Through Mutual Denunciation

> 115 El piange qui l'argento de' Franceschi:  
> (He weeps here for the silver of the French:)
>
> 116 “Io vidi”, potrai dir, “quel da Duera  
> (‘I saw,’ you will be able to say, ‘the man from Duera)
>
> 117 là dove i peccatori stanno freschi”.  
> (there where the sinners stay cold.’)

Bocca retaliates against the soul who exposed him by identifying Buoso da Duera. According to the accusation Dante adopts, Buoso accepted French money and failed to obstruct troops connected with Charles of Anjou's advance into Italy in 1265.

The silver makes political betrayal a transaction: collective obligation is exchanged for private gain. Bocca's expression about sinners staying cold adds a bitterly comic touch, reducing eternal punishment to a sneering description of their temperature.

Most importantly, naming becomes contagious. The prisoners expose one another not from a commitment to justice but from spite. Treachery survives within its own punishment.

> 121 Gianni de' Soldanier credo che sia  
> (Gianni de' Soldanieri I believe to be)
>
> 122 più là con Ganellone e Tebaldello,  
> (farther on with Ganelon and Tebaldello,)
>
> 123 ch'aprì Faenza quando si dormia».  
> (who opened Faenza while people slept.”)

The list joins recent Italian history with literary legend. Gianni de' Soldanieri was remembered for abandoning his Ghibelline party during Florentine upheavals. Tebaldello betrayed Faenza by opening its gates to enemies in 1280.

Ganelon is the legendary betrayer whose actions lead to Roland's destruction in the French epic tradition. His presence places local factional treachery beside one of medieval literature's defining examples of betrayal.

The sleeping city is an especially forceful image. Sleep requires confidence that others will keep watch. Tebaldello turns that dependence into vulnerability, making the violation of political trust almost bodily.

## 10. The Gnawed Skull and the Promise of Another Story

> 130 non altrimenti Tidëo si rose  
> (not otherwise did Tydeus gnaw)
>
> 131 le tempie a Menalippo per disdegno,  
> (the temples of Menalippus in fury,)
>
> 132 che quei faceva il teschio e l'altre cose.  
> (than this one did the skull and the other parts.)

Dante encounters two souls frozen together, one positioned above the other and gnawing the back of his head. They will be identified in the next canto as Count Ugolino della Gherardesca and Archbishop Ruggieri degli Ubaldini.

The comparison recalls the Theban warrior Tydeus, who, mortally wounded, savagely attacked the head of his enemy Menalippus. In the classical tradition, this atrocity costs Tydeus the immortality Athena intended for him: vengeance carries him beyond the limits of humanity.

The allusion also answers the earlier invocation of Amphion. Thebes first appeared as a city built by music; it now returns as the setting of savage hatred. The canto moves between art's capacity to construct community and violence's capacity to annihilate it.

> 136 che se tu a ragion di lui ti piangi,  
> (that if you have just cause to complain of him,)
>
> 137 sappiendo chi voi siete e la sua pecca,  
> (knowing who you both are and his offense,)
>
> 138 nel mondo suso ancora io te ne cangi,  
> (in the world above I may yet repay you for it,)
>
> 139 se quella con ch'io parlo non si secca».  
> (if that with which I speak does not dry up.”)

Dante offers a bargain: if the gnawing sinner has a justified grievance, the poet will repay his account by carrying it into the living world.

The conditional is essential. Dante has witnessed hatred but does not yet know its history. He asks for identities, wrongdoing, and grounds for judgment. The next canto will test the relationship between a sinner's undeniable cruelty and the suffering that may explain it without excusing it.

The final reference to his tongue returns the canto to its opening concern with speech. Dante began by doubting whether language could represent the lowest Hell. He ends by pledging that language to the transmission of another terrible story.

## Epilogue — Where Human Bonds Freeze

Canto XXXII presents treachery as the freezing of relationships that should sustain human life. Brothers collide, citizens sell their communities, prisoners expose one another, and hatred feeds upon a human skull.

Its ice is therefore more than scenery. It gives visible form to a world where closeness survives without affection, memory without reconciliation, and speech without trust. Against that desolation stands Dante's difficult poetic task: to name what happened, judge its meaning, and preserve it without allowing horror—or the desire for vengeance—to replace understanding.
