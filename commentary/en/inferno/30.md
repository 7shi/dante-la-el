# Inferno XXX: When Falsehood Deforms the Human

In the tenth and final bolgia of the eighth circle, Dante encounters the falsifiers: people who corrupted substances, identities, money, or speech. Their punishments turn fraud into bodily disorder. What they made unreliable in life—the distinction between genuine and counterfeit—returns as the breakdown of their own physical and social existence.

The canto moves from tragic mythology to grotesque violence, from an unexpectedly beautiful recollection of running water to an abusive quarrel. Its final question concerns Dante himself: can looking at wrongdoing become another kind of moral failure?

## 1. Mythological Madness: The Destruction of the Family

> 4 Atamante divenne tanto insano,  
> (Athamas became so insane,)
>
> 5 che veggendo la moglie con due figli  
> (that, seeing his wife with two children)
>
> 6 andar carcata da ciascuna mano,  
> (go burdened on either hand,)

Dante begins with a story drawn from classical mythology, especially Ovid's *Metamorphoses*. Juno's hostility toward the family of Semele drives Athamas mad. Mistaking his wife Ino and their children for a lioness and cubs, he kills his son Learchus; Ino then throws herself into the water with the other child.

The crucial distortion is one of recognition: a husband and father sees his family as animals to be hunted. Before introducing the falsifiers of identity, Dante presents a mind that disastrously confuses one being with another. The family, ordinarily a place of recognition and protection, becomes a scene of predation.

> 20 forsennata latrò sì come cane;  
> (out of her mind, she barked like a dog;)
>
> 21 tanto il dolor le fé la mente torta.  
> (so greatly did grief twist her mind.)

The second example is Hecuba, queen of fallen Troy. Having lost her kingdom and witnessed the death of her daughter Polyxena, she discovers the body of her murdered son Polydorus. Grief destroys her capacity for human speech.

Both stories establish the canto's imagery of humanity collapsing into animal behavior. But Dante is not equating bereavement with criminal fraud. These legendary catastrophes supply a measure of extremity: even the madness of Athamas and Hecuba will prove less savage than the fury he now witnesses in Hell.

## 2. Human Beings Turned Predators

> 28 L'una giunse a Capocchio, e in sul nodo  
> (One reached Capocchio, and at the joint)
>
> 29 del collo l'assannò, sì che, tirando,  
> (of his neck sank its fangs into him, so that, dragging,)
>
> 30 grattar li fece il ventre al fondo sodo.  
> (it made his belly scrape along the hard ground.)

Two raging souls rush through the bolgia, biting other sinners. One seizes Capocchio, the alchemist encountered in the preceding canto, and drags him along the ground.

The language makes the attacker a beast: *assannò* describes an assault with fangs. These are human souls whose relationship to other people has become purely predatory.

Their punishment is a form of *contrapasso*, the correspondence between sin and its consequences. Those who violated personal identity now display a violent disintegration of human self-command. They cannot maintain the stable, recognizable humanity that they once deliberately counterfeited.

The assault also shows that Hell is not simply a collection of isolated punishments. The damned torment one another. Fraud's destruction of trust becomes a community in which every neighbor may be an attacker.

## 3. Myrrha and Gianni Schicchi: Counterfeiting a Person

> 38 di Mirra scellerata, che divenne  
> (of wicked Myrrha, who became)
>
> 39 al padre, fuor del dritto amore, amica.  
> (to her father, outside rightful love, a lover.)
>
> 40 Questa a peccar con esso così venne,  
> (She came thus to sin with him,)
>
> 41 falsificando sé in altrui forma,  
> (counterfeiting herself in another's form,)

One of the raging souls is Myrrha, another figure from Ovid. Through deception, she entered her father Cinyras's bed without his knowing her identity.

Dante's emphasis falls on the impersonation that enabled the incest. Myrrha is not placed here merely because her desire was illicit: she falsified who she was. Her father's recognition and consent were founded on a false identity.

The phrase “outside rightful love” also recalls the destruction of family relationships in the canto's opening. In this case, however, the confusion is deliberately produced rather than imposed by madness or overwhelming grief.

> 43 per guadagnar la donna de la torma,  
> (to gain the lady of the herd,)
>
> 44 falsificare in sé Buoso Donati,  
> (to counterfeit Buoso Donati in himself,)
>
> 45 testando e dando al testamento norma».  
> (making a will and giving the testament its form.”)

Myrrha's companion is the Florentine Gianni Schicchi. According to the story behind Dante's account, Schicchi impersonated the dead Buoso Donati and dictated a fraudulent will, securing a valuable mare for himself. The elevated expression “lady of the herd” comically dignifies his prize.

The pairing crosses an enormous social and literary distance: an ancient princess stands beside a Florentine trickster. Their motives differ—sexual desire in one case, material gain in the other—but their method is the same.

A person's identity should authorize intimate relationships and legal acts. Both sinners turn that identity into a usable disguise. Schicchi's deception is particularly corrosive because it makes a legal instrument, designed to preserve someone's wishes, speak with a stolen voice.

## 4. Master Adam: The Body as a Broken Instrument

> 49 Io vidi un, fatto a guisa di lëuto,  
> (I saw one, shaped like a lute,)
>
> 50 pur ch'elli avesse avuta l'anguinaia  
> (if only he had had the groin)
>
> 51 tronca da l'altro che l'uomo ha forcuto.  
> (cut off from the other part that a man has forked.)

Dante next sees Master Adam, a counterfeiter afflicted with dropsy, the historical term for severe swelling through accumulated fluid. His swollen torso and comparatively thin upper parts resemble the body and neck of a lute, provided one imagines his legs removed.

The comparison is grotesque rather than decorative. A musical instrument implies proportion and harmony; Adam's body has become a distorted shape whose parts no longer correspond properly.

His condition also suits the larger logic of falsification. A counterfeiter produces an object whose outward authority conceals an inward defect. Adam's own body now exposes disorder visibly: its proportions cannot sustain the appearance of normal integrity.

Later, when his belly sounds like a drum under a blow, this musical imagery returns in a degraded form. Instead of harmony, his body produces the accompaniment to a quarrel.

## 5. The Streams of Casentino: Memory Made Punitive

> 64 Li ruscelletti che d'i verdi colli  
> (The little streams that from the green hills)
>
> 65 del Casentin discendon giuso in Arno,  
> (of Casentino descend down into the Arno,)
>
> 66 faccendo i lor canali freddi e molli,  
> (making their channels cool and moist,)

Adam's speech suddenly introduces a landscape of green hills and refreshing water. Casentino is the upper valley of the Arno, where he committed his crime.

The diminutive *ruscelletti*, “little streams,” gives the memory an intimate tenderness. Against the swollen bodies, biting mouths, and feverish air of the bolgia, the landscape offers a vision of natural balance. Water moves freely through its proper channels, unlike the useless fluid trapped in Adam's body.

Yet this is not a moment of escape.

> 67 sempre mi stanno innanzi, e non indarno,  
> (they are always before me, and not in vain,)
>
> 68 ché l'imagine lor vie più m'asciuga  
> (for their image dries me out far more)
>
> 69 che 'l male ond' io nel volto mi discarno.  
> (than the illness through which my face grows wasted.)

Memory intensifies the punishment. Adam can imagine precisely what would relieve him, but imagination cannot supply even a drop of water.

This is one of the canto's most powerful reversals: beauty remains beautiful, yet its remembered presence becomes torment. The place of the crime supplies the material of the punishment.

The passage also distinguishes emotional eloquence from repentance. Adam can speak movingly about suffering without accepting full responsibility for his actions. His lyrical sensitivity will soon give way to vindictiveness.

## 6. Counterfeit Florins: Corrupting Public Trust

> 73 Ivi è Romena, là dov' io falsai  
> (There is Romena, there where I falsified)
>
> 74 la lega suggellata del Batista;  
> (the alloy stamped with the Baptist;)
>
> 75 per ch'io il corpo sù arso lasciai.  
> (for which I left my body burned above.)

At Romena, a castle in Casentino, Adam manufactured counterfeit Florentine gold florins. He was executed by burning in 1281.

The florin bore the image of John the Baptist, Florence's patron saint. Dante's description therefore stresses more than a technical monetary offense. The coin carried both civic authority and sacred imagery: its stamp promised that the metal possessed the value attributed to it.

Counterfeiting separates that authoritative sign from the reality it is supposed to guarantee. The result is a compact image of fraud itself—an apparently trustworthy surface concealing a corrupted substance.

> 88 Io son per lor tra sì fatta famiglia;  
> (Because of them I am among such a household;)
>
> 89 e' m'indussero a batter li fiorini  
> (they induced me to strike the florins)
>
> 90 ch'avevan tre carati di mondiglia».  
> (that contained three carats of base metal.”)

Adam says that the counts of Romena induced him to make the coins. Three carats of the twenty-four were base metal rather than gold: the coins claimed the authority of pure gold while withholding part of its substance.

His accusation introduces the political background of the crime. Counterfeiting could serve powerful patrons, not merely an isolated craftsman's greed.

Nevertheless, “because of them” is morally revealing. Adam acknowledges his activity but shifts the weight of blame toward his employers. His account is accurate enough to identify the fraud while remaining evasive about his own responsibility.

The bitter word “household” describes his infernal company. The fraudulent association that enriched him in life has yielded a grotesque substitute for fellowship.

## 7. Revenge Stronger Than Thirst

> 76 Ma s'io vedessi qui l'anima trista  
> (But if I saw here the wretched soul)
>
> 77 di Guido o d'Alessandro o di lor frate,  
> (of Guido or Alessandro or their brother,)
>
> 78 per Fonte Branda non darei la vista.  
> (I would not give up the sight for Fonte Branda.)

Adam declares that seeing one of his former patrons damned would be worth more than access to a fountain. The exact identification of this Fonte Branda has been debated, but its function is clear: it represents the abundant water he desperately lacks.

His thirst is physical; his desire for revenge is stronger still. Rather than wishing simply to escape suffering, he wants someone else to share it.

> 82 S'io fossi pur di tanto ancor leggero  
> (If only I were still light enough)
>
> 83 ch'i' potessi in cent' anni andare un'oncia,  
> (that I could move an inch in a hundred years,)
>
> 84 io sarei messo già per lo sentiero,  
> (I would already have set out along the path,)

Adam imagines undertaking an almost impossibly slow search through the bolgia to find the damned patron reportedly already there.

The disproportion is revealing. Even negligible movement over a century would satisfy him if it served hatred. His body is immobilized, but his will remains energetically directed toward vengeance.

This is why his beautiful recollection of Casentino cannot be mistaken for spiritual recovery. He remembers a good world, yet his deepest desire still binds him to the destructive relationships of his crime.

## 8. False Witness: Joseph's Accuser and Sinon

> 97 L'una è la falsa ch'accusò Gioseppo;  
> (One is the false woman who accused Joseph;)
>
> 98 l'altr' è 'l falso Sinon greco di Troia:  
> (the other is false Sinon, the Greek of Troy:)

Beside Adam lie two feverish falsifiers of speech. The unnamed woman is Potiphar's wife from Genesis. After Joseph rejected her advances, she falsely accused him of attempting to violate her, causing his imprisonment.

Sinon belongs to the story of Troy's fall, most famously told in Virgil's *Aeneid*. His deceptive account persuaded the Trojans to accept the wooden horse into their city.

The pairing places biblical and classical history within the same moral framework. One lie destroys an innocent person's standing; the other helps destroy a city. False testimony weaponizes the ordinary human need to believe what another person says.

Their fever makes them emit vapor like wet hands in winter. Alongside the restless fury of the impersonators and Adam's dropsy, this illness extends the canto's vision of falsification as a corruption of human functioning.

The appearance of Sinon also returns the narrative to Troy, whose destruction had framed Hecuba's grief at the beginning.

## 9. The Quarrel: Truth Used as a Weapon

> 103 Quella sonò come fosse un tamburo;  
> (It sounded as though it were a drum;)
>
> 104 e mastro Adamo li percosse il volto  
> (and Master Adam struck his face)
>
> 105 col braccio suo, che non parve men duro,  
> (with his arm, which seemed no less hard,)

Offended by Adam's description, Sinon punches his swollen belly. Adam strikes back. The body compared earlier to a lute now produces a drumbeat.

The scene descends into the register of comic abuse: grotesque anatomy, physical blows, and competitive insults. Its low style is deliberate. Fraud, which may initially appear clever or sophisticated, ends in an undignified contest over whose corruption is worse.

> 115 «S'io dissi falso, e tu falsasti il conio»,  
> (“If I spoke falsely, you too falsified the coinage,”)
>
> 116 disse Sinon; «e son qui per un fallo,  
> (said Sinon; “and I am here for one offense,)
>
> 117 e tu per più ch'alcun altro demonio!».  
> (and you for more than any other demon!”)

Sinon minimizes his guilt by counting it as a single offense, while treating Adam's repeated manufacture of false coins as innumerable crimes. The reasoning substitutes arithmetic for moral judgment: one deception may have catastrophic consequences.

Both speakers expose real faults in the other. Yet their occasional truthfulness does not bring them closer to truth as a moral good. Accurate accusations become instruments of humiliation.

This distinction is central to the canto. Speech can be factually correct and still be vicious in purpose. The quarrel does not repair falsification; it continues the same antagonistic misuse of language through a different means.

## 10. Dante's Shame: The Ethics of Watching

> 130 Ad ascoltarli er' io del tutto fisso,  
> (I was wholly intent on listening to them,)

Dante becomes absorbed in the exchange. His attention has shifted from understanding the consequences of sin to enjoying a degrading spectacle.

Virgil's angry rebuke interrupts that fascination. The correction matters because the poem has made the quarrel vivid and entertaining for its readers too. Dante's lapse exposes a danger within the act of reading: condemning vicious behavior does not automatically prevent us from taking pleasure in it.

> 140 che disïava scusarmi, e scusava  
> (for I desired to excuse myself, and was excusing)
>
> 141 me tuttavia, e nol mi credea fare.  
> (myself all the while, and did not believe I was doing so.)

Dante is so ashamed that he cannot deliver the apology he wishes to make. Yet the shame itself already speaks in his favor.

This response sharply distinguishes the pilgrim from the damned. Adam and Sinon defend themselves by transferring blame and attacking each other. Dante recognizes that his own desire has gone wrong.

Virgil consequently reassures him: his shame is sufficient, and he should lay aside his distress. The rebuke is educational rather than merely punitive.

> 148 ché voler ciò udire è bassa voglia».  
> (for wishing to hear such things is a base desire.”)

The canto ends by judging not only actions or statements but appetite: what a person wants to hear.

Virgil does not forbid the examination of evil. The entire journey requires it. He distinguishes morally attentive witnessing from an appetite for humiliation and abuse. Dante must learn to look without becoming a consumer of the degradation he describes.

## Epilogue — Falsehood and the Disorder of Desire

*Inferno* XXX presents falsification as an attack on the bonds that make human life intelligible: personal identity, family recognition, trustworthy money, and credible speech. In Hell, those broken bonds appear as bodily distortion, animal fury, fever, and endless hostility.

But the canto's final lesson reaches beyond the falsifiers. Adam's eloquence does not free him from revenge, and truthful accusations do not redeem a malicious quarrel. Even Dante's attention requires correction. The alternative to fraud is therefore more than factual accuracy: it is a rightly directed desire to understand, speak, and recognize the truth without turning other people's degradation into pleasure.
