# Inferno XXVII — The Fraud That Cannot Absolve Itself

In the eighth bolgia of the eighth circle of Hell, Dante encounters souls concealed inside flames: figures traditionally identified as fraudulent counselors, whose intelligence became an instrument of deception. After the departure of Ulysses, another flame approaches. It contains Guido da Montefeltro, a celebrated military leader who became a Franciscan friar but, in Dante's account, returned to fraud at the urging of Pope Boniface VIII.

Set during Dante's imagined journey in 1300, the canto moves from Italian politics to a fundamental moral question: can religious authority cancel guilt when someone deliberately chooses to do wrong?

## 1. The Voice Inside the Fire

> 7 Come 'l bue cicilian che mugghiò prima  
> (Like the Sicilian bull that first bellowed)
>
> 8 col pianto di colui, e ciò fu dritto,  
> (with the lament of that man, and this was just,)
>
> 9 che l'avea temperato con sua lima,  
> (who had fashioned it with his file,)

Dante introduces Guido's voice through the story of the bronze bull made for Phalaris, the ancient tyrant of Acragas in Sicily. According to the traditional account, its inventor, Perillus, designed it so that the cries of someone roasted inside would sound like the bellowing of an animal. Phalaris made the inventor its first victim.

The comparison establishes a world in which ingenuity turns against its practitioner. Perillus devised an instrument of cruelty; Guido used intelligence to devise deception. Both become trapped within a suffering associated with their own kind of invention.

Dante's parenthetical judgment that this was just concerns the inventor's punishment, not a general endorsement of the tyrant. The story anticipates the canto's larger concern with the relationship between culpable intelligence and retribution.

> 13 così, per non aver via né forame  
> (so, through having neither passage nor opening)
>
> 14 dal principio nel foco, in suo linguaggio  
> (at first within the fire, into its language)
>
> 15 si convertïan le parole grame.  
> (the sorrowful words were transformed.)

Guido's words initially become the indistinct noise of the flame. Only afterward do they find their way through its tip.

This is part of the punishment's *contrapasso*: the correspondence between sin and suffering. Those who concealed destructive intentions within persuasive speech are themselves concealed. Their eloquence survives, but it must struggle through the fire that imprisons them. The flame simultaneously hides the speaker and exposes his guilt.

## 2. Romagna: Peace Outside, War Within

> 37 Romagna tua non è, e non fu mai,  
> (Your Romagna is not, and never was,)
>
> 38 sanza guerra ne' cuor de' suoi tiranni;  
> (without war in the hearts of its tyrants;)
>
> 39 ma 'n palese nessuna or vi lasciai.  
> (but openly I left none there just now.)

Guido first asks about Romagna, the region whose political struggles had shaped his life. Dante distinguishes between outward peace and inward aggression: there may be no open fighting at the moment, but the rulers' hearts remain at war.

That contrast prepares the canto's central moral pattern. Just as apparent peace can conceal political violence, a religious habit or a promise of absolution can conceal an unchanged willingness to deceive. External appearances are unreliable guides to inward intention.

> 46 E 'l mastin vecchio e 'l nuovo da Verrucchio,  
> (And the old mastiff and the new one from Verrucchio,)
>
> 47 che fecer di Montagna il mal governo,  
> (who dealt cruelly with Montagna,)
>
> 48 là dove soglion fan d'i denti succhio.  
> (where they are accustomed, make augers of their teeth.)

The two mastiffs are Malatesta da Verrucchio and his son Malatestino, rulers associated with Rimini. Montagna de' Parcitati, a political enemy, was killed while in Malatesta custody. Their teeth become instruments that bore into their victims.

Dante's survey repeatedly turns rulers into animals, drawing partly on heraldic emblems: the Polenta eagle at Ravenna, the Ordelaffi's green lion at Forlì, and the lion associated with Maghinardo Pagani, who controlled Faenza and Imola. Heraldry becomes moral characterization. These rulers do not merely possess territories; they brood over them, grip them, or bite into them.

The political background is the competition among local lordships and the unstable alignments of Guelf and Ghibelline factions. Guido himself had been a prominent Ghibelline commander. His personal history belongs to precisely this world of force, calculation, and shifting loyalties.

## 3. A Confession Made Under False Security

> 64 ma però che già mai di questo fondo  
> (but because never from this depth)
>
> 65 non tornò vivo alcun, s'i' odo il vero,  
> (has anyone returned alive, if what I hear is true,)
>
> 66 sanza tema d'infamia ti rispondo.  
> (without fear of infamy I answer you.)

Guido speaks because he believes his listener cannot return to the living world. His condition for disclosure is secrecy: he will reveal his story only because he thinks his earthly reputation cannot suffer.

The dramatic irony is decisive. Dante is alive, and the poem itself will make this supposedly private confession public. Guido's attempt to control the consequences of his speech fails before his narrative even begins.

His concern with infamy also complicates his self-presentation. He is not simply a repentant sinner eager to acknowledge the truth. He still calculates what disclosure might cost him. The intelligence that once managed political risks now misjudges the identity and future of its listener.

## 4. From Soldier to Friar: Conversion and Its Limits

> 67 Io fui uom d'arme, e poi fui cordigliero,  
> (I was a man of arms, and then a wearer of the cord,)

The cord identifies Guido as a Franciscan friar. Historically, he entered the Franciscan order in 1296 and died in 1298. His movement from military command to religious life gives his story the shape of a late conversion.

Yet Dante immediately makes clear that Guido's military identity had depended less on straightforward courage than on strategic cunning.

> 73 Mentre ch'io forma fui d'ossa e di polpe  
> (While I was a form of bones and flesh)
>
> 74 che la madre mi diè, l'opere mie  
> (that my mother gave me, my deeds)
>
> 75 non furon leonine, ma di volpe.  
> (were not those of a lion, but of a fox.)

The lion and the fox distinguish force from fraud. Guido characterizes himself not as an openly attacking predator but as an expert in concealment, indirect action, and traps.

Within the moral structure of *Inferno*, fraud is especially serious because it corrupts the rational capacities distinctive to human beings. Guido's intelligence is not itself condemned; its deliberate misuse is.

> 82 ciò che pria mi piacëa, allor m'increbbe,  
> (what formerly pleased me then displeased me,)
>
> 83 e pentuto e confesso mi rendei;  
> (and repentant and confessed, I entered religious life;)
>
> 84 ahi miser lasso! e giovato sarebbe.  
> (ah, wretched and weary me! and it would have availed.)

These lines matter because the canto does not simply declare Guido's conversion fraudulent from the beginning. His repentance could have benefited him. The tragedy lies in a subsequent choice that reverses his moral direction.

The religious habit therefore represents a real opportunity, not an automatic guarantee. Entering an order cannot make future conduct irrelevant, and an earlier repentance cannot excuse a later, deliberate return to sin.

## 5. Boniface VIII and the Corruption of Spiritual Office

> 85 Lo principe d'i novi Farisei,  
> (The prince of the new Pharisees,)
>
> 86 avendo guerra presso a Laterano,  
> (being at war near the Lateran,)
>
> 87 e non con Saracin né con Giudei,  
> (and not with Saracens or with Jews,)
>
> 88 ché ciascun suo nimico era cristiano,  
> (for every enemy of his was Christian,)

The unnamed pope is Boniface VIII. Calling him the prince of the new Pharisees invokes the Gospel denunciations of religious hypocrisy: sacred authority is being used to pursue worldly domination.

The immediate background is Boniface's conflict with the Colonna family. Palestrina, their stronghold, surrendered and was destroyed in 1298. Dante presents the pope as directing his energies against fellow Christians rather than fulfilling his spiritual responsibilities. The reference to non-Christian enemies reflects the crusading assumptions of Dante's period.

The historical conflict is real, but Guido's precise role as the author of the treacherous advice is not securely established. The episode should be read as Dante's morally charged representation, not as an independently verified transcript.

> 94 Ma come Costantin chiese Silvestro  
> (But as Constantine sought Sylvester)
>
> 95 d'entro Siratti a guerir de la lebbre,  
> (from within Soracte to heal him of leprosy,)
>
> 96 così mi chiese questi per maestro  
> (so this man sought me as a master)
>
> 97 a guerir de la sua superba febbre;  
> (to heal him of his proud fever;)

According to medieval legend, the emperor Constantine sought Pope Sylvester, who had taken refuge on Mount Soracte, and received healing from leprosy through baptism.

Dante reverses that model. Instead of a secular ruler seeking spiritual healing from a holy man, a pope seeks practical instruction in deception from a former strategist. Boniface's illness is pride, but the proposed cure will satisfy rather than remove it.

The comparison exposes an inversion of proper authority: the spiritual ruler asks to be taught the very vice he should help others overcome.

## 6. The Keys of Heaven Used as Leverage

> 103 Lo ciel poss' io serrare e diserrare,  
> (Heaven I can lock and unlock,)
>
> 104 come tu sai; però son due le chiavi  
> (as you know; for that purpose there are the two keys)
>
> 105 che 'l mio antecessor non ebbe care”.  
> (which my predecessor did not hold dear”.)

Boniface invokes the keys entrusted to Peter, the biblical foundation of papal authority to bind and loose. His predecessor is Celestine V, who resigned the papacy in 1294. Boniface presents that renunciation contemptuously, as a failure to value the power of the office.

In the surrounding speech, the pope promises absolution before Guido has supplied the sinful counsel. Spiritual authority becomes bargaining power: advise me how to destroy my enemies, and I will secure your immunity.

The canto does not deny the significance of the keys. It denies that their holder can use them to abolish the conditions of repentance. Boniface treats salvation as something his office can dispense at will, independently of the recipient's actual moral choice.

## 7. The Counsel: Promise Much, Perform Little

> 110 lunga promessa con l'attender corto  
> (a long promise with a short fulfillment)
>
> 111 ti farà trïunfar ne l'alto seggio”.  
> (will make you triumph upon the lofty throne”.)

Guido's advice is brutally concise: obtain surrender through generous promises, then fulfill little of what has been promised. Here *attender* concerns keeping or fulfilling a promise, not merely waiting.

In Dante's narrative, this is the fraudulent strategy for overcoming Palestrina. Trust becomes the means of destroying those who trust.

The promised triumph on the lofty throne is therefore deeply ironic. Success in holding supreme religious office is achieved through conduct incompatible with that office's purpose. The pope wins politically by losing morally.

Guido, meanwhile, tries to make his wrongdoing safe through the promised absolution. He is both deceiver and deceived: he supplies a method for betraying others because he accepts a false assurance about his own salvation.

## 8. The Devil's Logic: Absolution Requires Repentance

> 118 ch'assolver non si può chi non si pente,  
> (for one cannot absolve someone who does not repent,)
>
> 119 né pentere e volere insieme puossi  
> (nor can one repent and will at the same time,)
>
> 120 per la contradizion che nol consente”.  
> (because of the contradiction that does not permit it”.)

At Guido's death, Saint Francis comes for him, but a black cherub claims the soul. The devil's argument supplies the canto's theological center.

Repenting of an action means rejecting it. Deliberately willing that same action while relying on advance forgiveness is incompatible with such rejection. The argument is not that a sinner can never repent afterward; it is that the intention to commit a sin cannot itself count as repentance for it.

The devil wins the dispute not by being stronger than Francis, but by identifying Guido's actual moral condition. Neither papal assurance nor Franciscan membership can substitute for the missing repentance.

The scene also overturns Guido's confidence in superior cleverness. Having built his career on calculation, he discovers that his final calculation rests on a contradiction. The devil's subsequent boast that he too is a logician makes reason itself the instrument that exposes Guido's self-deception.

## 9. Judgment and the Fire That Steals the Sinner from Sight

> 124 A Minòs mi portò; e quelli attorse  
> (He carried me to Minos; and Minos wound)
>
> 125 otto volte la coda al dosso duro;  
> (his tail eight times around his hard back;)
>
> 126 e poi che per gran rabbia la si morse,  
> (and after he bit it in great rage,)
>
> 127 disse: “Questi è d'i rei del foco furo”;  
> (he said: “This is one of the guilty of the thieving fire”;)

Minos, the infernal judge, indicates the soul's destination by the number of turns of his tail. Eight turns assign Guido to the eighth circle, the realm of fraud; they do not by themselves designate the eighth bolgia.

The thieving fire conceals its inhabitants, stealing them from ordinary sight. Guido's punishment makes his former methods visible through their effects: the man who worked through hidden intentions now exists inside an appearance that hides him.

The sequence also corrects his account of responsibility. Guido repeatedly emphasizes Boniface's role in bringing him back to his former sins. The pope's corruption is essential, but Minos judges Guido for the counsel he himself chose to give. Temptation and abuse of authority do not erase his agency.

## 10. Toward the Next Ditch: From Deception to Division

> 134 su per lo scoglio infino in su l'altr' arco  
> (up along the rocky ridge as far as the next arch)
>
> 135 che cuopre 'l fosso in che si paga il fio  
> (that spans the ditch where the penalty is paid)
>
> 136 a quei che scommettendo acquistan carco.  
> (by those who incur guilt through sowing division.)

After Guido's flame departs, Dante and Virgil move toward the ninth bolgia, where the sowers of discord are punished.

The transition connects two forms of social destruction. Fraudulent counsel manipulates relationships of trust; the next sin tears communities and bonds apart. Guido's story has already shown their overlap: deception helps political authority destroy its opponents.

The canto therefore ends by widening its perspective. What appeared to be one man's private failure belongs to a broader disorder in which intelligence, government, and religious office become instruments of conflict.

## Epilogue — No Authority Can Make Deception Innocent

*Inferno* XXVII brings together political cunning, corrupted spiritual authority, and the failure of self-exculpation. Guido seeks safety first in religious retirement and then in a pope's advance absolution, but neither can protect a deliberate return to fraud.

The canto's governing contrast is between appearance and inward reality: peace and concealed hostility, religious clothing and renewed wrongdoing, promised forgiveness and absent repentance. Its final judgment is that salvation cannot be secured by clever wording or institutional power. No authority can make the simultaneous willing and repudiation of the same sin cease to be a contradiction.
