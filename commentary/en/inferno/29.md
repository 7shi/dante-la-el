# Inferno XXIX: Broken Kinship, Diseased Bodies, and Counterfeit Nature

Canto XXIX moves from the ninth bolgia of the eighth circle, where the sowers of discord are mutilated, into the tenth and final bolgia, where falsifiers suffer bodily corruption. Its movement is also moral: Dante passes from an unresolved family grievance into a world where the falsification of substances appears as the disintegration of human bodies.

The canto continually tests the relationship between compassion and judgment. Its sufferers are sometimes pitiable, sometimes threatening, sometimes ridiculous. Their humanity remains recognizable even when their bodies—and the values they defended in life—have become grotesque.

## 1. The Temptation to Remain and Weep

> 1 La molta gente e le diverse piaghe  
> (The multitude of people and the various wounds)
>
> 2 avean le luci mie sì inebrïate,  
> (had so intoxicated my eyes,)
>
> 3 che de lo stare a piangere eran vaghe.  
> (that they longed to remain and weep.)

Dante begins by describing sight as a kind of intoxication. The spectacle of wounded bodies has overwhelmed his capacity to move forward. His eyes almost acquire a will of their own: they want to stay with what horrifies them.

The immediate setting is still the ninth bolgia, where those who divided communities, families, or religious bodies undergo repeated bodily division. Yet Dante's absorption introduces another problem: suffering can arrest the spectator without necessarily deepening understanding. Compassion must not become an endless, fascinated gaze.

> 10 E già la luna è sotto i nostri piedi;  
> (And already the moon is beneath our feet;)
>
> 11 lo tempo è poco omai che n'è concesso,  
> (little time now remains that is granted to us,)
>
> 12 e altro è da veder che tu non vedi».  
> (and there is more to see that you do not see.”)

Virgil counters emotional paralysis with the demands of the journey. The moon's position serves as an astronomical time marker; the passage is generally understood to place the travelers on Holy Saturday morning.

More importantly, their time is “granted.” This is not unrestricted sightseeing but a journey permitted for a purpose. Virgil, the guide associated with reason, insists that Dante's attention must remain educable: what he already sees must not prevent him from discovering what he does not yet understand.

## 2. Geri del Bello and the Unfinished Claims of Vendetta

> 25 ch'io vidi lui a piè del ponticello  
> (for I saw him at the foot of the little bridge)
>
> 26 mostrarti e minacciar forte col dito,  
> (pointing at you and threatening fiercely with his finger,)
>
> 27 e udi' 'l nominar Geri del Bello.  
> (and I heard him named Geri del Bello.)

Dante has been searching for a relative, Geri del Bello, traditionally identified as a cousin of Dante's father. Medieval accounts associate Geri with violent quarrels and his murder with a Florentine family feud.

Virgil has seen what Dante missed: Geri's threatening gesture. Dante had been absorbed in Bertran de Born, the decapitated troubadour encountered in the preceding canto. The failure to notice his own kinsman brings the public spectacle of discord abruptly into Dante's private history.

> 34 fece lui disdegnoso; ond' el sen gio  
> (made him indignant; therefore he went away)
>
> 35 sanza parlarmi, sì com' ïo estimo:  
> (without speaking to me, as I suppose:)
>
> 36 e in ciò m'ha el fatto a sé più pio».  
> (and in this he has made me more compassionate toward him.”)

The subject of “made him indignant” is Geri's violent death, still unavenged by a relative who shares the family's dishonor. Dante interprets the threatening gesture through the culture of vendetta: an injury to one family member could be understood as an obligation inherited by the others.

The poem does not simply ratify that obligation. Dante feels increased compassion, while Virgil urges him to leave Geri behind. The tension remains unresolved. Within a region punishing discord, the demand for revenge reveals how violence perpetuates itself through kinship, honor, and inherited resentment.

## 3. An Infernal Cloister and the Arrows of Pity

> 40 Quando noi fummo sor l'ultima chiostra  
> (When we were above the last cloister)
>
> 41 di Malebolge, sì che i suoi conversi  
> (of Malebolge, so that its lay brothers)
>
> 42 potean parere a la veduta nostra,  
> (could appear within our sight,)

The tenth bolgia is introduced through an ironic monastic vocabulary. A “cloister” normally suggests ordered communal life, spiritual discipline, and healing through devotion. Its “lay brothers” are instead condemned souls.

This is a bitter inversion rather than necessarily an attack on a particular religious institution. The damned form a community, but one without mutual restoration. Their enclosure brings them together while exposing the damage that fraud has done to human fellowship.

> 43 lamenti saettaron me diversi,  
> (various laments shot arrows at me,)
>
> 44 che di pietà ferrati avean li strali;  
> (which had their shafts tipped with pity;)
>
> 45 ond' io li orecchi con le man copersi.  
> (so I covered my ears with my hands.)

Before Dante clearly sees the sufferers, he hears them. Their cries become weapons whose arrowheads are compassion.

The metaphor makes pity physically invasive. Dante cannot contemplate pain from a secure distance: it penetrates him through the senses. Covering his ears parallels the earlier intoxication of his eyes. Throughout the canto, perception threatens to overwhelm the person who perceives.

## 4. The Bolgia as a Hospital Without Healing

> 46 Qual dolor fora, se de li spedali  
> (What suffering there would be, if from the hospitals)
>
> 47 di Valdichiana tra 'l luglio e 'l settembre  
> (of Valdichiana between July and September)
>
> 48 e di Maremma e di Sardigna i mali  
> (and of Maremma and Sardinia the ailments)
>
> 49 fossero in una fossa tutti 'nsembre,  
> (were all together in a single ditch,)
>
> 50 tal era quivi, e tal puzzo n'usciva  
> (such was it here, and such a stench came out of it)
>
> 51 qual suol venir de le marcite membre.  
> (as usually comes from rotting limbs.)

Dante gathers several notorious landscapes of disease into one imaginary hospital. Valdichiana, Maremma, and Sardinia were associated with unhealthy conditions and recurrent fevers, particularly during the warmer months. Marshland diseases, including what would later be identified as malaria, form part of this historical background.

The comparison makes Hell recognizable through earthly suffering. Dante does not begin with an impossible monster but with hospitals, seasonal illness, overcrowding, and the smell of decay.

Yet this hospital has no prospect of recovery. The concentration of ailments in a single ditch turns a place of possible care into a permanent exhibition of corruption. The falsifiers' diseased bodies provide the basic logic of their punishment: those who corrupted substances and appearances now suffer the corruption of their own bodily substance and appearance.

## 5. Divine Justice and the Plague of Aegina

> 55 giù ver' lo fondo, la 've la ministra  
> (down toward the bottom, where the minister)
>
> 56 de l'alto Sire infallibil giustizia  
> (of the high Lord, infallible justice,)
>
> 57 punisce i falsador che qui registra.  
> (punishes the falsifiers whom it registers here.)

The governing power is explicitly named: divine justice, acting as the minister of God. What appears to the senses as a chaotic accumulation of disease belongs, within the poem's theology, to an exact moral order.

These are the *falsador*, the falsifiers. The alchemists encountered in this canto are one group within a wider category developed further in Canto XXX. The essential offense is fraudulent alteration: making something pass for what it is not.

> 61 che li animali, infino al picciol vermo,  
> (that the animals, down to the little worm,)
>
> 62 cascaron tutti, e poi le genti antiche,  
> (all fell, and afterward the ancient peoples,)
>
> 63 secondo che i poeti hanno per fermo,  
> (according to what the poets hold as certain,)
>
> 64 si ristorar di seme di formiche;  
> (were replenished from the seed of ants;)

These lines belong to Dante's comparison with the plague of Aegina, narrated in Ovid's *Metamorphoses*. After a devastating pestilence, King Aeacus obtains a new population through Jupiter's transformation of ants into human beings: the Myrmidons.

The myth supplies an image of near-total biological collapse. Even the smallest creatures die. But its ending also sharpens the contrast with Hell: Aegina can be repopulated, whereas the bolgia cannot be healed.

Ovidian transformation thus stands beside fraudulent transformation. The classical story restores a human community; the falsifiers' arts have instead helped earn them a grotesquely damaged one.

## 6. Bodies Reduced to Utensils and Fish

> 73 Io vidi due sedere a sé poggiati,  
> (I saw two sitting propped against each other,)
>
> 74 com' a scaldar si poggia tegghia a tegghia,  
> (as pan is propped against pan for warming,)
>
> 75 dal capo al piè di schianze macolati;  
> (stained from head to foot with scabs;)

The two souls are Griffolino d'Arezzo and Capocchio. Their mutual support might initially resemble companionship, but Dante immediately compares them to kitchen pans.

The domestic simile strips the scene of heroic dignity. These bodies resemble objects placed together for convenience. For men associated with manipulating material substances, being described as utensils is particularly suggestive: the manipulators of matter now appear almost reduced to matter themselves.

> 82 e sì traevan giù l'unghie la scabbia,  
> (and their nails scraped off the scabs)
>
> 83 come coltel di scardova le scaglie  
> (as a knife does the scales of a bream)
>
> 84 o d'altro pesce che più larghe l'abbia.  
> (or of another fish that has them larger.)

Their itching produces ceaseless self-scraping. Dante compares their nails to a knife scaling a fish, making their own bodies the material on which they labor.

The punishment combines pain with futile activity. Scratching cannot cure them; it merely repeats the exposure and destruction of their surfaces. The imagery answers the falsifier's manipulation of appearances with an appearance that cannot be repaired. There is no need to force a modern diagnosis onto the scene: its scabs, itching, and disfigurement create a deliberately comprehensive image of bodily corruption.

## 7. The Living Visitor and the Hope of Being Remembered

> 94 E 'l duca disse: «I' son un che discendo  
> (And the guide said: “I am one who descends)
>
> 95 con questo vivo giù di balzo in balzo,  
> (with this living man down from ledge to ledge,)
>
> 96 e di mostrar lo 'nferno a lui intendo».  
> (and I intend to show Hell to him.”)

Virgil's announcement transforms the encounter. A living body has entered this community of ruined bodies, and its presence makes the sufferers turn toward Dante.

His exceptional status also introduces a connection to the world they have lost. Unlike the damned, he can leave, speak, and transmit their stories. The journey therefore produces not only knowledge for Dante but testimony for his readers.

> 103 «Se la vostra memoria non s'imboli  
> (“May your memory not steal away)
>
> 104 nel primo mondo da l'umane menti,  
> (in the first world from human minds,)
>
> 105 ma s'ella viva sotto molti soli,  
> (but may it live beneath many suns,)

Dante offers remembrance as an inducement to speak. The “first world” is earthly life, and the succession of suns suggests the passing of many days.

This is not a promise of salvation. The damned cannot change their eternal condition through literary fame. Nevertheless, their concern for their names preserves something recognizably human within their degradation.

There is also a pointed tension: men punished for falsification now depend on a poet to represent them truthfully. Their bodily identities are damaged, but their stories can still recover distinctions between persons, motives, and acts.

## 8. Griffolino: Human Error and Infallible Judgment

> 109 «Io fui d'Arezzo, e Albero da Siena»,  
> (“I was from Arezzo, and Albero of Siena”,)
>
> 110 rispuose l'un, «mi fé mettere al foco;  
> (one replied, “had me put to the fire;)
>
> 111 ma quel per ch'io mori' qui non mi mena.  
> (but that for which I died does not bring me here.)

The speaker is Griffolino d'Arezzo. According to his account, he jokingly claimed that he knew how to fly. Albero of Siena believed him and wanted to learn the art. When Griffolino failed to make him another Daedalus, Albero brought about his execution.

The figure who ordered the burning is traditionally identified as the bishop of Siena, who treated Albero as a son. The details come through a mixture of Dante's narrative and early commentary, and should not all be treated as securely established biography.

The essential distinction is clear: the charge that caused Griffolino's earthly death is not the offense for which he is damned.

> 118 Ma ne l'ultima bolgia de le diece  
> (But into the last bolgia of the ten)
>
> 119 me per l'alchìmia che nel mondo usai  
> (me, for the alchemy that I practiced in the world,)
>
> 120 dannò Minòs, a cui fallar non lece».  
> (Minos condemned, who is not permitted to err.”)

Griffolino separates fallible human punishment from infallible infernal judgment. Earthly authorities killed him because of another man's foolishness and resentment; Minos assigned him his place for his actual wrongdoing.

Here alchemy means its fraudulent use in falsifying metals, not necessarily every medieval investigation of material transformation. The canto's concern is counterfeit substance and deceptive value.

The Daedalus allusion adds another dimension. Griffolino's joke invoked genuine technical mastery, but Albero could not distinguish a boast from an achievable art. The episode joins fraud to credulity: deception operates in a society eager to believe extravagant promises.

## 9. Siena and the Irony of “Moderate” Spending

> 125 rispuose al detto mio: «Tra'mene Stricca  
> (replied to my words: “Except Stricca for me)
>
> 126 che seppe far le temperate spese,  
> (who knew how to make moderate expenditures,)
>
> 127 e Niccolò che la costuma ricca  
> (and Niccolò who the costly custom)
>
> 128 del garofano prima discoverse  
> (of the clove first discovered)
>
> 129 ne l'orto dove tal seme s'appicca;  
> (in the garden where such seed takes root;)

Griffolino's story provokes Dante to attack Sienese vanity. Capocchio responds by pretending to name exceptions. The praise is sarcastic: Stricca's supposedly moderate spending actually exemplifies extravagance.

Niccolò is associated with an expensive use of cloves, a luxury spice. The precise practice has been variously explained, but its function here is unmistakable: costly novelty finds fertile ground in Siena. The city becomes a “garden” where fashionable excess readily takes root.

The surrounding references evoke the Sienese *brigata spendereccia*, the spendthrift company remembered for consuming fortunes in lavish living. The satire links foolish aspiration, conspicuous consumption, and distorted judgment of value.

It also changes the canto's tone. These sufferers are not merely objects of pity; they retain wit, hostility, and the pleasure of mocking others. Hell preserves personality without curing its defects.

## 10. Capocchio, Nature's “Good Ape”

> 136 sì vedrai ch'io son l'ombra di Capocchio,  
> (thus you will see that I am the shade of Capocchio,)
>
> 137 che falsai li metalli con l'alchìmia;  
> (who falsified metals through alchemy;)
>
> 138 e te dee ricordar, se ben t'adocchio,  
> (and you should remember, if I recognize you rightly,)
>
> 139 com' io fui di natura buona scimia».  
> (how I was a good ape of nature.”)

Capocchio, who was burned at Siena in 1293, ends the canto by appealing to Dante's recognition. The exchange suggests personal acquaintance, though its precise circumstances are uncertain.

His description of himself as a “good ape of nature” is both boast and confession. An ape imitates; Capocchio possessed an aptitude for reproducing natural appearances. “Good” describes his skill, not his moral goodness.

The phrase brings the canto's central concern into focus. Human art can learn from nature, but imitation becomes fraud when it produces a deceptive substitute and passes it off as genuine. Capocchio's cleverness did not disappear when it became corrupt; it made the corruption more effective.

There is also a suggestive contrast with Dante's poetry. Both poet and counterfeiter reproduce appearances. The moral difference lies in whether representation discloses truth or conceals it.

## Epilogue — When Falsehood Becomes Flesh

Canto XXIX makes corruption visible and tangible. Broken social bonds appear first in Geri del Bello's demand for vengeance; corrupted substances then appear in bodies that rot, itch, and lose their integrity.

Yet the canto refuses to reduce its sinners to abstract examples. They remain relatives, conversationalists, victims of earthly injustice, satirists, and skilled imitators. Their recognizable humanity makes judgment more difficult to contemplate, not less binding within the poem's moral world.

Dante must therefore learn to look without becoming immobilized, to pity without confusing pity with acquittal, and to distinguish inventive skill from its fraudulent use. In this bolgia, falsehood is no longer a convincing surface: its hidden corruption has become flesh.
