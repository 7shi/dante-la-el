# Inferno VIII: Anger, the Living Body, and the Gates of Dis

Canto VIII carries Dante and Virgil across the marsh of Styx and brings them to the fortified city of Dis. The journey moves through two connected crises: Dante's furious encounter with a Florentine sinner, and Virgil's failure to secure passage through the city's gates. Together, they raise difficult questions about righteous anger, human reason, and the need for divine assistance.

The setting is the fifth circle of Hell, where wrath receives its punishment. But the canto also approaches a threshold: beyond Dis lie the deeper regions of damnation.

## 1. Signals across the Marsh

> 4 per due fiammette che i vedemmo porre,  
> (because of two little flames that we saw placed there,)
>
> 5 e un'altra da lungi render cenno,  
> (and another from afar return the signal,)
>
> 6 tanto ch'a pena il potea l'occhio tòrre.  
> (so far that the eye could scarcely catch it.)

The canto opens with communication that Dante can see but cannot interpret. Flames appear on the tower, and another flame answers from across the marsh. They function as signals announcing the travellers and summoning transportation, although the precise significance of their number is not explained.

Hell here resembles a guarded territory with watchtowers, communications, and controlled approaches. The travellers are entering an organized system of hostility, not merely a landscape of isolated punishments.

> 7 E io mi volsi al mar di tutto 'l senno;  
> (And I turned to the sea of all wisdom;)

The “sea of all wisdom” is Virgil. The metaphor expresses Dante's immense trust in his guide's understanding. It also prepares an important contrast: by the canto's end, even this seemingly inexhaustible wisdom will encounter an obstacle it cannot immediately overcome.

Allegorically, Virgil represents the powers of human reason and moral understanding. Those powers are indispensable, but they are not unlimited.

## 2. Phlegyas: Anger Becomes the Ferryman

> 19 «Flegïàs, Flegïàs, tu gridi a vòto»,  
> (“Phlegyas, Phlegyas, you shout in vain”,)
>
> 20 disse lo mio segnore, «a questa volta:  
> (said my lord, “this time:)
>
> 21 più non ci avrai che sol passando il loto».  
> (you shall have us only while we cross the mire”.)

The boatman Phlegyas mistakes Dante for a newly arrived damned soul. Virgil corrects him: the travellers are passengers, not permanent captives.

In classical mythology, Phlegyas burned Apollo's temple after Apollo violated his daughter Coronis. He therefore embodies furious retaliation that turns against divine authority. Virgil's *Aeneid* also places him among the punished in the underworld.

Dante adapts this classical figure to the Christian geography of Hell. A man associated with destructive anger now serves the region of wrath. His frustration at Virgil's correction shows that he remains governed by the very passion he embodies: even his assigned office becomes another occasion for rage.

## 3. The Boat Registers a Living Body

> 25 Lo duca mio discese ne la barca,  
> (My guide descended into the boat,)
>
> 26 e poi mi fece intrare appresso lui;  
> (and then made me enter after him;)
>
> 27 e sol quand' io fui dentro parve carca.  
> (and only when I was inside did it seem laden.)

Virgil enters without visibly weighing down the boat. Dante's entrance changes its load. The small physical detail confirms the enormous difference between them: Virgil is a shade, while Dante is travelling through Hell with his living body.

This distinction has moral as well as narrative importance. The dead have reached their final condition; Dante still belongs to the world of choice, repentance, and change. His journey is meant to alter how he lives.

The body also makes his presence disruptive. Hell's ordinary procedures are designed for the dead, and his living weight becomes evidence that this journey is exceptional.

## 4. Filippo Argenti: Recognition beneath the Mud

> 34 E io a lui: «S'i' vegno, non rimango;  
> (And I to him: “Though I come, I do not stay;)
>
> 35 ma tu chi se', che sì se' fatto brutto?».  
> (but who are you, who have become so foul?”.)
>
> 36 Rispuose: «Vedi che son un che piango».  
> (He answered: “You see that I am one who weeps”.)

A mud-covered sinner asks why Dante has arrived before his time. Dante insists on the distinction between visiting and belonging: he comes to Hell, but will not remain there.

The sinner avoids giving his name, identifying himself only through suffering. Soon, however, Dante recognizes him, and the other damned name him as Filippo Argenti.

Filippo belonged to the powerful Florentine Adimari family. Early commentators portray him as wealthy, arrogant, and violent; some also connect his family with hostility toward Dante. Details of their personal relationship remain uncertain, so the episode should not be reduced to a securely documented private feud.

Nevertheless, Florence has unmistakably entered the marsh. The encounter exposes how civic pride, factional hostility, and personal resentment can survive as spiritual deformities.

## 5. Righteous Indignation—or Anger Mirroring Anger?

> 43 Lo collo poi con le braccia mi cinse;  
> (Then he encircled my neck with his arms;)
>
> 44 basciommi 'l volto e disse: «Alma sdegnosa,  
> (he kissed my face and said: “Indignant soul,)
>
> 45 benedetta colei che 'n te s'incinse!  
> (blessed is she who bore you in her womb!)

After Dante rejects the sinner and Virgil pushes him away from the boat, Virgil embraces and praises his pupil. The blessing recalls the Gospel blessing of the mother who bore Jesus in Luke 11:27, giving the moment unusually elevated language.

A traditional interpretation distinguishes Dante's indignation from the wrath punished in Styx. Christian moral thought does not condemn every form of anger: anger governed by reason and directed against evil can be just. On this reading, Dante is learning not to confuse compassion with approval of sin.

Yet the scene remains unsettling. Dante knows this particular sinner, and his hostility is personal as well as moral. Virgil's approval strongly supports the interpretation of righteous indignation, but the episode also invites readers to examine how easily apparent zeal for justice can resemble the passion it condemns.

> 49 Quanti si tegnon or là sù gran regi  
> (How many now regard themselves up there as great kings)
>
> 50 che qui staranno come porci in brago,  
> (who here will stand like pigs in mire,)
>
> 51 di sé lasciando orribili dispregi!».  
> (leaving behind horrible contempt for themselves!”.)

Virgil broadens the encounter into a judgment on worldly prestige. People who imagine themselves sovereign figures will be reduced to animals in mud.

The contrast strips social power of its protective appearance. Wealth, family standing, and arrogance cannot establish genuine worth. Although Filippo is punished among the wrathful, pride helps explain the anger that defines him: an inflated sense of importance makes resistance or insult intolerable.

## 6. Wrath Turns against Its Own Body

> 61 Tutti gridavano: «A Filippo Argenti!»;  
> (They all shouted: “At Filippo Argenti!”;)
>
> 62 e 'l fiorentino spirito bizzarro  
> (and the hot-tempered Florentine spirit)
>
> 63 in sé medesmo si volvea co' denti.  
> (turned his teeth upon himself.)

The other sinners attack Filippo, and he bites himself. The violence of wrath becomes both collective and self-destructive.

This is *contrapasso*: punishment that manifests the character of the sin. Those who allowed anger to destroy human fellowship now inhabit a community whose only shared activity is assault. Filippo's attack on himself makes the inward damage of anger outwardly visible.

Dante has asked to see him submerged, and afterwards says that he still praises and thanks God for what he witnessed. The retrospective voice matters: the narrator, not merely the frightened traveller, endorses the event. The passage thus presses its difficult moral question forcefully: what separates assent to divine justice from pleasure in another person's suffering?

## 7. Dis: A City Illuminated by Punishment

> 73 fossero». Ed ei mi disse: «Il foco etterno  
> (had come”. And he said to me: “The eternal fire)
>
> 74 ch'entro l'affoca le dimostra rosse,  
> (that burns them within makes them appear red,)
>
> 75 come tu vedi in questo basso inferno».  
> (as you see in this lower hell”.)

The towers of Dis glow red because fire burns within them. Their light does not guide or comfort: it reveals punishment.

“Dis” is a classical name for the ruler of the underworld, associated in Dante's poem with Lucifer, and also names this infernal city. Its walls enclose the sixth through ninth circles. The travellers are approaching the region that includes heresy, violence, fraud, and treachery.

Dante describes its buildings as *meschite*, “mosques.” The term draws on the architectural and religious associations of a medieval Christian observer: the skyline looks foreign and religiously hostile. It should not be mistaken for a precise claim that the city's inhabitants are Muslims.

The city is an anti-community. It has the visible structures of civilization—walls, towers, gates, inhabitants—but its organizing principles are exclusion, suffering, and rebellion.

## 8. The Fallen Angels Try to Separate Guide and Traveller

> 89 e disser: «Vien tu solo, e quei sen vada  
> (and said: “You come alone, and let that one go)
>
> 90 che sì ardito intrò per questo regno.  
> (who so boldly entered this realm.)
>
> 91 Sol si ritorni per la folle strada:  
> (Let him return alone along the foolish road:)

The defenders are fallen angels, described earlier as having rained down from Heaven. Unlike the human sinners encountered in the marsh, they are spiritual beings whose rebellion against God precedes the human journey.

Their strategy is separation. They invite Virgil forward while ordering Dante to retrace the route alone. The threat attacks the relationship on which the pilgrimage depends.

Calling the journey “foolish” also contests its legitimacy. From the devils' perspective, a living man has no right to cross their territory. From the poem's larger perspective, however, the journey is divinely authorized. Infernal resistance can obstruct that purpose temporarily, but cannot annul it.

## 9. Fear, Trust, and the Limits of Reason

> 104 mi disse: «Non temer; ché 'l nostro passo  
> (said to me: “Do not fear; for our passage)
>
> 105 non ci può tòrre alcun: da tal n'è dato.  
> (no one can take from us: by such a one is it granted to us.)

Dante begs Virgil not to abandon him and proposes that they retreat together. Virgil answers by appealing to the authority that granted the journey.

His confidence rests not simply on his own persuasive ability, but on a higher power. Allegorically, reason can recognize that divine purpose is trustworthy even when it cannot itself remove every obstacle.

> 115 Chiuser le porte que' nostri avversari  
> (Those adversaries of ours shut the gates)
>
> 116 nel petto al mio segnor, che fuor rimase  
> (against my lord's breast, who remained outside)
>
> 117 e rivolsesi a me con passi rari.  
> (and turned back toward me with slow steps.)

The gates nevertheless close against Virgil. His earlier authority over infernal guardians does not work here.

The failure dramatizes a limit rather than the worthlessness of reason. Virgil has brought Dante this far, explained what he sees, and protected him. But understanding evil and arguing against it do not necessarily confer the power to overcome its resistance. The pilgrimage now requires assistance beyond the guide's unaided resources.

## 10. The Broken Gate and the Promise of Help

> 124 Questa lor tracotanza non è nova;  
> (This arrogance of theirs is not new;)
>
> 125 ché già l'usaro a men segreta porta,  
> (for they once exercised it at a less hidden gate,)
>
> 126 la qual sanza serrame ancor si trova.  
> (which still stands without a fastening.)

Virgil recalls an earlier defeat of infernal resistance. The “less hidden gate” is the outer entrance to Hell, whose inscription Dante read in Canto III.

The reference is traditionally understood through the Harrowing of Hell: Christ's descent among the dead after his crucifixion. The gate remains unsecured as a sign that infernal power has already been broken by a greater authority.

This background places the immediate setback within Christian salvation history. The devils behave as though they possess final sovereignty, but the architecture of their own kingdom bears witness against them.

> 128 e già di qua da lei discende l'erta,  
> (and already on this side of it descends the slope,)
>
> 129 passando per li cerchi sanza scorta,  
> (passing through the circles without escort,)
>
> 130 tal che per lui ne fia la terra aperta».  
> (one through whom the city shall be opened to us”.)

The canto ends with help approaching, not with the gates opening. The figure will appear in Canto IX as a heavenly messenger.

His ability to travel without an escort contrasts with Dante's dependence on guidance. He acts under an authority that Hell cannot finally resist. The suspense therefore contains reassurance: the travellers have reached the limit of their present power, not the limit of divine provision.

## Epilogue — From the Violence of Anger to the Need for Grace

Canto VIII moves from passions that imprison the soul to gates that imprison the travellers. Filippo Argenti reveals anger's degradation of personal and civic life; the defenders of Dis reveal rebellion hardened into organized resistance.

Between these scenes stand Dante's uncertain moral growth and Virgil's indispensable but limited guidance. The canto asks readers to distinguish justice from vindictiveness, genuine worth from worldly status, and rational confidence from self-sufficiency. Its final promise is that the journey can continue—but not through human wisdom alone.
