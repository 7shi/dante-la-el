# Inferno, Canto II — Fear, Grace, and the Authority to Begin

Canto II is the threshold of Dante's journey through the afterlife. In Canto I, Virgil rescued him from the beasts blocking his ascent toward a sunlit hill. Now, as evening falls, Dante questions whether he should follow his guide at all.

Set within the poem's imagined journey of 1300, this canto supplies both a personal reassurance and a justification for the *Comedy* itself. Why should Dante, rather than a biblical saint or an ancient hero, receive knowledge of the world beyond death? The answer unfolds through the relationship between human reason, divine grace, and love.

## 1. Evening: The Solitary Beginning of a Spiritual Struggle

> 1 Lo giorno se n'andava, e l'aere bruno  
> (The day was departing, and the darkening air)
>
> 2 toglieva li animai che sono in terra  
> (was releasing the living creatures that are on earth)
>
> 3 da le fatiche loro; e io sol uno  
> (from their labors; and I alone)
>
> 4 m'apparecchiava a sostener la guerra  
> (was preparing myself to sustain the warfare)
>
> 5 sì del cammino e sì de la pietate,  
> (both of the journey and of pity,)

The natural world moves toward rest, but Dante prepares for an ordeal. This contrast isolates him: his exceptional journey begins when ordinary activity ends.

The “warfare” has two fronts. There is the difficulty of traveling through Hell, but also the emotional struggle of encountering suffering. Here *pietate* suggests pity and distress, anticipating the pilgrim's troubled responses to the damned. He must learn not only what divine justice does, but how to understand suffering without allowing sympathy to obscure moral judgment.

Allegorically, spiritual renewal begins as a struggle against the conditions in which the self has become comfortable. Darkness is therefore both a literal setting and an image of uncertainty: Dante must enter what he cannot yet comprehend.

## 2. The Invocation: Poetry Must Preserve an Extraordinary Experience

> 7 O muse, o alto ingegno, or m'aiutate;  
> (O Muses, O high genius, now help me;)
>
> 8 o mente che scrivesti ciò ch'io vidi,  
> (O memory that wrote down what I saw,)
>
> 9 qui si parrà la tua nobilitate.  
> (here your nobility will be revealed.)

The invocation gives the canto the character of a formal epic beginning. Ancient poets called upon the Muses for inspiration; Dante adopts that convention while directing it toward a Christian account of salvation.

He also invokes his own intellectual power and his memory. The latter is imagined as having already written down the experience internally. The poem must now make that inner record available to others.

This distinguishes Dante the frightened traveler from Dante the retrospective narrator. The traveler does not know what lies ahead; the narrator undertakes to represent what he has seen. The invocation thus establishes a central tension of the *Comedy*: an apparently inadequate individual claims responsibility for an exceptionally authoritative poem.

## 3. Aeneas: Rome's Imperial History Within Providence

> 20 ch'e' fu de l'alma Roma e di suo impero  
> (for he was, of nurturing Rome and of its empire,)
>
> 21 ne l'empireo ciel per padre eletto:  
> (in the Empyrean heaven chosen as father:)
>
> 22 la quale e 'l quale, a voler dir lo vero,  
> (which city and which empire, to speak the truth,)
>
> 23 fu stabilita per lo loco santo  
> (were established for the holy place)
>
> 24 u' siede il successor del maggior Piero.  
> (where sits the successor of the great Peter.)

The unnamed “he” is Aeneas, whose descent into the underworld is narrated in Book VI of Virgil's *Aeneid*. There he encounters his father Anchises and learns about the future Roman people. Dante understands this journey as preparation for a historical mission.

His interpretation is explicitly Christian. Aeneas was chosen in the Empyrean—the highest heaven and dwelling of God—to become the ancestor of Rome. Rome and its empire, in turn, prepared the historical setting for the seat of Peter's successor, the pope.

This is more than praise of classical civilization. Dante incorporates pagan history into providence: events whose participants did not know Christianity could nevertheless advance God's purposes. It also reflects his broader conviction that Roman universal empire had a legitimate place in the ordering of human affairs.

Aeneas therefore possessed what Dante fears he lacks: a world-historical reason for entering the realm beyond death.

## 4. Paul: Revelation Requires a Mission

> 28 Andovvi poi lo Vas d'elezïone,  
> (Then the Vessel of Election went there,)
>
> 29 per recarne conforto a quella fede  
> (to bring back from there support for that faith)
>
> 30 ch'è principio a la via di salvazione.  
> (which is the beginning of the way of salvation.)
>
> 31 Ma io, perché venirvi? o chi 'l concede?  
> (But I, why come there? Or who permits it?)
>
> 32 Io non Enëa, io non Paulo sono;  
> (I am not Aeneas, I am not Paul;)

The “Vessel of Election” is Saint Paul, echoing the description of him as a chosen vessel in Acts 9:15. His otherworldly experience is associated with his account of being caught up to the third heaven and paradise in 2 Corinthians 12. Medieval traditions, including the apocryphal *Vision of Paul*, also connected him with revelations of the afterlife.

Dante places Aeneas and Paul beside one another as complementary precedents. Aeneas serves the providential history of Rome; Paul strengthens the faith through which humanity moves toward salvation.

The question “who permits it?” is decisive. Dante does not merely doubt his endurance. He doubts his authorization. An attempt to penetrate divine mysteries without permission might be presumption rather than courage.

At the same time, these denials establish the poem's extraordinary ambition. By insisting that he is neither Aeneas nor Paul, Dante brings his own journey into comparison with theirs.

## 5. Cowardice: When Reflection Becomes Retreat

> 45 «l'anima tua è da viltade offesa;  
> (“your soul is afflicted by cowardice;)
>
> 46 la qual molte fïate l'omo ingombra  
> (which many times obstructs a person)
>
> 47 sì che d'onrata impresa lo rivolve,  
> (so that it turns him away from an honorable undertaking,)
>
> 48 come falso veder bestia quand' ombra.  
> (as mistaken seeing turns a beast when it shies.)

Virgil identifies Dante's condition as *viltade*: faintheartedness or cowardice. His apparently reasonable objections have become an impediment to an honorable action.

The animal comparison explains the mechanism. A creature may shy because it mistakes something harmless for a threat. Similarly, Dante's imagination has magnified uncertainty into a reason for abandoning the journey.

This does not mean that every fear is irrational, or that humility is wrong. The problem is a failure to judge the present situation correctly. Dante does not yet know the heavenly circumstances behind Virgil's arrival.

Virgil therefore answers fear with an account of what has actually happened. Reason begins its work by correcting an incomplete picture of reality.

## 6. Beatrice: Love Descends to Rescue the Lost

> 52 Io era tra color che son sospesi,  
> (I was among those who are suspended,)
>
> 53 e donna mi chiamò beata e bella,  
> (and a lady called me, blessed and beautiful,)
>
> 54 tal che di comandare io la richiesi.  
> (such that I asked her to command me.)

Virgil begins a story within the story. Before meeting Dante, he was among the souls “suspended” in Limbo. Canto IV will explain their condition more fully: they lack the vision of God, though they do not undergo the bodily punishments of the lower circles.

His position matters allegorically. Virgil represents the nobility and power of human reason, but also its limits. His wisdom cannot by itself bring him into Christian blessedness.

The visiting woman comes from a realm he cannot reach. Her beauty and blessedness establish an authority that he immediately recognizes. The journey is therefore not an initiative of unaided reason: reason has been summoned into the service of a higher aid.

> 70 I' son Beatrice che ti faccio andare;  
> (I am Beatrice, who make you go;)
>
> 71 vegno del loco ove tornar disio;  
> (I come from the place where I desire to return;)
>
> 72 amor mi mosse, che mi fa parlare.  
> (Love moved me, which makes me speak.)

Beatrice is rooted in Dante's personal history and earlier poetry, especially the *Vita nuova*. Traditionally identified with Beatrice Portinari, she had died before the poem's imagined journey.

Yet she is more than a remembered beloved. In the *Comedy*, she becomes a bearer of revelation and divine wisdom. Her personal concern for Dante and her theological significance reinforce one another: love becomes the means through which saving truth reaches him.

Her descent also reverses Dante's movement. Before he can rise, help comes down. The canto presents rescue as an initiative that precedes the pilgrim's renewed willingness to act.

## 7. Beyond the Moon: Reason Recognizes Its Limits

> 76 “O donna di virtù sola per cui  
> (‘O lady of virtue, through whom alone)
>
> 77 l'umana spezie eccede ogne contento  
> (the human species surpasses everything contained)
>
> 78 di quel ciel c'ha minor li cerchi sui,  
> (within that heaven whose circles are smallest,)

The heaven with the smallest circles is the sphere of the Moon, the nearest celestial sphere in the medieval Earth-centered cosmos. Everything beneath it belongs to the changeable terrestrial world.

Virgil's praise thus assigns Beatrice a significance larger than that of an individual woman. She represents the wisdom and grace through which humanity rises beyond merely earthly existence toward its supernatural destiny.

The traditional association of Virgil with reason and Beatrice with theology is useful, provided it does not turn them into rigid abstractions. They remain distinct personalities with emotional and literary histories.

Their encounter presents cooperation rather than hostility between classical learning and Christian revelation. Virgil's eloquence and judgment are genuinely valuable. What he needs is not their abolition, but direction toward an end they cannot independently attain.

## 8. True Fear and the Security of Blessedness

> 88 Temer si dee di sole quelle cose  
> (One should fear only those things)
>
> 89 c'hanno potenza di fare altrui male;  
> (that have the power to do one harm;)
>
> 90 de l'altre no, ché non son paurose.  
> (not the others, for they are not fearsome.)

Virgil asks why Beatrice is not afraid to descend into Hell. Her response distinguishes genuine danger from a terrifying appearance.

This principle directly addresses Dante's hesitation. Fear should be governed by what can actually harm someone, not simply by what feels overwhelming. Virgil has diagnosed the pilgrim's distorted perception; Beatrice supplies a more general account of rightly ordered fear.

Her claim does not imply that Hell is harmless. Its power must be understood in relation to the condition of the person who enters it.

> 91 I' son fatta da Dio, sua mercé, tale,  
> (I am made by God, through his grace, such,)
>
> 92 che la vostra miseria non mi tange,  
> (that your misery does not touch me,)
>
> 93 né fiamma d'esto 'ncendio non m'assale.  
> (nor does a flame of this burning assail me.)

Beatrice's safety comes from God's grace, not from personal daring. As a blessed soul, she cannot be deprived of her blessedness by entering the infernal realm.

Her immunity also clarifies the difference between compassion and subjection. She can care deeply about Dante's distress without becoming spiritually endangered by it. Divine charity does not require helplessness before evil.

For Dante, the implication is reassuring but specific: the aid sent to him originates in a realm that Hell cannot overthrow.

## 9. The Three Heavenly Women: Grace Before Merit

> 94 Donna è gentil nel ciel che si compiange  
> (There is a gracious lady in heaven who grieves)
>
> 95 di questo 'mpedimento ov' io ti mando,  
> (over this impediment to which I send you,)
>
> 96 sì che duro giudicio là sù frange.  
> (so that she breaks stern judgment above.)

The gracious lady is understood to be the Virgin Mary, though she is not named. Her compassionate initiative begins the sequence that eventually brings Virgil to Dante.

Mary calls upon Lucia, and Lucia turns to Beatrice. Beatrice then seeks Virgil, who reaches the lost pilgrim. Dante discovers that his apparently solitary crisis has already stirred concern in heaven.

Mary's action should not be understood as making divine justice unjust. The language dramatizes the efficacy of mercy and intercession: the sinner is not simply left to the consequences of his predicament.

> 103 Disse:—Beatrice, loda di Dio vera,  
> (She said: Beatrice, true praise of God,)
>
> 104 ché non soccorri quei che t'amò tanto,  
> (why do you not help the one who loved you so much,)
>
> 105 ch'uscì per te de la volgare schiera?  
> (who for you came forth from the common crowd?)

Lucia urges Beatrice to act by recalling Dante's love and the transformation associated with it. His devotion raised him above ordinary pursuits, with an evident connection to the poetic and spiritual development narrated in the *Vita nuova*.

Saint Lucy's name evokes light, making her especially appropriate to a poem about recovering spiritual vision. The canto also presents Dante as her devotee.

Traditional allegorical readings associate Mary with merciful or initiating grace, Lucia with illumination, and Beatrice with revelation or theology. These associations illuminate the scene without exhausting it. The essential point is the ordered movement of care: divine mercy reaches a particular person through several forms of mediation.

Dante does not establish his worthiness and thereby compel heaven's assistance. Assistance comes first, enabling him to move again.

## 10. Flowers Reopening: Courage Restored Through Trust

> 127 Quali fioretti dal notturno gelo  
> (As little flowers, by the night's frost)
>
> 128 chinati e chiusi, poi che 'l sol li 'mbianca,  
> (bent and closed, after the sun whitens them,)
>
> 129 si drizzan tutti aperti in loro stelo,  
> (straighten themselves, all open, upon their stems,)
>
> 130 tal mi fec' io di mia virtude stanca,  
> (so I became in my exhausted strength,)

The flower simile answers the opening darkness. The external evening has not ended, but Dante experiences an inward renewal resembling sunrise.

The image is deliberately delicate. His recovery is not presented as the triumph of a self-sufficient hero. Like a frost-stricken flower, he responds to warmth received from elsewhere.

Virgil's explanation has changed his understanding of the journey. What seemed an unauthorized adventure now appears as a task sustained by heavenly concern. Restored perception produces restored courage.

> 136 Tu m'hai con disiderio il cor disposto  
> (You have disposed my heart with desire)
>
> 137 sì al venir con le parole tue,  
> (so toward coming, through your words,)
>
> 138 ch'i' son tornato nel primo proposto.  
> (that I have returned to my first resolve.)
>
> 139 Or va, ch'un sol volere è d'ambedue:  
> (Now go, for one single will belongs to us both:)

Dante's renewed willingness is the canto's practical conclusion. Grace has not removed the need for choice; it has made a more informed and confident choice possible.

Virgil succeeds through speech rather than force. His account awakens desire and brings their wills into agreement. Dante can now accept guidance without imagining that obedience means abandoning his own agency.

The difficult path remains difficult. What changes is his relation to it: he enters it as someone helped, instructed, and willing.

## Epilogue — The Journey Begins Because Help Has Already Come

Canto II turns hesitation into consent. Dante's fear raises questions of worthiness, authority, and the limits of human strength; Virgil answers by revealing the heavenly love behind his mission.

The canto's central allegory is not simply that reason conquers fear. Reason becomes effective because it serves a larger movement of grace. Mary's compassion, Lucia's intervention, Beatrice's love, and Virgil's guidance together restore the pilgrim's freedom to act.

Before Dante can begin his ascent toward God, he learns that he has not been abandoned. His journey starts in earnest when he trusts that truth and takes the next step.
