# At the Bottom of Hell: Allegory and Background in Dante's *Inferno*, Canto XXXIV

The final canto of *Inferno* brings Dante and his guide, Virgil, to Lucifer at the center of the earth. Yet the ruler of Hell is not a commanding intelligence enthroned in fire. He is a grotesque prisoner immobilized in ice. Dante's encounter with him completes the poem's account of sin—and reveals that the way beyond evil begins by recognizing its impotence.

The canto combines Christian theology, Roman history, medieval cosmology, and the physical drama of an escape. Its central reversal is both geographical and spiritual: at the lowest possible point, descent becomes ascent.

## 1. The Banners of an Infernal King

> 1 «Vexilla regis prodeunt inferni  
> (“The banners of the king of Hell advance)
>
> 2 verso di noi; però dinanzi mira»,  
> (toward us; therefore look ahead,”)
>
> 3 disse 'l maestro mio, «se tu 'l discerni».  
> (said my master, “if you can make him out.”)

Virgil announces Lucifer by adapting the opening of *Vexilla regis prodeunt*, a sixth-century Christian hymn by Venantius Fortunatus. The original celebrates the Cross: “The banners of the King advance.” Dante's addition of *inferni*, “of Hell,” turns a sacred proclamation into an infernal parody.

The allusion establishes the canto's governing contrast between Christ and Satan. Christ's apparent defeat on the Cross becomes victory; Lucifer's apparent sovereignty conceals complete defeat. His “banners,” soon recognizable as enormous wings, announce no victorious advance. Lucifer himself cannot move from his prison.

Dante initially compares the distant shape to a windmill glimpsed through fog or darkness. Before readers see a fallen angel, they encounter something like a vast mechanism: obscure, repetitive, and without meaningful direction.

## 2. Judecca: Betrayal Frozen into Immobility

> 10 Già era, e con paura il metto in metro,  
> (Already I was, and with fear I put it into verse,)
>
> 11 là dove l'ombre tutte eran coperte,  
> (where the shades were entirely covered,)
>
> 12 e trasparien come festuca in vetro.  
> (and showed through like a straw in glass.)

Dante has reached Judecca, the innermost division of Cocytus, the frozen lake occupying Hell's ninth circle. Its name evokes Judas, and its inhabitants are traditionally identified as traitors to their benefactors or rightful lords.

Unlike sinners elsewhere in Hell, these souls cannot speak to Dante. Entirely enclosed in ice, they appear as objects trapped inside a transparent substance. Their various contorted positions reinforce the loss of freedom, dignity, and relationship.

The punishment is a *contrapasso*: it exposes in physical form the character of the sin. Treachery destroys the bonds of trust that make human community possible. Here that destruction becomes absolute isolation.

Hell's deepest region is therefore cold rather than fiery. Within the poem's Christian symbolism, love gives warmth and movement; the ultimate rejection of love produces frozen separation.

## 3. Before Lucifer: The Failure of Life and Language

> 25 Io non mori' e non rimasi vivo;  
> (I did not die, and I did not remain alive;)
>
> 26 pensa oggimai per te, s'hai fior d'ingegno,  
> (think now for yourself, if you have any spark of understanding,)
>
> 27 qual io divenni, d'uno e d'altro privo.  
> (what I became, deprived of both.)

Dante describes his terror through a contradiction: neither dead nor alive. The immediate meaning is psychological—ordinary language cannot express his condition—but the formulation also belongs to Hell's wider paradox. The damned continue to exist while being deprived of the blessed life for which human beings were created.

The direct address to the reader makes imagination take over where narration fails. At the climax of his descent, Dante does not offer effortless mastery of his subject. He emphasizes the inadequacy of speech.

> 34 S'el fu sì bel com' elli è ora brutto,  
> (If he was as beautiful as he is now ugly,)
>
> 35 e contra 'l suo fattore alzò le ciglia,  
> (and raised his brows against his maker,)
>
> 36 ben dee da lui procedere ogne lutto.  
> (surely every sorrow must proceed from him.)

Lucifer's ugliness measures the ruin of an originally magnificent creature. Dante draws on the Christian tradition of Satan as a glorious angel whose pride led him to rebel against God.

Raising the eyebrows becomes a compact gesture of defiance: the creature refuses his proper relation to the Creator. Lucifer's present condition is not evidence that God created an independent evil power. It displays the corruption of something originally good.

His immense size reinforces the contrast. Physically overwhelming, he possesses none of the freedom or authority that his proportions seem to promise.

## 4. Three Faces and Six Wings: A Counterfeit Divinity

> 38 quand' io vidi tre facce a la sua testa!  
> (when I saw three faces on his head!)
>
> 39 L'una dinanzi, e quella era vermiglia;  
> (One was in front, and that one was red;)

Lucifer's three faces are widely understood as a grotesque parody of the Trinity. Where the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit possess perfect unity in love, Lucifer presents a monstrous triplicity occupied with violence.

The other faces are yellowish-white and black. Their exact symbolism is debated. Commentators have associated them with corrupted spiritual attributes or with the geographical divisions of humanity. Dante's description supports the broader significance more securely than any single detailed scheme: Lucifer counterfeits divine fullness without possessing it.

This is not a battle between equal gods of good and evil. Lucifer remains a created being, radically subordinate to the power he opposed.

> 49 Non avean penne, ma di vispistrello  
> (They had no feathers, but of a bat)
>
> 50 era lor modo; e quelle svolazzava,  
> (was their form; and he kept flapping them,)
>
> 51 sì che tre venti si movean da ello:  
> (so that three winds arose from him:)
>
> 52 quindi Cocito tutto s'aggelava.  
> (therefore Cocytus froze entirely.)

The six wings can recall the six-winged seraphim of Isaiah's vision, but their batlike form transforms celestial imagery into something dark and degraded.

More important is their function. Wings should permit ascent; these produce the cold that keeps Lucifer imprisoned. His activity perpetuates his confinement.

The image gives evil a self-defeating structure. Lucifer is not merely restrained by an external barrier: his own ceaseless motion sustains the frozen world that holds him. The supposed emperor cannot escape the consequences of his rebellion.

## 5. The Three Traitors: Church, Empire, and Devoured Humanity

> 55 Da ogne bocca dirompea co' denti  
> (From each mouth he was tearing with his teeth)
>
> 56 un peccatore, a guisa di maciulla,  
> (a sinner, in the manner of a flax brake,)
>
> 57 sì che tre ne facea così dolenti.  
> (so that he thus made three of them suffer.)

The three mouths transform Lucifer's parody of divine unity into a machinery of destruction. A *maciulla* is a device for breaking the woody stalks of flax or hemp. The comparison makes his action brutally mechanical.

He neither judges nor explains. Like the windmill glimpsed earlier, he operates repetitively. At Hell's center, Dante finds not a profound alternative wisdom but the endless mutilation of persons.

> 62 disse 'l maestro, «è Giuda Scarïotto,  
> (said my master, “is Judas Iscariot,)
>
> 63 che 'l capo ha dentro e fuor le gambe mena.  
> (who has his head inside and flails his legs outside.)

Judas, who betrayed Christ, receives the greatest punishment. His head is inside the central mouth, and Lucifer's claws also strip the skin from his back.

His position reflects the identity of the person betrayed. In Dante's Christian framework, Judas violated his relationship with both his benefactor and the incarnate God. His punishment gives the innermost region of Hell its defining example.

> 65 quel che pende dal nero ceffo è Bruto:  
> (the one hanging from the black muzzle is Brutus:)
>
> 66 vedi come si storce, e non fa motto!;  
> (see how he writhes, and says not a word!)
>
> 67 e l'altro è Cassio, che par sì membruto.  
> (and the other is Cassius, who appears so powerfully built.)

Brutus and Cassius helped assassinate Julius Caesar in 44 BCE. Their placement beside Judas depends on Dante's understanding of Roman history as providential.

For Dante, universal empire had a legitimate role in establishing earthly peace and justice. Caesar stood at a decisive point in the emergence of that order. His assassins therefore represent more than private disloyalty: their betrayal attacks an institution Dante believed served God's historical purposes.

The arrangement places betrayal of sacred and temporal authority together without making the victims equivalent. Judas remains the worst punished. Readers who regard Brutus as a defender of republican liberty are encountering a sharply different political judgment.

## 6. Lucifer Becomes a Ladder

> 73 appigliò sé a le vellute coste;  
> (he fastened himself to the shaggy sides;)
>
> 74 di vello in vello giù discese poscia  
> (then from tuft to tuft he descended)
>
> 75 tra 'l folto pelo e le gelate croste.  
> (between the thick hair and the frozen crusts.)

Dante clings to Virgil while his guide climbs down Lucifer's body. The emperor of Hell becomes climbing equipment.

This humiliation is central to the scene. Lucifer does not challenge the travelers or prevent their departure. His monstrous body provides the route by which they leave his kingdom.

Virgil's practical competence also matters. Traditionally associated with the powers of human reason, he reads the physical situation correctly and acts. Dante must trust his guide before he understands what is happening.

> 79 volse la testa ov' elli avea le zanche,  
> (he turned his head where he had had his legs,)
>
> 80 e aggrappossi al pel com' om che sale,  
> (and gripped the hair like a man climbing,)
>
> 81 sì che 'n inferno i' credea tornar anche.  
> (so that I thought I was returning again into Hell.)

At Lucifer's hips, Virgil reverses his position. Dante mistakes the movement for a return toward Hell.

The misunderstanding makes the canto's spiritual reversal tangible. Escape initially feels like retreat because Dante still interprets direction from the perspective he has just left.

Allegorically, the moment can suggest conversion: genuine progress requires a reorientation of the whole person. Yet the symbolism rests on a precise physical event, not merely on an abstract change of heart.

## 7. Crossing the Center: A New Meaning of “Up”

> 110 quand' io mi volsi, tu passasti 'l punto  
> (when I turned, you passed the point)
>
> 111 al qual si traggon d'ogne parte i pesi.  
> (to which weights are drawn from every side.)

Virgil explains that they have crossed the earth's center. In the Aristotelian cosmology underlying the scene, heavy bodies naturally move toward that central point.

Before the crossing, movement toward Lucifer's lower body was downward. After it, movement in the same general direction takes the travelers away from the center and therefore upward.

The episode depends on a spherical earth, not a flat one. Dante uses the accepted learned geography of his period to construct a moment in which ordinary spatial intuition fails.

> 118 Qui è da man, quando di là è sera;  
> (Here it is morning when there it is evening;)
>
> 119 e questi, che ne fé scala col pelo,  
> (and this one, who made himself a ladder for us with his hair,)
>
> 120 fitto è ancora sì come prim' era.  
> (is still fixed just as he was before.)

Lucifer has not turned upside down. The travelers have changed sides.

The apparent change from evening to morning similarly follows their passage beneath the opposite hemisphere. Dante's disorientation is corrected by an explanation of his new position.

The spiritual implication is subtle: liberation requires more than movement. Dante must learn to understand the world from beyond Hell's organizing perspective. What once seemed the ultimate depth has become the starting point of ascent.

## 8. The Fall That Reshaped the Earth

> 121 Da questa parte cadde giù dal cielo;  
> (From this side he fell down from heaven;)
>
> 122 e la terra, che pria di qua si sporse,  
> (and the land, which formerly extended on this side,)
>
> 123 per paura di lui fé del mar velo,  
> (for fear of him made a veil of the sea,)
>
> 124 e venne a l'emisperio nostro; e forse  
> (and came to our hemisphere; and perhaps)

Virgil presents a mythic explanation of the earth's arrangement. Lucifer fell from the side of the southern hemisphere. The land recoiled from him, hid beneath the sea, and emerged in the northern hemisphere.

The earth's personified fear makes Lucifer appear as something creation itself rejects. Evil is not creation's proper fulfillment but a deformation that leaves a wound.

This account combines inherited cosmology with Dante's imaginative sacred geography. The inhabited northern hemisphere centers spiritually on Jerusalem, the place of Christ's Passion; opposite it lies the region toward which the travelers are now moving.

> 125 per fuggir lui lasciò qui loco vòto  
> (to flee from him left an empty place here)
>
> 126 quella ch'appar di qua, e sù ricorse».  
> (that land which appears on this side, and rushed upward.”)

The land now visible in the southern hemisphere forms the mountain of Purgatory. Virgil suggests that its upward displacement left the underground cavity through which the travelers can escape.

The word “perhaps” in the preceding line qualifies this part of the explanation. Dante distinguishes the confident account of their position from a conjecture about how the landscape arose.

The resulting geography nevertheless has a powerful coherence: the infernal depth and the mountain of purification belong to one world. Lucifer's fall does not defeat divine order. Even the landscape marked by his rebellion contains a route toward restoration.

## 9. The Hidden Stream: Movement Returns

> 130 d'un ruscelletto che quivi discende  
> (of a little stream that descends there)
>
> 131 per la buca d'un sasso, ch'elli ha roso,  
> (through the opening of a rock, which it has worn away,)
>
> 132 col corso ch'elli avvolge, e poco pende.  
> (with the course it winds along, and which slopes gently.)

Beyond Lucifer, the travelers find a passage recognizable through the sound of running water rather than through sight. After the enormous spectacle of the frozen king, a small stream becomes the important guide.

The contrast with Cocytus is suggestive. Water imprisoned in ice belongs to the landscape of treachery; flowing water accompanies the movement out of it.

The stream is often connected by commentators with the waters descending from Purgatory, particularly Lethe, though this canto does not explicitly identify it. Its immediate narrative function is clear: it marks a hidden connection between Hell's depths and the world above.

Escape is not instantaneous. The path remains dark and difficult, but movement has become purposeful rather than compulsive.

## 10. The Stars: A Reopened World

> 133 Lo duca e io per quel cammino ascoso  
> (The guide and I by that hidden path)
>
> 134 intrammo a ritornar nel chiaro mondo;  
> (entered to return to the bright world;)
>
> 135 e sanza cura aver d'alcun riposo,  
> (and without taking thought for any rest,)
>
> 136 salimmo sù, el primo e io secondo,  
> (we climbed upward, he first and I second,)

The travelers leave without seeking rest. Unlike Lucifer's repetitive wingbeats, their exertion leads somewhere.

Virgil remains in front. Dante has survived Hell, but he is not yet spiritually independent or purified. The exit completes one stage of his education and opens another.

The “bright world” first means the world outside the subterranean prison. Its moral resonance comes from the contrast with everything Dante has witnessed: enclosure gives way to openness, and fixed punishment to the possibility of change.

> 137 tanto ch'i' vidi de le cose belle  
> (until I saw some of the beautiful things)
>
> 138 che porta 'l ciel, per un pertugio tondo.  
> (that heaven bears, through a round opening.)
>
> 139 E quindi uscimmo a riveder le stelle.  
> (And from there we came out to see the stars again.)

The first recovered view is limited: beauty appears through a small circular opening. Yet that glimpse is sufficient to announce a transformed horizon.

The stars are real celestial bodies, but they also evoke order, orientation, and divine governance. After a realm in which desire has become destructive or immobilized, Dante can once again look upward.

Each of the *Comedy*'s three canticles ends with the word *stelle*, “stars.” Here they signify renewed possibility, not completed salvation. Dante has escaped damnation's landscape; he must still undergo the education of desire that awaits him in *Purgatorio*.

## Epilogue — Evil's Impotence and the Beginning of Ascent

Canto XXXIV strips evil of its glamour. Lucifer is enormous but powerless, active but trapped, three-faced but incapable of communion. His supposed kingdom culminates in silence, mechanical violence, and ice.

The canto's decisive act is therefore not a battle but a passage. Guided by Virgil, Dante crosses the lowest point, discovers that his understanding of direction must change, and begins to climb. The return of the stars announces the possibility that Hell excludes: movement toward a good beyond oneself.
