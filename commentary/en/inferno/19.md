# Sacred Things for Sale: Allegory and Background in Inferno XIX

In Canto XIX, Dante and Virgil reach the third bolgia of Malebolge, the eighth circle of Hell, where fraud is punished. Here are the simoniacs: those who turn spiritual authority and sacred offices into merchandise. Dante's encounter with a buried pope becomes an indictment of ecclesiastical corruption—and a defense of the spiritual mission that corruption betrays.

The distinction between the poem's two timelines matters. Dante's journey takes place in 1300, but he wrote the canto later. Events that had already happened when he composed it can therefore appear as prophecies spoken by the damned.

## 1. Simony: The Betrayal of a Sacred Marriage

> 1 O Simon mago, o miseri seguaci  
> (O Simon Magus, O wretched followers)
>
> 2 che le cose di Dio, che di bontate  
> (who the things of God, which to goodness)
>
> 3 deon essere spose, e voi rapaci  
> (ought to be wedded, and you, rapacious ones,)
>
> 4 per oro e per argento avolterate,  
> (for gold and for silver prostitute,)

The canto opens with an accusation rather than a description. Simon Magus, whose story appears in Acts 8, tried to purchase the power of conferring the Holy Spirit. His name gave rise to *simony*: the buying and selling of spiritual offices, privileges, or sacred things.

Dante describes this transaction as a violation of marriage. The things of God should be joined to goodness, not exchanged for money. The verb *avolterate* makes commercial corruption into spiritual adultery: the clergy betray a relationship they should protect.

This metaphor establishes the canto's governing contrast. Spiritual authority is a trust, but the simoniacs treat it as property. Their fraud is particularly grave because their religious office gives their wrongdoing the appearance of holiness.

## 2. The Baptistery Turned Upside Down

> 16 Non mi parean men ampi né maggiori  
> (They seemed to me neither less spacious nor greater)
>
> 17 che que' che son nel mio bel San Giovanni,  
> (than those that are in my beautiful San Giovanni,)
>
> 18 fatti per loco d'i battezzatori;  
> (made as places for those who baptize;)

The sinners occupy round holes in the stone, which remind Dante of openings in Florence's Baptistery of San Giovanni. The precise arrangement of the medieval baptismal structure remains debated, but Dante explicitly associates these openings with the people administering baptism.

The comparison turns the bolgia into a grotesque counterpart of sacred architecture. Baptism marks entry into Christian life; these holes imprison those who have corrupted the institutions entrusted with sustaining that life.

The affectionate phrase “my beautiful San Giovanni” also matters. Dante's attack on corrupt churchmen does not arise from indifference to religion. His love for a real place of worship sharpens his disgust at its infernal parody.

> 19 l'un de li quali, ancor non è molt' anni,  
> (one of which, not many years ago,)
>
> 20 rupp' io per un che dentro v'annegava:  
> (I broke for someone who was drowning inside it:)
>
> 21 e questo sia suggel ch'ogn' omo sganni.  
> (and let this be a seal that undeceives everyone.)

Dante interrupts the description with an autobiographical claim: he once broke one of these structures to rescue someone drowning inside. He presents the statement as an authoritative correction of misunderstanding, though the circumstances are not independently established in detail.

The episode also creates a suggestive moral contrast. Dante damaged sacred property to save a human being; the simoniacs preserve the outward prestige of sacred institutions while exploiting the people those institutions should serve. The passage distinguishes genuine care from merely material reverence.

## 3. The Contrapasso: Inverted Bodies and Burning Feet

> 22 Fuor de la bocca a ciascun soperchiava  
> (Out of the mouth of each there protruded)
>
> 23 d'un peccator li piedi e de le gambe  
> (a sinner's feet and the legs)
>
> 24 infino al grosso, e l'altro dentro stava.  
> (up to the thick part, and the rest remained inside.)
>
> 25 Le piante erano a tutti accese intrambe;  
> (Both soles were aflame on every one of them;)

The sinners are buried headfirst, with only their lower legs exposed. Flames burn their soles, making their limbs jerk violently.

This is a *contrapasso*: a punishment that reveals the character of the sin. The simoniacs overturned the proper relationship between spiritual and material goods, subordinating heaven to earthly profit. Their bodies now enact that reversal.

Their position also evokes their attachment to earthly things: their heads are thrust down into stone rather than raised toward God. Those who should have guided others can neither stand upright nor see the people above them.

The fire admits a further religious resonance. Pentecostal fire signifies the Holy Spirit's empowering presence; here flame torments the feet of those who traded in spiritual authority. Together with the baptismal architecture, it makes the scene resemble a distorted sacred ceremony rather than merely an ingenious torture.

## 4. A Confession Without Repentance

> 49 Io stava come 'l frate che confessa  
> (I stood like the friar who shrives)
>
> 50 lo perfido assessin, che, poi ch'è fitto,  
> (the treacherous assassin, who, after being fixed in place,)
>
> 51 richiama lui per che la morte cessa.  
> (calls him back so that death is delayed.)

Virgil carries Dante down to the sinner whose movements are most violent. Leaning toward the buried man, Dante compares himself to a friar hearing the confession of an assassin condemned to death by burial.

The comparison invokes a contemporary form of execution. The condemned man calls the confessor back to postpone the completion of his punishment.

This resemblance turns Dante's conversation with a pope into an inverted pastoral encounter. The lay pilgrim occupies the confessor's position; the former spiritual father appears as a criminal in the earth.

Yet this is not a sacrament leading to absolution. The sinner will disclose his identity and wrongdoing, but disclosure in Hell is not saving repentance. Religious forms persist here as reminders of opportunities already lost.

## 5. The Expected Arrival of Boniface VIII

> 52 Ed el gridò: «Se' tu già costì ritto,  
> (And he cried: “Are you already standing there,)
>
> 53 se' tu già costì ritto, Bonifazio?  
> (are you already standing there, Boniface?)
>
> 54 Di parecchi anni mi mentì lo scritto.  
> (By several years the writing lied to me.)

The buried sinner mistakes Dante for Pope Boniface VIII. Because he cannot see his visitor, he assumes that the next pope destined for this punishment has arrived.

In the fictional year 1300, Boniface is still alive; he will die in 1303. The mistake draws on the limited foresight possessed by Dante's damned: they can perceive aspects of the future without clearly understanding the immediate present. The mysterious “writing” suggests a record or foreknowledge of Boniface's appointed arrival.

Dante thus condemns a living pope within the chronology of the journey. The scene makes future damnation appear not as a possibility but as something already expected in Hell.

> 55 Se' tu sì tosto di quell' aver sazio  
> (Are you so soon sated with that wealth)
>
> 56 per lo qual non temesti tòrre a 'nganno  
> (for which you did not fear to take by deceit)
>
> 57 la bella donna, e poi di farne strazio?».  
> (the beautiful lady, and then to ravage her?”)

The “beautiful lady” is the Church. The opening image of spiritual adultery returns, now directed against a particular pope: Boniface allegedly obtained his position deceitfully and then abused the Church entrusted to him.

The accusation belongs to the fierce controversies surrounding Boniface's accession after Celestine V's resignation. It also reflects Dante's political hostility toward a pope whose intervention in Florentine affairs helped bring about the defeat of Dante's faction and his exile.

These lines should therefore be read as Dante's polemical judgment, not as a neutral historical finding. Their larger claim, however, exceeds the personal feud: a pope who treats the Church as a source of wealth violates his relationship to her.

## 6. Nicholas III: The Church as a Family Purse

> 69 sappi ch'i' fui vestito del gran manto;  
> (know that I was clothed in the great mantle;)
>
> 70 e veramente fui figliuol de l'orsa,  
> (and truly I was a son of the she-bear,)
>
> 71 cupido sì per avanzar li orsatti,  
> (so greedy to advance the bear cubs,)
>
> 72 che sù l'avere e qui me misi in borsa.  
> (that above I put wealth, and here myself, into a purse.)

The speaker identifies himself indirectly as Pope Nicholas III, who reigned from 1277 to 1280. The “great mantle” signifies papal office, while the she-bear and her cubs point to his family, the Orsini.

His confession centers on nepotism. He sought wealth and influence to advance his relatives, treating the resources of the Church as instruments of family promotion.

The final line compresses his wrongdoing and punishment into a single image. In life he filled a purse with wealth; in Hell he has put himself into a purse-like hole. The accumulator becomes the accumulated object.

> 76 Là giù cascherò io altresì quando  
> (Down there I too shall fall when)
>
> 77 verrà colui ch'i' credea che tu fossi,  
> (the one whom I believed you to be arrives,)
>
> 78 allor ch'i' feci 'l sùbito dimando.  
> (when I made the sudden inquiry.)

Nicholas explains that other simoniacal popes lie below him. When Boniface arrives, Nicholas will be pushed deeper into the rock.

This is a terrifying parody of succession in office. Instead of receiving an apostolic inheritance through service, each corrupt successor occupies the same place of disgrace and drives his predecessor downward.

The poem does not place every pope in this sequence. Its target is a corrupted succession within the papacy, not the mere existence of papal authority.

## 7. Clement V: A Papacy Entangled with Royal Power

> 82 ché dopo lui verrà di più laida opra,  
> (for after him will come, of fouler deeds,)
>
> 83 di ver' ponente, un pastor sanza legge,  
> (from the direction of the west, a lawless shepherd,)
>
> 84 tal che convien che lui e me ricuopra.  
> (such that he must cover both him and me.)

The “lawless shepherd” is Clement V, the Gascon pope elected in 1305, who died in 1314. During his pontificate the papal court became established at Avignon, beginning the period commonly called the Avignon papacy.

Nicholas has already endured his punishment for almost twenty years in 1300. Boniface, he predicts, will occupy the exposed position for a shorter period before Clement displaces him.

The title “shepherd” intensifies the accusation. The pope's proper task is to guide and protect the flock; this shepherd violates the law he should uphold.

> 85 Nuovo Iasón sarà, di cui si legge  
> (He will be a new Jason, of whom one reads)
>
> 86 ne' Maccabei; e come a quel fu molle  
> (in Maccabees; and as yielding to that one was)
>
> 87 suo re, così fia lui chi Francia regge».  
> (his king, so to this one will be he who rules France.”)

This Jason is not the Greek hero who sought the Golden Fleece. He is the figure in 2 Maccabees 4 who obtained the Jewish high priesthood by promising money to King Antiochus IV.

The analogy makes Clement's relationship with Philip IV of France another instance of sacred office entangled with royal power. Dante presents the partnership as mutually accommodating and spiritually destructive.

The biblical reference also widens the canto's scope. Simony is not merely a contemporary Italian scandal: it belongs to an enduring pattern in which religious leadership becomes a commodity negotiated with rulers.

## 8. Apostolic Poverty Against Ecclesiastical Greed

> 90 «Deh, or mi dì: quanto tesoro volle  
> (“Ah, now tell me: how much treasure was required)
>
> 91 Nostro Segnore in prima da san Pietro  
> (by Our Lord first from Saint Peter)
>
> 92 ch'ei ponesse le chiavi in sua balìa?  
> (before he placed the keys in his keeping?)
>
> 93 Certo non chiese se non “Viemmi retro”.  
> (Certainly he asked only “Follow me”.)

Dante answers Nicholas with a scriptural test. What did Christ charge Peter for the authority represented by the keys? Nothing: he demanded discipleship.

The passage brings together the Gospel image of Peter's keys and Christ's call to follow him. Their conjunction establishes the principle that spiritual authority originates in vocation, not purchase.

Dante then reinforces the point through the election of Matthias to replace Judas: the apostles did not sell the vacant position. Apostolic beginnings expose the corruption of later practice.

Importantly, Dante distinguishes office from officeholder. He says that reverence for the papal keys prevents him from speaking even more harshly. His denunciation claims to defend the sacred authority that Nicholas abused.

> 104 ché la vostra avarizia il mondo attrista,  
> (for your avarice brings misery to the world,)
>
> 105 calcando i buoni e sollevando i pravi.  
> (trampling the good and raising up the wicked.)

The consequences extend beyond individual misconduct. When religious advancement depends on money and influence, good people are suppressed while the corrupt rise.

The inversion of the sinners' bodies thus reflects a social inversion. Simony turns both the human person and the moral order upside down. For Dante, ecclesiastical greed damages the world because spiritual institutions help determine whom society honors, empowers, and follows.

## 9. Revelation: The Church as Prostitute and Idolater

> 106 Di voi pastor s'accorse il Vangelista,  
> (Of you shepherds the Evangelist took notice,)
>
> 107 quando colei che siede sopra l'acque  
> (when she who sits upon the waters)
>
> 108 puttaneggiar coi regi a lui fu vista;  
> (was seen by him committing whoredom with kings;)

Dante invokes the Book of Revelation, traditionally attributed to John. Its vision of the great prostitute associated with kings becomes an image of ecclesiastical corruption.

This is a deliberately shocking application. The Church that should oppose spiritual Babylon has, through its corrupt leaders, come to resemble it. The faithful bride has become a prostitute through her dealings with worldly rulers.

The charge is not simply that religion encounters politics. It is that spiritual fidelity has been traded for wealth, protection, and power.

> 109 quella che con le sette teste nacque,  
> (she who was born with the seven heads,)
>
> 110 e da le diece corna ebbe argomento,  
> (and from the ten horns received support,)
>
> 111 fin che virtute al suo marito piacque.  
> (so long as virtue pleased her husband.)

Dante freely reshapes Revelation's imagery, combining features of the woman and the beast. The symbolism is not entirely settled.

A traditional interpretation associates the seven heads with the seven sacraments or the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit, and the ten horns with the Ten Commandments. On this reading, institutions originally supporting the Church become part of the image of its deformation.

The “husband” is commonly understood here as the pope in his relationship to the Church. The decisive change occurs when virtue ceases to govern that relationship. Dante's image is therefore historical and moral: something founded for holiness has been perverted.

> 112 Fatto v'avete dio d'oro e d'argento;  
> (You have made yourselves a god of gold and silver;)
>
> 113 e che altro è da voi a l'idolatre,  
> (and what other difference is there between you and the idolater,)
>
> 114 se non ch'elli uno, e voi ne orate cento?  
> (except that he worships one, and you worship a hundred?)

The accusation reaches its theological conclusion: simony is idolatry. Money has become the effective object of worship.

The contrast between one idol and a hundred is bitterly mocking. Those claiming authority over Christian worship multiply their false gods through their devotion to wealth.

This returns to the opening reference to gold and silver. What began as a corrupt transaction is now exposed as a rival religion.

## 10. Constantine's Donation and Virgil's Approval

> 115 Ahi, Costantin, di quanto mal fu matre,  
> (Ah, Constantine, of how much evil was mother,)
>
> 116 non la tua conversion, ma quella dote  
> (not your conversion, but that dowry)
>
> 117 che da te prese il primo ricco patre!».  
> (which the first wealthy father received from you!”)

Dante locates a major historical source of corruption in the supposed Donation of Constantine: the grant by which the emperor was believed to have transferred extensive temporal possessions and authority to Pope Sylvester I.

The document supporting this story was a medieval forgery, exposed as such most famously by Lorenzo Valla in the fifteenth century. Dante accepted the donation as historical, though elsewhere he challenged its legal validity.

His distinction is precise. Constantine's conversion was not the evil; the disastrous development was the enrichment of the papacy. The “dowry” carries the canto's marriage imagery into institutional history: material wealth helped corrupt a spiritual relationship.

Dante's criticism is therefore not a rejection of Christianity, but an argument that the Church's worldly possessions have compromised its vocation.

> 121 I' credo ben ch'al mio duca piacesse,  
> (I firmly believe that it pleased my guide,)
>
> 122 con sì contenta labbia sempre attese  
> (with so contented a face he listened throughout)
>
> 123 lo suon de le parole vere espresse.  
> (to the sound of the true words spoken.)

Virgil's approving expression validates Dante's denunciation within the poem. The guide associated with reason recognizes the truth of an argument grounded in Scripture, moral responsibility, and the proper purpose of authority.

Virgil then carries Dante back up the difficult slope toward the next bolgia. His practical care offers a quiet contrast to the predatory leadership exposed below: a true guide supports the person entrusted to him rather than exploiting that person.

## Epilogue — Spiritual Authority Betrayed, Not Denied

Canto XIX turns ecclesiastical corruption into a landscape of inversions: baptismal openings become prisons, popes are buried headfirst, succession becomes downward displacement, and the Church's marriage becomes prostitution.

Its central claim is that sacred authority cannot be treated as private property without betraying its source. Against purchased office, family enrichment, and royal bargains, Dante sets Christ's simple demand: follow me.

The canto's anger depends on its reverence. The simoniacs are condemned so fiercely because the spiritual goods they sold should never have been for sale.
