# Inferno XXIII: The Weight of False Appearances

Canto XXIII takes Dante and Virgil from the violent disorder of the barrators' ditch into the slow procession of the hypocrites. Both belong to Malebolge, the eighth circle of Hell, where different forms of fraud receive different punishments. The transition brings a change of atmosphere: frantic pursuit gives way to almost unbearable stillness.

Yet the two episodes remain connected. The demons disguise malice with apparent cooperation; the hypocrites disguise corruption with apparent virtue. Meanwhile, Virgil's care for Dante supplies a contrasting image of goodness that acts without needing to advertise itself.

## 1. Silence, Friars, and Aesop's Fable

> 1 Taciti, soli, sanza compagnia  
> (Silent, alone, without company)
>
> 2 n'andavam l'un dinanzi e l'altro dopo,  
> (we went on, one in front and the other behind,)
>
> 3 come frati minor vanno per via.  
> (as Friars Minor go along the road.)

After the quarrelling demons of the previous canto, this opening creates a sudden hush. The repeated expressions of isolation strip the scene down to the two travellers.

The comparison is specifically to the Friars Minor, the Franciscans. Their modest, single-file movement evokes humility and religious discipline. It also prepares an important contrast: later in the canto, religious clothing and religious titles will become associated with hypocrisy. Dante does not condemn religion indiscriminately; he distinguishes its authentic ideals from their counterfeit forms.

> 4 Vòlt' era in su la favola d'Isopo  
> (Turned toward the fable of Aesop was)
>
> 5 lo mio pensier per la presente rissa,  
> (my thought because of the recent brawl,)
>
> 6 dov' el parlò de la rana e del topo;  
> (where he spoke of the frog and the mouse;)

Dante recalls a medieval version of the fable in which a frog binds a mouse to itself and attempts to drown it, only for a bird of prey to seize them both. Treachery turns into mutual destruction.

The immediate parallel is the struggle between the demons Alichino and Calcabrina, whose quarrel leaves both trapped in boiling pitch. The precise assignment of each fable-animal to each participant is less important than the shared pattern: an attempted advantage produces a disastrous reversal. Fraud proves unstable even among its practitioners.

## 2. Fear and the Mirror of Thought

> 19 Già mi sentia tutti arricciar li peli  
> (Already I felt all my hairs bristling)
>
> 20 de la paura e stava in dietro intento,  
> (with fear, and I kept my attention behind me,)

Dante suspects that the humiliated demons will pursue the travellers to avenge their embarrassment. His fear is bodily before it is fully articulated: his hair rises, and his attention fixes on what may be approaching from behind.

This is not simply cowardice. The pursuit soon becomes real. In a realm governed by fraud, reasonable suspicion can be more accurate than confident trust.

> 25 E quei: «S'i' fossi di piombato vetro,  
> (And he: “If I were made of lead-backed glass,)
>
> 26 l'imagine di fuor tua non trarrei  
> (your outward image I would not draw)
>
> 27 più tosto a me, che quella dentro 'mpetro.  
> (more quickly to myself than I receive the one within.)

Virgil compares himself to a mirror: he receives Dante's inward thought as readily as a mirror receives an outward image. The technical detail refers to glass backed with lead or another reflective metallic layer.

The image presents the guide as emotionally attentive, not merely intellectually authoritative. Virgil, commonly read as an embodiment of human reason, recognizes the same danger as Dante and begins planning an escape.

Lead will soon reappear as the substance of the hypocrites' punishment. Here it helps an image become visible; there it becomes the hidden reality beneath a deceptive surface.

## 3. Virgil's Maternal Rescue

> 37 Lo duca mio di sùbito mi prese,  
> (My guide suddenly seized me,)
>
> 38 come la madre ch'al romore è desta  
> (like the mother who is awakened by the noise)
>
> 39 e vede presso a sé le fiamme accese,  
> (and sees the flames blazing close beside her,)
>
> 40 che prende il figlio e fugge e non s'arresta,  
> (who takes her child and flees and does not stop,)
>
> 41 avendo più di lui che di sé cura,  
> (having more concern for him than for herself,)

The demons appear before Virgil has finished speaking. He immediately takes Dante into his arms and slides down the steep slope into the next ditch.

The mother escaping a fire is one of the canto's most intimate comparisons. Her action is urgent, practical, and unselfconscious. She does not first consider her dignity or appearance; she protects the child.

Against the surrounding world of calculated deception, Virgil's goodness appears as spontaneous care. His authority is demonstrated through responsibility rather than display.

> 50 portandosene me sovra 'l suo petto,  
> (carrying me away upon his chest,)
>
> 51 come suo figlio, non come compagno.  
> (like his son, not like a companion.)

The shift from companion to son makes Dante's dependence explicit. Virgil's maternal action becomes paternal protection without contradiction: both images express a relationship deeper than a formal arrangement between guide and traveller.

The episode also complicates any reading of Virgil as cold or abstract reason. His rational guidance includes tenderness, courage, and the willingness to bear another person's weight.

## 4. Providence Sets Limits to Evil

> 55 ché l'alta provedenza che lor volle  
> (for the high providence that willed them)
>
> 56 porre ministri de la fossa quinta,  
> (to be placed as ministers of the fifth ditch,)
>
> 57 poder di partirs' indi a tutti tolle.  
> (takes from them all the power to leave it.)

The demons reach the ridge above the fugitives but cannot follow them into the sixth ditch. Their jurisdiction ends at the boundary of the fifth.

This limitation is theological as well as geographical. The demons are malicious, but they are not independent rivals to God. Even their punitive role operates within an order they did not create and cannot overturn.

Dante and Virgil escape through quick judgment and physical action, yet their safety ultimately depends on providence. The canto thus distinguishes the real danger posed by evil from any claim that evil possesses unlimited power.

## 5. Hypocrisy: Gold Outside, Lead Within

> 64 Di fuor dorate son, sì ch'elli abbaglia;  
> (On the outside they are gilded, so that it dazzles;)
>
> 65 ma dentro tutte piombo, e gravi tanto,  
> (but within entirely lead, and so heavy,)
>
> 66 che Federigo le mettea di paglia.  
> (that Frederick put them on made of straw.)

The hypocrites wear cloaks whose shining exterior conceals an immense weight of lead. This is the canto's central *contrapasso*: the punishment makes the structure of the sin physically visible.

Hypocrisy offers an attractive surface while hiding a corrupt interior. The gold represents the appearance of holiness or virtue; the lead reveals its oppressive falsity. What once helped these sinners obtain admiration now makes every movement a torment.

The preceding description compares the cloaks' cut to the habits associated with the monks of Cluny. The religious appearance matters, but the target is counterfeit sanctity, not monastic life as such.

The reference to Frederick II invokes a medieval story that he punished traitors with leaden garments exposed to heat. Its historical reliability is doubtful. Dante's comparison is hyperbolic: beside these infernal cloaks, Frederick's supposedly terrible garments would have seemed as light as straw.

## 6. Living Breath amid Exhausted Appearance

> 88 «Costui par vivo a l'atto de la gola;  
> (“This man seems alive from the movement of his throat;)
>
> 89 e s'e' son morti, per qual privilegio  
> (and if they are dead, by what privilege)
>
> 90 vanno scoperti de la grave stola?».  
> (do they go uncovered by the heavy robe?”)

Two sinners notice Dante's breathing and wonder why the travellers are not wearing the compulsory cloaks. The movement of his throat identifies him as a living body among the dead.

Their observation sharpens the difference between Dante and the souls he meets. His journey can still lead to change; theirs expresses an identity permanently fixed by judgment.

The word translated as robe, *stola*, also carries elevated and ecclesiastical associations. Clothing that might signify dignity has become an instrument of degradation. The hypocrites remain concerned with visible signs and apparent exemptions, but here those signs disclose a genuine difference rather than a fabricated superiority.

Their painfully slow movement supplies another reversal: Dante's ordinary walking seems like running to people crushed beneath the weight of their own false appearances.

## 7. The Jovial Friars and Florence's Broken Peace

> 103 Frati godenti fummo, e bolognesi;  
> (We were Jovial Friars, and Bolognese;)
>
> 104 io Catalano e questi Loderingo  
> (I Catalano and this man Loderingo)

The speakers are Catalano dei Malavolti and Loderingo degli Andalò. They belonged to the military-religious order commonly called the Jovial Friars, founded with aims that included peacemaking and the defence of vulnerable people.

The order's popular name had acquired associations with comfortable living and insufficient religious seriousness. In this setting, its declared ideals stand uneasily beside the friars' infernal condition.

Catalano was associated with the Guelf party and Loderingo with the Ghibellines. Their differing allegiances supposedly qualified them to act together as impartial governors.

> 105 nomati, e da tua terra insieme presi  
> (named, and chosen together by your city)
>
> 106 come suole esser tolto un uom solingo,  
> (as one solitary man is usually chosen,)
>
> 107 per conservar sua pace; e fummo tali,  
> (to preserve its peace; and we were such,)
>
> 108 ch'ancor si pare intorno dal Gardingo».  
> (that it still shows around the Gardingo.”)

Florence appointed the two men jointly as *podestà* in 1266, entrusting to a pair the office normally held by one outsider. The arrangement promised balance above faction.

The reference to the Gardingo points to the area associated with the Uberti, a leading Ghibelline family, whose buildings were destroyed during partisan violence. The ruined city becomes evidence against the governors' claim to have preserved peace.

Their precise historical responsibility is more complicated than Dante's compressed indictment. Within the poem, however, they exemplify political hypocrisy: the language of reconciliation conceals, or fails to restrain, destructive factional interests.

## 8. Caiaphas: Expediency Crucified

> 116 consigliò i Farisei che convenia  
> (advised the Pharisees that it was expedient)
>
> 117 porre un uom per lo popolo a' martìri.  
> (to subject one man to torment for the people.)

Catalano identifies a naked figure pinned to the ground: Caiaphas, the high priest associated in John's Gospel with the argument that one man should die rather than the whole nation perish.

The hypocrisy lies in presenting an unjust death as a measure of public welfare. A supposedly responsible political calculation conceals the sacrifice of an innocent person.

In the Gospel, Caiaphas's words also possess an unintended prophetic meaning: Christ's death will indeed have a saving significance beyond the speaker's intention. Dante's punishment concentrates instead on the guilt of the human counsel.

> 118 Attraversato è, nudo, ne la via,  
> (He lies crosswise, naked, in the road,)
>
> 119 come tu vedi, ed è mestier ch'el senta  
> (as you see, and it is necessary that he feel)
>
> 120 qualunque passa, come pesa, pria.  
> (whoever passes, how much he weighs, first.)

Caiaphas must bear the weight of every passing hypocrite. The man who recommended that one person suffer for others is himself made the suffering foundation beneath the whole procession.

Unlike the cloaked sinners, he is naked: nothing disguises his shame. His posture grotesquely recalls the Crucifixion, but without its voluntary love or redemptive purpose.

Catalano adds that Caiaphas's father-in-law Annas and other members of the council suffer similarly. Dante's presentation belongs to a medieval Christian polemical tradition that could generalize responsibility for Christ's death in anti-Jewish ways. Its theological assumptions should be recognized as part of that historical context, not accepted as grounds for collective blame against Jewish people.

## 9. The Broken Bridge and the Failure of Trust

> 136 salvo che 'n questo è rotto e nol coperchia;  
> (except that over this one it is broken and does not cover it;)
>
> 137 montar potrete su per la ruina,  
> (you will be able to climb up by the wreckage,)
>
> 138 che giace in costa e nel fondo soperchia».  
> (which lies on the slope and rises above the bottom.”)

Asked for an exit, Catalano explains that a bridge crosses the other ditches but is broken over this one. Its fallen stones nevertheless provide a route upward.

This information exposes Malacoda's earlier deception. The demon had suggested that another crossing would be available; the travellers now discover that no intact bridge spans the sixth ditch.

The collapse belongs to the earthquake associated elsewhere in the poem with Christ's death. Sacred history has altered Hell's very architecture.

There is also a suggestive practical reversal: the obstruction itself becomes the means of escape. Virgil must abandon the convenient route supplied by a liar and work with the difficult terrain actually before him.

## 10. The Devil's Lie and Reason's Humiliation

> 142 E 'l frate: «Io udi' già dire a Bologna  
> (And the friar: “I once heard it said in Bologna)
>
> 143 del diavol vizi assai, tra ' quali udi'  
> (of many vices of the devil, among which I heard)
>
> 144 ch'elli è bugiardo, e padre di menzogna».  
> (that he is a liar, and the father of falsehood.”)

When Virgil recognizes the deception, Catalano answers with a cuttingly obvious lesson: devils lie. The final phrase echoes John 8:44.

The appeal to something heard in Bologna, a celebrated centre of learning, gives the reply a dry scholarly flavour. Virgil has been outmanoeuvred, and a damned hypocrite supplies the elementary correction.

The irony does not make Catalano morally superior. It shows that a truthful statement may come from a corrupt speaker, just as apparently useful advice may come from a malicious one. Discernment requires more than judging a statement by its confident delivery.

Virgil leaves visibly angry. Whether his anger falls chiefly on the demon, himself, or the friar's mockery is not settled. His authority survives, but it is chastened: human reason remains indispensable without being infallible.

## Epilogue — What False Appearances Make Us Carry

Canto XXIII turns deception into weight. Golden cloaks expose the burden beneath counterfeit virtue; failed peacemakers reveal the violence behind public assurances; Caiaphas embodies the cruelty hidden in arguments of expediency.

Against these distortions stands Virgil's uncalculated rescue of Dante. Genuine care bears another person's weight; hypocrisy burdens others while seeking honour for itself.

The canto ends not with perfect understanding but with corrected error. Progress requires seeing through appearances, accepting the limits of one's judgment, and continuing by the difficult path that truth makes available.
