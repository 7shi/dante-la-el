# Inferno XXI: Corruption, Concealment, and the Comedy of Evil

In Canto XXI, Dante and Virgil reach the fifth ditch of Malebolge, the eighth circle of Hell. Here the **barrators**—people who sold or manipulated public office for private gain—are submerged in boiling pitch and guarded by devils called the Malebranche.

The canto combines political satire, physical terror, and coarse comedy. Its central question is not simply how corruption is punished, but how fraud works: through concealment, intimidation, and reassuring words that cannot be trusted.

## 1. A “Comedy” That Chooses What to Tell

> 1 Così di ponte in ponte, altro parlando  
> (Thus from bridge to bridge, speaking of other things)
>
> 2 che la mia comedìa cantar non cura,  
> (that my comedy does not care to sing,)

Dante begins by acknowledging that his poem does not record everything said during the journey. The conversation between the bridges is omitted; the encounter with the barrators deserves to be narrated.

The word *comedìa* also helps frame the canto's extraordinary stylistic freedom. Medieval comedy was not simply a work intended to provoke laughter. Among other distinctions, it could accommodate humble language and everyday experience rather than maintain the elevated register associated with tragedy. Here Dante's sacred poem includes shipbuilding, municipal corruption, threatening dogs, kitchen work, and finally a bodily obscenity.

This range is essential to the canto's meaning. Evil is not always grand or impressive: it can be petty, ridiculous, and degrading without becoming any less dangerous.

## 2. The Venetian Arsenal: Useful Work Turned into Punishment

> 7 Quale ne l'arzanà de' Viniziani  
> (As in the arsenal of the Venetians)
>
> 8 bolle l'inverno la tenace pece  
> (the clinging pitch boils in winter)
>
> 9 a rimpalmare i legni lor non sani,  
> (to coat anew their unsound ships,)

The Venetian Arsenal was a major centre of shipbuilding and naval maintenance. Dante evokes it as a busy collective workplace: some workers build ships, while others repair hulls, make oars, twist rigging, or mend sails.

Pitch waterproofed vessels and helped keep them seaworthy. The comparison therefore begins with a material serving a useful civic purpose. The Arsenal represents coordinated labour sustaining a powerful maritime community.

> 16 tal, non per foco ma per divin' arte,  
> (so, not through fire but through divine art,)
>
> 17 bollia là giuso una pegola spessa,  
> (a thick pitch boiled down there,)
>
> 18 che 'nviscava la ripa d'ogne parte.  
> (which coated the bank with stickiness on every side.)

In Hell, the same material serves a radically different purpose. Instead of preserving ships, it imprisons corrupt officials.

The explicit cause of its boiling is “divine art,” not an ordinary furnace. The devils may dominate the action, but they do not create the punishment or possess ultimate authority over it.

The contrast also suggests a civic inversion: the Arsenal's workers maintain the instruments of communal prosperity, whereas barrators exploit the institutions they ought to maintain. Their punishment turns a substance associated with preservation into an instrument of confinement.

## 3. Opaque Pitch and the Hidden Workings of Fraud

> 19 I' vedea lei, ma non vedëa in essa  
> (I could see it, but I could not see in it)
>
> 20 mai che le bolle che 'l bollor levava,  
> (anything except the bubbles that the boiling raised,)
>
> 21 e gonfiar tutta, e riseder compressa.  
> (and all of it swell, and settle back compressed.)

Dante can see the pitch but cannot see through it. Its surface reveals movement while concealing what causes that movement below.

This is central to the punishment's **contrapasso**, the relationship between a sin and its infernal consequence. Barratry operates through hidden arrangements: private payments, secret favours, and decisions whose public appearance conceals their real motivation. The sinners who concealed their transactions are now themselves concealed.

The pitch's stickiness also gives physical form to corruption's entangling power. These are suggestive correspondences rather than a symbolic code explicitly explained by the narrator. What the poem makes unmistakable is the loss of visibility: neither Dante nor the reader can immediately inspect the human world beneath the surface.

That uncertainty prepares for a canto in which appearances and assurances repeatedly mislead.

## 4. Lucca: Public Office for Sale

> 38 ecco un de li anzïan di Santa Zita!  
> (here is one of the elders of Saint Zita!)

A black devil arrives carrying a sinner and identifies him through his civic office. The “elders” were magistrates of Lucca; Saint Zita, a revered local holy woman, stands for the city.

The sinner is not securely identified by the text. What matters first is that an office associated with public responsibility has become a passport to this ditch.

The devil's delivery makes the corrupt magistrate look like a piece of cargo. His earthly dignity has disappeared: he is handled, transported, and thrown away.

> 41 ogn' uom v'è barattier, fuor che Bonturo;  
> (everyone there is a barrator, except Bonturo;)
>
> 42 del no, per li denar, vi si fa ita».  
> (out of no, for money, there they make yes”.)

The apparent exception is sarcastic. Bonturo Dati was a prominent Lucchese political figure associated with corruption: exempting him is a way of naming the most notorious example.

The Latin *ita*, meaning “yes” here, gives the accusation a sharp administrative flavour. Money changes an official refusal into approval. Public judgment no longer follows justice or communal interest; it follows payment.

This subject had particular force for Dante. In 1302, the Florentine proceedings that led to his exile included accusations of barratry, which he rejected. The canto is not merely an autobiographical defence, but its attack on corrupt civic government belongs to the political world that destroyed his own career.

## 5. Sacred Protection Replaced by Hooks

> 48 gridar: «Qui non ha loco il Santo Volto!  
> (they shouted: “Here the Holy Face has no place!)
>
> 49 qui si nuota altrimenti che nel Serchio!  
> (here one swims differently than in the Serchio!)

After the sinner surfaces, the devils mock him with references to Lucca. The **Holy Face**, or *Volto Santo*, is the celebrated wooden crucifix venerated in the city; the Serchio is its nearby river.

Their taunt brings familiar sacred and geographical landmarks into an utterly hostile setting. The pitch is no local swimming place, and the sinner cannot rely on civic religious identity to protect him.

This is demonic mockery, not a rejection by Dante of the Holy Face's sanctity. The sharper moral implication is that association with holy things does not cancel corrupt conduct.

> 53 disser: «Coverto convien che qui balli,  
> (they said: “Covered you must dance here,)
>
> 54 sì che, se puoi, nascosamente accaffi».  
> (so that, if you can, you may grab secretly”.)

The devils turn the sinner's former behaviour into a vicious joke. He must remain hidden, just as his illicit grabbing once took place in secret.

“Dance” grotesquely disguises suffering as entertainment. The surrounding comparison with cooks forcing meat beneath the surface of a cauldron makes the degradation still more concrete: the magistrate becomes something to be prodded and cooked.

The devils enforce punishment, but their delight is sadistic. The poem does not ask us to mistake their cruelty for moral goodness merely because they operate within Hell.

## 6. Virgil Appeals to an Authority Above Hell

> 82 sanza voler divino e fato destro?  
> (without divine will and favourable destiny?)
>
> 83 Lascian' andar, ché nel cielo è voluto  
> (Let us go, for in heaven it is willed)
>
> 84 ch'i' mostri altrui questo cammin silvestro».  
> (that I show another this wild path”.)

Virgil confronts the devils and demands safe passage on the grounds that Dante's journey is divinely authorized. Malacoda, their spokesman, responds by lowering his hook and ordering that the travellers not be harmed.

This establishes the hierarchy underlying the episode. However threatening the devils appear, their power is subordinate. They cannot simply overturn the heavenly purpose of the journey.

Allegorically, Virgil often represents human reason, but this scene should not be reduced to a neat equation. He is also a particular guide who knows how to assert the authority granted to him. His immediate success is real.

Yet the canto will distinguish between **compelling an enemy to acknowledge superior authority** and **knowing whether that enemy is telling the truth**. Virgil can accomplish the first without being immune to deception in the second.

## 7. Caprona: Safe Conduct Among Enemies

> 94 così vid' ïo già temer li fanti  
> (thus I once saw the foot soldiers fear)
>
> 95 ch'uscivan patteggiati di Caprona,  
> (who were leaving Caprona under agreed terms,)
>
> 96 veggendo sé tra nemici cotanti.  
> (seeing themselves among so many enemies.)

When Dante emerges from hiding, the devils crowd forward. Their agreement not to attack provides little emotional reassurance.

He compares his fear to that of soldiers leaving Caprona after a negotiated surrender. The reference recalls the capture of the fortress near Pisa in 1289, during a campaign in which Dante participated.

The comparison places infernal danger within the experience of Italian warfare. A surrender agreement may formally guarantee safety, but the vulnerable soldiers must still pass through armed enemies.

Likewise, Dante understands the difference between an agreement and the hostile intentions of those expected to honour it. His fear is not simply cowardice. It registers something that Virgil's confidence risks overlooking: a promise does not transform the character of its maker.

## 8. Malacoda's Lie: Accurate History, False Directions

> 109 E se l'andare avante pur vi piace,  
> (And if going onward still pleases you,)
>
> 110 andatevene su per questa grotta;  
> (make your way along this rocky bank;)
>
> 111 presso è un altro scoglio che via face.  
> (nearby is another ridge that provides a way.)

Malacoda correctly says that the bridge immediately ahead is broken. He then falsely claims that another crossing remains available nearby.

The deception will become clear later: the bridges across the sixth ditch have all collapsed. His directions therefore mix a true account of the immediate obstacle with a false promise about the route onward.

This is a particularly appropriate danger in the circle of fraud. Malacoda does not merely deny an obvious fact. He presents himself as a helpful local authority whose knowledge can solve the travellers' problem.

> 112 Ier, più oltre cinqu' ore che quest' otta,  
> (Yesterday, five hours later than this hour,)
>
> 113 mille dugento con sessanta sei  
> (one thousand two hundred and sixty-six)
>
> 114 anni compié che qui la via fu rotta.  
> (years were completed since the road here was broken.)

The elaborate dating strengthens his appearance of reliability. The collapse belongs to the earthquake associated with Christ's death, which the poem presents as having damaged Hell itself.

The figure of 1,266 years, understood through Dante's chronology of Christ's life, places the journey in 1300. The relative timing also helps locate this encounter within the Easter sequence of the journey.

The theological background matters: Christ's victory has physically marked the infernal landscape. But Malacoda turns knowledge of that sacred history into an aid to deception. Precision is not the same thing as honesty; a speaker can supply accurate details while directing a listener toward a false conclusion.

## 9. The Malebranche: An Official Escort of Predators

> 121 Libicocco vegn' oltre e Draghignazzo,  
> (Let Libicocco come forward, and Draghignazzo,)
>
> 122 Cirïatto sannuto e Graffiacane  
> (tusked Ciriatto and Graffiacane)
>
> 123 e Farfarello e Rubicante pazzo.  
> (and Farfarello and mad Rubicante.)

Malacoda appoints ten devils to patrol the pitch and escort the travellers, with Barbariccia in command. The roll call gives the group a grotesque individuality.

The names work through sound and suggestion as well as meaning. Some evoke animals, claws, disfigurement, or violent temper; “tusked” Ciriatto is explicitly bestial. Not every name has a certain derivation, so they should not all be treated as transparent allegorical labels.

Collectively, however, they make the escort sound anything but reassuring. This is an organized company whose official function barely disguises its appetite for aggression.

The arrangement also darkly resembles earthly administration. There is a leader, a subordinate commander, an assigned patrol, and instructions about whom to protect and whom to punish. Hell's bureaucracy mirrors the compromised civic institutions whose officials it torments.

## 10. Misread Threats and the Trumpet of the Body

> 133 Ed elli a me: «Non vo' che tu paventi;  
> (And he to me: “I do not want you to be afraid;)
>
> 134 lasciali digrignar pur a lor senno,  
> (just let them grind their teeth as they please,)
>
> 135 ch'e' fanno ciò per li lessi dolenti».  
> (for they do that for the suffering boiled ones”.)

Dante asks to proceed without the escort. Virgil dismisses his concern, explaining that the devils' threatening expressions are intended for the damned rather than for them.

The disagreement exposes a limit in Virgil's judgment. He interprets the devils' behaviour in the most reassuring way, while Dante recognizes continuing hostility.

Allegorically, reason becomes vulnerable when confidence outruns evidence. Yet Virgil's error does not mean reason is useless. The more precise lesson is that legitimate authority and rational assurance do not eliminate the need for vigilance when dealing with fraud.

> 137 ma prima avea ciascun la lingua stretta  
> (but first each had held his tongue)
>
> 138 coi denti, verso lor duca, per cenno;  
> (between his teeth, toward their leader, as a signal;)
>
> 139 ed elli avea del cul fatto trombetta.  
> (and he had made a trumpet of his arse.)

The devils exchange a signal, and Barbariccia answers with a fart. The canto ends by replacing the dignified trumpet of military command with the lowest bodily imitation imaginable.

This ending completes the degradation running through the episode. Magistrates become cargo and meat; official protection becomes a dangerous escort; military discipline becomes obscene performance.

The comedy does not neutralize the danger. Instead, it strips evil of grandeur. These devils are ridiculous, but their hooks remain real, and their apparent cooperation remains untrustworthy.

## Epilogue — Corruption Beneath the Surface

Canto XXI makes concealment both a punishment and a problem of interpretation. The pitch hides the barrators, while Malacoda's useful information hides a lie. Corrupt public office and fraudulent speech share the same structure: an outward claim to service conceals a private intention.

Against this instability stands the divine authority permitting Dante's journey. Yet authorization does not spare the travellers fear or mistaken judgment. They must still learn how to read what confronts them.

The canto's coarse laughter therefore has a serious purpose: it exposes corruption as degrading rather than magnificent, while warning that even a ridiculous enemy can deceive.
