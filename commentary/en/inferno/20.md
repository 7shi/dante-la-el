# Looking Backward: Allegory and Background in Dante's *Inferno*, Canto 20

In the fourth bolgia of the eighth circle of Hell, Dante encounters diviners: people who claimed to discover the future through prophecy, astrology, or magical practices. Their heads are twisted backward, forcing them to walk in reverse.

The punishment makes their spiritual error visible. Yet the canto does more than condemn forbidden knowledge. It tests Dante's compassion, revisits classical mythology, examines the origins of Virgil's hometown, and distinguishes the legitimate observation of nature from the fraudulent claim to master its secrets.

## 1. A Procession of Distorted Worship

> 7 e vidi gente per lo vallon tondo  
> (and I saw people through the circular valley)
>
> 8 venir, tacendo e lagrimando, al passo  
> (coming, silent and weeping, at the pace)
>
> 9 che fanno le letane in questo mondo.  
> (that the litany processions keep in this world.)

The damned advance with the slow, solemn movement of a religious procession. The reference to litanies recalls public acts of prayer, often performed to ask for divine assistance or protection.

Here, however, the procession offers no hope of deliverance. The sinners weep but do not pray aloud; their ritual movement has become an endless punishment. The resemblance to worship is therefore an inversion: those who sought knowledge through forbidden spiritual means now participate in a procession emptied of its saving purpose.

The setting matters. This is Malebolge, the region of fraudulent wrongdoing. Dante treats divination not simply as an intellectual mistake but as an abuse of the human desire for certainty: the diviner claims access to truths that other people cannot verify.

## 2. The Body Becomes the Meaning of the Sin

> 13 ché da le reni era tornato 'l volto,  
> (for the face was turned toward the loins,)
>
> 14 e in dietro venir li convenia,  
> (and they had to move backward,)
>
> 15 perché 'l veder dinanzi era lor tolto.  
> (because seeing ahead was taken from them.)

This is one of the clearest examples of *contrapasso*: punishment that answers the sin through resemblance, opposition, or both.

The diviners tried to look too far ahead. Now they cannot look ahead at all. Their supposed superiority of vision has become a grotesque incapacity to perform the simplest human action: walking while looking where they are going.

The punishment also makes an inward disorder outwardly visible. Intelligence should help human beings act responsibly within their limits. Here, the effort to escape those limits has dislocated the relationship between sight, movement, and purpose.

Dante is not condemning every attempt to anticipate events. Practical foresight and observation are not identical to divination. The target is the presumptuous claim to possess hidden knowledge of the future, especially through magical or spiritually illicit means.

## 3. Dante's Tears and Virgil's Rebuke

> 22 quando la nostra imagine di presso  
> (when our human image, close at hand,)
>
> 23 vidi sì torta, che 'l pianto de li occhi  
> (I saw so twisted that the weeping of the eyes)
>
> 24 le natiche bagnava per lo fesso.  
> (bathed the buttocks along the cleft.)

Dante first responds not to an abstract category of sin but to the destruction of recognizable humanity. The phrase “our human image” joins the observer to the punished. These bodies are not wholly alien: they share his form.

For a Christian reader, the phrase may also evoke humanity's creation in God's image. The distortion is therefore more than physical ugliness. It represents the degradation of a nature originally endowed with dignity.

The degrading course of the tears intensifies the scene. Weeping, normally visible on the face and capable of awakening sympathy, now travels down the back of the body. Even the expression of grief has been anatomically disordered.

> 28 Qui vive la pietà quand' è ben morta;  
> (Here pity lives when it is wholly dead;)
>
> 29 chi è più scellerato che colui  
> (who is more wicked than the one)
>
> 30 che al giudicio divin passion comporta?  
> (who brings compassion to the divine judgment?)

Virgil answers with a deliberately troubling paradox. The Italian *pietà* can suggest both pity and piety: rightly ordered reverence for God requires the death of a pity that implicitly condemns divine justice.

The distinction is not simply between kindness and cruelty. Within the poem's theology, the danger is allowing immediate emotion to become a verdict against God's judgment. Dante the pilgrim must learn that feeling strongly does not necessarily mean understanding rightly.

Nevertheless, the poem does not erase the force of his tears. It first makes the reader experience the horror of the punishment and only then supplies Virgil's correction. The tension between human sympathy and the claimed perfection of divine justice remains part of the canto's power.

## 4. Amphiaraus: Seeing Too Far Ahead

> 37 Mira c'ha fatto petto de le spalle;  
> (Look how he has made a chest of his shoulders;)
>
> 38 perché volle veder troppo davante,  
> (because he wished to see too far ahead,)
>
> 39 di retro guarda e fa retroso calle.  
> (he looks behind and makes his way backward.)

Virgil's first named example is Amphiaraus, the warrior and prophet associated with the expedition of the Seven against Thebes. Knowing that the campaign would bring his death, he initially tried to avoid joining it. During the fighting, the earth opened and swallowed him.

Dante knew this story through the classical tradition, especially Statius's *Thebaid*. In the canto, the ancient catastrophe becomes the beginning of a descent that ends before Minos, the judge who assigns souls their places in Hell.

The quoted lines explicitly explain the punishment. “Too far ahead” is the crucial qualification: the fault is not sight itself but the will to exceed its rightful limits.

Amphiaraus also exposes the inadequacy of foreknowledge as salvation. Knowing what will happen does not necessarily give someone the power to escape it, much less the wisdom to live well.

## 5. Tiresias and Aruns: Transformation and Elevated Vision

> 40 Vedi Tiresia, che mutò sembiante  
> (See Tiresias, who changed his appearance)
>
> 41 quando di maschio femmina divenne,  
> (when from male he became female,)
>
> 42 cangiandosi le membra tutte quante;  
> (changing every one of his bodily members;)

Tiresias, the Theban prophet, belongs to a different kind of story about crossing boundaries. In the myth recounted by Ovid, he struck two intertwined serpents and became a woman. Years later, another encounter with the serpents restored him to male form.

His experience of both sexes subsequently qualified him to arbitrate a dispute between Jupiter and Juno. In that larger narrative, blindness and prophetic knowledge become linked: ordinary sight is lost while extraordinary insight is gained.

Dante now places the celebrated seer among those whose vision has been physically perverted. The classical prestige of prophetic knowledge does not exempt its possessor from Christian judgment.

The sexual transformation is background, not the offense for which Tiresias is placed here. His defining role in this bolgia is that of a diviner.

> 49 ebbe tra ' bianchi marmi la spelonca  
> (had among the white marbles the cave)
>
> 50 per sua dimora; onde a guardar le stelle  
> (as his dwelling; from there, to observe the stars)
>
> 51 e 'l mar non li era la veduta tronca.  
> (and the sea, his view was not cut off.)

The next figure, Aruns, is an Etruscan diviner known from Lucan's *Civil War*. Dante associates him with the mountains above the territory of Luni and Carrara, famous for their white marble.

His elevated dwelling gave him an unobstructed view of the heavens and sea. The contrast with his present condition is pointed: someone once positioned to survey an immense expanse can no longer look straight ahead.

The cave and mountains also show Dante's method of joining literary antiquity to recognizable Italian geography. These are not merely remote mythological figures. Their stories are brought into contact with landscapes familiar to Dante's world.

## 6. Manto and the Turn toward Geography

> 55 Manto fu, che cercò per terre molte;  
> (was Manto, who wandered through many lands;)
>
> 56 poscia si puose là dove nacqu' io;  
> (afterward she settled where I was born;)
>
> 57 onde un poco mi piace che m'ascolte.  
> (therefore I wish you to listen to me a little.)

Manto, the daughter of Tiresias, provides the transition from a catalogue of diviners to an extended account of Mantua, Virgil's hometown.

After her father's death and the subjugation of Thebes, she wandered before settling in Italy. Her story allows Virgil to become not just a guide to Hell but an interpreter of his own place of origin.

The digression is unusually long, but it belongs to the canto's concern with knowledge. Instead of revealing secret future events, Virgil traces a landscape through publicly describable features: mountains, a lake, a river, and marshland.

> 76 Tosto che l'acqua a correr mette co,  
> (As soon as the water begins to run,)
>
> 77 non più Benaco, ma Mencio si chiama  
> (it is called no longer Benaco, but Mincio)
>
> 78 fino a Governol, dove cade in Po.  
> (as far as Governolo, where it falls into the Po.)

Benaco is Lake Garda. Its outflow becomes the Mincio, which passes through the Mantuan landscape before joining the Po.

The careful tracing of water offers a different model of explanation from divination. One feature follows from another through an intelligible physical sequence. Water leaves the lake, becomes a river, and spreads into low-lying ground.

This does not make Virgil's account modern science, but it does give the passage a distinctive sobriety. In a canto about distorted claims to knowledge, the reader encounters sustained attention to the actual shape of the world.

## 7. Mantua's Foundation: Magic, Community, and Political Fraud

> 85 Lì, per fuggire ogne consorzio umano,  
> (There, to flee all human fellowship,)
>
> 86 ristette con suoi servi a far sue arti,  
> (she remained with her servants to practice her arts,)
>
> 87 e visse, e vi lasciò suo corpo vano.  
> (and lived, and there left her empty body.)

Manto chooses an isolated patch of land in the marshes, where she practices her arts and eventually dies.

Her withdrawal contrasts with the community that will later occupy the place. She seeks separation from human society; the future inhabitants gather together. The contrast suggests two different uses of the same landscape: a refuge for private magical practice and a defensible site for communal life.

The phrase “empty body” marks the separation of body and soul at death. The account strips away any suggestion that her remains possess a continuing sacred or magical authority.

> 91 Fer la città sovra quell' ossa morte;  
> (They built the city over those dead bones;)
>
> 92 e per colei che 'l loco prima elesse,  
> (and after her who first chose the place,)
>
> 93 Mantüa l'appellar sanz' altra sorte.  
> (they called it Mantua, without any further casting of lots.)

The settlers name the city after Manto, but do not consult an oracle or determine its name through divination. “Without any further casting of lots” is particularly significant in this setting: a city associated with a sorceress receives a foundation story that does not depend on a magical act.

Virgil's account also differs from the *Aeneid*, where Ocnus, Manto's son, is credited with founding Mantua. Dante thus makes his character Virgil authorize a version that revises the tradition found in Virgil's own poem.

This complicates literary authority. The ancient poet is indispensable, but his inherited narratives are not beyond correction.

> 94 Già fuor le genti sue dentro più spesse,  
> (Once its people were more numerous within,)
>
> 95 prima che la mattia da Casalodi  
> (before the foolishness of Casalodi)
>
> 96 da Pinamonte inganno ricevesse.  
> (received deception from Pinamonte.)

The foundation narrative ends with recent political history. Alberto da Casalodi lost control of Mantua after being manipulated by Pinamonte dei Bonacolsi, who seized power in 1272.

According to the traditional account, Pinamonte encouraged measures against prominent families that weakened Casalodi's support and prepared his overthrow. Dante links the resulting political upheaval to the city's diminished population.

The episode brings fraud back into view in an ordinary political form. A community does not need supernatural manipulation to be ruined: ambition, deception, and foolish trust are sufficient.

## 8. Eurypylus and the Problem of Literary Authority

> 112 Euripilo ebbe nome, e così 'l canta  
> (Eurypylus was his name, and thus sings of him)
>
> 113 l'alta mia tragedìa in alcun loco:  
> (my lofty tragedy in a certain passage:)
>
> 114 ben lo sai tu che la sai tutta quanta.  
> (you know it well, you who know it all.)

Virgil next identifies Eurypylus, whom the canto associates with Calchas and the determination of the Greeks' departure from Aulis for Troy.

The “lofty tragedy” is the *Aeneid*. Here “tragedy” identifies an elevated poetic mode, not necessarily a stage play or a narrative defined simply by an unhappy ending. Virgil's remark also acknowledges Dante's intimate knowledge of his poem.

There is, however, a difficulty. In Book 2 of the *Aeneid*, Eurypylus appears as someone sent to consult Apollo's oracle, rather than straightforwardly as an augur who helped determine the original departure. The reference also occurs within Sinon's deceptive account to the Trojans.

Commentators differ over how to explain Dante's treatment. What remains clear is the canto's dependence on classical literature even while it judges the religious practices that literature preserves. Poetic greatness and theological reliability are not the same thing.

## 9. Learned Magicians and Everyday Fortune-Tellers

> 115 Quell' altro che ne' fianchi è così poco,  
> (That other one who is so slight in the flanks,)
>
> 116 Michele Scotto fu, che veramente  
> (was Michael Scot, who truly)
>
> 117 de le magiche frode seppe 'l gioco.  
> (knew the game of magical frauds.)

Michael Scot was a learned thirteenth-century translator and intellectual associated with the court of Emperor Frederick II. His work helped transmit Arabic learning and Aristotelian philosophy into Latin Europe.

He also acquired a powerful reputation as an astrologer and magician. Dante presents that legendary identity here, rather than offering a balanced account of his scholarship.

The distinction matters: the passage should not be read as a general condemnation of translation, philosophy, or scientific learning. It shows instead how easily extraordinary learning could become associated with claims to occult power.

> 118 Vedi Guido Bonatti; vedi Asdente,  
> (See Guido Bonatti; see Asdente,)
>
> 119 ch'avere inteso al cuoio e a lo spago  
> (who to have attended to leather and thread)
>
> 120 ora vorrebbe, ma tardi si pente.  
> (would now wish, but he repents too late.)

Guido Bonatti was a prominent astrologer whose advice was sought in political and military affairs. Asdente, by contrast, was a shoemaker from Parma known for his predictions.

Their juxtaposition collapses distinctions of prestige. The celebrated adviser and the artisan-turned-prophet share the same punishment.

Asdente's abandoned leather and thread represent an honest, useful occupation. His mistake is not simply rising above his social position, but exchanging a productive craft for pretensions to forbidden knowledge. His regret is now too late to change his condition.

> 121 Vedi le triste che lasciaron l'ago,  
> (See the wretched women who abandoned the needle,)
>
> 122 la spuola e 'l fuso, e fecersi 'ndivine;  
> (the shuttle and the spindle, and made themselves diviners;)
>
> 123 fecer malie con erbe e con imago.  
> (they made enchantments with herbs and with an image.)

The catalogue concludes with unnamed women who practiced magic through herbs and images, such as figures used in spells.

The parallel with Asdente is clear: productive tools are abandoned for occult practices. At the same time, the contrast reflects medieval gender assumptions, especially the association of women's proper work with sewing, weaving, and spinning.

The movement from famous ancient prophets to anonymous practitioners broadens the canto's reach. Divination is not confined to epic heroes or royal courts; it also belongs to the everyday marketplace of hope, fear, and promised control.

## 10. The Moon: Reading Nature without Divining

> 124 Ma vienne omai, ché già tiene 'l confine  
> (But come now, for already it occupies the boundary)
>
> 125 d'amendue li emisperi e tocca l'onda  
> (of both hemispheres and touches the wave)
>
> 126 sotto Sobilia Caino e le spine;  
> (below Seville, Cain and the thorns;)

Virgil ends the catalogue by reading the sky. “Cain and the thorns” names the moon through a popular legend that identified its markings with Cain carrying a bundle of thorns.

Seville serves as a western geographical reference. The moon's position helps establish the approaching dawn and reminds the travelers that their journey must continue.

There is a deliberate contrast between this astronomical observation and the practices condemned in the bolgia. Virgil uses the heavens to locate the present moment, not to claim occult mastery over future events.

The language remains imaginative and folkloric. Dante does not require legitimate knowledge to be stripped of poetry; he distinguishes its purpose and limits.

> 127 e già iernotte fu la luna tonda:  
> (and already last night the moon was full:)
>
> 128 ben ten de' ricordar, ché non ti nocque  
> (you should well remember it, for it did not harm you)
>
> 129 alcuna volta per la selva fonda».  
> (at a certain time through the deep wood.”)

The reference returns the reader to the dark wood where the poem began. Virgil's understated observation that the moon “did not harm” Dante suggests that its light helped him during his confusion.

That recollection distinguishes assistance from domination. Light can help a traveler find a way without revealing the entire future.

The journey proceeds through guidance, memory, and limited understanding. Dante must accept enough illumination for the next step rather than demand the comprehensive foresight claimed by the damned.

## Epilogue — Knowledge without Presumption

Canto 20 turns the desire to see the future into the bodily impossibility of looking forward. Its central warning concerns intelligence distorted by presumption: the wish to possess knowledge without accepting the limits of human understanding.

But the canto is not hostile to knowledge itself. Geography, historical explanation, literary learning, and astronomical observation all contribute to the journey. They become valuable when exercised with discrimination rather than treated as guarantees of absolute authority.

Dante's challenge is therefore double: to learn how to judge without surrendering to immediate emotion, and how to seek understanding without claiming mastery over providence. The diviners move backward forever; the pilgrim advances by accepting guidance and the limits of his own sight.
