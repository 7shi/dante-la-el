The place where, to descend the bank,
we came was rugged and, for what was there besides,
such that every eye would shrink from it.
Like that landslide which, on the flank,
on this side of Trent, struck the Adige,
either through earthquake or through failed support,
for from the mountain's summit, whence it moved,
down to the plain the rock is so broken,
that it would offer some path to one above:
such was the descent of that ravine;
and on the edge of the shattered hollow
the infamy of Crete lay stretched out,
which was conceived in the counterfeit cow;
and when it saw us, it bit itself,
like one whom anger consumes within.
My sage cried toward it: “Perhaps
you think that here is the duke of Athens,
who dealt you death in the world above?
Depart, beast, for this man does not come
instructed by your sister,
but goes to see your punishments.”
Like that bull which breaks loose just when
it has already received the mortal blow,
which cannot walk, but leaps this way and that,
so I saw the Minotaur behave;
and the wary one cried: “Run to the passage;
while it rages, it is well that you descend.”
Thus we took our way down over the heap
of those stones, which often shifted
beneath my feet under their unfamiliar load.
I went thinking; and he said: “You think
perhaps of this ruin, which is guarded
by that bestial rage I just now quenched.
Now I would have you know that the other time
I descended here into the depths of Hell,
this rock had not yet fallen.
But certainly shortly before, if I judge rightly,
there came the one who the great spoil
took from Dis out of the uppermost circle,
on every side the deep, foul valley
shook so that I thought the universe
felt love, through which some believe
the world repeatedly turned to chaos;
and at that moment this ancient rock,
here and elsewhere, fell in such a heap.
But fix your eyes below, for there approaches
the river of blood in which boils
whoever harms another through violence.”
O blind greed and mad anger,
which so spur us in our brief life,
and then in the eternal steep us in such misery!
I saw a broad ditch curved into an arc,
as one that embraces the whole plain,
just as my escort had said;
and between the foot of the bank and it, in file,
centaurs ran, armed with arrows,
as they used to go hunting in the world.
Seeing us descend, each stood still,
and three detached themselves from the troop,
with bows and arrows chosen beforehand;
and one cried from afar: “To what torment
do you come, you who descend the slope?
Tell us from there; if not, I draw my bow.”
My master said: “The answer
we shall give to Chiron there, close by:
to your harm your will was always so rash.”
Then he touched me, and said: “That is Nessus,
who died for the beautiful Deianira,
and himself took vengeance for his death.
And the one in the middle, who gazes at his breast,
is the great Chiron, who reared Achilles;
that other is Pholus, who was so full of anger.
Around the ditch they go by thousands,
shooting any soul that pulls itself
farther out of the blood than its guilt allotted it.”
We drew near those fleet beasts:
Chiron took an arrow, and with its notch
pushed his beard back along his jaws.
When he had uncovered his great mouth,
he said to his companions: “Have you noticed
that the one behind moves what he touches?
The feet of the dead do not usually do so.”
And my good guide, who was already at his breast,
where the two natures are joined,
replied: “He is indeed alive, and thus alone
I must show him the dark valley;
necessity brings him here, and not delight.
One departed from singing alleluia
who entrusted me with this new task:
he is no robber, nor am I a thieving soul.
But by that power through which I move
my steps along so savage a road,
give us one of yours, whom we may follow,
and who may show us where to ford,
and who may carry this man on his back,
for he is no spirit that can travel through the air.”
Chiron turned toward his right breast,
and said to Nessus: “Go back, and guide them,
and make any other troop you meet stand aside.”
Now we moved with our faithful escort
along the bank of the crimson boiling,
where those being boiled uttered loud shrieks.
I saw people submerged up to their brows;
and the great centaur said: “These are tyrants
who laid their hands on blood and possessions.
Here they bewail their merciless wrongs;
here is Alexander, and fierce Dionysius,
who gave Sicily years of sorrow.
And that brow with hair so black
is Ezzelino; and that other who is blond
is Obizzo of Este, who in truth
was slain by his stepson in the world above.”
Then I turned to the poet, and he said:
“Let him now be first for you, and me second.”
A little farther on the centaur stopped
over some people who, up to the throat,
seemed to emerge from that boiling pool.
He showed us a shade alone on one side,
saying: “That one cleft, in God's lap,
the heart that beside the Thames is still honored.”
Then I saw people who out of the river
held their heads and also their entire chests;
and I recognized many of these.
Thus little by little grew shallow
that blood, so that it scorched only the feet;
and there was our passage across the ditch.
“Just as on this side you see
the boiling pool continually grow shallower,”
said the centaur, “I would have you believe
that on this other side, little by little, sinks
its bed, until it comes round again
to where tyranny must groan.
Divine justice on this side torments
that Attila who was a scourge on earth,
and Pyrrhus and Sextus; and forever draws out
the tears, which with the boiling it releases,
from Rinier da Corneto, from Rinier Pazzo,
who waged such warfare on the roads.”
Then he turned and crossed the ford again.
