# Stolen Bodies: Allegory and Background in Dante's *Inferno*, Canto 25

In the seventh bolgia of the eighth circle, Dante encounters thieves whose bodies cannot remain their own. Serpents bind them, invade them, fuse with them, and exchange shapes with them. Their punishment is a particularly unsettling example of *contrapasso*: those who violated other people's ownership now lose secure possession of their own bodily identity.

The canto continues directly from Canto 24, where the Pistoian thief Vanni Fucci confessed to stealing sacred treasure and delivered a malicious political prophecy against Dante. It then moves from Fucci's defiance to increasingly elaborate metamorphoses involving Florentine thieves. Along the way, Dante challenges ancient poets and tests the capacity of language to describe the destruction of recognizable human form.

## 1. Vanni Fucci's Gesture Against God

> 2 le mani alzò con amendue le fiche,  
> (he raised his hands with both making the figs,)
>
> 3 gridando: «Togli, Dio, ch'a te le squadro!».  
> (shouting: “Take that, God, for I aim them at you!”.)
>
> 4 Da indi in qua mi fuor le serpi amiche,  
> (From then onward the serpents were my friends,)
>
> 5 perch' una li s'avvolse allora al collo,  
> (because one then wound itself around his neck,)
>
> 6 come dicesse ‘Non vo' che più diche’;  
> (as if saying, ‘I do not want you to speak any more’;)

Making the *figs* means thrusting the thumb between the fingers of a closed fist: an obscene gesture of contempt. Fucci directs it at God with both hands. His punishment has not produced repentance; he answers divine judgment with the same aggressive self-assertion that characterized his earthly conduct.

The serpents immediately restrain him. One coils around his neck, while another binds the arms responsible for the gesture. Speech and bodily action become the particular targets of punishment.

Dante's declaration that the serpents became his friends is deliberately shocking. Creatures conventionally associated with evil now act as instruments of divine justice. The biblical associations of the serpent remain disturbing, but here the human sinner is so defiant that the reptiles appear, comparatively, to defend moral order.

## 2. Pistoia and the Self-Destruction of Civic Identity

> 10 Ahi Pistoia, Pistoia, ché non stanzi  
> (Ah Pistoia, Pistoia, why do you not resolve)
>
> 11 d'incenerarti sì che più non duri,  
> (to burn yourself to ashes so that you endure no more,)
>
> 12 poi che 'n mal fare il seme tuo avanzi?  
> (since in wrongdoing you surpass your seed?)

The attack on Fucci expands into an attack on his city. Dante addresses Pistoia as though it were a single person capable of judging and destroying itself. The repeated name gives the passage the force of both lament and denunciation.

The reference to the city's *seed* invokes a medieval tradition connecting Pistoia's origins with the defeated followers of Catiline, the Roman conspirator. On that reading, the city has surpassed even its supposedly criminal founders in wickedness. This is polemical civic genealogy, not reliable historical explanation.

The wish that Pistoia reduce itself to ashes also recalls the bodily destruction of thieves in the preceding canto. Individual punishment becomes an image of communal corruption: a city that perpetuates wrongdoing destroys the moral basis of its own existence.

> 14 non vidi spirto in Dio tanto superbo,  
> (I saw no spirit so proud against God,)
>
> 15 non quel che cadde a Tebe giù da' muri.  
> (not even the one who fell at Thebes down from the walls.)

The unnamed comparison is Capaneus, encountered in Canto 14. One of the seven kings who attacked Thebes, he defied Jupiter and was struck down by lightning.

Fucci therefore exceeds a celebrated pagan blasphemer. Although he is punished here for theft, his character also expresses pride and blasphemy. Dante's classification of sins does not mean that each sinner possesses only one vice.

## 3. Cacus: Violence in the Service of Fraud

> 25 Lo mio maestro disse: «Questi è Caco,  
> (My master said: “This is Cacus,)
>
> 26 che, sotto 'l sasso di monte Aventino,  
> (who, beneath the rock of Mount Aventine,)
>
> 27 di sangue fece spesse volte laco.  
> (often made a lake of blood.)

Virgil identifies Cacus, a monster whose story he had told in Book 8 of the *Aeneid*. Cacus inhabited a cave on the Aventine, terrorized the surrounding country, and was eventually killed by Hercules.

Dante reshapes the classical figure into a centaur covered with snakes and carrying a fire-breathing dragon on his shoulders. This is not simply a faithful reproduction of Virgil's monster. The hybrid body brings together animal violence, reptilian cunning, and destructive fire.

Cacus rushes through the bolgia pursuing Fucci. His appearance connects the opening scene of defiance with the transformations that follow: even the creatures policing this region have unstable or composite bodily forms.

> 28 Non va co' suoi fratei per un cammino,  
> (He does not go along one path with his brothers,)
>
> 29 per lo furto che frodolente fece  
> (because of the theft that he committed fraudulently)
>
> 30 del grande armento ch'elli ebbe a vicino;  
> (from the great herd that he had nearby;)

Cacus is separated from the other centaurs, who guard the violent in Canto 12, because his defining offense here is fraudulent theft.

In the classical story, he stole cattle from Hercules and dragged them backward into his cave, making their tracks point away from their actual destination. This calculated deception explains his placement among thieves rather than among the merely violent.

The distinction matters to the architecture of Dante's Hell. Fraud corrupts the intelligence that should distinguish human beings from beasts. Cacus's monstrous appearance gives visible form to the conjunction of brute force and misused cunning.

## 4. The Reader Is Asked to Believe the Impossible

> 46 Se tu se' or, lettore, a creder lento  
> (If you are now, reader, slow to believe)
>
> 47 ciò ch'io dirò, non sarà maraviglia,  
> (what I shall say, it will be no wonder,)
>
> 48 ché io che 'l vidi, a pena il mi consento.  
> (for I who saw it scarcely allow myself to believe it.)

Three spirits approach, and one asks where Cianfa has gone. Recognizing the significance of the name, Dante signals Virgil to be silent. The encounter is moving into the social world of Florence.

Before describing the first transformation, however, the narrator turns directly to the reader. He admits that the event strains even his own belief, despite his claim to have witnessed it.

This address performs two functions. It presents the account as testimony rather than casual invention, while also preparing the reader for an extraordinary poetic achievement. Dante's authority rests on an apparent paradox: he claims credibility partly by acknowledging how incredible his story sounds.

The passage also makes bewilderment part of the punishment's meaning. These transformations violate the categories by which people ordinarily understand bodies, persons, and ownership.

## 5. Cianfa and Agnello: Two Become Neither One nor Two

> 61 Poi s'appiccar, come di calda cera  
> (Then they stuck together, as if of warm wax)
>
> 62 fossero stati, e mischiar lor colore,  
> (they had been made, and mingled their color,)
>
> 63 né l'un né l'altro già parea quel ch'era:  
> (and neither one nor the other now seemed what it had been:)

A six-footed serpent attacks one of the men, wrapping itself around him and biting his cheeks. The man is Agnello, traditionally identified as Agnello dei Brunelleschi; the serpent is generally understood to be the missing Cianfa Donati.

The comparison with warm wax makes the impossible process materially imaginable. What should distinguish one body from another becomes soft, adhesive, and indistinct. Their colors mingle before their anatomy is fully recomposed.

This is theft enacted upon the self. Neither participant retains exclusive possession of his own visible form. Instead of one thief simply taking something from another, both become implicated in a mutual loss of bodily boundaries.

> 68 gridava: «Omè, Agnel, come ti muti!  
> (cried: “Alas, Agnel, how you change!)
>
> 69 Vedi che già non se' né due né uno».  
> (See that already you are neither two nor one.”)

The observers identify the central horror: the resulting creature cannot be counted coherently. Two beings have fused, but the product is not a unified person.

Dante subsequently describes a face in which both original identities are lost. The hybrid departs without achieving a stable new individuality.

The allegory should not be taken to mean that God has literally confused or annihilated the souls. These remain distinct, accountable sinners. What collapses is their bodily expression of identity—the outward integrity they can no longer command.

## 6. The Navel Wound and the Exchange of Smoke

> 85 e quella parte onde prima è preso  
> (and that part through which first is taken)
>
> 86 nostro alimento, a l'un di lor trafisse;  
> (our nourishment, it pierced in one of them;)
>
> 87 poi cadde giuso innanzi lui disteso.  
> (then fell down stretched out before him.)

A second serpent, small, dark, and fiercely active, strikes another thief in the navel. Dante identifies that location indirectly as the place through which human beings first receive nourishment.

The reference brings prenatal life into a scene of infernal unmaking. The bodily connection that once sustained growth now becomes the entry point for a destructive transformation. The punishment can therefore be read as an inversion of generation: rather than forming a human body, it begins dismantling one.

The wounded man does not react with ordinary resistance. He stares and yawns as though overcome by fever or sleep. His passivity intensifies the sense that his own body has slipped beyond his control.

> 91 Elli 'l serpente e quei lui riguardava;  
> (He gazed at the serpent, and it at him;)
>
> 92 l'un per la piaga e l'altro per la bocca  
> (the one through the wound and the other through the mouth)
>
> 93 fummavan forte, e 'l fummo si scontrava.  
> (smoked heavily, and the smoke met.)

The reciprocal gaze establishes the symmetry of the coming exchange. Each creature confronts the shape it is about to assume.

Smoke links the wound of one to the mouth of the other, giving the transformation a visible medium without explaining it mechanically. The scene has the atmosphere of a sinister experiment: two bodies are connected while their defining properties begin to pass in opposite directions.

Unlike the earlier fusion, this metamorphosis will leave two separate bodies. The horror now lies in exchanging their forms.

## 7. Dante Challenges Lucan and Ovid

> 94 Taccia Lucano ormai là dov' e' tocca  
> (Let Lucan now be silent where he tells)
>
> 95 del misero Sabello e di Nasidio,  
> (of wretched Sabellus and of Nasidius,)
>
> 96 e attenda a udir quel ch'or si scocca.  
> (and let him attend to hear what now shoots forth.)

Dante interrupts the transformation to challenge his classical predecessors. In Book 9 of Lucan's *Pharsalia*, poisonous snakes inflict spectacular deaths upon Roman soldiers in Libya. Sabellus's body dissolves, while Nasidius's swells grotesquely.

These episodes supplied important precedents for Dante's combination of serpents, bodily horror, and detailed description. His command that Lucan fall silent is therefore also an acknowledgment of literary debt.

The language of something shooting forth presents Dante's own verse as a forceful discharge. The poet is not merely reporting a contest between bodies; he is staging a contest between literary powers.

> 97 Taccia di Cadmo e d'Aretusa Ovidio,  
> (Let Ovid be silent about Cadmus and Arethusa,)
>
> 98 ché se quello in serpente e quella in fonte  
> (for if the former into a serpent and the latter into a spring)
>
> 99 converte poetando, io non lo 'nvidio;  
> (he transforms through poetry, I do not envy him;)

Ovid's *Metamorphoses* provides the great classical storehouse of transformed bodies. Cadmus becomes a serpent; Arethusa becomes a spring.

Dante does not deny the brilliance of these stories. Instead, he argues that his own scene poses a more difficult imaginative problem.

> 100 ché due nature mai a fronte a fronte  
> (for two natures never face to face)
>
> 101 non trasmutò sì ch'amendue le forme  
> (did he transmute so that both the forms)
>
> 102 a cambiar lor matera fosser pronte.  
> (were ready to exchange their matter.)

The claim is specific: Dante depicts two beings transforming simultaneously and reciprocally, each acquiring the form the other loses.

His innovation is also moral. Classical metamorphosis becomes an instrument of Christian judgment. The changing bodies do not merely astonish; they reveal what theft means when the violation of possession is turned upon the thief.

There is a deliberate tension between this confident literary challenge and the narrator's repeated professions of bewilderment. Dante claims extraordinary poetic control over a scene whose horror consists in the loss of control.

## 8. An Anatomy of Reciprocal Theft

> 109 Togliea la coda fessa la figura  
> (The split tail was taking on the shape)
>
> 110 che si perdeva là, e la sua pelle  
> (that was being lost there, and its skin)
>
> 111 si facea molle, e quella di là dura.  
> (was becoming soft, and the skin over there hard.)

The serpent's tail divides into legs while the man's legs join into a tail. The same process governs their skin: one surface softens toward humanity as the other hardens toward reptilian form.

The repeated movement between one body and the other makes the exchange exact and relentless. A feature gained on one side corresponds to a feature lost on the other.

This is the canto's most precise embodiment of *contrapasso*. Human form behaves like stolen property, passing from one possessor to another. Yet no legitimate ownership is restored. The exchange only prepares further cycles of dispossession.

> 112 Io vidi intrar le braccia per l'ascelle,  
> (I saw the arms withdraw through the armpits,)
>
> 113 e i due piè de la fiera, ch'eran corti,  
> (and the beast's two feet, which were short,)
>
> 114 tanto allungar quanto accorciavan quelle.  
> (lengthen as much as those arms shortened.)

The balance becomes almost mathematical: the beast's limbs lengthen by precisely the amount that the man's arms shorten.

Such anatomical detail prevents the metamorphosis from becoming a vague magical event. Dante forces the reader to imagine each stage. The body is treated as a collection of transferable features rather than as the secure dwelling of an individual person.

The process also reverses the proper hierarchy between reason and animal appetite. Those who used human intelligence for fraud now move repeatedly across the boundary between human and beast.

## 9. Speech Returns, but Humanity Does Not

> 133 e la lingua, ch'avëa unita e presta  
> (and the tongue, which he had whole and ready)
>
> 134 prima a parlar, si fende, e la forcuta  
> (before for speech, splits, and the forked tongue)
>
> 135 ne l'altro si richiude; e 'l fummo resta.  
> (in the other closes together; and the smoke ceases.)

The exchange culminates in the tongue. The human tongue becomes forked, while the serpent's becomes capable of articulated speech.

Speech is a central sign of rational humanity, so this final change completes more than an anatomical transformation. One sinner loses the bodily instrument through which a person can explain, name, and assert himself.

Yet recovering that instrument does not produce moral recovery. The newly human figure speaks with vindictiveness, not repentance.

> 140 e disse a l'altro: «I' vo' che Buoso corra,  
> (and said to the other: “I want Buoso to run,)
>
> 141 com' ho fatt' io, carpon per questo calle».  
> (as I have done, on all fours along this path.”)

The speaker identifies the transformed man as Buoso. His precise historical identity is disputed, with different commentators connecting him to different Florentine families.

The important point is the speaker's satisfaction. He welcomes Buoso's degradation because it temporarily relieves his own. Hell preserves the competitive selfishness that made the sinners guilty: another person's suffering becomes a private advantage.

The exchange therefore does not offer liberation. The newly upright figure has recovered a human appearance without recovering humane conduct.

## 10. Florentine Names Amid the Wreckage

> 142 Così vid' io la settima zavorra  
> (Thus I saw the seventh ballast)
>
> 143 mutare e trasmutare; e qui mi scusi  
> (change and transmute; and here may it excuse me,)
>
> 144 la novità se fior la penna abborra.  
> (the novelty, if the pen goes somewhat astray.)

Dante calls the inhabitants of the seventh bolgia its *ballast*: a demeaning collective image that reduces once-distinguished citizens to a burden or mass of material.

His request for indulgence returns to the problem raised in the address to the reader. The unprecedented subject may strain the pen. After commanding Lucan and Ovid to fall silent, the poet again presents himself as struggling to keep pace with what he has seen.

This alternation between boldness and hesitation is essential to the canto's literary design. The narrative demonstrates mastery while insisting that the reality of divine punishment exceeds ordinary representation.

> 148 ch'i' non scorgessi ben Puccio Sciancato;  
> (that I did not clearly recognize Puccio Sciancato;)
>
> 149 ed era quel che sol, di tre compagni  
> (and he was the one who alone, of three companions)
>
> 150 che venner prima, non era mutato;  
> (who had come before, had not been changed;)
>
> 151 l'altr' era quel che tu, Gaville, piagni.  
> (the other was the one whom you, Gaville, mourn.)

Puccio Sciancato, usually identified as a member of the Florentine Galigai family, is the only one of the original three arrivals who has not changed during this encounter. That does not imply exemption from the bolgia's punishments; it describes what Dante has just witnessed.

The other figure, now restored to human shape, is traditionally identified as Francesco de' Cavalcanti. Early commentary connects his death at the hands of people from Gaville with bloody reprisals against that community. Gaville's mourning thus points beyond a single death to the violence associated with its aftermath.

The canto ends by bringing its fantastic spectacle back into recognizable Tuscan society. These are not anonymous monsters but named men embedded in families, towns, and histories of wrongdoing. Their unstable bodies expose the corruption beneath a familiar civic surface.

## Epilogue — When Thieves Lose Possession of Themselves

Canto 25 turns theft into a crisis of bodily ownership. Those who appropriated what belonged to others cannot retain an uncontested claim even to their own appearance. They are bound, merged, divided, and exchanged, while their hostility and selfishness endure.

The canto also transforms classical poetry. Dante takes the serpents of Lucan and the metamorphoses of Ovid and makes them instruments of moral revelation. His extraordinary technical control serves a disturbing insight: human intelligence, speech, and outward form do not guarantee humanity when the will remains committed to violating others.
