Now along a secret path goes
between the city wall and the torments,
my master, and I behind his shoulders.
“O highest virtue, who through the impious circles
lead me,” I began, “as you please,
speak to me, and satisfy my desires.
The people who lie within the tombs,
could they be seen? already raised are
all the lids, and no one keeps guard.”
And he to me: “All shall be shut
when from Jehoshaphat they return here
with the bodies that they have left up above.
Their cemetery on this side have
with Epicurus all his followers,
who make the soul dead together with the body.
Therefore the question that you ask me
here within shall soon be satisfied,
and also the desire that you keep silent from me.”
And I: “Good leader, I do not keep hidden
my heart from you except to speak little,
and not just now have you disposed me to this.”
“O Tuscan who through the city of fire
go alive, speaking so honorably,
may it please you to remain in this place.
Your speech makes you manifest
as a native of that noble homeland,
to which perhaps I was too troublesome.”
Suddenly this sound came forth
from one of the tombs; therefore I drew,
in fear, a little closer to my leader.
And he said to me: “Turn around! What are you doing?
See there Farinata who has risen upright:
from the waist upward you shall see all of him.”
I had already fixed my gaze on his;
and he rose with his chest and with his brow
as though he held Hell in great contempt.
And the bold and ready hands of my leader
pushed me among the tombs toward him,
saying: “Let your words be well considered.”
When I was at the foot of his tomb,
he looked at me a little, and then, almost disdainfully,
asked me: “Who were your ancestors?”
I, who was eager to obey,
did not hide it from him, but revealed it all;
at which he raised his brows a little;
then said: “Fiercely opposed were they
to me and to my forebears and to my faction,
so that twice I scattered them.”
“If they were driven out, they returned from every quarter,”
I answered him, “both the first and the second time;
but yours did not learn that art well.”
Then at the uncovered opening rose
a shade, beside this one, as far as the chin:
I believe it had risen on its knees.
It looked around me, as though a desire
it had to see if another was with me;
and when its searching gaze was wholly stilled,
weeping it said: “If through this blind
prison you go through loftiness of intellect,
where is my son? and why is he not with you?”
And I to him: “I do not come of myself:
the one who waits there leads me through here
whom perhaps your Guido held in disdain.”
His words and the manner of his punishment
had already read out this man's name to me;
therefore my answer was so full.
Suddenly straightening, he cried: “What?
did you say “he held”? does he not still live?
does the sweet light not strike his eyes?”
When he perceived some delay
that I was making before my answer,
he fell back supine and appeared outside no more.
But that other great-hearted soul, at whose bidding
I had remained, did not change expression,
nor move his neck, nor bend his side;
and continuing his earlier speech,
“If they have that art,” he said, “ill learned,
that torments me more than this bed.
But not fifty times will be rekindled
the face of the lady who rules here,
before you know how heavily that art weighs.
And so may you ever return to the sweet world,
tell me: why is that people so merciless
toward mine in every law of theirs?”
At which I to him: “The slaughter and the great carnage
that made the Arbia colored red,
cause such a prayer to be offered in our temple.”
After he had shaken his head with a sigh,
“In that I was not alone,” he said, “nor certainly
without cause would I have moved with the others.
But I alone was, there where consent
came from everyone to do away with Florence,
the one who defended her openly.”
“Ah, so may your descendants ever find rest,”
I begged him, “untie for me that knot
which here has entangled my judgment.
It seems that you see, if I hear rightly,
beforehand what time brings with it,
and in the present you follow another way.”
“We see, like one who has poor sight,
the things,” he said, “that are far from us;
so much still shines upon us from the supreme ruler.
When they draw near or are, wholly vain is
our understanding; and if others bring us no news,
we know nothing of your human condition.
Therefore you can understand that wholly dead
will our knowledge be from that point
when the door of the future shall be shut.”
Then, as though stung by my own fault,
I said: “Now tell that fallen one, then,
that his son is still joined with the living;
and if, just now, I was silent in answering,
let him know that I did so because I was thinking
already of the error that you have cleared up for me.”
And already my master was calling me back;
therefore I begged the spirit with greater haste
to tell me who was there with him.
He said to me: “Here I lie with more than a thousand:
here within is Frederick the Second
and the Cardinal; and of the others I keep silent.”
Then he hid himself; and I toward the ancient
poet turned my steps, thinking back
on those words that seemed hostile to me.
He moved on; and then, walking thus,
said to me: “Why are you so troubled?”
And I satisfied his question.
“Let your mind preserve what heard
you have against you,” that sage commanded me;
“and now attend here,” and he raised his finger:
“when you shall be before the sweet radiance
of her whose beautiful eye sees everything,
from her you shall learn the journey of your life.”
Then he turned his steps to the left:
we left the wall and went toward the middle
along a path that strikes into a valley,
whose stench was foul even up there.
