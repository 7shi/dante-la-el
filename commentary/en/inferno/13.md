# The Forest of Broken Selves: Allegory and Background in Inferno XIII

In Canto XIII, Dante and Virgil enter the second ring of Hell's seventh circle, where violence against oneself is punished. Here, suicides have become trees, while those who violently squandered their possessions are hunted through the undergrowth. The canto turns the forest into an image of damaged humanity: speech emerges through wounds, bodies become alien possessions, and the desire to escape suffering produces an enduring imprisonment within it.

## 1. The Wood That Reverses Nature

> 4 Non fronda verde, ma di color fosco;  
> (Not green foliage, but of a dark color;)
>
> 5 non rami schietti, ma nodosi e 'nvolti;  
> (not straight branches, but knotted and twisted;)
>
> 6 non pomi v'eran, ma stecchi con tòsco.  
> (there were no fruits, but thorns with poison.)

The forest is introduced through three repeated negations: “not … but.” Everything normally associated with flourishing vegetation has been replaced by its opposite. Green becomes darkness, straight growth becomes distortion, and nourishing fruit becomes poison.

This is more than a frightening landscape. The trees are the souls of people who destroyed their own bodies, so the forest makes their broken relationship with embodied life visible. They have not escaped existence; they have exchanged human movement and agency for rooted, wounded vegetation.

The setting also reverses the familiar literary image of the pleasant grove—a place of shade, love, and refreshment. Dante's wood offers neither shelter nor renewal. His comparison with the rough country between Cecina and Corneto locates its horror against the recognizable background of the Tuscan Maremma, then makes even that wilderness seem comparatively mild.

## 2. The Harpies: Classical Monsters in a Christian Hell

> 10 Quivi le brutte Arpie lor nidi fanno,  
> (Here the foul Harpies make their nests,)
>
> 11 che cacciar de le Strofade i Troiani  
> (who drove the Trojans from the Strophades)
>
> 12 con tristo annunzio di futuro danno.  
> (with a sorrowful announcement of future harm.)

The Harpies come from classical mythology and, more immediately, from Book III of Virgil's *Aeneid*. On the Strophades, they foul the Trojans' food; the Harpy Celaeno foretells a future hunger so severe that the Trojans will eat their tables.

Their presence therefore brings Virgil's poetry into the landscape through which Virgil himself now guides Dante. But Dante gives these inherited monsters a new function within Christian judgment: they feed upon the leaves of the transformed suicides.

Their mixed anatomy—human faces joined to predatory bird bodies—also suits a place where the boundary between human and nonhuman has become agonizingly unstable. They embody a grotesque corruption of nourishment: feeding here does not sustain communal life but continually produces pain.

## 3. A Wound Reveals the Human Being

> 32 e colsi un ramicel da un gran pruno;  
> (and I plucked a little branch from a great thorn tree;)
>
> 33 e 'l tronco suo gridò: «Perché mi schiante?».  
> (and its trunk cried: “Why do you break me?”.)

Dante initially hears cries without seeing anyone who could be making them. Virgil instructs him to break a twig, and the experiment reveals that the apparently lifeless vegetation possesses consciousness.

Knowledge arrives through an act of injury. Dante cannot understand the forest merely by looking at it; he learns its nature by inadvertently participating in its violence. The tree's question abruptly turns an object into a suffering person.

> 37 Uomini fummo, e or siam fatti sterpi:  
> (We were human beings, and now we are made brushwood:)
>
> 38 ben dovrebb' esser la tua man più pia,  
> (surely your hand ought to be more merciful,)
>
> 39 se state fossimo anime di serpi».  
> (even if we had been souls of serpents”.)

The appeal insists that humanity survives beneath the punishment's dehumanizing appearance. Even serpents, conventionally associated with evil, would deserve gentler treatment.

This complicates the reader's response to Hell. The punishment classifies the soul according to its sin, but the voice demands recognition of the person who suffers it. Dante's horror and compassion do not erase the theological judgment; they prevent that judgment from becoming emotionally simple.

> 46 «S'elli avesse potuto creder prima»,  
> (“If he could have believed beforehand”,)
>
> 47 rispuose 'l savio mio, «anima lesa,  
> (my wise guide replied, “wounded soul,)
>
> 48 ciò c'ha veduto pur con la mia rima,  
> (what he has seen through my verse alone,)

Virgil refers to another episode in *Aeneid* III. Aeneas tears branches from a plant growing above the murdered Polydorus, and blood and speech emerge from the wound.

Dante adapts that literary precedent into a more comprehensive transformation: this is not simply a plant associated with a buried victim, but a human soul made into a tree. Virgil's apology also acknowledges the ethical cost of his teaching. He has made Dante learn through an action that pains the teacher himself.

## 4. Pier della Vigna and the Keys of Imperial Power

> 58 Io son colui che tenni ambo le chiavi  
> (I am the one who held both keys)
>
> 59 del cor di Federigo, e che le volsi,  
> (of Frederick's heart, and who turned them,)
>
> 60 serrando e diserrando, sì soavi,  
> (locking and unlocking, so gently,)
>
> 61 che dal secreto suo quasi ogn' uom tolsi;  
> (that I removed almost everyone from his confidence;)

The speaker is Pier della Vigna, a distinguished legal official, diplomat, and writer in the service of Emperor Frederick II. Rising from relatively humble origins, he became one of the emperor's most influential ministers before falling from favor in 1249. He was imprisoned and blinded, and was reported to have died by suicide; the precise circumstances remain uncertain.

The keys represent privileged access to the emperor's inner life and decisions. Pier presents himself not merely as an administrator but as the trusted keeper of the imperial heart.

Yet his account contains a tension. Excluding almost everyone else from Frederick's confidence demonstrates his exceptional influence, but also suggests the competitive courtly world in which resentment could flourish. His intimacy with power made him both indispensable and vulnerable.

His elaborate language likewise matters. The balanced phrases, verbal repetitions, and polished imagery evoke the rhetorical culture of an accomplished imperial letter-writer. Even as a wounded tree, Pier speaks in the style through which he once exercised authority.

## 5. Envy: The Disease of the Court

> 64 La meretrice che mai da l'ospizio  
> (The prostitute who never from the household)
>
> 65 di Cesare non torse li occhi putti,  
> (of Caesar turned her harlot eyes,)
>
> 66 morte comune e de le corti vizio,  
> (the death of all and the vice of courts,)

The prostitute is Envy personified. She continually watches the imperial household, seeking opportunities to corrupt relationships between ruler and servant.

The sexual imagery presents envy as a degrading influence upon political life. What should be ordered by justice and faithful service is instead governed by rivalry, suspicion, and the appetite to bring another person down.

Pier also moves from his own experience to a general diagnosis: envy is not an isolated failing but a characteristic disease of courts. His fall becomes an example of the insecurity inherent in dependence upon princely favor.

> 67 infiammò contra me li animi tutti;  
> (inflamed every mind against me;)
>
> 68 e li 'nfiammati infiammar sì Augusto,  
> (and those inflamed so inflamed Augustus,)
>
> 69 che ' lieti onor tornaro in tristi lutti.  
> (that joyful honors turned into sorrowful griefs.)

The repeated forms of “inflame” enact the spread of hostility. Envy passes from the courtiers to Frederick, called “Augustus” in recognition of his imperial dignity.

Pier's account is a defense speech: he attributes his destruction to malicious accusation rather than disloyalty. Dante gives that defense extraordinary dignity, but it remains the testimony of an interested speaker, not an independent historical investigation.

The canto's moral distinction is nevertheless clear. Pier can be innocent of betraying his emperor and still, within Dante's theological framework, guilty of violence against himself.

## 6. The Paradox of Becoming Unjust to Oneself

> 70 L'animo mio, per disdegnoso gusto,  
> (My spirit, through a disdainful impulse,)
>
> 71 credendo col morir fuggir disdegno,  
> (believing through death to escape contempt,)
>
> 72 ingiusto fece me contra me giusto.  
> (made me unjust against my just self.)

These lines state the central paradox of Pier's story. He believed that death would free him from disgrace, but in attempting to escape an injustice he committed another injustice against himself.

The repetition of “me” splits the self into aggressor and victim. The man who regards himself as just in his political conduct becomes unjust in his treatment of his own person. The verbal structure anticipates the punishment's separation of soul and human body.

This reasoning belongs to the medieval Christian understanding of life as a good entrusted by God, not a possession over which the individual has unlimited authority. The canto presents suicide as a mistaken attempt to resolve suffering by destroying the embodied self.

> 76 E se di voi alcun nel mondo riede,  
> (And if either of you returns to the world,)
>
> 77 conforti la memoria mia, che giace  
> (let him restore my memory, which lies)
>
> 78 ancor del colpo che 'nvidia le diede».  
> (still beneath the blow that envy dealt it”.)

Although Pier sought escape from worldly dishonor, his reputation still matters intensely to him. He asks not for release from Hell but for the restoration of his name among the living.

This gives Dante's future poem a reparative function. It cannot reverse divine judgment, but it can challenge a damaging account of a person's earthly life. The damned retain histories that are more complicated than the single category of their punishment.

## 7. How a Soul Becomes a Tree

> 97 Cade in la selva, e non l'è parte scelta;  
> (It falls into the wood, and no place is chosen for it;)
>
> 98 ma là dove fortuna la balestra,  
> (but wherever chance hurls it,)
>
> 99 quivi germoglia come gran di spelta.  
> (there it sprouts like a grain of spelt.)

Pier explains that after the soul has violently separated itself from its body, Minos assigns it to the seventh circle. It falls into the forest and germinates where it lands.

The agricultural comparison is deliberately disturbing. Germination usually signifies renewal, but here it produces confinement. The soul acquires a form of life while losing the mobility, agency, and bodily integrity proper to human existence.

Chance determines its particular location, not whether it is damned. Within the ordered framework of judgment, this local randomness emphasizes helplessness: the soul that attempted a final act of control can no longer choose even where to stand.

> 101 l'Arpie, pascendo poi de le sue foglie,  
> (the Harpies, feeding then upon its leaves,)
>
> 102 fanno dolore, e al dolor fenestra.  
> (make pain, and a window for the pain.)

The Harpies create both suffering and its means of expression. Their wounds become “windows” through which the imprisoned soul can voice its pain.

This is the canto's *contrapasso*: punishment reveals the nature of the sin through correspondence and reversal. Those who rejected their bodies become bound to an inferior bodily form; those who used injury to silence suffering can now speak through injury.

The metaphor also illuminates the poem's storytelling. Dante hears these lives only because their wounds have been opened.

## 8. Resurrection Without Reunion

> 103 Come l'altre verrem per nostre spoglie,  
> (Like the others, we shall come for our bodily remains,)
>
> 104 ma non però ch'alcuna sen rivesta,  
> (but not so that any of us may clothe herself in them again,)
>
> 105 ché non è giusto aver ciò ch'om si toglie.  
> (for it is not just to have what one takes from oneself.)

Pier looks forward to the Last Judgment, when souls return for their bodies. Yet he describes an exceptional condition for the suicides: they will retrieve their bodies without inhabiting them again.

The legal sharpness of the explanation suits the former imperial jurist. The body is treated as something wrongfully renounced, whose possession cannot simply be restored.

This is Dante's particular imaginative elaboration of the punishment, rather than a straightforward summary of standard Christian teaching about bodily resurrection. It gives the consequences of self-separation a permanent visible form.

> 106 Qui le strascineremo, e per la mesta  
> (Here we shall drag them, and throughout the mournful)
>
> 107 selva saranno i nostri corpi appesi,  
> (wood our bodies will be hung,)
>
> 108 ciascuno al prun de l'ombra sua molesta».  
> (each upon the thorn tree of its suffering shade”.)

The completed image is a forest bearing human corpses instead of fruit. Soul and body will be brought near each other without recovering their proper unity.

The body becomes an enduring external witness to what the soul has done. Rather than abolishing the relationship with the body, the suicidal act has made that relationship inescapably painful.

## 9. The Squanderers and the Hunt for Death

> 118 Quel dinanzi: «Or accorri, accorri, morte!».  
> (The one in front: “Now come, come, death!”.)

The conversation is interrupted by two naked men fleeing through the forest. These are squanderers: not merely people who spent too freely, but figures understood to have violently dissipated their possessions.

The first is Lano, conventionally identified as a Sienese squanderer killed at the battle of Pieve al Toppo in 1288. His companion mocks the contrast between his present speed and his failure to escape that battle. Early commentary associated his battlefield death with desperation after impoverishment.

His appeal to death echoes the central error of the suicides: death is imagined as a refuge from distress. In Hell, however, no further death can end the punishment.

> 127 In quel che s'appiattò miser li denti,  
> (Into the one who crouched in hiding they sank their teeth,)
>
> 128 e quel dilaceraro a brano a brano;  
> (and they tore him apart piece by piece;)
>
> 129 poi sen portar quelle membra dolenti.  
> (then they carried away those suffering limbs.)

The pursuers are ravenous black female hounds. The second fugitive, Iacopo da Santo Andrea, hides within a bush, but the pack tears him apart.

The punishment turns the destruction of possessions into bodily dismemberment: the person who scattered his substance is himself scattered. The hounds enact the relentless consumption that characterized his worldly conduct.

The scene also shows violence spreading beyond its immediate target. Iacopo's attempt to save himself damages the bush sheltering him—another human soul already undergoing punishment.

## 10. Florence and the House Turned Gallows

> 133 «O Iacopo», dicea, «da Santo Andrea,  
> (“O Iacopo”, it said, “of Santo Andrea,)
>
> 134 che t'è giovato di me fare schermo?  
> (what has it profited you to make a shield of me?)
>
> 135 che colpa ho io de la tua vita rea?».  
> (what fault have I in your wicked life?”.)

The damaged bush protests against being forced to suffer for someone else's wrongdoing. Its complaint returns the canto to the moral question raised by Dante's broken twig: what responsibility does one person bear for injuring another?

The fugitives' violence has no constructive result. Iacopo is not saved, and the bush's suffering is increased. The episode compresses a wider pattern in which desperate self-preservation creates additional victims.

> 143 I' fui de la città che nel Batista  
> (I was from the city that for the Baptist)
>
> 144 mutò 'l primo padrone; ond' ei per questo  
> (exchanged its first patron; therefore he, for this,)
>
> 145 sempre con l'arte sua la farà trista;  
> (will always make it sorrowful through his art;)

The unnamed speaker identifies himself as Florentine. Florence replaced its supposed ancient patron, Mars, with Saint John the Baptist. According to the civic legend invoked here, the displaced war god continued to afflict the city through his “art”: warfare and civil conflict.

The soul subsequently mentions the surviving fragment of a statue associated with Mars near the Arno and the city's legendary rebuilding after destruction by Attila. This is medieval civic mythology, not reliable ancient history; the destruction story involves confused historical traditions.

Within the canto, the legend broadens the theme of self-destruction. Florence is a community perpetually wounded by its own divisions. The individual's violence against himself finds a civic counterpart in factional violence.

> 151 Io fei gibetto a me de le mie case».  
> (I made a gallows for myself out of my own home”.)

The final line states that the speaker hanged himself at home. His precise identity is uncertain, and the anonymity gives his fate a significance beyond a single recoverable biography.

The home, ordinarily a place of protection, becomes an instrument of death. This completes the canto's sequence of inversions: nourishing plants become poisonous, human beings become brushwood, bodies become burdens, and shelter becomes a gallows.

## Epilogue — The Self That Cannot Be Escaped

*Inferno* XIII portrays self-destruction as a failed attempt to escape pain, dishonor, or loss. Its punishments make that failure visible: the rejected body remains an unavoidable concern, and the wounded self continues to speak.

Yet the canto is not simply a demonstration of retributive logic. Pier's dignity, Dante's compassion, and the anonymous Florentine's distress preserve the humanity that the forest's outward forms conceal. Its deepest tension lies between judgment and recognition: the souls are condemned for what they did, but their voices insist that they are more than their final acts.
