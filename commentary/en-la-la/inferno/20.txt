I must make verses of a new punishment,
And give matter to the twentieth canto
Of the first song, which is of the submerged.
I was already wholly disposed
To look down into the uncovered bottom,
Which was bathed in anguished weeping:
And I saw people in the round valley
Coming silently and weeping, at a slow pace,
As litanies proceed in this world.
As my gaze descended lower among them,
Each one appeared wondrously twisted
Between the chin and the top of the chest:
For the face had turned back from the loins,
And they had to walk backward,
Since sight in front had been taken from them.
Perhaps the force of paralysis once
Twisted someone wholly in this way;
But I have not seen it, nor believe it so.
If God grant you, reader, to gain fruit
From this reading, consider for yourself
How I could keep my eyes dry,
When I saw our human likeness nearby
So twisted that the weeping from their eyes
Bathed their buttocks through the cleft.
Indeed I wept, leaning against one of the rocks
Of the hard crag, so that my guide
Said to me: Are you still one of the other fools?
Here pity lives when it is truly dead.
Who is more wicked than one
Who grieves over divine judgment?
Lift your head, lift it, he said, and look at him
Before whose eyes the earth opened
In front of the Thebans, who all cried: Where are you rushing,
Amphiaraus? Why do you leave the war?
And he did not cease plunging down the valley
Until Minos, who seizes each one.
Look how he has made his chest of his back:
Because he wished to see too far ahead,
He walks backward and takes a backward path.
Look at Tiresias, who changed his appearance
When he became a woman from a man,
Changing all his limbs,
And first, and then again, he had to strike
The two entwined snakes with his staff
Before he could regain his manly form.
Aruns is the one who has his back turned toward his belly,
Who, in the mountains of Luni, where the Carrarese
Who lives below hews stone,
Had a cave among the white marble
For his dwelling; from there, when he looked at the stars
And sea, his view was not cut off.
And she who covers her breasts
That you cannot see, with her tresses loose,
And has all her hairy skin on the other side,
Was Manto, who wandered through many lands,
Then settled where I was born;
So I would like you to listen to me a little.
After her father had left this life,
And the city of Bacchus had become enslaved,
She wandered the world for a long time.
Up in Italy there lies a beautiful lake
At the foot of the Alps that enclose Germany
Above Tyrol, called Benacus.
It is watered, I believe, by a thousand springs and more,
Between Garda, Val Camonica, and the Apennines,
By the waters that gather in that lake.
There is a place in the middle where the bishop of Trent,
And the one of Brescia, and the Veronese,
Could each give a blessing if they made that journey.
Peschiera stands, a strong and handsome fortress,
To face the Brescians and Bergamese,
Where the bank slopes down most around the lake.
There all that cannot fit
In Benacus's basin must flow out,
And become a river through green pastures.
As soon as the water sets its head to running,
It is no longer called Benacus but Mincio,
Until Governo, where it falls into the Po.
It has not run far before it finds a marsh,
Where it spreads out into a swamp,
Which in summer is sometimes foul.
Passing through there, the virgin who remained unmarried
Saw land in the middle of the marsh,
Uncultivated and bare of inhabitants.
There, to flee all human company,
She stayed with her servants to practice her art,
And lived there, and left her lifeless body.
The people afterward, who were scattered around,
Gathered at that place, which was well defended
By the marsh on every side.
They built the city above those dead bones;
And after her, who had first chosen the place,
They called it Mantua, without casting lots.
Its people were once more numerous within,
Before the folly of the Casalodi
Was deceived by Pinamonte.
Therefore I warn you: if ever you hear
My homeland's origin told otherwise,
Let no lie defraud the truth.
And I said: Master, your arguments
Are so certain and so firmly take hold of my faith
That all others would be burnt-out coals to me.
But tell me of the people going by,
If you see anyone worthy of note;
For that alone occupies my mind.
Then he said to me: The one whose beard
Falls from his cheek across his dark shoulders
Was there when Greece was emptied of men,
So that scarcely any remained in their cradles;
He is an augur, and with Calchas gave the signal
At Aulis to cut the first ship's rope.
He was called Eurypylus, and so my lofty
Tragedy describes him in one passage;
You know it well, for you know it all.
The other, so slight in the flanks,
Was Michael Scot, who truly
Knew the tricks of magic arts.
Look at Guido Bonatti, look at Asdente,
Who now would wish he had stuck to leather and cord,
But repents too late.
Look at the wretched women who abandoned the needle,
The shuttle and spindle, and made themselves diviners;
They worked sorceries with herbs and images.
But come now, for it already reaches the boundary
Of both hemispheres and touches the sea
Beneath Seville, Cain, and the thorns.
And last night the moon was full:
You should remember well that it did not harm you
At any time in the deep forest.
So he spoke to me, and we went on meanwhile.
