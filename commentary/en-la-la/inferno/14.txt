After the love of my native place
Moved me to gather the scattered leaves,
And to give them back to him who was already hoarse;
Then we came to the edge where there is divided
The second ring from the third, and where
The horrid art of justice is seen.
To make the new things clearly known,
I say that we came to a plain
That banishes every plant from its surface.
The sorrowful wood encircles it,
As the sad ditch does around the wood;
There we halted, close beside the edge.
The ground was dry and dense sand,
Made of no other kind than that
Which Cato once trampled underfoot.
O vengeance of God, how greatly you must
Be feared by everyone who reads
What was made plain to my eyes!
I saw many companies of naked souls,
All weeping most pitifully;
And it seemed that different rules were laid upon them.
Some people lay supine on the ground;
Some sat huddled entirely together,
And others walked continually.
Those who went around were more numerous;
And those lying in torment were fewer,
But their tongues were freer in their pain.
Over all the sand, in a slow fall,
Broad flakes of fire were raining down,
As snow falls in the Alps without wind.
As Alexander, in those hot regions
Of India, saw above his army
Flames fall whole and solid to the ground;
So he arranged to trample the ground
With his ranks, so that the vapor
Would be quenched better while it was alone:
So the everlasting fire came down,
And the sand caught fire, like tinder
Beneath a fire steel, doubling their pain.
Never at rest was the dance
Of their wretched hands, now here, now there,
Brushing the fresh burning away from themselves.
I began: Master, you who overcome
All things except the stubborn demons
Who came out against us at the entrance to the gate,
Who is that great one who seems not to care
About the fire, and lies scornful and twisted,
So that the rain seems not to ripen him?
And that same one, who noticed
That I was asking my guide about him,
Shouted: As I was in life, so am I in death.
If Jove tires his smith, from whom,
In anger, he took the sharp thunderbolt
By which I was struck on the last day;
And if he tires the others in turn
At the black forge on Mongibello,
Calling, Good Vulcan, help! Help!
As he did in the battle of Phlegra,
And shoots me with all his strength,
He could not have joyful revenge for it.
Then my guide spoke forcefully,
To such a degree that I had never heard him speak so forcefully:
O Capaneus, in that there is not subdued
Your pride, you are all the more severely punished:
No torment except your own rage
Would be a fitting pain for your fury.
Then he turned to me with a gentler face
And said: That one was one of the seven kings
Who besieged Thebes; he held, and seems still to hold,
God in contempt, and seems to value Him little.
But, as I said to him, his scorn
Is a quite fitting ornament upon his breast.
Now come after me, and take care not to set
Your feet again upon the scorching sand;
But keep your feet always close to the wood.
In silence we came to a place where there spurts
Out from the wood a little stream;
Its redness still makes me shudder.
As the little stream flows out of the Bulicame,
Which the sinful women then divide among themselves,
So that stream flowed across the sand.
Its bed and both its banks
Were made of stone, with margins on either side;
So I realized the path was there.
Among all the other things I have shown you,
Since we entered through the gate
Whose entrance is denied to no one,
Nothing has been seen by your eyes
As notable as this present stream,
Which quenches all the little flames above it.
These were my guide's words.
So I asked him to give me the food
For which he had already whetted my desire.
In the middle of the sea lies a ruined land,
He said then, which is called Crete,
Under whose king the world was once chaste.
There is a mountain there, once joyful
With water and leaves, called Ida;
Now it is deserted like a forbidden thing.
Rhea once chose it as a trusty cradle
For her son, and, to hide him better
When he cried, she had loud shouts made there.
Inside the mountain stands upright a great old man,
And he keeps his back turned toward Damietta,
And Rome he looks at as his mirror.
His head is formed of fine gold,
And his arms and breast are pure silver,
Then he is bronze down to the fork;
From there downward he is all fine iron,
Except his right foot, which is baked clay,
And he stands upright, more heavily on that than on the other.
Every part except the gold is cracked
By a fissure that drips tears;
Gathered, they pierce this cavern.
Their course descends into this valley;
They form Acheron, Styx, and Phlegethon;
Then they flow down through this narrow channel
To where no farther descent is possible;
They form Cocytus; and what that pool is like
You will see; therefore it is not told here.
And I said to him: If this present stream
Flows in this way from our world,
Why does it appear to us only at this edge?
And he said to me: You know the place is round,
And although you have come a long way
Farther to the left, descending toward the bottom,
You have not yet gone around the whole circle.
So if something new appears to us,
Your face should not be filled with wonder.
And I again: Master, where are
Phlegethon and Lethe, of which one you are silent?
And what is made from this rain of the other?
All your questions certainly please me,
He replied; but the boiling of the red water
Should well have answered one of the questions you ask.
You will see Lethe, but outside this pit,
Where souls go to wash themselves
When their guilt has been repented and removed.
Then he said: Now it is time to depart
From the wood; make sure you follow behind me;
The margins provide a path that is not scorched,
And above them every vapor is extinguished.
