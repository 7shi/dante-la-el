The many people and the diverse wounds
Had so intoxicated my eyes,
That they longed to stay and weep.
But Virgil said to me: Why gaze still?
Why does your sight keep sinking
Down there among the sad, mutilated shades?
You did not do this at the other pits.
And consider, if you think to count them,
That the valley runs twenty-two miles around.
And now the moon is beneath our feet;
The time granted us from now on is short,
And there is more to see than you have seen.
Had you turned, I replied, a little earlier,
Your attention to the reason for my looking,
Perhaps you would still have let me stay there.
Meanwhile he was walking on, and I followed,
The Guide already answering,
And adding: Inside that hollow,
Where I kept my eyes so fixed just now,
I believe a spirit of my blood weeps for
The guilt that costs so much down there.
Then the Master said: Let it not be troubled,
Your thought, henceforth, by him;
Attend to something else, and let him remain there.
For I saw him at the foot of a little bridge
Point at you and threaten fiercely with his finger,
And heard him called Geri del Bello.
You were then so wholly absorbed
In the one who once held Altaforte,
That you did not look that way; he had left.
O my Guide, his violent death,
Still unavenged for him, I said,
By anyone who shares in the dishonor,
Made him disdainful; so he left
Without speaking to me, I suppose;
And for this he has made me pity him more.
Thus we spoke until the first place
Where the cliff shows the next valley,
If there had been more light, all the way to its bottom.
When we were above the last cloister
Of Malebolge, so that its lay brothers
Could appear to our sight,
Various laments shot into me,
Their arrows barbed with pity;
So I covered my ears with my hands.
What suffering there would be if from the hospitals
Of Valdichiana, between July and September,
And of Maremma and Sardinia the illnesses
Were all together in one ditch;
Such was it here, and such rot issued forth,
As comes from putrefying limbs.
We descended to the last bank
Of the long cliff, still to the left,
And then my sight grew clearer
Down toward the bottom, where the minister
Of the High Lord, infallible Justice,
Punishes the falsifiers whom she records here.
I do not think a greater sorrow to see
Was in Ægina, with all its people sick,
When the air was so full of malignity,
That the animals, down to the tiny worm,
All fell dead, and then the ancient peoples,
As the poets hold for certain,
Were restored from a seed of ants;
Than it was to see, in that dark valley,
Spirits languish in many heaps.
Some on the belly, some on the shoulders,
Lay one across another, while some, flat on their backs,
Shifted along the dismal path.
Step by step we went without speaking,
Watching and listening to the sick,
Who could not lift their bodies.
I saw two sitting, leaning against each other,
Like pan against pan set to warm,
From head to foot spotted with scabs.
And I never saw a currycomb moved
By a lad whose master waits for him,
Nor by one who begrudges staying awake,
As each of them often moved the bite
Of his nails over himself, in the fierce rage
Of an itch with no other relief.
They drew the scab off themselves with their nails,
As a knife scrapes scales from a bream,
Or from another fish with larger scales.
O you who dismail yourself with your fingers,
My Guide began to one of them,
And sometimes make pincers of them,
Tell me if any Latian is among these
Who are in here, if your nails will last
Forever at this work.
We are Latins, whom you see so disfigured,
Both of us here, one replied, weeping;
But who are you, who ask about us?
And the Guide said: I am one who descends
With this living man, down from ledge to ledge,
And I intend to show him Hell.
Then their mutual support broke;
And trembling, each turned toward me,
With others who had heard by the echo.
The good Master turned wholly toward me,
Saying: Tell them what you wish.
And I began, since he desired it:
May your memory not be erased
From the first world, from human minds,
But may it live beneath many suns.
Tell me who you are and of what people;
Your ugly and loathsome punishment
Must not frighten you from revealing yourselves to me.
I was of Arezzo, and Albero of Siena,
Made me be put in the fire, one replied;
But the reason I died does not bring me here.
It is true that I said to him, joking,
I could lift myself and fly through the air.
And he, who had desire but little sense,
Wanted me to show him the art; and just
Because I did not make him Daedalus, he had me
Burned by one who held him as a son.
But in the last of the ten pits,
Me, for the alchemy I practiced in the world,
Minos condemned, who cannot err.
And I said to the Master: Now, was there ever
A people so vain as the Sienese?
Certainly not even the French so foolish.
The other leper, who heard me,
Replied to my words: Except Stricca,
Who knew how to spend with moderation;
And Niccolò, who the costly custom
Of the clove first discovered
In the garden where such seed takes root;
And except the company in which was wasted
By Caccia of Asciano his vineyard and great estate,
And Abbagliato, who displayed his wit.
But so that you may know who thus supports you
Against the Sienese, sharpen your eye toward me,
So that my face may answer you clearly:
And then you will see that I am Capocchio's shade,
Who falsified metals through alchemy;
And you should remember this, if I see you rightly,
How I was a good ape of nature.
