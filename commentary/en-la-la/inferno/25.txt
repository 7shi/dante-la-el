At the end of all his words,
He raised both hands toward heaven, making the figs,
And cried, Take them, God, for I aim them at you.
Since then the serpents have been my friends,
For one of them coiled around his neck,
As if to say, I do not want you to speak more.
And another wound around his arms and bound him,
Bracing itself in front so tightly
That he could not make the slightest movement with them.
Ah, Pistoia, Pistoia, why do you not resolve
To burn yourself to ashes, so that you may last no longer,
Since your own seed surpasses you in evil doing?
Through all the dark circles of Hell
I saw no spirit so proud against God,
Not even the one who fell from Thebes' walls.
He went away without speaking another word;
And I saw a Centaur, full of rage,
Shouting, Where is he? Where is that villain?
I do not think Maremma has as many
Snakes, that is, vipers, as he had upon his haunches,
Up to the place where our lips begin.
On his shoulders, behind his rump,
A dragon lay there with its wings spread wide,
And it suffocates anyone it meets.
My Master said, That is Cacus,
Who beneath the rock of Mount Aventine
Often made a pool of blood.
He does not go along the same path as his brothers,
Because he stole by fraud
From the great herd that was near him.
Hence his crooked deeds were brought to an end
Beneath Hercules' club, who perhaps
Struck him a hundred blows, and he felt not ten.
While he was speaking and the other went on ahead,
And three spirits came beneath us,
Of whom neither I nor my guide took notice,
Except when they cried, Who are you?
At which our conversation came to a halt,
And from then on we attended only to them.
I did not know them; but it happened,
As often happens by chance,
That one had to name the other,
Saying, Where can Cianfa have gone?
So I, to make my guide attentive,
Placed my finger from my chin to my nose.
If you are now slow to believe, reader,
What I shall tell, it will be no wonder,
For I, who saw it, scarcely believe it myself.
While I held my brows raised toward them,
Behold, a serpent with six feet leapt
Before one of them and clung all over him.
And with its middle feet it wound around his belly,
And with its forefeet it seized his arms;
Then bit each of his cheeks.
It stretched its hind feet along his thighs,
And put its tail between the two,
Then drew it back up along his loins.
Ivy never clung
To a tree so fast as the horrid beast
Wrapped its limbs around the other's.
Then they joined together, as if of warm wax
They had been made, and mingled their colors;
Neither one nor the other looked as it had been.
As there spreads, before the burning,
Upward through paper a brown color,
Not black, while the white fades away.
The other two watched, and each:
Oh, Agnello, how you are changing!
See, you are now neither two nor one.
The two heads had already become one
When two mixed forms appeared to us
In one face, where the two were lost.
The two arms became four strips;
Thighs, legs, belly, and chest
Became limbs never seen before.
Every first appearance there was gone:
Both two and none the monstrous image
Seemed, and as such went away with slow steps.
As a lizard, under the great lash
Of the dog days, crossing from hedge to hedge,
Seems lightning when it darts across the road,
So there appeared, coming toward the bellies
Of the other two, a little serpent on fire,
Dark blue and black as a peppercorn.
And in the part whence first is received
Our nourishment, he pierced one of them;
Then it fell down before him, stretched out.
The pierced man looked at it, but said nothing;
Instead, with his feet planted, he yawned,
As if sleep or fever were coming over him.
He looked at the serpent, and the serpent at him;
One through its wound, and the other through its mouth,
They smoked fiercely, and their smoke met.
Let Lucan now be silent where he tells
Of wretched Sabellus and Nasidius,
And listen to what is now being launched.
Let Ovid be silent about Cadmus and Arethusa:
For if the one into a serpent, the other into a spring,
He changed in poetry, I do not envy him that.
For two natures ever face to face
He did not change, so that both forms
To alter their substance were so ready.
Together they so answered one another in this way,
That the serpent split its tail into a fork,
And the wounded man drew his feet together.
The legs and thighs, to each other,
Joined so that in a short while the joint
Showed no visible sign.
The divided tail took on the shape
That was disappearing there; its skin
Became soft, while the man's became hard.
I saw the arms draw back into the armpits,
And the beast's two feet, which had been short,
Lengthen as much as those arms shortened.
Then its hind feet, twisted together,
Became the member a man conceals,
And the wretch put forth two of his own.
While smoke veiled both
With a new color, it raised hair above
On one side, and stripped it from the other,
One rose, and the other fell down,
Yet neither turned its impious eyes,
Beneath which each changed its muzzle.
The one who stood drew it toward the temples;
From the excess of matter that came to him,
Ears emerged from the smooth cheeks.
What did not run stayed behind;
From that excess he made a nose for his face,
And thickened his lips as much as was fitting.
The one who lay thrusts his muzzle forward,
And draws his ears back through his head,
As a snail does with its horns;
And the tongue, which he previously had whole and ready
For speech, splits; and the forked one
Closes back in the other, and the smoke ceases.
The soul that had become a beast,
Hissing, fled along the valley,
And the other spat as he spoke behind it.
Then he turned his new shoulders toward him,
And said to the other, I want Buoso to run,
Crawling along this path, as I have done.
Thus I saw the seventh ballast
Change and transform; and here may I be excused
By novelty, if my pen recoils a little.
And although confused my eyes
Were somewhat, and my mind bewildered,
Yet they could not flee so secretly
That I did not clearly recognize Puccio Sciancato:
And he was the one who alone of the three companions
Who first came had not been changed;
The other was he whom you, Gaville, mourn.
