He lifted his mouth from the savage meal,
That sinner, wiping it on the hair
Of the head he had mauled behind.
Then he began: You want me to renew
The despairing grief that weighs on my heart,
Merely thinking, before I speak of it.
But if my words are to be seed
That bears infamy for the traitor I gnaw,
You will see me speak and weep together.
I do not know who you are, nor how
You came here; but a Florentine
You truly seem to me, when I hear you.
You must know that I was Count Ugolino,
And this man is Archbishop Ruggieri;
Now I will tell you why I am such a bad neighbour to him.
That through the effect of his evil designs,
Trusting him, I was captured
And later killed, needs no telling.
But what you could not have understood,
That is, how cruel my death was,
You will hear, and learn whether he wronged me.
A narrow opening inside the Muda,
Which because of me bears the name of Hunger,
And must yet imprison others,
Had shown me through its opening
More light already, when I had the evil dream
That tore for me the veil from the future.
This man seemed to me a master and lord,
Hunting the wolf and its whelps up the mountain,
Because of which the Pisans cannot see Lucca.
With lean, eager, well-trained hounds,
Gualandi with Sismondi and Lanfranchi
He had set in front of him.
In a short chase there seemed to me weary
The father and sons, and with sharp teeth
I seemed to see their flanks torn.
When I awoke before morning,
I heard my sons weeping in their sleep,
They who were with me, and asking for bread.
You are cruel indeed if you do not already grieve,
Thinking of what was foretold to me and to my heart;
And if you do not weep, what do you usually weep for?
They were already awake, and the hour was near
When food was usually brought to us,
And each of us feared what his dream foretold.
I heard the lower door being shut
Of the dreadful tower; so I looked
Into the faces of my sons without a word.
I did not weep; I grew stone within;
They wept; and my little Anselm
Said: You look so, father! What is wrong?
Therefore I did not weep or answer
All that day, nor the following night,
Until the next sun rose in the world.
When a little ray had entered
The sorrowful prison, and I saw
My own likeness in the faces of all four.
Both my hands I bit in grief.
And they, thinking I did it from desire
To eat, suddenly rose up,
And said: Father, our pain will be much less
If you eat us: you clothed us
In this miserable flesh, so strip it away.
Then I calmed myself so as not to sadden them more.
That day and the next we all were mute.
Ah, hard earth, why did you not open?'
When we had come to the fourth day,
Gaddo threw himself stretched out at my feet,
And said: My father, why do you not help me?
Thus he died; and as you see,
So I saw the three fall one by one
Between the fifth and sixth day; whereupon I began
Already blind, to grope over each one,
And for two days I called them after they were dead.
Then hunger proved stronger than grief.
When he had said this, with his eyes twisted,
He seized again with his teeth the wretched skull,
Which were strong on the bone, like a dog's.
Ah, Pisa, shame of the peoples
Of the fair land where it resounds!
Since your neighbours are slow to punish you,
May Capraia and Gorgona move
And make a barrier at the Arno's mouth,
So that it drowns every person in you,
For if Count Ugolino was accused
Of treachery concerning your castles,
You should not have put his sons to such torment.
Their tender age made them innocent,
New Thebes! Uguccione and Brigata,
And the other two whom the song above named.
We went farther on, where the ice
Roughly enfolds another people,
Not turned downward, but wholly reversed.
The very act of weeping does not let them weep;
And the grief, finding an obstacle above,
Turns inward and increases their anguish.
For the first tears form a knot,
And, like crystal visors,
Fill the whole hollow beneath the brows.
And although thus, as if from a callus,
Because of the cold every sensation
That condition had banished from my face,
I already seemed to feel a little wind.
So I said: My master, what causes this?
Has every vapor not been extinguished down here?
So he said to me: Soon you will be where
Your own eyes will answer this for you,
When you see the cause that pours out the blast.
And one of the wretches in the cold crust
Cried: O cruel souls,
So far that the last place has been assigned to you,
Remove the hard veils from my face,
So that I may vent the grief that fills my heart
A little, before the weeping freezes.
So I said to him: If you want me to help you,
Tell me who you are; and if I do not free you,
May I have to go to the bottom of the ice.
He replied: Then I am Friar Alberigo;
I am the one from the fruit of the evil garden,
Who here receives dates in place of figs.
O, I said to him, are you still dead?
And he said to me: How my body fares
In the world above, I know nothing.
This Ptolomæa has this privilege:
That a soul often falls here
Before Atropos has given it death.
And so that you may more willingly scrape for me
The glassed-over tears from the face,
Know that as soon as a soul betrays,
As mine did, its body is taken
By a devil, who then governs it
Until all its time has run its course.
It falls into this kind of cistern,
And perhaps the body still appears above
Of the shade that winters here behind me.
You must know, if you have only just come down;
It is Branca d'Oria, and many years have
Passed since he was shut in like this.
I said: I think you are deceiving me,
For Branca d'Oria has not yet died,
And eats, drinks, sleeps, and wears clothes.
He said: In the ditch of the Malebranche,
Where sticky pitch boils,
Michael Zanche had not yet arrived,
When that man left a devil in his place
In his body, and in the body of a kinsman
Who committed treachery with him.
But now stretch your hand here toward me;
Open my eyes; and I did not open them,
For courtesy toward him would have been discourtesy.
Ah, Genoese, people departing
From all custom, and full of malice,
Why are you not banished from the world?
For with the worst spirit of Romagna
I found just one of you who, by his deed,
Already bathes in Cocytus in soul,
Yet above still seems alive in body.
