In the middle of the journey of our life
I found myself in a dark wood,
Whose straight way was astray.
Alas, how hard a thing it is to tell what it was like,
This savage wood, both harsh and dense,
Which in thought renews fear.
So bitter is it that death is scarcely more bitter than it.
Yet, to treat of the good that I found in it,
I will speak of the other things that I perceived therein.
I could not well say how I entered it;
So full of sleep was I at that moment,
That I forsook the true way.
But after I came to the foot of a hill,
Where that valley ended
Which had pierced my heart with fear,
I looked on high, and saw its shoulders
Already clothed with the rays of the planet
Which leads everyone straight along every path.
Then the fear was somewhat quieted,
Which in the depth of my heart had endured
During the night that I passed with such anguish.
And even as he who with spent breath,
Having come forth from the sea unto the shore,
Turns to the perilous water, and gazes;
So my mind, which still was fleeing,
Turned back to look upon the pass
That never left a single person alive.
After the weary body had been rested,
I resumed the way along the desert ridge,
So that the firm foot was always the lower.
And behold, almost at the beginning of the slope,
A leopard light and very swift,
Which was covered with a spotted coat.
And it did not withdraw before my face,
Nay, it hindered my journey so much
That I was turned many times to go back.
The time was at the beginning of the morning;
And the sun was mounting up with those stars
Which were with it, when divine love
First set in motion those fair things;
So that cause to hope well was given me
By the pleasant skin of that wild beast,
The hour of the time, and the sweet season:
Yet not so, but that fear was given me
By the sight that appeared to me of a lion.
This seemed as though it were coming against me
With head held high and with rabid hunger,
So that it seemed the air feared it:
And a she-wolf, that is greedy for all things,
Appeared laden in her leanness,
Which has already forced many peoples to live in misery.
She laid upon me so much heaviness
With the fear that issued from her aspect,
That I lost the hope of the height.
And as is he who gladly acquires,
And the time comes that makes him lose,
Who in his every thought weeps and grieves;
Such did the beast without peace make me,
Which, advancing against me, little by little
Drove me to the place where the sun is silent.
While I was falling downward into the lowest place,
Before my eyes was offered one
Who through long silence seemed hoarse.
When I saw this one in the great desert,
Have mercy on me, I cried out to him,
Whoever you be, whether shade or actual man.
He answered me: Not a man, once I was a man,
And my parents were Lombards,
Both Mantuans by native land.
I was born still under Julius, albeit late,
And I lived at Rome under good Augustus,
In the time of the false and lying gods.
I was a poet, and I sang of that just
Son of Anchises, who came from Troy,
After proud Ilium was burned,
But you, why do you return to so great a hurt?
Why do you not ascend the delectable mount,
Which is the beginning and cause of all joy?
Oh, are you then that Virgil, and that fount
Which pours forth so broad a river of eloquence?
I answered him with bashful brow.
O honor and light of the other poets,
May the long study and the great love avail me,
Which made me search through your volume.
You are my master and my author,
You alone are he from whom I took
The fair style that has done me honor.
Behold the beast because of which I turned back:
Help me from her, famous and wise one,
For she makes my veins and pulses tremble.
You must hold another course,
He answered me, after he saw me weep,
If you wish to escape from this savage place:
Because that beast, on whose account you cry out,
Suffers no one to pass along her way,
But hinders him so much that she slays him:
And she has a nature so wicked and depraved,
That she never satisfies her greedy desire,
And after feeding is more hungry than before.
Many are the animals to which she is wedded,
And more there will yet be, until the Greyhound
Shall come, who will make her die with grief.
He shall not feed on earth nor on pewter,
But on wisdom, love, and virtue;
And his birth shall be between Feltro and Feltro.
He shall be the salvation of that humble Italy,
For which the virgin Camilla died,
Euryalus, Turnus, and Nisus of their wounds:
He shall hunt her through every town,
Until he has driven her back into Hell,
Whence envy first set her loose.
Wherefore I for your better good think and discern
That you should follow me, and I will be your guide,
And lead you forth from here through an eternal place,
Where you shall hear the desperate shrieks
Of those ancient sorrowing spirits,
Of whom each one cries out for the second death.
And you shall see those who are content
In the fire, because they hope to come,
Whenever it may be, unto the blessed people.
To whom if you thereafter wish to ascend,
A soul shall be for this worthier than I;
With her I will leave you at my departure:
Because that Emperor who reigns there,
Since I was rebellious to His law,
Wills not that into His city one come through me.
In all parts He commands, and there He reigns;
There is His city and His high throne:
O happy he whom there He chooses.
And I to him: Poet, I beseech you,
And by that God whom you knew not,
So that I may flee this evil and worse,
That you lead me thither where you just said,
So that I may see the gate of Saint Peter,
And those whom you make out so sorrowful.
Then he moved on, and I kept behind him.
