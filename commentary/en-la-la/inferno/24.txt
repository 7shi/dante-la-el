In that part of the young year,
When the sun tempers his rays beneath Aquarius,
And already the nights turn toward midday;
When frost on the ground forms
The image of its white sister,
But its feathery temper lasts only a little;
The farmer, whose stores are failing,
Rises and looks out, and sees the countryside
All whitening, so he strikes his thigh;
He returns home, and frets here and there,
Like a poor wretch who knows not what to do;
Then goes out again, and hope returns to him,
Seeing the world change its face
Within a little hour, and he takes his staff,
And drives the little sheep out to pasture;
So my master made me afraid,
When I saw his brow troubled,
And so quickly the hurt was plastered over:
For as soon as we came to the broken bridge,
My guide turned toward me with that look,
Gentle, which I had first seen at the foot of the mountain.
He opened his arms, and after some course of action
Chosen in his own mind, first looking
Carefully at the ruin, and then took hold of me.
And like one who acts and judges,
So that he always seems to look ahead for himself,
So, lifting me up toward the summit
Of a jutting crag, he marked another projection,
Saying, Cling to that one next;
But first test whether it is such that it will bear you.
There was no way for one wearing a cape,
For we scarcely, he light and I being pushed,
Could climb up from ledge to ledge.
And had it not been that at that enclosure,
More than at the other, the slope was short,
I cannot say about him, but I would certainly have been defeated.
But since Malebolge toward the entrance
Of the deepest pit slopes wholly,
Each valley's position entails this:
For one bank rises and the other falls;
At last we reached the point
Where the final rock face breaks away.
My lungs' breath was so spent
When I reached the top that I could go no farther;
Instead, at my first arrival, I sat down.
Now you must indeed rouse yourself,
Said the Master, for by lying on feathers,
One does not win fame, nor beneath a coverlet.
Without which, whoever spends his life
Leaves such a trace of himself on earth
As smoke in air or foam on water.
And therefore rise; overcome your anguish
With the spirit that conquers every battle,
Unless it lets itself be weighed down by the heavy body.
You must climb a longer staircase;
It is not enough to have left these behind.
If you understand me, now act so that this may help you.
Then I rose, showing myself supplied
Better with breath than I felt I was,
And said, Go on, for I am strong and bold.
We took our way up over the crag,
Which was rocky, narrow, and difficult,
And much steeper than the one before.
I went along talking so as not to seem weak,
When a voice came out of the next ditch,
Forming words that were improper.
I do not know what it said, although on the back
Of the arch that crosses there I already was;
But the speaker seemed moved by anger.
I was looking downward, but my living eyes
Could not reach the bottom through the darkness;
So I said, Master, make your way
To the next bank, and let us descend the wall;
For though I hear from here, I cannot understand,
And though I look below, I can make out nothing.
I will give you no other answer,
Except to do it; for an honest request
Should be followed by action, without a word.
We descended the bridge from its head,
Where it joins the eighth bank,
And then the bolgia became clear to me.
And there inside I saw a dreadful mass
Of snakes, and of such varied kinds,
That memory and blood still shudder at it.
Let Libya boast no more of its sands,
For even if the chelydri, jaculi, and phareae
It produces, and the centri with amphisbaenae.
Not so many plagues nor ones so evil
Did it ever show with all of Ethiopia,
Nor with all that lies above the Red Sea.
Among that cruel and most sorrowful horde
Naked, terrified people ran,
With no hope of a hiding hole or heliotrope.
They had their hands bound behind them with snakes;
And these thrust their tails through their loins,
And their heads, and were knotted in front.
And behold, at one who was on our bank
A snake sprang and pierced him
Where neck and shoulders are joined.
O was never written, nor I, so swiftly,
As he caught fire and burned, and ash all of him
Had to become as he fell.
And after he was thus destroyed on the ground,
The dust gathered itself together, and by itself
Returned at once to its former state.
So the great sages affirm
That the Phœnix dies and afterward is born again
When the five-hundredth year draws near.
In its life it feeds on neither grass nor grain,
But only on incense and tears of amomum;
Both nard and myrrh are its final wrappings.
And like one who falls, not knowing how,
By force of a demon dragging him to earth,
Or by some other seizure that binds a person,
Who, when he rises, looks all around,
Wholly dazed by the great anguish
He has endured, and sighs as he looks;
Such was the sinner after he had risen.
O power of God, how severe it is,
To rain down such blows in vengeance!
My guide then asked him who he was;
And he replied, I fell from Tuscany
Only a short time ago, into this savage gullet.
I liked a beast's life, not a human one,
Like a mule, as I was. I am Vanni Fucci,
The beast; and Pistoia was a fitting den for me.
And I said to my guide, Tell him not to flee,
And ask him what guilt thrust him down here,
For I knew him as a man of blood and brawls.
And the sinner heard and did not pretend otherwise,
But turned his mind and face toward me,
And was painted with bitter shame.
Then he said, It pains me more that you caught me
In the wretched state where you see me
Than when I was taken from the other life.
I cannot deny what you ask:
I was sent so far down because I was
The thief of the sacristy's beautiful treasures;
And another was falsely accused of it.
But so that you will not enjoy this sight,
If ever you get out of these dark places,
Open your ears to my grim announcement and hear:
Pistoia first grows thin of its Blacks,
Then Florence renews its people and customs.
Mars draws a vapour from Valdimagra,
Which will be wrapped in turbulent clouds,
And with a storm both violent and fierce
Battle will be joined over the field of Piceno;
Then suddenly it will break through the cloud,
So that every White will be struck by it;
And I said this so you would grieve.
