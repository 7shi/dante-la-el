The flame had already risen upright and grown still,
So as to say no more; and already it was leaving us
With the leave of the sweet Poet;
When another, following behind it,
Made us turn our eyes toward its tip,
Because of a confused sound issuing from within.
As the Sicilian bull, which lowed for the first time
With the cries of the man (and this was just)
Who had fashioned it with his file,
It first lowed with the voice of a tormented man,
So that, although it was made of bronze,
It nevertheless seemed itself pierced through with pain;
So, because it had no passage or opening
At the beginning of the fire, into its own language
Were transformed the sorrowful words.
But after they had found their way
Up through the tip, giving it the quiver
That the tongue had given in their passing,
We heard it say: O you to whom I direct
My voice, and who were just now speaking Lombard,
Saying, Now go on; I ask you no more;
Although I may have arrived somewhat late,
Do not mind staying to speak with me:
See, I do not mind, though I am burning.
If you only just now into this blind world
Have fallen from that sweet land
Latin, from which I bring back my guilt,
Tell me whether the Romagnols are at peace or war;
For I came from the mountains there, between Urbino
And the ridge from which the Tiber flows.
I was still below, intent and bent over,
When my guide nudged me at the side,
Saying: You speak, for this man is Latin.
And I, who already had my answer ready,
Began to speak without delay:
O soul, hidden down there,
Your Romagna is not, and never has been,
Without war in the hearts of its tyrants;
But in the open country I left no war there now.
Ravenna stands as it has for many years:
The eagle of Polenta broods there,
So that it shades Cervia with its wings.
The land that once endured the long trial,
And made a bloody heap of the French,
Is found beneath green claws.
The old and the new mastiff of Verrucchio,
Who ruled Montagna badly,
Where they are accustomed to suck with their teeth.
The city of the Lamone and the Santerno
Is governed by the little lion from the white nest,
Who changes sides from summer to winter.
And that city whose flank the Savio bathes,
Just as it sits between plain and mountain,
Lives between tyranny and a free state.
Now, who are you? I beg you to tell us.
Do not be more hard-hearted than others have been,
If your name holds its place in the world.
After the flame had murmured a little
In its own way, it moved its tip
From side to side, and then gave this breath:
If I believed my answer would be
To someone who would ever return to the world,
This flame would stand still without another shake;
But since never from this pit
Has anyone returned alive, if I hear the truth,
I answer you without fear of infamy.
I was a man-at-arms, and then I was a Cordelier,
Believing that, thus girded, I would make amends;
And indeed my belief was complete,
If it were not for the great priest, may evil befall him,
Who returned me to my first sins.
And I want you to understand me as to how and why.
While I was formed of bones and flesh
That my mother gave me, my deeds
Were not those of a lion, but of a fox.
Stratagems and hidden paths
I knew them all, and practiced their arts so well
That my fame spread to the ends of the earth.
When I saw that I had reached that stage
Of my life when everyone should
Lower the sails and gather in the ropes,
What had once pleased me then displeased me;
I made myself both penitent and confessed;
Alas, wretched and fallen me! And it would have helped me.
The prince of the new Pharisees,
Waging war near the Lateran,
And not against Saracens or Jews,
For every enemy of his was Christian,
And none had gone to conquer Acre,
Nor traded in the Sultan's land;
Neither his supreme office nor the sacred orders
Did he respect in himself, nor in me that cord,
Which used to make his girded ones leaner.
But just as Constantine called on Silvester
At Soracte to cure him of leprosy,
So this man called on me as his master,
To cure him of his proud fever.
He asked my counsel, and I fell silent,
For his words seemed drunken to me.
And then he said to me: Let your heart have no fear;
From now I absolve you; and teach me to act,
How I may cast Penestrino down to the ground.
I can close and open heaven,
As you know; therefore there are two keys,
Which my predecessor did not value.
Then weighty arguments pressed me,
And I thought that keeping silent would be worse;
So I said: Father, since you absolve me
Of the sin into which I must now fall,
A long promise with short performance
Will make you triumph on the high throne.
Francis came afterward, as soon as I had died,
For me; but one of the black Cherubim
Said: Do not take him; do me no wrong.
He must come down here among my wretches,
Because he gave fraudulent counsel.
Ever since, I have had him by the hair.
For no one can be absolved who does not repent;
Nor can repentance and willing occur together,
Because of the contradiction that does not permit it.
Alas for me in my grief! How I shook
When he seized me, saying to me: Perhaps
You did not think that I knew logic!
He carried me to Minos, who for me wrapped
His tail eight times around his hard back,
And after, in great rage, he had bitten it,
Said: This one is among the guilty of the thieving fire.
So where you see me, I am lost,
And, thus dressed, I go on lamenting.
When he had finished speaking,
The flame departed in pain,
Twisting from side to side and beating its sharp tip.
We went onward, my guide and I,
Over the rock up to the next arch,
Which spans the pit where the debt is paid
By those who, sowing discord, earn their burden.
