In the time when Juno was enraged
Because of Semele against the Theban blood,
As she showed on more than one occasion,
Athamas became so mad
That, seeing his wife with two children
Coming burdened, one in either hand,
He cried: Let us spread the nets, so I may catch
The lioness and her cubs at the pass.
And then he stretched out his pitiless claws,
Seizing one named Learchus,
And he whirled him around and struck him against a rock;
And she drowned herself with her other burden.
And when Fortune brought low
The might of the Trojans, which had dared so much,
So that king and kingdom together were destroyed,
Hecuba, grieving, wretched, and captive,
After she saw Polyxena dead,
And of her Polydorus on the shore
Of the sea she, sorrowing, became aware;
Out of her mind, she barked like a dog;
So greatly had grief warped her mind.
But never Theban Furies nor Trojan ones
Were ever seen so cruel to anyone,
Not in rending beasts, let alone human limbs,
As I saw in two pale and naked shades,
Who ran biting in that way
That a pig runs when driven from its sty.
One reached Capocchio and, at the knot
Of his neck, bit him so that, dragging him,
It made him scrape his belly against the hard ground,
And the Aretine, left trembling,
Said to me: That madman is Gianni Schicchi,
And in his rage he goes about treating others so.
Oh, I said to him, if the other does not plant in you
His claws, in the back, may it be no trouble to you
To say who he is before he goes away.
And he said to me: That is the ancient soul
Of wicked Myrrha, who became
Her father's lover, against proper love.
She joined with him in sin,
Disguising herself in another's form,
As the other one going there contrived,
To gain the lady of the herd,
To disguise himself as Buoso Donati,
Making a will and setting its terms.
And after the two raging spirits had passed,
On whom I had long kept my gaze,
I turned to look at the other damned souls.
And I saw one shaped like a lute,
If only he had had his groin
Cut away from the side where a man is forked.
The great dropsy, which so distorts
The limbs with fluid that it turns to ill,
So that the face does not match the belly,
Made him keep his lips apart,
As the consumptive does, who from thirst
Turns one lip toward the chin and the other toward the nose.
O you who are without any punishment
(though I do not know why) in this wretched world,
He said to us, look and pay attention
To the misery of Master Adam.
Alive, I had plenty of what I wanted,
And now, alas for me now! I long for one drop of water.
The little streams that from the green hills
Of Casentino descend into the Arno,
Making their channels cool and soft,
Always stand before me, and not in vain;
For their image dries me far more
Than the illness that wastes my face.
The stern justice that goads me
Draws its cause from the place where I sinned,
To drive my sighs still further away.
There lies Romena, where I falsified
The stamped alloy of the Baptist,
For which I left my body burned above.
But if I could see here the sad souls
Of Guido, Alessandro, and their brother,
I would not trade the sight for Branda's spring.
One of them is already inside here, if the rabid
Shades that circle about speak the truth;
But what good is it, with my limbs bound?
If only I were still light enough
To travel an ounce in a hundred years,
I would already have set out along the path,
Seeking him among this wretched crowd,
Though it circles for eleven miles,
And is no more than half a mile across.
I am among such company because of them:
They persuaded me to strike florins
That had three carats of dross.
And I said to him: Who are the two poor souls
Who smoke like wet hands in winter,
Lying close at your right-hand side?
I found them here, and they have not turned since,
He answered, when I rained into this ditch,
And I do not think they ever will.
One is the false woman who accused Joseph;
The other is the false Greek Sinon from Troy;
Their acute fever makes them stink so.
And one of them, taking offense
Perhaps at being named so obscurely,
Struck his hard belly with a fist.
It sounded as if it were a drum;
And Master Adam struck his face
With his arm that seemed no less hard,
Saying to him: Though I have been deprived
Of the movement of my heavy limbs,
My arm is still free for such a task.
Then he replied: When you went
To the fire, you did not have it so nimble;
But you had it so, and more so, when you coined.
And the dropsical man: You speak truly of that;
But you were not such a truthful witness
When truth was demanded of you at Troy.
If I told lies, you falsified the coin,
Said Sinon, and I am here for one fault,
But you for more than any other demon.
Remember your perjury about the horse,
Replied the one with the swollen belly;
And may it shame you, for all the world knows it.
And may it be your shame, the thirst that cracks your
Said the Greek, tongue, and the rotten fluid,
Since your belly fences off your sight.
Then the coiner said: So is split
Your mouth to speak evil, as it always does;
For if I am thirsty, the fluid stands before my face.
But you have the burning fever and aching head;
And to lick Narcissus' mirror
You would not need many words of urging.
I was wholly intent on listening
When my master said: Now just look,
For I am very nearly angry with you.
When I heard him speak to me in anger,
I turned toward him with such shame
That it still turns in my memory.
And as one who dreams of his own harm
And, dreaming, longs to be dreaming,
So that he yearns for what is, as though it were not,
So I became, unable to speak,
Longing to excuse myself, and doing so
And all the while believing I did not do it.
A greater fault is washed away by lesser shame
Than yours has been, my master said;
So cast off all your sadness:
And consider me always at your side,
If it should happen that Fortune brings you
Where people are in such a dispute;
For wanting to hear it is a base desire.
