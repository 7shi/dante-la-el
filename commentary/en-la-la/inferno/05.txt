Thus I descended from the first circle
Down into the second, which encloses less space,
And so much greater pain, which pierces with wailing.
Minos stands there, horribly roaring:
He examines sins at the entrance,
Judges and dispatches, according to what he finds.
I say that when the ill-born soul
Comes before him, it confesses everything;
And that knower of sins
Sees what place in Hell is meant for it:
He girds himself with his tail so many times
As are the levels he wills it to be sent down.
Many always stand before him:
They go in turn, each one to judgment;
They speak and hear, and then are turned below.
O you who come to this house of sorrow,
Minos said to me when he saw me,
Leaving aside the work of so great an office,
Look how you enter, and in whom you trust:
Let not the breadth of the entrance deceive you.
And my guide to him: Why do you still cry out?
Do not obstruct his fated journey:
So it is willed there, where power resides
To do whatever is willed; ask no more.
Now the doleful notes begin
To make themselves heard by me; now I have come
Where much wailing strikes me.
I came to a place mute of all light,
Which bellows as the sea does in a storm,
When it is battered by opposing winds.
The infernal whirlwind, which never rests,
Drives the spirits with its violence,
Whirling and striking them, torments them.
When they reach before the ruin,
There are shrieks, groans, and lamentation;
There they blaspheme the divine power.
I understood that to such torment
Were condemned carnal sinners,
Who subject reason to appetite.
And as starlings are borne on their wings
In the cold season, in a broad and crowded flock,
So that wind carries the evil spirits
Here and there, up and down:
No hope ever comforts them,
Not of rest, nor even of lesser pain.
And as cranes go singing their lays,
Making a long line of themselves in the air,
So I saw them come, drawing out their wails,
Shades borne by that same strife.
So I said: Master, who are those people
Whom the black air chastises so?
The first of those whose story
You wish to know, he then told me,
Was empress of many tongues.
She was so broken by the vice of lust
That she made lust lawful in her law,
To remove the shame into which she had fallen.
She is Semiramis, of whom we read
That she succeeded Ninus and was his wife;
She ruled the land governed by the Sultan.
The other is she who killed herself for love
And broke faith with the ashes of Sychaeus;
The other is lustful Cleopatra.
I saw Helen, for whom such evil
An age of time was turned about, and I saw great Achilles,
Who fought to the end for love.
I saw Paris, Tristan; and more than a thousand
Shades he showed me and named with his finger,
Whom love had parted from our life.
After I had heard my guide
Name the ancient ladies and knights,
Pity overcame me, and I was almost overcome.
And I began: Poet, I would gladly
Speak to those two who go together
And seem so light upon the wind.
And he said to me: You will see them when they are
Closer to us; then ask them,
By the love that leads them, and they will come.
As soon as the wind bends them toward us,
I raised my voice: O souls in anguish,
Come speak with us, if none forbids it.
Like doves, summoned by desire,
With wings open and steady, to their sweet nest
They come through the air, borne by their will,
So they left the flock where Dido is,
Coming toward us through the malign air,
So strong was my loving cry.
O gracious and kindly creature,
Who travels through the darkened air
To visit us, who stained the world with our blood,
If we were friends of the King of the universe,
We would pray to him for your peace,
Since you pity our perverse woe.
What you would like to hear and to speak of,
We shall hear and speak to you,
For as long as the wind, as now, gives us pause.
The land where I was born lies
On the shore where the Po descends
To make peace with its tributaries.
Love, which quickly seizes upon a noble heart,
Took hold of him because of the fair body
That was taken from me, and the manner still wounds me.
Love, which spares no beloved from loving,
Seized me with such delight in this man
That, as you see, it still does not leave me.
Love led us to one death;
Caïna awaits the one who took our life.
These words were spoken to us by them.
When I heard those injured souls,
I bowed my face and held it so low
Until the Poet said to me: What are you thinking?
And I replied, beginning: Alas,
How many sweet thoughts, how much desire
Led these two to this sorrowful pass!
Then I turned to them and spoke,
Beginning: Francesca, your torments
Make me sorrowful and compassionate, and bring me to tears.
But tell me: in the time of sweet sighs,
How and by what signs did love allow
You to know your uncertain desires?
And she said to me: There is no greater sorrow
Than to remember a happy time
In misery; and your guide knows this.
But if you have such desire
To learn the first root of our love,
I shall speak as one who weeps while speaking.
One day, for pleasure, we were reading
Of Lancelot, how love overcame him;
We were alone, and without any suspicion.
Many times that reading drew our eyes
Together, and drained the color from our faces;
But one passage alone overcame us.
When we read of the desired smile
Being kissed by so great a lover,
This man, who will never be parted from me,
Kissed my mouth, all trembling:
Galeotto was the book and he who wrote it;
That day we read no farther.
While one spirit spoke thus,
The other wept so that, out of pity,
I fainted as though I were dying;
And I fell as a dead body falls.
