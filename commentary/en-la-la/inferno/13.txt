Nessus had not yet reached the other bank,
When we set out through a wood,
Marked by no path.
No green leaves, but of a dark hue,
No straight branches, but knotty and twisted,
No fruit, but thorns with poison.
The wild beasts that hate cultivated places
Between Cecina and Corneto
Have no thickets so harsh or dense.
There the foul Harpies make their nests,
They who drove the Trojans from the Strophades
With a sad omen of future harm.
They have broad wings, necks, and human faces,
Clawed feet and a great feathered belly;
They lament in the strange trees.
And the good master began to say to me: Before you go farther,
Know that you are in the second ring,
And you will remain there until—
For you will come to the dreadful sand.
So look carefully, and you will see
Things that would make my words unbelievable.
I heard cries of woe from every side,
Yet saw no person making them;
So, wholly bewildered, I stopped.
I think he thought that I thought
So many voices came from those bushes
From people hidden from us there.
Therefore, said the master, if you break
A little twig from one of these plants,
All the thoughts you have will be cut short.
Then I reached my hand a little forward
And plucked a small branch from a great thorn bush;
And its trunk cried out: Why do you tear me?
When it had turned dark with blood,
It began to cry: Why do you tear me up?
Have you no spirit of pity at all?
We were men, and now have become trunks;
Your hand should be more compassionate
If we had been souls of serpents.
As from a green log burning
At one end, while at the other it groans,
And hisses in the wind that passes;
So from the broken splinter came together
Words and blood; whereat I let the top
Fall and stood like a man afraid.
If he had been able to believe before,
My wise master replied, wounded soul,
What he has seen in my poem,
He would not have laid a hand on you;
But the incredible thing made me
Lead him to do what grieves me too.
But tell him who you are, so that, in return
For some reparation, he may refresh your fame
In the world above, where he may return.
And the trunk: You so sweetly entice me
With your words that I cannot be silent; and may it not burden you
If I become entangled in speaking a little.
I am the one who held both keys
To Frederick's heart and turned them,
Locking and unlocking so gently,
That I kept almost everyone from his secrets.
I was faithful to my glorious office,
So much that I lost my senses and pulse through it.
The harlot who never took her wanton eyes
From Cæsar's lodging,
Common death and vice of courts,
Inflamed every mind against me;
And those inflamed ones so inflamed Augustus
That my joyous honors turned to sorrowful mourning.
My mind, through scornful bitterness,
Believing that by dying I could flee disdain,
Made me unjust against myself, who was just.
By the new roots of this tree
I swear to you I never broke faith
With my lord, so worthy of honor.
And if any of you returns to the world,
Let him restore my memory, which still lies
Stricken by the blow envy dealt it.
He waited a little, then said: Since he is silent,
the Poet said to me, do not lose the hour;
Speak, and ask him what else you wish.
Then I said to him: Ask him again
About what you think would satisfy me,
For I cannot; such pity weighs on my heart.
So he began again: If a man freely grants you
What your words ask,
Prisoned spirit, may it please you still
To tell us how the soul is bound
In these knots; and tell us, if you can,
Whether any soul ever frees itself from such limbs.
Then the trunk breathed hard, and afterward
The wind turned into these words: A brief reply
Will be given to you.
When the fierce soul departs
From the body from which it tore itself away,
Minos sends it to the seventh maw.
It falls into the wood, not to a chosen place,
But wherever Fortune hurls it;
There it sprouts like a grain of spelt.
It rises as a shoot and a wild plant;
The Harpies, feeding then on its leaves,
Cause pain and open a path for pain.
Like the others, we shall return for our bodies,
But none of us will put them on again,
For it is not just to have what one takes from oneself.
Here we shall drag them, and through the sad
Wood our bodies will hang,
Each from the thorn of its troublesome shade.
We were still attentive to the trunk,
Believing it wished to say something more,
When a noise surprised us,
Like one who hears a wild boar coming
To the hunter waiting at his stand,
Hearing beasts and branches rustle.
And behold, two from the left slope,
Naked and scratched, fled so fast
That they broke every thicket in the wood.
And the one in front cried: Come, come, O death!
And the other, who thought he was too slow,
Shouted: Lano, your legs were not so quick—
At the jousts of Toppo.
But perhaps his breath was failing,
And he made a knot of himself and a bush.
Behind them the wood was full
Of black, hungry, running hounds,
Like greyhounds loosed from their chains.
They sank their teeth into the one who hid,
And tore him apart, limb by limb;
Then carried off his suffering limbs.
Then my guide took me by the hand
And led me to the bush that wept,
In vain, over its bloody wounds.
O Jacomo, it said, da Sant' Andrea,
What did it profit you to use me as a shield?
What fault was mine in your wicked life?
When the master stood over it,
He said: Who were you, that through so many wounds
You breathe a sorrowful speech with blood?
And it said to us: O souls who have come
To see the shameful tearing
That has separated my branches from me,
Gather them at the foot of the sad bush.
I was from the city that changed its first patron
For the Baptist; and because of this,
It will always make that city sad by its art.
Were it not that some image of him still remains
Above the passage of the Arno,
Those citizens who later rebuilt it
Over the ashes left by Attila
Would have labored in vain. I made a gallows
For myself from my own house.
