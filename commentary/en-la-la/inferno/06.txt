At the return of my awareness, which had closed
Before the pity for the two kinsmen
That had wholly confused me with sorrow,
New torments and new tormented souls
I see around me, however I move,
However I turn, and wherever I look.
I am in the third circle of rain
Eternal, accursed, cold, and heavy;
Its pattern and nature never change.
Thick hail, and tainted water, and snow
Pour through the darkened air;
The earth stinks as it receives them.
Cerberus, a cruel and monstrous beast,
Barks like a dog with his three throats
Over the people submerged there.
His eyes are red, his beard greasy and black,
His belly broad, his hands clawed;
He scratches the spirits with his claws, swallows, and tears them apart.
The rain makes them howl like dogs:
They shield one side with the other;
They often roll, wretched and profane.
When Cerberus, the great worm, saw us,
He opened his mouths and showed us his fangs:
Not a limb of his stayed still.
And my guide opened his hands wide;
He took earth, and with both fists full
Threw it into their ravenous gullets.
Like a dog that barks and begs,
Then quiets when it bites its food,
And is intent on devouring it and strives for that alone,
So those foul faces became
Of the demon Cerberus, who thunders
At the souls until they would wish to be deaf.
We passed among the shades oppressed
By the heavy rain, and placed our feet
Upon their emptiness, which seems a body.
They all lay on the ground,
Except one who quickly rose to sit
When he saw us passing before him.
O you, drawn through this Hell,
He said to me, recognize me, if you can:
You were born before I was undone.
And I said to him: The anguish you suffer
May perhaps draw you out of all memory,
So that I cannot recall ever seeing you.
But tell me who you are, who in so painful
A place are set, and under such punishment,
That if another is greater, none is more loathsome.
And he said to me: Your city, which is full
Of envy to such a degree that its sack is already bursting,
Kept me with it in my tranquil life.
You citizens called me Ciacco:
For the ruinous sin of gluttony,
As you see, I languish in the rain.
And I, a sad soul, am not alone,
For all these suffer a like punishment
For a like sin. And he said no more.
I answered him: Ciacco, your suffering
Weighs on me so that it makes me weep;
But tell me, if you know, what will become
Of the citizens of the divided city?
Is anyone there just? And tell me the cause
Why such discord has come upon it.
And he said to me: After long contention
They will come to blood, and the wild faction
Will drive the other out with great injury.
Soon afterward this faction must fall
Within three suns, and the other rise
With the strength of one who now comes to shore.
It will hold its head high for a long time,
Keeping the other beneath heavy burdens,
Though I grieve and resent this.
There are two just men, and they are not heeded there:
Pride, envy, and avarice are the three sparks
That set hearts ablaze.
Here he brought his lamenting speech to an end.
And I said to him: I still want you to teach me,
And to grant me the gift of speaking further.
Farinata and Tegghiaio, so worthy,
Jacopo Rusticucci, Arrigo, and Mosca,
And the others who devoted their minds to doing good,
Tell me where they are, and let me know them;
For a great desire presses me to learn
Whether Heaven sweetens them or Hell embitters them.
And he said to me: They are among the darkest souls;
Different sins weigh them down below, to the bottom;
If you descend that far, you will be able to see them.
But when you have returned to the sweet world,
I beg you to bring me to others' minds:
I will say no more to you, nor answer you further.
Then he turned his straight eyes askew:
He looked at me briefly, then lowered his head;
And he fell down, level with the other blind ones.
And my guide said to me: He will rise no more
On this side of the sound of the angel's trumpet,
When he sees the hostile power,
Each will see again his grievous tomb,
Will take back his flesh and shape,
And hear what resounds for all eternity.
Thus we passed through the foul mixture
Of shades and rain, with slow steps,
Touching a little on the life to come.
Then I said: Master, will these torments
Themselves grow greater after the great judgment,
Or be less, or burn with equal force?
And he said to me: Return to your knowledge,
Which teaches that the more perfect a thing is,
The more it feels the good, and likewise the pain.
Although this accursed people
Will never attain true perfection,
It expects to exist longer hereafter than here.
We circled around that road,
Speaking much more than I report;
We came to the point where the path descends;
There we found Pluto, the great enemy.
