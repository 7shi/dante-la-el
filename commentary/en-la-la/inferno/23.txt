Silent, alone, without companionship,
We went, one ahead and the other behind,
As Minor Friars go along the road.
My thought was turned toward Æsop's fable
By the present quarrel,
Where he spoke of the frog and the mouse:
For mo and issa are no more alike,
Though one with the other makes, if joined well,
A beginning and an end held firmly in mind.
And as one thought bursts forth from another,
So another thought then arose from that one,
Which doubled my first fear.
I was already thinking: If these demons, because of us,
Have been mocked, with loss and disgrace
So great that I believe it vexes them:
If anger is joined to their evil will,
They will come after us more cruelly
Than a dog after the hare it has seized.
I already felt all my hairs bristle
With fear, and was alert behind,
When I said: Master, if you do not hide
Yourself and me at once, I fear
The Malebranche; they are already behind us.
I imagine them so clearly that I can feel them.
And he: If your image were made of leaded glass,
I would not draw your outward image
More quickly to me than I grasp the one within.
Just now your thoughts were coming into mine
With the same act and the same expression,
So I formed one plan from both of us.
If the right-hand slope lies as they said,
So that we can descend into the other bolgia,
We shall escape the chase we have imagined.
He had not finished setting out this plan
When I saw them coming with wings outstretched,
Not far away, intent on seizing us.
Then my guide swiftly seized me,
Like a mother roused by a noise
Who sees flames burning close by,
Who takes her child and flees without delay,
Caring for him more than for herself,
So that she puts on only a single shirt:
Down from the brow of the hard bank
He threw himself supine toward the hanging rock,
Which seals one side of the next bolgia.
Never did water run so swiftly through a channel
To turn the wheel of a land mill,
When it draws closest to the paddles,
As my master sped along that ledge,
Carrying me on his breast,
As his son, and not as his companion.
His feet had scarcely reached the bed
Of the lower bottom when they were on the ridge
Above us; but there was no danger there,
For the high Providence that chose
To set them as ministers of the fifth pit
Takes from all of them the power to leave it.
Down there we found a painted people,
Walking round at a very slow pace,
Weeping, and in appearance weary and defeated.
They wore cloaks with low hoods
Drawn before their eyes, made like those
That monks in Cologne wear:
Outside they are gilded, so that they dazzle the eye;
But all within is lead, and so heavy
That Frederick made them of straw.
O wearisome mantle for all eternity!
We still turned left
Along with them, intent on their sad weeping;
But because of the weight those weary people
Came so slowly that at every movement of the hip
We found ourselves with new companions.
So I said to my guide: See if you can find
Someone whose deeds and name are known,
And keep your eyes moving as you walk around.
One who understood the Tuscan tongue
Shouted after us: Hold your feet,
You who hurry so through the dark air;
Perhaps you will get from me what you ask.
Then my guide turned and said: Wait,
Then proceed at his pace.
I stopped, and saw two of them show great eagerness,
In the look on their faces, to join me;
But their burden and the narrow path delayed them.
When they reached us, they looked at me askance
For a long while, without saying a word;
Then they turned to each other and said among themselves:
This man seems alive, judging by his breathing;
And if they are dead, by what privilege
Do they go uncovered by the heavy robe?
Then they said to me: O Tuscan, who have come
To the company of sorrowful hypocrites,
Tell us who you are, and do not disdain us.
And I replied: I was born and raised
Above the fair river Arno, in the great city,
And I am here with the body I have always had.
But who are you, from whose cheeks, as I see,
So much sorrow streams down?
What punishment is in you that so torments you?
One of them answered: Alas, our rusty-coloured cloaks
Are so thickly made of lead that their weights
Make our scales creak like this.
We were Friars of the Joyous Order, and Bolognese;
I am Catalano, and this one is Loderingo,
So named, and chosen together from your city,
As one solitary man is often chosen
To preserve the city's peace; and we were such
That traces of us still appear around Gardingo.
I began: O Friars, your evils...
But I said no more, for before my eyes
Appeared one crucified on the ground with three stakes.
When he saw me, he writhed all over,
Breathing into his beard with sighs;
And Friar Catalano, who noticed this,
Said to me: That man fixed there, whom you see,
Advised the Pharisees that it was fitting
To put one man to torment for the people.
He lies across the path, naked,
As you see, and everyone who passes
Must feel how heavy he is first:
His father-in-law suffers in the same way
In this pit, as do the others of the Council
Who were evil seed for the Jews.
Then I saw Virgil gaze in wonder
At the man stretched on the cross,
So shamefully, in eternal exile.
Then he addressed the Friar in these words:
Please do not take offence, if it is permitted,
To tell us whether on the right-hand side there lies an opening,
By which we two might leave this place
Without compelling the black angels
To come from this bottom and lead us out.
He answered, then: Closer than you expect
There is a rock that begins at the great circle,
Moves across all the savage valleys,
Except that this one is broken and does not span it.
You can climb upward by the ruin
That lies along the slope and rises from the bottom.
My guide stood with his head bowed for a little while,
Then said: He who hooks sinners over there
Told us the situation badly.
And the Friar said: I have long heard said in Bologna,
Among the many faults of the devil,
That he is a liar and the father of lies.
After that my guide strode away,
His face somewhat troubled by anger;
So I left the burdened ones
And followed the footprints of his dear feet.
