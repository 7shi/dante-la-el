Benign will, in which is made to flow
Always the love that breathes aright,
As greed does in the wicked one,
Set silence to that sweet lyre,
And stilled the holy strings,
Which heaven's right hand loosens and draws taut.
How could those beings be deaf
To just prayers, who, to give me the wish
To pray to them, agreed to be silent?
It is right that he suffer without end
Who, for love of something that does not last
Forever, strips himself of that love.
As through tranquil, clear skies
A sudden fire now and then darts,
Moving eyes that had been at rest,
And seems a star changing place,
Except that where it is kindled
Nothing is lost, and it lasts but briefly;
So from the arm that stretches to the right
A star ran to the foot of that Cross,
From the constellation shining there;
The jewel did not leave its band,
But sped along the radiant strip,
Like fire behind alabaster.
So tenderly Anchises' shade reached out,
If our greater Muse deserves belief,
When in Elysium he saw his son.
O my blood! O grace of God poured
Over you, to whom heaven's gate
Was never closed twice!
So spoke that light; and I fixed my gaze on him,
Then turned my face toward my lady,
And was amazed on either side;
For in her eyes a smile was burning,
So that I thought my own eyes would reach
The depths of my grace and my paradise.
Then, joyful to see and hear,
The spirit added to its first words things
I did not understand: so deeply did he speak.
He did not hide them from me by choice,
But from necessity, since his thought
Rose above the measure of mortals.
When the bow of ardent feeling
Had spent itself so that speech descended
Toward the measure of our understanding,
The first thing I understood was: Blessed
Are you, Triune and One, who are so gracious
To my offspring.
And he continued: You have ended
A grateful, long fast, sustained by reading
The great book where black and white never change,
My son, within this light in which I speak
To you, thanks to her who gave your wings
The feathers for their lofty flight.
You believe that your thought comes to me
From the First, as from the One, when known,
Come forth the five and the six.
And so you do not ask who I am,
Nor why I seem more joyful to you
Than any other in this happy throng.
You believe rightly: the least and greatest
Of this life gaze into the mirror
In which you reveal your thought before you think.
But so that the holy love in which I keep vigil
With perpetual vision, and which makes me thirst
With sweet longing, may be more fulfilled,
Let your confident, bold, joyful voice
Sound out your will, sound out your desire,
To which my answer is already decreed.
I turned to Beatrice, and she, before I spoke,
Turned to God and smiled at me with a sign
That gave my will stronger wings.
Then I began: Feeling and understanding,
As the first equality appeared to me,
Were of equal weight for each of you;
For the Sun that lights and fires you
With warmth and light is so equal
That every likeness falls short.
But in mortals, will and ability,
For the reason clear to you,
Fly on wings of different strength.
Since I am mortal, I feel this
Inequality, and so I thank you
Only with my heart for your fatherly welcome.
I earnestly beg you, living topaz
Who adorn this precious jewel,
To satisfy me with your name.
O my branch, in whom I took delight
Simply by waiting, I was your root.
Such was the beginning of his reply.
Then he said: He from whom your family
Takes its name, and who for a hundred years
And more circled the mountain at the first ledge,
Was my son and your great-grandfather.
It is right that you shorten for him
The long labor, through your works.
Florence, within the ancient circuit
From which she still takes the third and ninth hours,
Lived in peace, sober and chaste.
No necklace, no crown,
No ladies dressed like countesses, no belt
That looked more splendid than the person.
A daughter born did not yet bring fear
To her father, for time and dowry
Kept measure on both sides.
No houses stood empty of families;
Sardanapalus had not yet come there
To show what can be done in a bedroom.
Montemalo had not yet been surpassed
By your Uccellatoio; as it is surpassed
In rising, so it will be in its decline.
I saw Bellincion Berti go belted
With leather and bone, and his wife come
From the mirror with her face unpainted;
And I saw the Nerli and the Vecchio
Content with uncovered leather,
And their women with spindle and distaff.
O fortunate women! Each was sure
Of her burial place, and none yet
Was abandoned in bed in France.
One kept watch by the cradle,
And, soothing her child, used the language
That first delights fathers and mothers;
The other drawing her hair to the distaff
Told stories with her family
Of the Trojans, Fiesole, and Rome.
A Cianghella or a Lapo Saltarello
Would then have seemed as strange
As Cincinnatus or Cornelia would today.
In so peaceful and lovely a life
Of citizens, in such faithful civic fellowship,
In so sweet a home,
Mary gave me, invoked with loud cries;
And in your ancient Baptistery
I became both Christian and Cacciaguida.
Moronto and Eliseo were my brothers;
My wife came to me from the valley of the Po,
And from her your surname arose.
Then I followed Emperor Conrad,
And he girded me with knighthood;
My good service made me dear to him.
I followed him against the evil
Of that people whose law usurps,
Through the shepherds' fault, what is ours by right.
There, by that foul people, I was freed
From the deceitful world, whose love
Corrupts many souls, and through martyrdom
I came to this peace.
