The world was wont to believe, to its peril,
That lovely Cyprian and foolish love
Shone, turning in the third epicycle;
For not only did they honor her
With sacrifice and votive cry,
The ancient peoples in their ancient error;
They also honored Dione and Cupid,
The former as his mother and the latter as her son,
And said he sat in Dido's lap;
And from her, with whom I begin,
They took the name of the star
That the sun courts now from behind, now from the brow.
I did not perceive my ascent into it;
But that I was within it was amply confirmed for me
By my lady, whom I saw grow fairer.
And as a spark is seen in a flame,
Or as one voice is distinguished from another,
When one is steady and the other comes and goes;
In that light I saw other lights
Moving in circles, some faster than others,
According, I believe, to the measure of their eternal visions.
Winds did not descend from a cold cloud,
Visible or unseen, so swiftly
That they would not seem hindered and slow
To anyone by whom those divine lights
Had been seen to come toward us, leaving the circuit
They had first begun among the high Seraphim.
And behind those that appeared foremost to us,
Hosanna sounded so that never afterward
Have I lacked the wish to hear it again.
Then one of them drew nearer to us,
And alone began: We are all ready
To please you, so that you may rejoice in us.
We revolve with the heavenly princes
In one way, with one circling and one thirst,
To whom you once said on earth:
You who, by understanding, move the third heaven;
And we are so full of love that, to please you,
A little rest will be no less sweet.
After my eyes had turned
Toward my lady in reverence, and she
Had made them content and sure of her,
They turned again toward the light, which had promised
So much of itself, and what said, Who are you? was
My voice, stamped with great affection.
And how much and in what a way I saw her grow
Through the new joy that grew
When I spoke, to her beauty!
As such, she said to me, the world held me
Below for a short time; and had I stayed longer,
There will be much evil that would not have come to pass.
My joy keeps me hidden from you,
Shining around me and concealing me,
Like an animal wrapped in its own silk.
You loved me greatly, and had good reason;
For, had I remained below, I would have shown you
More of my love than its leaves.
That left bank, washed
By the Rhône after it mingles with the Sorgue,
Was waiting in time to have me as its lord;
And that horn of Ausonia, bordered
By Bari, Gaeta, and Catona,
Where the Tronto and Verde flow into the sea,
Already on my brow shone the crown
Of that land watered by the Danube
After it leaves the German banks.
And beautiful Trinacria, which sends up haze
Between Pachynus and Pelorus, above the gulf
That suffers greater disturbance from Eurus,
Not because of Typhoeus, but of rising sulfur,
Would still have awaited its kings,
Born through me of Charles and Rudolf;
If evil rule, which always disheartens
Subject peoples, had not moved
Palermo to cry: Death, death!
And if my brother could see ahead,
Catalonia's avaricious poverty
He would already flee, so that it might not harm him;
For precautions must truly be taken,
By him or by another, so that on his ship,
Laden, no greater burden be placed.
His nature, which, miserly, from a generous one
Descends, would need a chivalry such
As cared nothing for filling a chest.
Whereupon I said: I believe that the great joy
Your speech pours into me, my lord,
There where all good begins and ends,
Is seen by you as I see it;
And this is dearer to me, and I prize this too,
That you discern it by looking upon God.
You have made me glad, and so make me clear,
Since by speaking you stirred me to doubt
How bitter can come from sweet seed.
This I said to him. And he to me: If I can
Show you a truth, toward what you ask
You will hold your gaze as you hold your back.
The Good that the whole realm you climb
Turns and gladdens has caused a power to exist
Of its providence in these great bodies;
And not only foreseen natures
Are in the mind that is perfect in itself,
But they are together with their well-being.
And therefore whatever this bow shoots
Falls disposed toward a foreseen end,
Like an arrow aimed at its mark.
If this were not so, the heaven you walk through
Would produce its effects in such a way
That they would be not works of art, but ruins;
And this cannot be, if the intellects
That move these stars are not defective,
And the First, who did not make them perfect, is defective too.
Would you like this truth made clearer to you?
And I: No longer, for I see it is impossible
That nature fail in what is necessary.
Then he continued: Now tell me, would it be worse
For a human on earth not to be a citizen?
Yes, I replied, and here I need no argument.
And can that be if below people do not live
In different ways through different duties?
No, if your teacher writes truly to you.
He reasoned thus far,
Then concluded: Therefore diverse are
Of necessity the roots of your inclinations;
So one is born Solon, and another Xerxes,
Another Melchizedek, and another that one
Who lost his son while flying through the air.
Circular nature, which is a seal
Upon mortal wax, practices its art well,
But does not distinguish one household from another.
Hence it happens that Esau differs
By seed from Jacob, and Quirinus comes
From a father so lowly that he gives himself to Mars.
Generated nature its course
Would always make like that of its begetters,
Were divine Providence not stronger.
Now what was behind you is before you;
But so you may know how glad I am for you,
I wish you to wear a corollary.
Nature always, if it finds fortune
At odds with it, like any other seed
Outside its proper region, fares badly.
And if the world below paid heed
To the foundation nature lays,
And followed it, its people would be good.
But you turn toward religion
One born to gird on a sword,
And make king one suited to preaching;
Thus your path is off the road.
