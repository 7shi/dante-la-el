As he came to Clymene, seeking assurance
About what he had heard said against him,
He who still makes fathers sparing toward their sons,
So was I, and so I was perceived
By Beatrice, and by the holy flame
That earlier had changed its place for my sake.
So my lady said: Send out the flame
Of your desire, she told me, so that it may emerge
Clearly marked by the eternal seal;
Not so that our knowledge may grow
Through your speech, but so that you may grow used
To voicing your thirst, and someone may give you drink.
O dear root of mine, who rise so high
That, as earthly minds can see
That two obtuse angles cannot fit in a triangle,
So you see contingent things
Before they exist, by gazing on the point
To which all times are present.
[While I was with Virgil
Up the mountain that heals the souls,
And descending into the world of the dead,
Grave words were spoken to me about my future life,
Though I feel myself a square
Against the blows of fortune.
So my will would be content
To learn what fortune draws near to me,
For a foreseen arrow flies more slowly.]
Thus I spoke to that same light
That had spoken to me before; and, as Beatrice wished,
I made my desire known.
Not by riddles, in which foolish people
Were already ensnared before the Lamb of God
Who takes away sins was slain,
But in clear words and precise Latin,
That fatherly love answered,
Enclosed in, yet visible through, its bright smile.
All contingency, which does not extend
Beyond the book of your matter,
Is painted in the eternal sight.
Yet it does not take necessity from there,
Any more than a ship descending a stream
Takes it from the gaze in which it is reflected.
From that point, as sweet harmony
Of an organ reaches the ear, the time prepared for you
Comes into my sight.
As Hippolytus left Athens
Because of his cruel and treacherous stepmother,
So you must leave Florence.
This is willed, this is already being sought,
And soon it will be done by those who plan it,
Where Christ is traded every day.
The injured party will be blamed
By public outcry, as is usual; but vengeance
Will bear witness to the truth that grants it.
You will leave everything you love most dearly;
This is the first arrow shot
By exile's bow.
You will learn how salty another's bread tastes,
And how hard a road it is
To go up and down another's stairs.
What will weigh most heavily on your shoulders
Will be the wicked, foolish company
With whom you will fall into this valley;
They will all turn against you, ungrateful,
Foolish, and wicked; but soon
They, not you, will blush with shame.
Their conduct will prove their brutishness,
So that you will be glad
To have made a party of yourself alone.
Your first refuge and first lodging
Will be the courtesy of the great Lombard,
Who bears the holy bird upon his ladder;
He will look on you so kindly
That between you, in giving and asking,
The slower part will come first.
With him you will see one who, at birth,
Was so strongly stamped by this star
That his deeds will be remarkable.
People have not yet noticed him,
Because of his youth: only nine years
Have these wheels revolved around him.
But before the Gascon deceives the lofty Henry,
Sparks of his virtue will appear
In his disregard for silver and hardship.
His magnificence will become so well known
That even his enemies
Will not be able to keep silent about it.
Look to him and to his benefits;
Through him many people will change their condition,
The rich and the poor changing places.
You will carry what you learn of him written in your mind
But will not speak of it. He also told things
Incredible to those who are here now.
Then he added: My son, these are the explanations
Of what was told you; here are the traps
Hidden within a few turns of the years.
Yet do not envy your neighbors,
For your life will continue in the future
Longer than the punishment of their treachery.
After, in silence, the holy soul showed itself ready
To put the weft into the cloth
Whose warp I had given it.
I began, like one eager,
Uncertain, to seek counsel from someone
Who sees, wills rightly, and loves.
My father, I clearly see how time spurs
Toward me to strike me, a blow
Heavier for one who abandons himself.
Therefore it is wise that I arm myself with foresight,
So that if my dearest place is taken from me,
I do not lose the rest because of my poems.
Down through the world of endless bitterness,
And over the mountain whose fair summit
My lady's eyes lifted me from,
And then through heaven, from light to light,
I learned what, if I repeat it,
Will taste sharply bitter to many;
And if I am a timid friend to truth,
I fear losing life among those
Who will call this time the past.
The light in which my treasure smiled,
The one I found there, grew brighter,
Like a golden mirror in a sunbeam.
Then it replied: A dark conscience,
Whether ashamed of its own or another's shame,
Will surely feel your harsh words.
Nevertheless, remove all falsehood
And make your whole vision known;
Let whoever has the itch scratch it.
For if your voice is unpleasant
At first taste, it will leave vital nourishment
Once it has been digested.
Your cry will be like the wind
That strikes the highest towers hardest;
This will be no small proof of honor.
Therefore, in these spheres, on the mountain,
And in the sorrowful valley, you were shown
Only souls known for their fame;
For the mind of one who hears does not rest
Nor firmly trust an example whose roots
Are unknown and hidden,
Or any other argument that is not clear.
