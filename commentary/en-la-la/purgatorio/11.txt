Pater noster, who art in heaven,
Not contained there, but by greater love
Which thou hast for the first effects above,
Hallowed be thy name and thy power
By every creature, as it is worthy
To give thanks to thy highest vapor.
May the peace of thy kingdom come to us,
For we cannot go to it by ourselves,
Unless it comes, with all our ingenuity.
As thy angels make a sacrifice to thee
Of their will, singing Hosanna,
So may human beings make one of theirs.
Give us today our daily manna,
Without which through this harsh wilderness
He who strives hardest to go falls back.
And as we forgive each person
The evil we have suffered, so forgive us,
In thy mercy, and look not to our merit.
Our strength, which is so easily overcome,
Do not test with the ancient adversary,
But free us from him, who spurs it so.
This last prayer, dear Lord,
Is no longer made for us, for we need it not,
But for those who remained behind us.
Thus, praying for themselves and for us,
Those shades went beneath the weight,
Like one who sometimes dreams,
Distressed in different ways all around,
And weary along the first cornice,
Purging the gloom of the world.
If over there good is always said for us,
What can those on this side say or do for them,
Whose will has a good root?
We should indeed help them wash away the marks
They brought from here, so that cleansed and light
They may leave the starry spheres.
Ah, may justice and mercy soon unburden you,
So that you may move your wing
Which lifts you according to your desire.
Show us which hand leads most directly
To the stairway; and if there is more than one passage,
Teach us the one that descends least steeply;
For this one who comes with me, because of the burden
Of Adam's flesh with which he is clothed,
Is slow to climb upward against his will.
Their words, which they returned to these
That the one I followed had spoken,
Did not make clear who had uttered them;
But this was said: To the right, along the bank,
Come with us, and you will find a passage
That a living person can climb.
And if I were not hindered by the stone,
Which tames my proud neck,
So that I must keep my face lowered,
I would look at this one who still lives and gives no name,
To see whether I know him,
And to make him pity this burden.
I was a Latin, born of a great Tuscan;
Guglielmo Aldobrandesco was my father;
I do not know whether you ever knew his name.
The ancient blood and noble deeds
Of my ancestors made me so arrogant
That, giving no thought to our common mother,
I held every person in such contempt
That I died for it, as the Sienese know,
And every child in Campagnatico knows it.
I am Umberto; and pride harms not only me,
For it has dragged all my kin
Along with it into misfortune.
And here I must carry this weight
Because of it, until I make satisfaction to God,
Since I did not do so among the living, here among the dead.
As I listened, I lowered my face;
And one of them, not the one who spoke,
Turned beneath the weight that hindered them,
And saw me, recognized me, and called out,
Holding his eyes fixed with effort
On me, who went bent entirely like them.
Oh, I said to him, are you not Oderisi,
The honor of Gubbio and the honor of that art
Which in Paris is called illuminating?
Brother, he said, the pages smile more
Under Franco Bolognese's brush;
The honor is wholly his now, and partly mine.
I would not have been so gracious
While I lived, because of my great desire
For the excellence on which my heart was set.
Here one pays the penalty for such pride;
And I would not even be here yet, were it not
That, while able to sin, I turned to God.
O vainglory of human powers,
How brief is the green at the summit,
Unless rough ages come to sustain it!
Cimabue thought that in painting
He held the field; now Giotto has the fame,
So that the other's name is dimmed.
Thus one Guido has taken from the other
The glory of language; and perhaps one is born
Who will drive both from the nest.
The world's rumor is nothing but a breath
Of wind, now coming from here, now from there,
And changing its name because it changes direction.
What greater fame will you have if, old, you separate
Your flesh from yourself than if you had died
Before leaving baby talk and pennies,
Before a thousand years pass? They are a shorter span
Compared with eternity than the blink of an eye
Compared with the slowest turning sphere in heaven.
He who walks so slowly along the road
Before me was renowned throughout Tuscany,
And now in Siena they scarcely whisper of him.
Of what city was he lord when the Florentine
Rage was destroyed, proud
At that time, as it is now a harlot?
Your fame is the color of grass,
Which comes and goes, and the sun discolors it,
The sun that brings it forth from the earth.
And I said to him: Your true words plant
Humility in my heart and calm my great swelling;
But who is the one you spoke of just now?
That is Provenzan Salvani, he replied.
He is here because he was presumptuous,
Trying to bring all Siena into his hands.
He went and still goes without rest
Since he died; such is the coin he pays
To make amends for excessive boldness beyond.
And I said: If that spirit waits
Until the end of life before he repents,
He remains down there and does not climb up here,
If a good prayer does not help him
Before a time equal to his life has passed,
How was his coming here granted?
When he lived in greatest glory, he said,
He freely set himself down in Siena's square,
Casting off all shame, and fixed his purpose there:
To draw his friend out of the suffering
He endured in King Charles's prison,
He made every vein in his body tremble.
I will say no more, and I know I speak obscurely;
But soon your neighbors will act so
That you will be able to interpret it. This deed
Won him these bounds.
