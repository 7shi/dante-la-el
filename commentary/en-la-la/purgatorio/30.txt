When the northern constellation of the first heaven,
Which never knew setting or rising,
Nor any veil of cloud but that of sin,
And which made everyone there attentive
To his duty, as the lower one does,
Like a rudder guiding a ship to harbor,
Stood firm, the faithful people,
Who had first come between the griffin and it,
Turned toward the chariot as toward their peace.
And one of them, as if sent from heaven,
Sang, Come, bride, from Lebanon,
And cried it three times, with all the others close by.
As the blessed at the final summons
Will swiftly rise, each from his grave,
Rejoicing in the voice restored to him,
So, on the divine chariot,
A hundred rose at the voice of that elder,
Ministers and messengers of eternal life.
All were saying, Blessed are you who come;
And, scattering flowers above and around,
Bring lilies in full handfuls.
I had already seen at the beginning of day
The eastern part all rosy,
And the rest of heaven adorned with fair calm,
And the sun's face rising, veiled,
So that, through the tempered vapors,
The eye could bear it for a long while:
So, within a cloud of flowers
That rose from angelic hands
And fell again inside and outside,
Above a white veil crowned with olive,
A lady appeared to me, beneath a green mantle,
Clothed in the color of living flame.
My spirit, though for so long
It had been with her presence,
Was not broken, trembling with wonder,
Without seeing more clearly with my eyes,
Through a hidden power that came from her,
I felt the great force of my old love.
As soon as the high power struck my sight,
The power that had pierced me already
Before I had left childhood,
I turned to the left with the look
With which a little child runs to his mother
When frightened or distressed,
To say to Virgil, Less than a drop
Of blood remains in me that does not tremble;
I know the signs of the old flame.
But Virgil had left me bereft.
I said, Virgil, sweetest father,
Virgil, to whom I gave myself for my salvation—
Yet all that the first mother lost
Could not keep my cheeks, bright with dew,
From turning dark as I wept.
Dante, though Virgil has gone,
Do not weep yet, do not weep again;
For another blade will make you weep.
Like an admiral who, at stern and bow,
Comes to inspect the crews at their work
On other ships and spur them to do well,
So, on the chariot's left side,
When I turned at the sound of my name,
Which must of necessity be recorded here,
I saw the lady who had first appeared to me,
Veiled beneath the angelic shower,
Turn her eyes toward me from across the stream.
Although the veil falling from her head,
Circled with Minerva's branch,
Did not let her appear plainly,
In queenly dignity, still fierce in bearing,
She began again, like someone who speaks
While keeping her harshest words in reserve:
Look well: I am, I am Beatrice.
How did you dare approach the mountain?
Did you not know that here man is happy?
My eyes fell down into the clear spring;
But seeing myself in it, I looked toward the grass;
So much shame weighed on my brow.
A mother seems stern to her child,
As she seemed to me, for the taste of harsh pity
Was bitter in my mouth.
She fell silent, and the angels at once sang,
In you, Lord, I have hoped;
But they did not go beyond My feet.
As snow among the living branches,
Along Italy's backbone, freezes,
Blown and packed tight by Slavic winds,
Then melts and seeps through itself,
When the land that loses its shadow breathes,
So that fire seems to melt a candle,
So I was without tears and sighs
Before the song of those who always mark
The notes of the eternal spheres.
But when I heard, in their sweet harmonies,
Their measured song, it seemed as if they said:
Lady, why do you distress him so?
The ice that had tightened around my heart
Became breath and water, and in anguish
Burst from my chest through mouth and eyes.
She, still standing firm by the chariot's right wheel,
Turned her words then
Toward the holy beings:
You keep watch in eternal day,
So neither night nor sleep steals from you
A step the world takes on its way;
Therefore my answer is given with greater care,
So that he who weeps beyond the stream understands me,
And guilt and grief may be of equal measure.
Not only through the work of the great wheels,
Which direct each seed toward some end,
According to the stars' conjunctions;
But through the abundance of divine grace,
Which sends such high vapors their rain
That our sight cannot approach them,
This man in his new life
Was so endowed in potential that every good habit
Would have shown wonderful results in him.
But a field grows more wicked and wild
With bad seed left unweeded,
The more fertile strength the soil has.
For a time I sustained him with my presence,
Showing him my youthful eyes;
With me I led him, turned toward the right path.
As soon as I reached the threshold
Of my second age and changed my life,
He withdrew from me and gave himself to another.
When I had risen from flesh to spirit,
And my beauty and virtue had grown,
I became less dear and pleasing to him;
He turned his steps toward a false road,
Following false images of good
That never fulfill their promises.
Nor did the inspirations I obtained help,
By which in dreams and otherwise
I called him back; he cared so little.
He fell so low that every means
For his salvation had become too weak,
Except showing him the lost souls.
For him I visited the gate of the dead,
And my prayers, weeping, were offered
To the one who brought him up here.
God's high decree would be broken
If he crossed Lethe and tasted this food
Without any payment,
Without repentance that pours out tears.
