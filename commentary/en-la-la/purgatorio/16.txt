Darkness of Hell, and of a night bereft
Of every planet beneath a poor sky,
As dark as clouds can possibly make it,
Made not so thick a veil before my sight
As that smoke which there covered us,
Nor so harsh a texture to the touch;
For the eye could not endure to stay open:
So my wise and trusty guide
Drew near to me and offered me his shoulder.
As a blind man follows behind his guide,
So as not to lose his way or strike his head
Against something that harms him or perhaps kills him,
So I went through the bitter, foul air,
Listening to my guide, who was saying
Always: Take care not to become parted from me.
I heard voices, and each seemed
To pray for peace and mercy
To the Angel of God, who takes away sins.
Lamb of God alone was their opening:
One word was on every tongue, in one manner,
So that complete harmony seemed to reign among them.
Are those spirits, Master, whom I hear?
I said. And he replied: You understand rightly;
And they go untying the knot of anger.
Now who are you, who cleave our smoke
And speak of us as if you
Still counted time by the calends?
Thus one voice spoke.
Then the Master said to me: Answer,
And ask whether one goes upward from here.
And I: O creature cleansing yourself
To return beautiful to the one who made you,
You shall hear a marvel if you follow me.
I will follow you as far as I may,
He replied; and if smoke bars our sight,
Our hearing will keep us joined.
Then I began: With that band
Which death dissolves, I journey upward,
And I came here through Hell's anguish.
And if God has enclosed me in his grace
So far that he wills me to see his court
In a way wholly outside modern custom,
Hide not from me who you were before your death,
But tell me that, and tell me if I go rightly to the pass;
And your words shall be our guides.
I was a Lombard, and was called Mark;
I knew the world, and loved that virtue
At which everyone now has unstrung his bow;
You are going straight on the way upward.
So he answered, and added: I beg you
To pray for me when you are above.
And I to him: By faith I bind myself to you
To do what you ask of me; but I am bursting
With a doubt, unless I free myself from it.
At first it was simple, but now has doubled
In your account, which makes me certain
Here and elsewhere of that with which I link it.
The world is surely so utterly bereft
Of every virtue, as you tell me,
And pregnant with evil, covered by it;
But I beg you to tell me the cause,
So that I may see it and show it to others;
For one places it in heaven and another here below.
A deep sigh, pressed from him by grief,
He gave forth first, then began: Brother,
The world is blind, and you surely come from it.
You who are living bring every cause
Only up to heaven, just as if everything
It moved with it by necessity.
If it were so, there would be destroyed in you
Free will, and there would be no justice
In having joy for good and grief for evil.
The heavens begin your movements;
I do not mean all of them; but even if I did,
You have been given light for good and evil,
And a free will that struggles
In its first battles with heaven, endures,
Then wins all, if it nourishes itself well.
To a greater power and a better nature
You are subject, free; and that creates
The mind in you, which heaven does not control.
Therefore, if the present world goes astray,
The cause is in you; let it be sought in you,
And I shall now be your guide in this.
There comes forth from the hand of him who regards it,
Before it exists, like a little girl
Who plays, laughing and crying,
The simple soul, which knows nothing,
Except that, moved by its lofty Maker,
It gladly returns to whatever gives it pleasure.
It first tastes the sweetness of a little good;
There it is deceived and runs after it,
Unless a guide or rein turns its love.
Therefore law had to be set as a rein;
There had to be a king who could discern
At least the tower of the true city.
Laws exist, but who lays hands on them?
No one; for the shepherd who leads
Can chew the cud, but lacks the cloven hoof.
For people, seeing their guide
Aim only at the good they themselves crave,
Feed on that and seek nothing beyond it.
You can well see that bad conduct
Is the cause that has made the world guilty,
And not a nature in you that has become corrupt.
Rome, which made the world good, used
To have two suns, which both roads
Made visible, that of the world and that of God.
One has quenched the other; the sword is joined
To the shepherd's staff, and the one with the other together
Must by sheer force go badly;
For joined together, neither fears the other.
If you do not believe me, look at the grain:
For every plant is known by its seed.
Over the land watered by the Adige and Po
Worth and courtesy were once to be found,
Before Frederick went to war;
Now there may pass through it in safety
Whoever, through shame, would have avoided
Speaking with the good or drawing near them.
Indeed three old men are still there, in whom flourish
The old age and the new; it seems to them long
Until God restores them to a better life:
Corrado da Palazzo, and good Gerard,
And Guido da Castel, better named
In French as the guileless Lombard.
Say from now on that Rome's Church,
By confounding two forms of rule within herself,
Falls in the mud, and disfigures herself and her burden.
O my Mark, I said, you reason well;
And now I understand why from inheritance
The sons of Levi were excluded.
But who is that Gerard? Is he the one you call wise,
Who remains from the vanished people,
A reproach to this wild age?
Either your speech deceives or tests me,
He answered me, for when you speak Tuscan to me,
You seem to know nothing of good Gerard.
By no other surname do I know him,
Unless I take it from his daughter Gaia.
God be with you, for I go no farther with you.
See the light that shines through the river
Already whitening; and I must leave,
The angel is there before he appears.
So he spoke and would hear no more.
