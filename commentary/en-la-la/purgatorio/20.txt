Against the better will, the worse will fights;
So, against my wish, to please him,
I drew from the water an unsated sponge.
I moved, and my guide moved too, along
The open places close beside the rock,
As one walks narrowly by a wall's battlements;
For the people who weep drop by drop
Through their eyes the evil that conquers all the world
Press too far outward on the other side.
Cursed be you, ancient she-wolf,
Who have more prey than all other beasts,
For your deep, endless hunger!
O heaven, whose turning seems to change
The conditions here below,
When will he come through whom this will depart?
We walked with slow and measured steps,
While I listened to the shades I heard
Piously weeping and lamenting;
And by chance I heard Mary sweetly
Called before us in such weeping,
As a woman cries while giving birth;
And then: You were so poor,
As may be seen by that lodging
Where you laid your holy burden.
Next I heard: O good Fabricius,
In poverty you chose virtue rather
Than to possess great riches with vice.
These words had pleased me so
That I drew nearer, to learn for certain
From what spirit they seemed to come.
He still spoke of the generosity
Nicholas showed to the maidens,
To lead their youth to honor.
O soul, who speak so well,
Tell me who you were, I said, and why alone
You renew these worthy praises.
Your words will not go unrewarded,
If I return to complete the brief journey
Of this life that flies toward its end.
And he replied: I shall tell you, not for comfort
That I expect from there, but because such
Grace shines in you before you die.
I was the root of the evil plant
That shades all Christian lands,
So that good fruit is rarely plucked from it.
But if Douay, Lille, Ghent, and Bruges
Could act, vengeance would soon be done;
And I ask it of Him who judges all.
In the world I was called Hugh Capet;
From me came the Philips and the Lewises,
By whom France has lately been ruled.
I was the son of a Paris butcher.
When all the ancient kings had died out,
Save one withdrawn in a grey habit,
I found the reins of the kingdom's rule
Firm in my hands, with such new power
And such a wealth of friends,
That my son's head was raised to the widowed crown;
From him began the consecrated bones
Of these kings.
While the great Provençal dowry
Did not remove the shame from my blood,
It had little worth, yet did no harm.
There he began his plunder with force and lies;
Then, as supposed recompense, he seized
Ponthieu, Normandy, and Gascony.
Charles came to Italy and, as recompense,
Made a victim of Conradin; then later
Sent Thomas back to heaven, as recompense.
A time I see, not far from now,
When another Charles will leave France
To make himself and his followers better known.
He leaves unarmed, alone, with the spear
With which Judas jousted; he thrusts it
So that Florence's belly bursts.
From this he will gain no land, but sin and shame,
All the heavier for him, the less weight
He gives to such a loss.
The other, whom I see once captured at sea,
Selling his daughter and bargaining over her,
As pirates do with others' slaves.
O avarice, what more can you do,
Since you have drawn my blood so far to you
That it cares nothing for its own flesh?
And, to show the future and past evil,
I see the French lily enter Alagna,
And Christ captured in his vicar.
I see him mocked once more;
I see vinegar and gall renewed,
And him slain among living thieves.
I see a new Pilate, so cruel
That this does not satisfy him; without judgment
He brings greedy banners into the Temple.
O my Lord, when shall I rejoice
To see the vengeance that, hidden,
Makes sweet your anger in your secret counsel?
What I said of that one bride
Of the Holy Spirit, which made you turn
Toward me to hear some explanation,
Is set aside for all our prayer
For as long as day lasts; but when night falls,
We take up a contrary sound instead.
At night we recall Pygmalion,
Whom his greed for gold made traitor, thief,
And murderer of his own kin;
And the misery of greedy Midas,
Which followed his grasping request,
For which he must forever be mocked.
All remember foolish Achan,
How he stole the spoils, so that Joshua's anger
Seems still to gnaw at him here.
Then we accuse Sapphira and her husband;
We praise the kicks Heliodorus received;
The whole world circles in infamy
For Polymnestor, who killed Polydorus.
At last we cry: O Crassus,
Tell us, you who know, what gold tastes like!
One of us speaks aloud, another softly,
According to the feeling that urges us to speak,
Now with a stronger, now with a weaker step.
For the good discussed here by day,
A little while ago I was not alone; but nearby
No other voice was raised.
We had already left him,
And strove to press along the road
As far as our strength allowed;
When I felt, as though something were falling,
The mountain tremble; then a chill seized me,
Such as grips one going to death.
Delos surely never shook so violently
Before Latona made her nest there
To give birth to the two eyes of heaven.
Then a cry began on every side,
So that my master turned toward me,
Saying: Do not fear while I guide you.
Glory in the highest, all, to God,
They cried, as I understood from those nearby,
By whom the shout could be made out.
We stood motionless and amazed,
Like the shepherds who first heard that song,
Until the trembling ceased and it was finished.
Then we resumed our holy way,
Watching the shades who lay upon the ground,
Returned now to their accustomed weeping.
No ignorance ever made me so eager
To know, with such struggle,
If my memory does not err,
As I then seemed, in thought, to be;
Nor dared I ask, because of our haste,
Nor could I see the thing for myself:
Thus I walked fearful and thoughtful.
