When the game of chance breaks up,
The loser stays behind in sorrow,
And, recalling his throws, learns sadly:
The whole crowd goes off with the winner;
Some go ahead, some seize him from behind,
And some at his side remind him of themselves;
He does not stop, but listens to this one and that;
To the one whose hand he offers, he gives no further press;
And so he escapes the crush.
So was I among that dense crowd,
Turning my face this way and that toward them,
And promising, I freed myself from them.
There was the man of Arezzo who met his death
At the fierce hands of Ghin di Tacco,
And the other who drowned while fleeing pursuit.
There, with hands outstretched, prayed
Federigo Novello, and the man from Pisa
Who made good Marzucco seem so strong.
[I saw Count Orso, and the soul parted
From his body through hatred and envy,
As he said, not for any fault he had committed];
I mean Pierre de la Brosse; and here, while she is still on earth,
May the lady of Brabant take care,
So that afterward she will not join a worse flock.
As soon as I was free of all those
Shades who kept asking that others pray for them,
So that their becoming holy might be hastened,
I began: It seems that you deny me,
My light, explicitly in some passage,
That prayer can bend heaven's decree;
Yet these people ask only for this.
Would their hope then be in vain?
Or have I failed to understand your words?
He answered me: My words are plain,
And their hope does not fail,
If one considers it with a sound mind.
For the summit of judgment does not bend,
Even if the fire of love in one moment completes
What must be satisfied by whoever remains here:
And where I fixed that point,
No fault was corrected by prayer,
For the plea was separated from God.
Truly, do not rest in so high a doubt
Unless she tells you so, the one who will be
A light between truth and understanding.
I do not know if you understand; I mean Beatrice.
You will see her above, on the summit
Of this mountain, smiling and joyful.
And I said to my guide: Let us go faster,
For I am no longer tired as before;
And you see that the slope is already casting a shadow.
We shall go forward with this day, he replied,
As far as we can from now on;
But things are not as you imagine.
Before you reach the place above, you will see
The one now hidden by the mountainside return,
So that you cannot block his rays.
But look there: a soul, standing
Alone, gazes toward us;
She will show us the shorter way.
We came to her: O Lombard soul,
How proud and disdainful you stood,
And how composed and deliberate your eyes!
She said nothing to us,
But let us pass, merely watching,
Like a lion at rest.
At last Virgil approached her, asking
That she show us the best ascent;
But she did not answer his request.
Instead she asked about our homeland and our life.
And the gentle guide began: Mantua...
The shade, wholly withdrawn into herself,
Rose from the place where she had stood,
Saying: O Mantuan, I am Sordello,
From your land. And each embraced the other.
Ah, enslaved Italy, home of sorrow,
Ship without a helmsman in a great storm,
No queen of provinces, but a brothel!
That noble soul was so quick,
Only at the sweet sound of his homeland,
To welcome his fellow citizen there;
Yet now your living people are never free of war,
And one gnaws at another, among those
Shut within the same walls and moat.
Wretched land, search all around your shores,
Then look within your own heart: does any part
Of you enjoy peace?
What good was it that Justinian restored your bridle
If the saddle is empty? Without him
Your shame would be less.
Ah, people, you ought to be devout
And let Caesar sit in the saddle,
If you understand what God teaches you!
See how this beast has grown treacherous,
For it is not checked by the spurs,
Though you placed your hand upon the stirrup.
O German Albert, who abandon
This woman, now wild and untamed,
Though you should mount her saddle,
May just judgment fall from the stars
Upon your blood, fresh and manifest,
So that your successor will fear it;
For you and your father, held fast
By greed for what lies there, have allowed
The garden of the Empire to become desolate.
Come see the Montagues and Capulets,
The Monaldis and Filippeschi, careless man:
Some already grieving, others fearful.
Come, cruel one, come and see the distress
Of your nobles, and tend their wrongs,
And see how Santafior is governed.
Come see your Rome, which weeps,
Widowed and alone, calling day and night:
My Caesar, why do you not stay with me?
Come see how much your people love one another;
And if pity for us does not move you,
Come, at least, to feel shame for your reputation.
And if I may say so, O highest Jove,
Who were crucified on earth for us,
Have your just eyes turned elsewhere?
Or is there some design, formed in the depths
Of your counsel, for some good,
Entirely beyond our understanding?
For all the cities of Italy are full
Of tyrants, and every peasant who takes sides
Becomes a Marcellus.
My Florence, you may well be pleased
That this digression does not touch you,
Thanks to your people, so resourceful.
Many have justice in their hearts, but are slow to shoot,
For fear of coming to the bow without counsel;
But your people keep it at the tip of their tongues.
Many refuse the common burden,
But your people answer promptly,
Without being called: I will shoulder it.
So rejoice, for you have good reason:
You are rich, at peace, and wise.
If I speak the truth, the result makes it plain.
Athens and Lacedaemon, which made
The ancient laws and were so well governed,
Made but a small contribution to good living
Compared with you, who make such subtle
Decrees that what you spin in October
Does not last until mid-November.
How often, in the time I remember,
Have you changed laws, offices, coinage, and customs,
And renewed your membership!
And if you remember well and see clearly,
You will see yourself like a sick woman
Who cannot find rest upon her feathers,
But eases her pain by turning over.
