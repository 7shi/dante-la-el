Eager to search within and all around
the divine forest, dense and green,
which tempered the new day's light.
I left the bank without delay
and slowly entered the open ground,
over soil that smelled on every side.
A gentle breeze, unchanged in itself,
struck my brow no harder
than a soft wind.
Its breath made the leaves tremble and bend
toward the side where the holy mountain
first casts its shadow.
Yet they did not stray so far from upright
that little birds among the treetops
ceased to practice all their art.
With joy they welcomed the early hours,
singing among the leaves,
which kept time with their rhymes.
Such was the sound from branch to branch
through the pinewood on Chiassi's shore,
when Aeolus releases Eurus.
My slow steps had carried me so far
into the ancient wood that I could no longer
see where I had entered.
Then a stream stopped me from going farther;
its little waves flowed to the left
and bent the grass along its bank.
The clearest waters in the world
would seem to hold some mixture
beside that water which conceals nothing,
though it moved darkly beneath the shade
that never lets the sun or moon
send rays down there.
I stood still and looked across
the little stream, wondering at
the great variety of fresh trees.
There appeared to me, as sometimes appears
something that suddenly drives away
all other thought through wonder,
a solitary lady walking
and singing, choosing flower by flower,
her whole path painted with them.
Ah, lovely lady, warmed by love's rays,
if I may trust the signs that bear
witness to the heart,
may you come forward,
I said, toward this bank,
close enough for me to hear your song.
You bring to mind where and what Proserpina was
when her mother lost her, and she lost
the season of spring.
As a dancing lady turns, feet close
to the ground and turned inward,
scarcely placing one foot before another,
so she turned among the red
and tawny little flowers toward me, as
a modest maiden lowers her eyes.
She granted my request, coming so near
that her sweet voice
reached me with its meaning.
When she reached the place where the grass
was bathed by the lovely river's waves,
she gave me the gift of raising her eyes.
I think such light never shone
beneath the brows of Venus, pierced by her son,
beyond her customary way.
She smiled, standing straight on the farther bank,
choosing many colors with her hands
from flowers the high earth yields without seed.
The river kept us three paces apart;
not even the Hellespont, crossed by Xerxes,
a check on all human pride,
brought Leander greater hatred
when he braved the waves between Sestos and Abydos
than this stream brought me, closed to my passage.
You are new here, perhaps because I smile,
she began, in this place chosen
as a nest for human nature,
some doubt holds you in wonder;
but the Psalm You have delighted me gives light
and can clear your understanding's mist.
And you before me, who asked me,
say if you wish to hear anything else;
I came ready to answer all your questions.
The water, I said, and the forest's sound
challenge in me a new belief
about what I heard that contradicts this.
Then she said: I will explain the cause
of what makes you wonder,
and clear the cloud that troubles you.
The highest Good, who alone delights in himself,
made man good, and gave him this good place
as a pledge of eternal peace.
Because of his fault he stayed here briefly;
because of his fault, fair laughter
and sweet play he changed to weeping and distress.
So that the turmoil below, from
exhalations of water and earth,
which follow heat as far as they can,
would not harm man, this mountain
rose high toward heaven
and freed you from the closed gate.
All the air around turns in a circle
with the first sphere, unless its circle
were broken somewhere;
at this height, free from change
in the clear air, that motion strikes
and makes the dense forest sound.
The struck plant has such power
it fills the air with its virtue,
then shakes it out as it revolves.
The other earth, fit by nature
and by its heaven, conceives and brings forth
different trees of differing virtues.
Knowing this, it would not seem a wonder
when a plant takes root in that world
without visible seed.
Know that the holy plain
where you are is full of every seed,
and bears a fruit never lost below.
The water you see does not rise from a spring
renewed by vapor turned to water by frost,
like a river that ebbs and loses flow;
it comes from a firm, certain source,
which draws from God's will as much
as it pours from its two open sides.
From this side it flows with power
and takes away the memory of sin;
from the other it restores each good deed to memory.
Here it is called Lethe, and there
Eunoe; it does not work
unless both waters have first been tasted.
It surpasses every other taste.
Though your thirst could be satisfied,
though I reveal no more,
I will give one further corollary as a grace;
I think my words will be dear to you
even if they go beyond my promise.
Those who in ancient times sang
of the Golden Age and its happy state
perhaps dreamed of this place on Parnassus.
Here the human root was innocent;
here spring and every fruit endure;
this is the nectar of which all speak.
Then I turned wholly back
toward my poets, and saw they had heard
the final words with a smile;
afterward I turned my face back toward the lovely lady.
