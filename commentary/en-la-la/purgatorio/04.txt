When through delights or pains,
Which one of our faculties apprehends,
The soul gathers itself wholly to it,
It seems to attend to no other power;
And this is against that error which believes
That one soul is kindled above another.
And so, when a thing is heard or seen
That holds the soul strongly turned toward itself,
Time passes, and a person does not notice it,
For one power is the one that listens,
And another is the one that wholly possesses the soul;
This one is almost bound, and that one is free.
Of this I had true experience,
Hearing that spirit and wondering
That a good fifty degrees had been climbed
By the sun, and I had not noticed, when
We came where those souls together
Shouted to us: Here is what you seek.
An opening a thousand times wider is hedged in,
With a little forkful of its thorns,
By a villager, when the grapes grow dark,
Than was the path by which climbed
My guide and I, almost alone,
As soon as that band had left us.
One goes to Sanleo and descends to Noli;
One climbs Bismantova and its summit
On foot; but here a person must fly.
I mean with the swift wings and feathers
Of great desire, following that guide
Who gave me hope and served as my light.
We climbed through a cleft in the rock,
And on every side its narrowness pressed us,
And hands and feet turned beneath us.
When we reached the highest edge
Of the high bank, on the open slope,
I said, My master, what path shall we take?
And he said to me, Let none of your steps falter;
Keep climbing the mountain behind me
Until some wise guide appears to us.
The summit was so high it defeated the sight,
And the slope was far steeper
Than a line drawn from a quadrant's midpoint to its center.
I was tired when I began:
O sweet father, turn and look
How I am left alone if you do not stop.
My son, he said, drag yourself as far as that,
Pointing out to me with his finger a ledge
That curves around the whole hillside on that side.
His words spurred me on so
That I forced myself, crawling close beside him,
Until the ledge lay beneath my feet.
We both sat down there,
Facing east, from where we had climbed,
For looking back often helps people.
First I turned my eyes to the lower shores;
Then I raised them to the sun and wondered
That its rays struck us on the left.
The poet clearly saw that I stood
All bewildered at the chariot of light,
Where it entered between us and the North.
So he said to me: If Castor and Pollux
Were in company with that mirror
That carries its light up and down,
You would see the ruddy Zodiac
Turning more closely still around the Bears,
Were it not to stray from its ancient course.
If you wish to be able to understand how this can be,
With your attention turned inward, imagine Mount Zion
Standing on earth with this mountain,
So that both have one horizon
And different hemispheres; then the path
Along which Phaeton failed to guide the chariot well,
You will see how this one must travel
On one side, while the other goes on the other,
If your intellect looks with clarity.
Certainly, my master, I said, ever
I have not seen so clearly as I now understand
Where my intellect seemed to fail:
For the middle circle of the heavenly motion,
Called the Equator in one art,
And always remaining between summer and winter,
For the reason you gave, moves from here
Toward the north, when the Hebrews
Saw it toward the warm region.
But if it pleases you, I would gladly know
How far we must go, since the hillside rises
Higher than my eyes can climb.
And he said to me: This mountain is such
That at first, lower down, it is always hard,
And the farther one climbs, the less it pains.
So when it seems gentle to you
To such a degree that climbing upward feels as easy
As going downstream by boat,
Then you will be at the end of this path;
There expect to rest from your toil.
I will say no more, and I know this for certain.
And as soon as he had spoken his word,
Then a voice nearby sounded: Perhaps
You will first need to sit down.
At the sound of it each of us turned,
And on our left we saw a great rock,
Which neither he nor I had noticed before.
We drew near; and there were people
Standing in the shade behind the rock,
As one takes up a place to stand through negligence.
And one of them, who seemed to me weary,
Sat embracing his knees,
Holding his face low between them.
O sweet master of mine, I said, look
At the one who seems more negligent,
Than if sloth were his own sister.
Then he turned toward us and looked up,
Moving his gaze only along his thigh,
And said, Go on up, then, you who are so capable.
I recognized him then; and the distress
That still made me somewhat short of breath
Did not keep me from going to him; and afterward,
When I reached him, he barely lifted his head,
Saying, Have you seen clearly how the sun
Drives its chariot on the left shoulder?
His sluggish gestures and short words
Moved my lips to a little smile;
Then I began: Belacqua, I do not grieve
For you anymore; but tell me, why, to sit,
Are you settled here? Are you waiting here for a guide,
Or has your old habit seized you again?
And he said, Brother, what good is climbing up?
For I would not be allowed to go to the torments
By God's bird, who sits above the gate.
First the heavens must circle around me for so long
Outside it as I spent in life,
Because I delayed the good sighs until the end,
Unless a prayer helps me first,
Rising from a heart that lives in grace;
What good is another, unheard in Heaven?
And already the poet was climbing ahead of me,
Saying, Come now, and see that I have reached
The sun's meridian, for on the shore
Night already covers Morocco with its foot.
