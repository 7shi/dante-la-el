Natural thirst, which is never quenched,
Except by the water for which the little woman
Of Samaria asked for grace,
Troubled me and stung me with haste
Along the blocked path behind my guide,
And I grieved for the just punishment.
Behold, as Luke writes,
That Christ appeared to two who were on the road,
Already risen from the sepulchral tomb,
A shade appeared to us, coming behind us,
Looking at the crowd lying at his feet;
We had not noticed him, so he spoke first,
Saying: My brothers, may God grant you peace.
We turned at once, and Virgil
Gave him the fitting reply.
Then he began: May the true court
Place you in peace in its blessed assembly,
While it binds me in eternal exile.
How so? he said, as we hurried along.
If you are shades whom God does not deem worthy above,
Who has guided you so far up his stairs?
And my teacher: If you look at the marks
This man bears, and those the angel inscribed,
You will see he is fit to reign among the good.
But because she who spins day and night
Had not yet drawn for him the thread
Which Clotho assigns and winds for each,
His soul, which is your sister and mine,
Could not come up alone;
For it does not see as we do.
So I was drawn out of Hell's broad maw
To show him the way, and I shall show him
As far as my school can lead him.
But tell me, if you know, why the mountain
Shook just now, and why all together
It seemed to cry out down to its soft foot?
By asking, he passed through the eye
Of my desire, and with hope alone
Made my thirst less parched.
He began: Nothing in the mountain's order
Is felt without cause, nor is anything
Outside its customary law.
Here it is free from every alteration:
Only what heaven receives from itself, within itself,
Can exist here, and nothing from another cause.
For no rain, hail, or snow falls higher
Than the short stair of three steps,
Nor dew nor frost.
Thick or thin clouds do not appear,
Nor lightning, nor Thaumas' daughter,
Who often changes her region below.
Dry vapor rises no farther
Than the top of the three steps I spoke of,
Where Peter's vicar sets his feet.
Perhaps it shakes a little or much lower down;
But though wind lies hidden in the earth,
Somehow it has never shaken up here.
It shakes when a soul feels itself cleansed
And rises, or begins to move
Upward, and such a cry follows.
The soul tests its release from impurity;
Now wholly free to change its dwelling,
It rises, and takes joy in flight.
First it desires the good, but desire does not release it,
For divine justice, against its will,
Sets it to the punishment as it once set it to sin.
I have lain in this pain
For five hundred years and more; only now
Have I felt a free will for a better ascent.
Therefore you felt the earthquake, and the devout
Spirits throughout the mountain praising
The Lord, who soon sends them above.
So he spoke; and as one enjoys a drink
In proportion to the greatness of thirst,
I cannot say how much good it did me.
And the wise guide: Now I see the net
That traps you here, and how it is escaped,
Why it shakes, and what makes you rejoice.
Now, who you were, I beg you to tell me,
And why you have lain here so many ages;
Let me understand it from your words.
In the time when good Titus, with the aid
Of the highest King, avenged the wounds
From which the blood sold by Judas flowed,
I was in the world, with a name
That lasts and honors more, replied that spirit,
Famed indeed, but not yet with faith.
So sweet was my poetic breath
That Rome drew me to herself, a man of Toulouse,
Where I deserved my temples crowned with myrtle.
Statius is still the name by which people
Know me there. I sang of Thebes, then great Achilles,
But fell on the way with my second burden.
The sparks that warmed my ardor
Were the seed of the divine flame,
By which more than a thousand have been lit.
I mean the Aeneid, which was my mother,
And my nurse as I wrote poetry;
Without it I never made a weight of a dram.
And to have lived in the world
When Virgil lived, I would accept one year
More than I ought to leave my exile.
These words turned Virgil toward me,
His face saying silently: Be quiet.
But not all that one wills can be controlled.
For laughter and tears follow so closely
The passion that grips each person
That even the truest wills cannot restrain them.
I nevertheless smiled, like one who signals;
Then the shade fell silent and looked at me
In the eyes, where the expression is most fixed.
And if you bring such labor to a good end,
He said, why has your face just now
Shown me a flash of laughter?
Now I am caught on both sides:
One makes me keep silent, the other urges me
To speak; I sigh, and my master understands.
Do not fear to speak, he tells me;
Speak, and tell him what he asks
So earnestly.
So I said to him: Perhaps you wonder,
Ancient spirit, at the smile I gave;
But I want you to wonder still more.
This one, who guides my eyes upward,
Is that Virgil from whom you drew
The strength to sing of men and gods.
If you thought my smile had another cause,
Let that thought go as false; believe instead
The words you spoke about him.
He was already bending to embrace the feet
Of my teacher, but he said: Brother,
Do not; you are a shade and see a shade.
Rising, he said: Now you can understand
The depth of the love that warms me toward you,
When I forget our insubstantial nature
And treat shades as solid things.
