CANTO VIII 101

**The Negligent Rulers** “So may that light which guideth thee on high,
find in thy will as much wax as is needful to
**Conrad Malaspina** reach the enamelled summit,”

it began, “if thou know true tidings of Valdi-
macra, or of neighbouring parts, tell it me who
once was mighty there.

I was called Conrad Malaspina: not the elder
am I, but descended from him: to mine own
I bore that love which here is purified.”

**Dante praises the Malaspini** “Oh,” said I to him, “through your lands I ne’er
have been, but where do men dwell through-
out Europe to whom they are not renowned?

The fame which honours your house proclaims
abroad its lords, and proclaims the country, so
that he knows of it who there hath never been.

And I swear to you, so may I go on high, that
your honoured race strips not itself of the
glory of the purse and of the sword.

Custom and nature so do privilege it, that for all
that the guilty head sets the world awry, it
alone goeth straight and scorns the path of evil.”

**and is rewarded by Conrad’s cheering prophecy** And he: “Now depart, for the sun goeth not
to rest seven times in the bed which the Ram
covers and bestrides with all four feet,

ere this courteous opinion shall be nailed in the
midst of thy head, with bigger nails than other
men’s words, if course of judgment be not
stayed.”

The pointless swords are usually taken to indicate
justice tempered with mercy (*cf.* below, Canto xxxi.
42, *note*); but perhaps they mean that the battle is in
truth already decided, the deadly thrust no longer
needed, and that the sword-edge alone is adequate
(see below, Canto xxxi. 2, 3).
