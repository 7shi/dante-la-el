CANTO XXII 277

**Statius’ conversion to Christianity** and thy words, touched on above, harmonised so
with the new preachers, that the habit took
me of visiting them.

They then became so holy in my sight, that
when Domitian persecuted them, their wail-
ings were not without tears of mine.

And while by me yon world was trod, I succoured
them, and their righteous lives made me despise
all other sects;

and ere in my poem I had brought the Greeks
to Thebes’ rivers, I received baptism, but
through fear I was a secret Christian,

long time pretending paganism; and this luke-
warmness made me speed round the fourth circle
more than four times a hundred years.

**The worthies of antiquity** Thou therefore, who hast lifted the covering
which hid from me the great good I tell of,
while we have time to spare on the ascent,

tell me, where is our ancient Terence, Cæcilius,
Plautus, and Varro if thou knowest; tell me
if they are damned, and in what ward.”

“They, and Persius, and I, and many others,”
my Leader answered, “are with that Greek
to whom the Muses gave suck more than to
any other,

in the first circle of the dark prison. Ofttimes
we talk of the mount which hath our foster-
mothers ever with it.

Euripides is there with us, and Antiphon,
Simonides, Agathon, and many other Greeks,
who once decked their brows with laurel.

There are seen of thy people Antigone,
Deiphyle and Argia, and Ismene so sad as
she was.
