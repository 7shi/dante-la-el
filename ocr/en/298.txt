INFERNO

D ANTE, after having seen and recognised the five
Noble Thieves, addresses his native city in bitter
concentrated sorrow and shame, mingled with heart-
felt longings and affection. The calamities which mis-
government, faction, and crime had been preparing for
many years before the date of his mystic Vision, and
which he himself as Chief Magistrate in 1300 had done
his utmost to prevent, are notified in form of prophecy.
His own exile, though not directly alluded to, and his
hopes of “morning”—of deliverance for Florence and
himself, and of justice on their enemies—were nearly
connected with those calamities (1-12). And when he
