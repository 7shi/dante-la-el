# Paradiso 22

## Lines 1–12 — The Guide Reassures Dante

> 1 Oppresso di stupore alla mia guida  
> 2 mi volsi, come parvol che ricorre  
> 3 sempre colà dove più si confida;  

Oppressed with stupor to my guide I turned, as doth a little child who hath recourse ever where most he hath his confidence;

- *di stupore* ‘by amazement’ → “with stupor”: the cause of the oppression is expressed with “with”, and amazement is rendered as “stupor”.
- *mi volsi* ‘myself I turned’ → “I turned”: English uses the verb intransitively, without the reflexive pronoun.
- *come parvol* ‘as a little child’ → “as doth a little child”: “doth” is supplied to stand for the act of turning.
- *ricorre* ‘runs for help’ → “hath recourse”: the single verb becomes a verb and noun.
- *colà dove* ‘thither where’ → “where”: the separate word indicating direction is dropped.
- *più si confida* ‘most he trusts’ → “most he hath his confidence”: the pronominal verb becomes “hath” with a possessive and a noun.

> 4 e quella, come madre che soccorre  
> 5 subito al figlio pallido ed anelo  
> 6 con la sua voce che il suol ben disporre,  

and she, like a mother who succoureth quick her pale and gasping child, with her own voice which still disposeth him aright,

- *quella* ‘that one’ → “she”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *come madre* ‘like mother’ → “like a mother”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *soccorre subito al figlio pallido ed anelo* ‘comes at once to the aid of the son pale and breathless’ → “succoureth quick her pale and gasping child”: “succoureth” takes a direct object in place of the Italian construction with *a*; the article becomes a possessive, “child” is less specific than ‘son’, and the adjectives precede the noun, with breathlessness expressed as “gasping”.
- *con la sua voce* ‘with her voice’ → “with her own voice”: “own” adds emphasis to the possessive.
- *il suol ben disporre* ‘is wont to dispose him well’ → “still disposeth him aright”: the habitual verb with its infinitive becomes the adverb “still” with a finite verb; the object pronoun moves from before the verbs to after “disposeth”, and *ben* is rendered as “aright” after the object.

> 7 mi disse: “Non sai tu che tu sei in cielo?  

said to me: “Knowst thou not thou art in heaven?

- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *Non sai tu* ‘not knowest thou’ → “Knowst thou not”: the negative moves after the subject.
- *che tu sei in cielo* ‘that thou art in heaven’ → “thou art in heaven”: the conjunction introducing the clause is omitted.

> 8 e non sai tu che il cielo è tutto santo,  
> 9 e ciò che ci si fa vien da buon zelo?  

and knowst thou not heaven is all holy, and that which here is done cometh of righteous zeal?

- *non sai tu* ‘not knowest thou’ → “knowst thou not”: the negative again follows the subject.
- *che il cielo* ‘that the heaven’ → “heaven”: both the conjunction and the definite article are omitted.
- *ci si fa* ‘there one does’ → “here is done”: the locative is rendered from the speaker’s standpoint as “here”, and the *si* construction becomes an English passive.
- *vien da buon zelo* ‘comes from good zeal’ → “cometh of righteous zeal”: “of” expresses the source, and “righteous” gives ‘good’ a specifically moral sense.

> 10 Come t' avrebbe trasmutato il canto,  
> 11 ed io ridendo, mo pensar lo puoi,  
> 12 poscia che il grido t' ha mosso cotanto;  

How the song had transmuted thee, and I in smiling, now mayst thou think since the cry hath so moved thee;

- *t' avrebbe trasmutato il canto* ‘thee would have transmuted the song’ → “the song had transmuted thee”: the subject moves from last to first and the object follows the verb; “had transmuted” has the counterfactual force of the Italian conditional perfect.
- *ed io ridendo* ‘and I smiling’ → “and I in smiling”: the gerund is rendered with the preposition “in”.
- *mo pensar lo puoi* ‘now think it thou canst’ → “now mayst thou think”: the modal precedes the infinitive, ability is expressed by “mayst”, and the pronoun resuming the preceding clause is omitted.
- *t' ha mosso cotanto* ‘thee has moved so much’ → “hath so moved thee”: the object moves after the participle, and the final expression of degree becomes “so” before “moved”.

## Lines 13–45 — Heaven’s Sword and Benedict’s Reply

> 13 nel qual, se inteso avessi i preghi suoi,  
> 14 già ti sarebbe nota la vendetta,  
> 15 che tu vedrai innanzi che tu muoi.  

wherein, hadst thou understood their prayers, already would be known to thee the vengeance which thou shalt see ere that thou die.

- *nel qual* ‘in which’ → “wherein”: the preposition and relative pronoun become one relative adverb.
- *se inteso avessi* ‘if understood thou hadst’ → “hadst thou understood”: inversion takes the place of the conditional conjunction, and the participle follows the auxiliary.
- *i preghi suoi* ‘the prayers of it’ → “their prayers”: the possessive precedes the noun and refers to the people praying rather than to their cry.
- *ti sarebbe nota* ‘to thee would be known’ → “would be known to thee”: the indirect object follows the predicate.
- *innanzi che tu muoi* ‘before thou diest’ → “ere that thou die”: the Italian present indicative becomes an English subjunctive.

> 16 La spada di quassù non taglia in fretta,  
> 17 nè tardo, ma che al parer di colui  
> 18 che disiando o temendo l' aspetta.  

The sword from here above cleaveth not in haste nor tardy, save to his deeming who in longing or in fear awaiteth it.

- *non taglia* ‘not cuts’ → “cleaveth not”: the verb is more specific, and the negative follows it.
- *tardo* ‘late’ → “tardy”: the Italian adjective used adverbially is matched by an English adjective rather than by an adverb of time.
- *ma che* ‘except’ → “save”: the two-word expression becomes a single exceptive.
- *al parer di colui* ‘to the judgment of him’ → “to his deeming”: the possessor is brought forward as a possessive, leaving the following relative to refer back to “his”.
- *disiando o temendo* ‘desiring or fearing’ → “in longing or in fear”: the two gerunds become prepositional phrases with nouns.
- *l' aspetta* ‘it awaits’ → “awaiteth it”: the object follows the verb.

> 19 Ma rivolgiti omai inverso altrui,  
> 20 ch' assai illustri spiriti vedrai,  
> 21 se com' io dico l' aspetto ridui.”  

But turn thee now to others; for many illustrious spirits shalt thou see, if thou again dost lead thy look accordant to my speaking.”

- *rivolgiti* ‘turn thyself’ → “turn thee”: English uses the simple personal pronoun reflexively.
- *omai* ‘by now’ → “now”: the temporal adverb is simplified.
- *ch'* ‘because’ → “for”: the causal subordinator becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *assai illustri spiriti vedrai* ‘many illustrious spirits thou wilt see’ → “many illustrious spirits shalt thou see”: the object stays first, but the English auxiliary precedes the subject.
- *com' io dico* ‘as I say’ → “accordant to my speaking”: a finite clause becomes an adjectival phrase and moves to the end of the condition.
- *l' aspetto ridui* ‘thy gaze thou leadest back’ → “thou again dost lead thy look”: the object follows the verb, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the sense of the verbal prefix is expressed separately by “again”.

> 22 Com' a lei piacque gli occhi dirizzai,  
> 23 e vidi cento sperule, che insieme  
> 24 più s' abbellivan coi mutui rai.  

As was her pleasure directed I mine eyes, and saw an hundred spherelets, which together were made more beauteous by their mutual rays.

- *Com' a lei piacque* ‘as it pleased her’ → “As was her pleasure”: the verb becomes a copula and noun, and the person pleased becomes the possessor.
- *gli occhi dirizzai* ‘the eyes I directed’ → “directed I mine eyes”: the object moves after the verb and takes a possessive in place of the article.
- *più s' abbellivan* ‘more they beautified themselves’ → “were made more beauteous”: the reflexive construction becomes a passive with an adjective of degree.
- *coi mutui rai* ‘with the mutual rays’ → “by their mutual rays”: the rays are construed as the agency of the passive, and the article becomes a possessive.

> 25 Io stava come quei che in sè repreme  
> 26 la punta del disio, e non s' attenta  
> 27 del domandar, sì del troppo si teme.  

I stood as one repressing in himself the prick of his desire, who doth not frame to ask, so feareth he to exceed.

- *stava* ‘was standing’ → “stood”: the imperfect is rendered by the simple past.
- *quei che in sè repreme* ‘one who in himself represses’ → “one repressing in himself”: the relative clause becomes a participial phrase, with the reflexive phrase after the participle.
- *la punta del disio* ‘the point of desire’ → “the prick of his desire”: the point is rendered by its felt effect, and desire is given a possessor.
- *e non s' attenta del domandar* ‘and does not venture on asking’ → “who doth not frame to ask”: a relative clause replaces the coordination; the reflexive verb is rendered by a nonreflexive one, and the preposition and nominalized infinitive give way to an English infinitive.
- *sì del troppo si teme* ‘so much does he fear excess’ → “so feareth he to exceed”: the substantivized *troppo* becomes a verbal infinitive following the verb of fear, and the pronominal construction is not retained.

> 28 E la maggiore e la più luculenta  
> 29 di quelle margherite innanzi fessi,  
> 30 per far di sè la mia voglia contenta.  

And the greatest and most shining of these pearls came forward to make my will content concerning him.

- *la maggiore e la più luculenta* ‘the greater and the more shining’ → “the greatest and most shining”: the comparative forms have superlative force in the group; English also uses one article for both.
- *di quelle margherite* ‘of those pearls’ → “of these pearls”: the demonstrative changes from distal to proximal.
- *innanzi fessi* ‘forward made itself’ → “came forward”: an intransitive verb of motion replaces the reflexive construction, and the adverb follows it.
- *per far di sè la mia voglia contenta* ‘in order to make, concerning himself, my desire content’ → “to make my will content concerning him”: the bare to-infinitive marks purpose, “will” renders the desire, and the phrase referring back to the subject moves to the end with a nonreflexive pronoun.

> 31 Poi dentro a lei udi': “Se tu vedessi,  
> 32 com' io, la carità che tra noi arde,  
> 33 li tuoi concetti sarebbero espressi;  

Then there within I heard: “Didst thou see, as I, the love which burneth amongst us, thy thoughts had been expressed;

- *dentro a lei* ‘inside it’ → “there within”: a locative adverb replaces the explicit pronominal reference.
- *Se tu vedessi* ‘if thou sawest’ → “Didst thou see”: inversion with an auxiliary replaces the conditional conjunction and imperfect subjunctive.
- *la carità* ‘the charity’ → “the love”: the broader English noun is used without the article.
- *li tuoi concetti* ‘thy conceptions’ → “thy thoughts”: the things conceived are called thoughts.
- *sarebbero espressi* ‘would be expressed’ → “had been expressed”: a past-perfect form with counterfactual force replaces the present conditional, presenting the expression as something that would already have happened.

> 34 ma perchè tu, aspettando, non tarde  
> 35 all' alto fine, io ti farò risposta  
> 36 pure al pensier di che sì ti riguarde.  

but, lest thou by waiting lag from the lofty goal, I will make answer only to the thought of which thou art thus circumspect.

- *perchè tu, aspettando, non tarde* ‘in order that thou, waiting, mayest not be late’ → “lest thou by waiting lag”: “lest” incorporates the negative purpose, “by” makes waiting the means of delay, and “lag” makes delay a matter of progress.
- *all' alto fine* ‘to the high end’ → “from the lofty goal”: English construes lagging in relation to what one falls away from rather than delay in reaching an end.
- *ti farò risposta* ‘to thee I shall make answer’ → “I will make answer”: the personal indirect object is omitted.
- *pure al pensier* ‘even to the thought’ → “only to the thought”: Wicksteed takes the adverb restrictively rather than as emphasizing that even an unspoken thought can be answered.
- *di che sì ti riguarde* ‘about which thou so guardest thyself’ → “of which thou art thus circumspect”: the reflexive verb becomes a copula and adjective, with “thus” qualifying that adjective.

> 37 Quel monte, a cui Casino è nella costa,  
> 38 fu frequentato già in sulla cima  
> 39 dalla gente ingannata e mal disposta.  

That mount, upon whose slope Casino lieth, was erst thronged on its summit by the folk deceived and ill-disposed.

- *a cui Casino è nella costa* ‘on the slope of which Casino is’ → “upon whose slope Casino lieth”: the relative becomes possessive, bringing the slope before the subject, and “lieth” makes the location explicit.
- *fu frequentato già* ‘was frequented formerly’ → “was erst thronged”: the verb suggests crowding rather than habitual resort, and the time adverb moves before the participle.
- *in sulla cima* ‘up on the summit’ → “on its summit”: one preposition does duty for the Italian combination, and a possessive replaces the article.

> 40 E quel son io che su vi portai prima  
> 41 lo nome di colui, che in terra addusse  
> 42 la verità che tanto ci sublima;  

And I am he who first bore up there his name, who brought to earth that truth which doth lift us so high;

- *quel son io* ‘that one am I’ → “I am he”: subject and predicate pronoun exchange places.
- *su vi portai prima* ‘up there I bore first’ → “first bore up there”: the time adverb moves before the verb and the adverbs of place after it; the Italian first-person verb, agreeing with the speaker, has no person distinction in the English past tense.
- *lo nome di colui* ‘the name of him’ → “his name”: the possessor becomes a preceding possessive, so the following relative refers back across “name”.
- *in terra addusse* ‘to earth brought’ → “brought to earth”: the destination follows the verb.
- *la verità* ‘the truth’ → “that truth”: a demonstrative replaces the article.
- *tanto ci sublima* ‘so much exalts us’ → “doth lift us so high”: an auxiliary and a phrase of upward motion replace the single verb, the object follows the main verb, and the degree is expressed at the end.

> 43 e tanta grazia sopra me rilusse,  
> 44 ch' io ritrassi le ville circostanti  
> 45 dall' empio culto che il mondo sedusse.  

and so great grace shone o’er me, that I drew the places round about back from the impious cult which did seduce the world.

- *sopra me rilusse* ‘over me shone’ → “shone o’er me”: the prepositional phrase follows the verb.
- *ritrassi* ‘I drew back’ → “I drew” and “back”: the sense of the prefix is carried by a separate particle placed after the object.
- *le ville circostanti* ‘the surrounding villages’ → “the places round about”: “places” is less specific, and an adverbial phrase renders the adjective.
- *il mondo sedusse* ‘the world seduced’ → “did seduce the world”: the object moves after the verb, and the simple past is rendered with an auxiliary.

## Lines 46–78 — The Contemplatives, the Ladder, and the Neglected Rule

> 46 Questi altri fochi tutti contemplanti  
> 47 uomini furo, accesi di quel caldo  
> 48 che fa nascere i fiori e i frutti santi.  

These other flames were all contemplatives kindled by that warmth which giveth birth to the holy flowers and fruits.

- *fochi* ‘fires’ → “flames”: the noun is changed.
- *tutti contemplanti uomini furo* ‘all contemplating men were’ → “were all contemplatives”: the verb comes first, and the noun “contemplatives” does the work of both the participle and *uomini*.
- *fa nascere* ‘makes be born’ → “giveth birth to”: the causative verb and infinitive become a verbal phrase with a preposition.
- *i fiori e i frutti santi* ‘the flowers and the fruits holy’ → “the holy flowers and fruits”: the adjective moves before both nouns, and the second article is dropped.

> 49 Qui è Maccario, qui è Romoaldo,  
> 50 qui son li frati miei, che dentro ai chiostri  
> 51 fermar li piedi e tennero il cor saldo.”  

Here is Maccarius, here is Romoaldus, here are my brothers who within the cloisters stayed their feet and kept sound their heart.”

- *li frati miei* ‘the brothers of mine’ → “my brothers”: the postposed possessive becomes a determiner before the noun.
- *fermar li piedi* ‘stayed the feet’ → “stayed their feet”: a possessive replaces the definite article with the body part.
- *tennero il cor saldo* ‘kept the heart steadfast’ → “kept sound their heart”: the article again becomes a possessive, and the adjective, rendered “sound”, moves before the object.

> 52 Ed io a lui: “L' affetto, che dimostri  
> 53 meco parlando, e la buona sembianza,  
> 54 ch' io veggio e noto in tutti gli ardor vostri,  

And I to him: “The love thou showest, speaking with me, and the propitious semblance which I perceive and note in all your glows,

- *L' affetto, che dimostri* ‘the affection which thou showest’ → “The love thou showest”: the noun is rendered “love”, and the object relative is left unexpressed.
- *meco parlando* ‘with me speaking’ → “speaking with me”: the participle precedes its complement.
- *la buona sembianza* ‘the good semblance’ → “the propitious semblance”: the general adjective is given the more specific sense of favour shown.
- *veggio* ‘see’ → “perceive”: a verb of perception replaces the verb of sight.
- *tutti gli ardor vostri* ‘all the ardours of yours’ → “all your glows”: the possessive moves before the noun, and “glows” renders the visible aspect of the ardours.

> 55 così m' ha dilatata mia fidanza,  
> 56 come il sol fa la rosa, quando aperta  
> 57 tanto divien quant' ell' ha di possanza;  

hath so outstretched my confidence as the sun doth the rose when it openeth to its utmost power;

- *così m' ha dilatata mia fidanza* ‘so for me has it expanded my confidence’ → “hath so outstretched my confidence”: “so” follows the auxiliary, the personal dative has no separate equivalent, and expansion is expressed as stretching.
- *come il sol fa la rosa* ‘as the sun does the rose’ → “as the sun doth the rose”: the order of the comparison is retained.
- *quando aperta tanto divien quant' ell' ha di possanza* ‘when open it becomes as much as it has of power’ → “when it openeth to its utmost power”: adjective and verb become one verb; the correlative construction of degree becomes a phrase with “utmost”, and the verb of possession gives way to a possessive.

> 58 però ti prego, e tu, padre, m' accerta  
> 59 s' io posso prender tanta grazia, ch' io  
> 60 ti veggia con imagine scoperta.”  

wherefore, I pray thee, and do thou, father, give me assurance whether I may receive so great grace as to behold thee with uncovered image.”

- *e tu, padre, m' accerta* ‘and thou, father, assure me’ → “and do thou, father, give me assurance”: the imperative gains emphatic “do”, and the verb with its object becomes a verb with an indirect object and a noun.
- *s' io posso prender* ‘if I can take’ → “whether I may receive”: the indirect question is marked by “whether”; ability is expressed as possibility or permission, and taking as receiving.
- *tanta grazia, ch' io ti veggia* ‘so much grace that I may see thee’ → “so great grace as to behold thee”: quantity becomes degree; the finite subjunctive clause becomes an infinitive with its object after it, and “behold” renders the simpler verb of sight.
- *con imagine scoperta* ‘with image uncovered’ → “with uncovered image”: the participle precedes the noun.

> 61 Ond' egli: “Frate, il tuo alto disio  
> 62 s' adempierà in sull' ultima spera,  
> 63 dove s' adempion tutti gli altri e il mio.  

Whereat he: “Brother, thy high desire shall be fulfilled in the last sphere, where all the rest have their fulfilment, and mine too.

- *s' adempierà* ‘will fulfil itself’ → “shall be fulfilled”: a passive renders the pronominal verb.
- *in sull' ultima spera* ‘up on the last sphere’ → “in the last sphere”: one preposition replaces the compound expression of place.
- *dove s' adempion tutti gli altri* ‘where are fulfilled all the others’ → “where all the rest have their fulfilment”: the subject moves before the verb, “the rest” replaces the substantivized adjective, and a verb of possession with a noun renders the pronominal verb.
- *e il mio* ‘and mine’ → “and mine too”: “too” makes the addition explicit.

> 64 Ivi è perfetta, matura ed intera  
> 65 ciascuna disianza; in quella sola  

There perfect, ripe, and whole is each desire;

- *è perfetta, matura ed intera* ‘is perfect, ripe, and whole’ → “perfect, ripe, and whole is”: the verb moves after the three adjectives.

> 66 è ogni parte là dove sempr' era,  
> 67 perchè non è in loco, e non s' impola,  

in it alone is every part there where it ever was, for it is not in space, nor hath it poles;

- *in quella sola* ‘in that one alone’ → “in it alone”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative. The phrase at the end of line 65 belongs to this English clause.
- *perchè non è in loco* ‘because it is not in place’ → “for it is not in space”: a coordinating conjunction replaces the subordinating one, and “space” renders *loco*.
- *e non s' impola* ‘and it does not pole itself’ → “nor hath it poles”: negative coordination replaces conjunction and negation; the pronominal verb formed from ‘pole’ becomes a verb of possession with a plural noun.

> 68 e nostra scala infino ad essa varca,  
> 69 onde così dal viso ti s' invola.  

and our ladder even to it goeth, wherefore it thus doth steal it from thy sight.

- *infino ad essa varca* ‘as far as it passes’ → “even to it goeth”: the limit of the movement is expressed by “even to”, and passing by the simpler verb of going.
- *onde così dal viso ti s' invola* ‘whence thus from sight it steals itself away from thee’ → “wherefore it thus doth steal it from thy sight”: the reflexive is rendered by a transitive verb with “it” as object, the phrase of separation moves to the end, and the personal dative becomes the possessive “thy”.

> 70 Infin lassù la vide il patriarca  
> 71 Jacob porgere la superna parte,  
> 72 quando gli apparve d' angeli sì carca.  

Right up to there the patriarch Jacob saw it stretch its upper part, when it was seen by him so with angels laden.

- *Infin lassù* ‘as far as up there’ → “Right up to there”: “Right” emphasizes the limit.
- *la vide il patriarca Jacob* ‘it saw the patriarch Jacob’ → “the patriarch Jacob saw it”: the subject moves before the verb and the object after it.
- *porgere la superna parte* ‘extend the upper part’ → “stretch its upper part”: “stretch” renders the infinitive, and a possessive replaces the article.
- *quando gli apparve* ‘when it appeared to him’ → “when it was seen by him”: appearing to someone becomes a passive construction with the perceiver as agent.
- *d' angeli sì carca* ‘with angels so laden’ → “so with angels laden”: the adverb of degree moves before the prepositional phrase.

> 73 Ma per salirla mo nessun diparte  
> 74 da terra i piedi, e la regola mia  
> 75 rimasa è giù per danno delle carte.  

But to ascend it now none severeth his feet from earth, and my rule abideth there for wasting of the parchments.

- *diparte da terra i piedi* ‘parts from earth the feet’ → “severeth his feet from earth”: the object moves before the phrase of separation, a possessive replaces the article, and “severeth” renders the verb more strongly.
- *la regola mia* ‘the rule of mine’ → “my rule”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *rimasa è giù* ‘has remained down below’ → “abideth there”: a simple present renders the compound perfect, and “there” does not express the downward reference.
- *per danno delle carte* ‘to the detriment of the sheets’ → “for wasting of the parchments”: harm is expressed as wasting, and the sheets are named by their material.

> 76 Le mura, che soleano esser badia,  
> 77 fatte sono spelonche, e le cocolle  
> 78 sacca son piene di farina ria.  

The walls which were wont to be a house of prayer, have become dens, and the hoods are sacks full of foul meal.

- *esser badia* ‘to be an abbey’ → “to be a house of prayer”: a descriptive phrase replaces the institutional noun.
- *fatte sono spelonche* ‘made are caves’ → “have become dens”: the participle-first construction is rendered by a perfect of becoming, and “dens” replaces ‘caves’.
- *le cocolle* ‘the cowls’ → “the hoods”: the monastic garment is named by its hood.
- *sacca son* ‘sacks are’ → “are sacks”: the verb precedes the predicate noun.
- *farina ria* ‘meal bad’ → “foul meal”: the adjective moves before the noun and is rendered more strongly.

## Lines 79–111 — Monastic Decline and the Swift Ascent

> 79 Ma grave usura tanto non si tolle  
> 80 contra il piacer di Dio, quanto quel frutto  
> 81 che fa il cor dei monaci sì folle.  

But heavy usury is not exacted so counter to God’s pleasure as that fruit which doth so madden the monks’ hearts.

- *tanto non si tolle* ‘so much does not raise itself’ → “is not exacted so”: Wicksteed takes the verb in the sense of taking or levying usury, rather than of rising up against something; the construction becomes an English passive, and the degree word follows the verb.
- *il piacer di Dio* ‘the pleasure of God’ → “God’s pleasure”: the possessive comes before the noun.
- *fa il cor dei monaci sì folle* ‘makes the heart of the monks so mad’ → “doth so madden the monks’ hearts”: the verb and its complement become a single verb with an auxiliary; “so” now modifies the verb rather than the adjective. The collective singular becomes a plural, with the possessive before it.

> 82 Chè, quantunque la Chiesa guarda, tutto  
> 83 è della gente che per Dio domanda,  
> 84 non di parenti, nè d' altro più brutto.  

For what the Church holdeth in her keeping, all pertaineth to the folk that make petition in God’s name; not unto kindred, or other filthier thing.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *quantunque la Chiesa guarda* ‘whatever the Church guards’ → “what the Church holdeth in her keeping”: the indefinite relative becomes “what,” and the single verb becomes a phrase with a possessive.
- *è della gente* ‘is of the people’ → “pertaineth to the folk”: possession is expressed by a verb of belonging with “to.”
- *per Dio domanda* ‘for God’s sake asks’ → “make petition in God’s name”: the verb becomes a verb and noun, agreeing in the plural with “folk”; the phrase qualifying the request is recast and moved after it.
- *non di parenti* ‘not of relatives’ → “not unto kindred”: the preposition changes to fit “pertaineth,” and a collective noun replaces the plural.
- *nè d' altro più brutto* ‘nor of anything else more foul’ → “or other filthier thing”: after the negative, English uses “or” without repeating the preposition; “thing” is supplied, and the comparative precedes it.

> 85 La carne dei mortali è tanto blanda,  
> 86 che giù non basta buon cominciamento  
> 87 dal nascer della quercia al far la ghianda.  

The flesh of mortals is so blandishing that down on earth good beginning sufficeth not for all the space from the upspringing of the oak to acorn-bearing.

- *blanda* ‘yielding’ → “blandishing”: the English takes the adjective in the active sense of coaxing or beguiling.
- *giù* ‘below’ → “down on earth”: the place implied by the adverb is made explicit.
- *non basta buon cominciamento* ‘does not suffice a good beginning’ → “good beginning sufficeth not”: the subject moves before the verb and the negative after it.
- *dal nascer della quercia* ‘from the sprouting of the oak’ → “for all the space from the upspringing of the oak”: the extent of time implicit in the two endpoints is stated separately.
- *al far la ghianda* ‘to the making of the acorn’ → “to acorn-bearing”: the infinitive with its definite object becomes a compound verbal noun.

> 88 Pier cominciò senz' oro e senza argento,  
> 89 ed io con orazioni e con digiuno,  
> 90 e Francesco umilmente il suo convento.  

Peter began his gathering without gold or silver, and I mine with prayers and fast, and Francis his in humbleness.

- *cominciò* ‘began’ → “began his gathering”: the object, deferred in Italian until the third clause, is supplied in the first.
- *senz' oro e senza argento* ‘without gold and without silver’ → “without gold or silver”: one preposition governs both nouns, joined by “or” under the negative.
- *ed io* ‘and I’ → “and I mine”: the possessive pronoun supplies an object in the second clause as well.
- *con orazioni e con digiuno* ‘with prayers and with fasting’ → “with prayers and fast”: the preposition is not repeated, and “fast” names the practice.
- *umilmente il suo convento* ‘humbly his community’ → “his in humbleness”: the noun, anticipated by “gathering,” is not repeated; the possessive stands alone, and the adverb becomes a prepositional phrase after it.

> 91 E se guardi al principio di ciascuno,  
> 92 poscia riguardi là dov' è trascorso,  
> 93 tu vederai del bianco fatto bruno.  

And if thou scan the beginning of each one, and scan again whither it hath gone astray, thou shalt see the white turned dusky.

- *guardi al principio* ‘look at the beginning’ → “scan the beginning”: a transitive verb of scrutiny replaces the verb with its preposition.
- *poscia riguardi* ‘then look again’ → “and scan again”: coordination replaces the adverb of sequence, and the sense of the verbal prefix is expressed separately.
- *là dov' è trascorso* ‘there where it has passed on’ → “whither it hath gone astray”: a single relative adverb expresses the destination, and the verb is taken to imply deviation.
- *del bianco fatto bruno* ‘of the white made dark’ → “the white turned dusky”: “the white” becomes the direct object of “see,” without “of”; “turned” expresses a change of state.

> 94 Veramente Giordan volto retrorso  
> 95 più fu, e il mar fuggir, quando Dio volse,  
> 96 mirabile a veder, che qui il soccorso.”  

But Jordan back returning, and the sea fleeing when God willed, are more wondrous sights than were the rescue here.”

- *Veramente* ‘truly’ → “But”: the adverb is taken adversatively rather than as an affirmation.
- *volto retrorso* ‘turned backward’ → “back returning”: a past participle becomes a present one, and the direction precedes it.
- *più fu … mirabile a veder* ‘more was … marvellous to see’ → “are more wondrous sights”: the interrupted predicate is brought together after both subjects; the singular past becomes a plural present, and the infinitive becomes a plural noun modified by the comparative.
- *e il mar fuggir* ‘and the sea to flee’ → “and the sea fleeing”: the infinitive becomes a participle parallel to “returning.”
- *quando Dio volse* ‘when God willed it’ → “when God willed”: the object of the divine willing is left understood.
- *che qui il soccorso* ‘than here the help’ → “than were the rescue here”: English supplies a hypothetical verb and places the adverb after the noun.

> 97 Così mi disse, ed indi si ricolse  
> 98 al suo collegio, e il collegio si strinse;  
> 99 poi, come turbo, tutto in su s' accolse.  

So spake he to me, and then gathered him to his assembly; and the assembly drew close; then like a whirlwind was all gathered upward.

- *mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “spake he to me”: the verb of saying becomes one of speaking, and the indirect object follows it.
- *si ricolse al suo collegio* ‘he gathered himself back to his company’ → “gathered him to his assembly”: “him” serves reflexively, without a separate word for the return.
- *il collegio si strinse* ‘the company tightened itself’ → “the assembly drew close”: a verb and adverb convey the closing together without a reflexive object.
- *tutto in su s' accolse* ‘the whole gathered itself upward’ → “was all gathered upward”: the reflexive becomes passive, “all” follows the auxiliary, and the direction comes last.

> 100 La dolce donna retro a lor mi pinse  
> 101 con un sol cenno su per quella scala,  
> 102 sì sua virtù la mia natura vinse;  

The sweet Lady thrust me after them, only with a sign, up by that ladder, so did her power overcome my nature;

- *retro a lor mi pinse* ‘after them me she pushed’ → “thrust me after them”: verb, object, and direction are reordered.
- *con un sol cenno* ‘with a single sign’ → “only with a sign”: the restriction is placed on the means used rather than on the number of signs.
- *su per quella scala* ‘up along that ladder’ → “up by that ladder”: “by” presents the ladder as the means of ascent.
- *sì sua virtù la mia natura vinse* ‘so her power my nature overcame’ → “so did her power overcome my nature”: after “so,” English inverts with an auxiliary and places the object after the main verb.

> 103 nè mai quaggiù, dove si monta e cala  
> 104 naturalmente, fu sì ratto moto,  
> 105 ch' agguagliar si potesse alla mia ala.  

nor ever here below, where we mount and descend by nature’s law, was so swift motion as might compare unto my wing.

- *si monta e cala* ‘one goes up and down’ → “we mount and descend”: the impersonal construction becomes a first-person plural.
- *naturalmente* ‘naturally’ → “by nature’s law”: the adverb becomes a prepositional phrase specifying a law.
- *ch' agguagliar si potesse alla mia ala* ‘that it could equal itself to my wing’ → “as might compare unto my wing”: “so … as” replaces the Italian result construction; an intransitive comparison replaces the reflexive, and the modal precedes the infinitive.

> 106 S' io torni mai, lettore, a quel devoto  
> 107 trionfo, per lo quale io piango spesso  
> 108 le mie peccata, e il petto mi percoto,  

O reader, by my hopes of turning back to that devout triumph, for the which I many a time bewail my sins, and smite upon my breast,

- *S' io torni mai* ‘if ever I return’ → “by my hopes of turning back”: the conditional with a subjunctive, used as an oath, becomes an explicit appeal to hope with a gerund; ‘ever’ has no separate equivalent.
- *lettore* ‘reader’ → “O reader”: the address moves to the front and takes a vocative particle.
- *piango spesso le mie peccata* ‘I weep for my sins often’ → “I many a time bewail my sins”: the adverb of frequency becomes a phrase before the verb, and “bewail” takes the sins as its direct object.
- *il petto mi percoto* ‘the breast I strike for myself’ → “smite upon my breast”: the reflexive indicating the person affected becomes a possessive, and the direct object becomes a phrase with “upon” after the verb.

> 109 tu non avresti in tanto tratto e messo  
> 110 nel foco il dito, in quanto io vidi il segno  
> 111 che segue il Tauro, e fui dentro da esso.  

thou hadst not drawn back and plunged thy finger in the flame in so short space as that wherein I saw the sign that followeth the Bull, and was within it.

- *tu non avresti* ‘thou wouldst not have’ → “thou hadst not”: an English pluperfect with hypothetical force renders the Italian past conditional.
- *in tanto … in quanto* ‘in as much [time] … as in’ → “in so short space as that wherein”: the first member of the comparison moves after the two actions; the duration, its shortness, and the relative antecedent are made explicit.
- *tratto e messo* ‘drawn and put’ → “drawn back and plunged”: the direction of the first action is specified, and the second verb is more forceful.
- *nel foco il dito* ‘in the fire the finger’ → “thy finger in the flame”: the object moves ahead of the place phrase, the definite article becomes a possessive, and “flame” replaces ‘fire.’
- *dentro da esso* ‘inside of it’ → “within it”: one English preposition does the work of the Italian locution.

## Lines 112–144 — The Stars and the Downward Gaze

> 112 O gloriose stelle, o lume pregno  
> 113 di gran virtù, dal quale io riconosco  
> 114 tutto, qual che si sia, lo mio ingegno,  

O stars of glory, O light impregnated with mighty power, from which I recognise all, whatsoe’er it be, my genius;

- *gloriose stelle* ‘glorious stars’ → “stars of glory”: the adjective before the noun becomes an *of*-phrase after it.
- *pregno di gran virtù* ‘pregnant with great virtue’ → “impregnated with mighty power”: a participle replaces the adjective; *virtù* is rendered in the sense of potency, with “mighty” for *gran*.
- *qual che si sia* ‘of whatever sort it may be’ → “whatsoe’er it be”: the indefinite expression of quality is compressed into one pronoun; *si* has no separate equivalent.

> 115 con voi nasceva e s' ascondeva vosco  
> 116 quegli ch' è padre d' ogni mortal vita,  
> 117 quand' io senti' da prima l' aer Tosco;  

with you was rising, and hiding him with you, he who is father of each mortal life, when I first felt the air of Tuscany;

- *nasceva e s' ascondeva vosco* ‘was born and hid himself with you’ → “was rising, and hiding him with you”: birth is rendered as rising; the two imperfect verbs become participles sharing “was”, and “him” does duty for the reflexive.
- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *quand' io senti' da prima* ‘when I felt for the first time’ → “when I first felt”: the adverbial phrase becomes one adverb placed before the verb.
- *l' aer Tosco* ‘the Tuscan air’ → “the air of Tuscany”: the adjective becomes an *of*-phrase.

> 118 e poi, quando mi fu grazia largita  
> 119 d' entrar nell' alta rota che vi gira,  
> 120 la vostra region mi fu sortita.  

and then when grace was bestowed on me to enter the lofty wheel that rolleth you, your region was assigned to me.

- *mi fu grazia largita* ‘to me was grace bestowed’ → “grace was bestowed on me”: the subject comes first, the two parts of the passive stand together, and the recipient follows them.
- *d' entrar nell' alta rota* ‘of entering into the lofty wheel’ → “to enter the lofty wheel”: English uses an infinitive without a linking *of* and makes the wheel the direct object of “enter”.
- *vi gira* ‘you turns’ → “rolleth you”: the verb is rendered by “rolleth”, with its object after it.
- *mi fu sortita* ‘to me was allotted’ → “was assigned to me”: the recipient moves after the passive, and “assigned” does not retain the suggestion of allotment by lot.

> 121 A voi devotamente ora sospira  
> 122 l' anima mia per acquistar virtute  
> 123 al passo forte, che a sè la tira.  

To you devoutly now my soul doth breathe, to gain the power for the hard passage that doth draw her to it.

- *sospira l' anima mia* ‘sighs the soul of mine’ → “my soul doth breathe”: the subject moves before the verb and the possessive before the noun; “doth breathe” replaces the single verb of sighing.
- *per acquistar virtute* ‘in order to acquire power’ → “to gain the power”: the bare English infinitive expresses purpose, “gain” replaces ‘acquire’, and the definite article is supplied.
- *al passo forte* ‘at the passage hard’ → “for the hard passage”: the preposition marks what the power is needed for, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *a sè la tira* ‘to itself her draws’ → “doth draw her to it”: the verb moves first, with auxiliary “doth”; its object and then its directional phrase follow, and a plain pronoun replaces the reflexive.

> 124 “Tu sei sì presso all' ultima salute,  
> 125 cominciò Beatrice, che tu dei  
> 126 aver le luci tue chiare ed acute.  

“Thou art so nigh to the supreme weal,” began Beatrice, “that thou shouldst have thine eyes clear and keen.

- *ultima salute* ‘final salvation’ → “supreme weal”: finality is expressed as supremacy, and salvation as well-being.
- *dei aver* ‘must have’ → “shouldst have”: the modal is rendered as expectation rather than necessity.
- *le luci tue* ‘the lights of thine’ → “thine eyes”: the figurative noun is rendered by what it designates, and the possessive comes first.

> 127 E però, prima che tu più t' inlei,  
> 128 rimira in giù, e vedi quanto mondo  
> 129 sotto li piedi già esser ti fei;  

And therefore, ere thou further wend thereinto, look down and see how great a universe I have already put beneath thy feet;

- *prima che tu più t' inlei* ‘before thou further enter into her’ → “ere thou further wend thereinto”: the pronominal verb formed on *lei* is unpacked into a verb of motion and a directional adverb; the feminine reference becomes “thereinto”.
- *rimira in giù* ‘look again downwards’ → “look down”: the renewed or returning look suggested by the verb is not separately expressed.
- *quanto mondo* ‘how much world’ → “how great a universe”: quantity is expressed as magnitude, with an indefinite article, and ‘world’ is rendered as “universe”.
- *sotto li piedi già esser ti fei* ‘under the feet already to be for thee I made’ → “I have already put beneath thy feet”: the causative with an infinitive becomes a single verb of placement in the present perfect instead of the simple past; the locative phrase moves to the end, and *ti* becomes a possessive modifying “feet”.

> 130 sì che il tuo cor, quantunque può, giocondo  
> 131 s' appresenti alla turba trionfante,  
> 132 che lieta vien per questo etera tondo.”  

so that thy heart, rejoicing to its utmost, may be presented to the throng triumphant which cometh glad through this sphered ether.”

- *quantunque può, giocondo* ‘as much as it can, joyful’ → “rejoicing to its utmost”: the adjective becomes a participle placed first, and a phrase of degree replaces the finite clause.
- *s' appresenti* ‘may present itself’ → “may be presented”: the pronominal construction becomes passive.
- *lieta vien* ‘glad comes’ → “cometh glad”: the predicate adjective follows the verb.
- *questo etera tondo* ‘this ether round’ → “this sphered ether”: “sphered” replaces ‘round’ and comes before the noun.

> 133 Col viso ritornai per tutte e quante  
> 134 le sette spere, e vidi questo globo  
> 135 tal ch' io sorrisi del suo vil sembiante;  

With my sight I turned back through all and every of the seven spheres, and saw this globe such that I smiled at its sorry semblance;

- *Col viso* ‘with the sight’ → “With my sight”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *ritornai* ‘I returned’ → “I turned back”: a phrasal verb renders the single verb.
- *vidi* ‘I saw’ → “saw”: the English verb shares the subject already expressed for “turned back”.
- *sorrisi del suo vil sembiante* ‘I smiled at its base appearance’ → “I smiled at its sorry semblance”: English idiom uses “at” for *di*; “sorry” renders the judgment of small worth in *vil*.

> 136 e quel consiglio per migliore approbo  
> 137 che l' ha per meno; e chi ad altro pensa  
> 138 chiamar si può veracemente probo.  

and that counsel I approve as best which holdeth it for least; and he whose thoughts are turned elsewhither may be called truly upright.

- *per migliore approbo* ‘as better I approve’ → “I approve as best”: the verb precedes its complement, and the comparative becomes a superlative.
- *l' ha per meno* ‘has it for less’ → “holdeth it for least”: ‘has’ is interpreted as reckoning or esteeming, the object follows the verb, and “least” replaces the comparative.
- *chi ad altro pensa* ‘whoever of something else thinks’ → “he whose thoughts are turned elsewhither”: the free relative becomes an antecedent with a possessive relative clause; the active verb becomes a noun with a passive predicate, and the object of thought becomes a direction.
- *chiamar si può* ‘called one can be’ → “may be called”: the pronominal passive is made explicit with “be”, and the modal precedes the infinitive.

> 139 Vidi la figlia di Latona incensa  
> 140 senza quell' ombra, che mi fu cagione  
> 141 per che già la credetti rara e densa.  

I saw the daughter of Latona kindled without that shade which erst gave me cause to deem her rare and dense.

- *mi fu cagione per che già la credetti* ‘to me was cause why formerly I believed her’ → “erst gave me cause to deem her”: being a cause becomes giving cause, with the recipient after the verb; an infinitive replaces the finite clause introduced by *per che*, its object follows it, and “erst” now modifies “gave” rather than ‘believed’.

> 142 L' aspetto del tuo nato, Iperione,  
> 143 quivi sostenni, e vidi com' si move  
> 144 circa e vicino a lui Maia e Dione.  

The aspect of thy son, Hyperion, I there endured, and saw how Maia and Dione move about and near him.

- *nato* ‘one born’ → “son”: the substantivized participle becomes a noun of kinship.
- *e vidi* ‘and I saw’ → “and saw”: the coordinated English verbs share one expressed subject.
- *si move circa e vicino a lui Maia e Dione* ‘moves about and near to him Maia and Dione’ → “Maia and Dione move about and near him”: the compound subject moves before the verb; the singular pronominal verb becomes a plain plural intransitive, and “near” takes “him” without a separate preposition.

## Lines 145–154 — The Seven Planets and the Earth

> 145 Quindi m' apparve il temperar di Giove  
> 146 tra il padre e il figlio; e quivi mi fu chiaro  
> 147 il variar che fanno di lor dove.  

Next appeared to me the tempering of Jove between his father and his son; and there was clear to me the varying they make in their position.

- *Quindi* ‘thence’ → “Next”: the English marks succession rather than a point of departure.
- *m’apparve* ‘to me appeared’ → “appeared to me”: the pronoun follows the verb.
- *il temperar* ‘the act of tempering’ → “the tempering”: the Italian uses an infinitive as a noun, the English an *-ing* form.
- *tra il padre e il figlio* ‘between the father and the son’ → “between his father and his son”: the possessives replace the definite articles.
- *mi fu chiaro* ‘to me was clear’ → “was clear to me”: the pronoun moves after the adjective.
- *il variar* ‘the act of varying’ → “the varying”: the substantivized infinitive again becomes an *-ing* form.
- *che fanno* ‘that they make’ → “they make”: the object relative pronoun is left unexpressed.
- *di lor dove* ‘of their where’ → “in their position”: the preposition changes, and *dove*, an adverb used as a noun, becomes an ordinary noun.

> 148 E tutti e sette mi si dimostraro  
> 149 quanto son grandi, e quanto son veloci,  
> 150 e come sono in distante riparo.  

And all the seven were displayed to me, how great they are and swift, and how distant each from other in repair.

- *tutti e sette* ‘all seven’ → “all the seven”: the English uses a definite article where the Italian idiom links *tutti* and the numeral with *e*.
- *mi si dimostraro* ‘to me showed themselves’ → “were displayed to me”: the pronominal verb becomes a passive, and the recipient follows the verb.
- *quanto son grandi* ‘how they are great’ → “how great they are”: the adjective moves forward to follow the word of degree.
- *e quanto son veloci* ‘and how they are swift’ → “and swift”: the repeated word of degree and verb are left understood.
- *e come sono in distante riparo* ‘and how they are in distant abode’ → “and how distant each from other in repair”: the verb is left understood; the adjective is detached from its noun to follow “how”, and “each from other” makes the relation explicit. “repair” is used in the sense of a place to which one repairs.

> 151 L' aiuola che ci fa tanto feroci,  
> 152 volgendom' io con gli eterni Gemelli,  
> 153 tutta m' apparve dai colli alle foci:  

The thrashing-floor which maketh us wax so fierce, as I rolled with the eternal Twins, was all revealed to me from ridge to river-mouth;

- *L’aiuola* ‘the little plot’ → “The thrashing-floor”: the English takes the word in the sense of *aia*, a thrashing-floor, without marking the diminutive.
- *ci fa tanto feroci* ‘us makes so fierce’ → “maketh us wax so fierce”: the object follows the causative verb, and “wax” supplies a verb of becoming before the adjective.
- *volgendom’ io* ‘turning myself, I’ → “as I rolled”: the reflexive gerund becomes a finite temporal clause, with the subject before an intransitive verb.
- *tutta m’apparve* ‘all of it appeared to me’ → “was all revealed to me”: appearing becomes a passive expression of disclosure, with “all” placed within the verb phrase.
- *dai colli alle foci* ‘from the hills to the mouths’ → “from ridge to river-mouth”: the two definite plurals become bare singulars; “ridge” replaces ‘hills’, and the compound specifies the kind of mouth.

> 154 poscia rivolsi gli occhi agli occhi belli.  

then to the beauteous eyes mine eyes again I turned.

- *rivolsi* ‘I turned back’ → “again I turned”: the sense of turning back is expressed by a separate adverb, and the verb moves to the end.
- *gli occhi* ‘the eyes’ → “mine eyes”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *agli occhi belli* ‘to the eyes beautiful’ → “to the beauteous eyes”: the adjective precedes its noun, and the whole phrase moves ahead of the direct object.

## Summary — Italian Order and English Idiom

Wicksteed often moves an object or complement from before the verb to after it: *sopra me rilusse* becomes “shone o’er me”. Yet he preserves Italian inversions where English can bear them, as in “Next appeared to me the tempering of Jove”, with the subject still following the verb. Two routine adjustments generally go unmarked in the comparison: English supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the person in the verb ending, and omits the article before a possessive, as in *il tuo alto disio*, “thy high desire”.

A single Italian verb often becomes an English phrase: *ricorre* becomes “hath recourse”, and *ritrassi* is divided into “drew” and “back”, with the object between them. The reverse happens when *fa il cor dei monaci sì folle* becomes “doth so madden the monks’ hearts”: “madden” absorbs both the causative and the adjective. Pronominal verbs frequently give way to passives, as when *mi si dimostraro* becomes “were displayed to me”. Words can also disappear when English syntax does their work: in *se inteso avessi*, “hadst thou understood”, inversion alone marks the condition.

Some departures sharpen or narrow the sense: *buon zelo* becomes “righteous zeal”, while *badia*, ‘abbey’, becomes the descriptive “house of prayer”. Elsewhere Wicksteed makes an implication explicit, as when *giù*, ‘below’, becomes “down on earth”. Yet he stays close not only to individual images but also to patterns of phrasing. He keeps the repeated *qui* in “Here is Maccarius, here is Romoaldus, here are my brothers”, and in the closing phrase he preserves the repetition of *occhi* even as he reverses the order of the two pairs of eyes.
