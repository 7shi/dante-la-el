# Paradiso 6

## Lines 1–12 — The Eagle Comes to Justinian

> 1 “Poscia che Costantin l' aquila volse  
> 2 contra il corso del ciel, ch' ella seguio  
> 3 dietro all' antico, che Lavina tolse,  

“After Constantine had wheeled back the eagle, counter to the course of heaven which it had followed in train of the ancient wight who took Lavinia,

- *Poscia che* ‘after that’ → “After”: one conjunction does the work of the two Italian words.
- *l' aquila volse* ‘the eagle turned’ → “had wheeled back the eagle”: the object moves after the verb, the simple past becomes a past perfect, and “wheeled back” makes the reversal explicit.
- *ch' ella seguio* ‘which she followed’ → “which it had followed”: the feminine pronoun becomes neuter, and the simple past becomes a past perfect.
- *dietro all' antico* ‘behind the ancient one’ → “in train of the ancient wight”: a phrase of attendance replaces the preposition of position, and the noun “wight” is supplied for the adjective used as a noun.
- *che Lavina tolse* ‘who Lavinia took’ → “who took Lavinia”: the object moves after the verb.

> 4 cento e cent' anni e più l' uccel di Dio  
> 5 nell' estremo d' Europa si ritenne,  
> 6 vicino ai monti de' quai prima uscio;  

a hundred and a hundred years and more the bird of God abode on Europe’s limit, neighbouring the mountains whence he first had issued;

- *nell' estremo d' Europa si ritenne* ‘at the extremity of Europe stayed itself’ → “abode on Europe’s limit”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb placed before the phrase of place; “on” replaces ‘at’, and the possessive precedes “limit” instead of following it in an ‘of’ phrase.
- *vicino ai monti* ‘near to the mountains’ → “neighbouring the mountains”: the expression of proximity becomes a participle taking a direct object.
- *de' quai prima uscio* ‘from which first issued’ → “whence he first had issued”: a relative adverb replaces preposition and pronoun, and the simple past becomes a past perfect.

> 7 e sotto l' ombra delle sacre penne  
> 8 governò il mondo lì di mano in mano,  
> 9 e sì cangiando in su la mia pervenne.  

and there he governed the world beneath the shadow of his sacred wings from hand to hand till by succeeding change he came to mine.

- *sotto l' ombra delle sacre penne* ‘beneath the shadow of the sacred feathers’ → “beneath the shadow of his sacred wings”: the phrase moves from before the verb to after its object; the possessive replaces the article, and “wings” names the whole rather than the feathers.
- *governò il mondo lì* ‘governed the world there’ → “there he governed the world”: the adverb moves from after the object to before the subject and verb.
- *di mano in mano* ‘from hand into hand’ → “from hand to hand”: English uses “to” rather than ‘into’ for the second preposition.
- *e sì cangiando* ‘and thus changing’ → “till by succeeding change”: coordination becomes a clause marking an endpoint; the gerund becomes a noun governed by “by”, and “succeeding” conveys the repeated passage just described rather than rendering *sì* as ‘thus’.
- *in su la mia pervenne* ‘upon mine arrived’ → “he came to mine”: “came to” replaces ‘arrived upon’, and the verb precedes its destination.

> 10 Cesare fui, e son Giustiniano,  

Caesar I was, and am Justinian,

- Literal.

> 11 che, per voler del primo amor ch' io sento,  
> 12 d' entro le leggi trassi il troppo e il vano;  

who, by will of the Primal Love which now I feel, withdrew from out the Laws excess and inefficiency;

- *del primo amor* ‘of the first love’ → “of the Primal Love”: “Primal” gives ‘first’ the sense of original or primary.
- *ch' io sento* ‘which I feel’ → “which now I feel”: “now” makes the present time explicit.
- *d' entro le leggi trassi* ‘from within the laws I drew’ → “withdrew from out the Laws”: the verb moves before the phrase of source, and “withdrew from out” puts the emphasis on removal rather than on drawing something from within.
- *il troppo e il vano* ‘the excessive and the vain’ → “excess and inefficiency”: the adjectives used as nouns become abstract nouns without articles; “inefficiency” interprets what is vain or ineffectual as a lack of efficacy.

## Lines 13–45 — Justinian’s Faith, His Work, and the Imperial Standard

> 13 e prima ch' io all' opra fossi attento,  
> 14 una natura in Cristo esser, non piùe,  
> 15 credeva, e di tal fede era contento;  

and ere I fixed my mind upon the work, one nature, and no more, I held to be in Christ, and with such faith was I content;

- *io all' opra fossi attento* ‘I to the work were attentive’ → “I fixed my mind upon the work”: the imperfect subjunctive after the conjunction of time becomes a simple past; the description of a state becomes an act, with “my mind” supplied as its object and the phrase about the work moved to the end.
- *una natura in Cristo esser, non piùe, credeva* ‘one nature in Christ to be, no more, I believed’ → “one nature, and no more, I held to be in Christ”: “and” is supplied, the qualification is brought next to “one nature”, and the verb of belief, rendered by “held”, now precedes the infinitive; “in Christ” follows it.
- *di tal fede era contento* ‘of such faith I was content’ → “with such faith was I content”: the adjective takes “with” rather than ‘of’, and the subject follows “was”.

> 16 ma il benedetto Agapito, che fue  
> 17 sommo pastore, alla fede sincera  
> 18 mi dirizzò con le parole sue.  

but the blessed Agapetus, who was high pastor, to the faith without alloy directed me by his discourse.

- *alla fede sincera* ‘to the faith pure’ → “to the faith without alloy”: a prepositional phrase expressing freedom from admixture replaces the adjective.
- *mi dirizzò* ‘me he directed’ → “directed me”: the object follows the verb.
- *con le parole sue* ‘with the words of his’ → “by his discourse”: “by” marks the means; the plural ‘words’ becomes the singular “discourse”, with the possessive before it.

> 19 Io gli credetti, e ciò che in sua fede era  
> 20 veggio ora chiaro, sì come tu vedi  
> 21 ogni contraddizion è falsa e vera.  

Him I believed, and now the content of his faith I see as clear as thou dost see that every contradiction is both false and true.

- *Io gli credetti* ‘I believed him’ → “Him I believed”: the pronoun, an indirect object in Italian, becomes a direct object and is placed first.
- *ciò che in sua fede era veggio ora chiaro* ‘that which in his faith was I see now clearly’ → “now the content of his faith I see as clear”: the relative clause becomes a noun phrase, with the relation expressed by “of” rather than ‘in’; “now” moves from after the verb to before the object. “Clear” retains the form of the Italian adjective used adverbially.
- *sì come tu vedi* ‘just as thou seest’ → “as thou dost see”: the comparison is distributed over two occurrences of “as”, the first before “clear”; the simple present is expanded with “dost”.
- *ogni contraddizion è falsa e vera* ‘every contradiction is false and true’ → “that every contradiction is both false and true”: “that” explicitly introduces the clause dependent on “see”, and “both” is supplied to pair the adjectives.

> 22 Tosto che con la Chiesa mossi i piedi,  
> 23 a Dio per grazia piacque d' inspirarmi  
> 24 l' alto lavoro, e tutto a lui mi diedi;  

So soon as with the Church I moved my feet, God of his grace it pleased to inspire me with the high task, and all to it I gave me;

- *mossi i piedi* ‘I moved the feet’ → “I moved my feet”: English uses the possessive for the body part.
- *a Dio per grazia piacque* ‘to God by grace it pleased’ → “God of his grace it pleased”: “God” stands without the Italian dative preposition; “of his grace” changes the preposition and makes the possessor explicit.
- *d' inspirarmi l' alto lavoro* ‘to inspire in me the high work’ → “to inspire me with the high task”: the work, the direct object in Italian, becomes the complement of “with”; its recipient becomes the direct object, and ‘work’ is rendered by “task”.
- *tutto a lui mi diedi* ‘wholly to it myself I gave’ → “all to it I gave me”: “all” conveys the totality of the self given; the reflexive pronoun follows the verb as “me”.

> 25 ed al mio Bellisar commendai l' armi,  
> 26 cui la destra del ciel fu sì congiunta,  
> 27 che segno fu ch' io dovessi posarmi.  

and to my Belisarius committed arms; to whom heaven’s right-hand was so conjoined it was a signal I should rest me from them.

- *commendai l' armi* ‘I entrusted the arms’ → “committed arms”: “committed” renders the act of entrusting; the subject is carried over from the preceding clause and the definite article is dropped.
- *la destra del ciel* ‘the right hand of heaven’ → “heaven’s right-hand”: the dependent noun becomes a possessive placed first.
- *fu sì congiunta* ‘was joined to such a degree’ → “was so conjoined”: “so” gives the degree of union, but the result that follows is left without an introductory ‘that’.
- *che segno fu* ‘that a sign it was’ → “it was a signal”: the result clause follows directly, and the predicate noun, rendered by “a signal”, comes after the verb.
- *ch' io dovessi posarmi* ‘that I ought to rest myself’ → “I should rest me from them”: the clause is introduced without ‘that’; “should” renders the imperfect subjunctive of obligation, and “from them” supplies a complement referring back to “arms”.

> 28 Or qui alla question prima s' appunta  
> 29 la mia risposta; ma sua condizione  
> 30 mi stringe a seguitare alcuna giunta.  

Now here already is my answer’s close to thy first question; but its conditions force me to go on to some addition.

- *qui alla question prima s' appunta la mia risposta* ‘here at the question first comes to its point my answer’ → “here already is my answer’s close to thy first question”: “already” and “thy” are supplied. The reflexive verb of reaching an endpoint becomes “is” with the noun “close”; the Italian subject becomes a possessive modifying that noun. The question phrase moves to the end and its adjective comes before the noun.
- *sua condizione* ‘its condition’ → “its conditions”: the singular becomes plural.
- *mi stringe* ‘me it constrains’ → “force me”: the object follows the verb, which agrees with the new plural subject.
- *a seguitare alcuna giunta* ‘to follow up with some addition’ → “to go on to some addition”: “go on” expresses the continuation, with “to” introducing the addition.

> 31 Perchè tu veggi con quanta ragione  
> 32 si move contra il sacrosanto segno,  
> 33 e chi 'l s' appropria, e chi a lui s' oppone,  

That thou mayst see with how good right against the sacred standard doth proceed both he who doth annex it to himself and he who doth oppose him to it,

- *Perchè tu veggi* ‘in order that thou see’ → “That thou mayst see”: “mayst” expresses the subjunctive of purpose.
- *con quanta ragione* ‘with how much right’ → “with how good right”: a question of degree is put in terms of quality.
- *si move contra il sacrosanto segno* ‘moves against the most holy sign’ → “against the sacred standard doth proceed”: the prepositional phrase moves before the verb; the pronominal verb of motion becomes “doth proceed”. “Sacred” does not reproduce the full intensity of the adjective, and “standard” specifies the sense of ‘sign’.
- *e chi 'l s' appropria, e chi a lui s' oppone* ‘both whoever appropriates it to himself and whoever to it opposes himself’ → “both he who doth annex it to himself and he who doth oppose him to it”: each indefinite relative is expanded to “he who”; both verbs take “doth”, and ‘appropriates’ becomes “annex”. In the second relative, the complement moves to the end and “him” has reflexive force.

> 34 vedi quanta virtù l' ha fatto degno  
> 35 di reverenza, e cominciò dall'ora  
> 36 che Pallante morì per dargli regno.  

see how great virtue hath made it worthy of reverence, beginning from the hour when Pallas died to give it sway.

- *quanta virtù* ‘how much virtue’ → “how great virtue”: degree is expressed by “great” rather than by a word of quantity.
- *l' ha fatto degno* ‘it hath made worthy’ → “hath made it worthy”: the object moves from before the auxiliary to after the participle.
- *e cominciò dall'ora* ‘and it began from the hour’ → “beginning from the hour”: a participle replaces the coordinated finite past verb, and the conjunction disappears.
- *che* ‘that’ → “when”: the relative is made explicitly temporal.
- *per dargli regno* ‘in order to give it dominion’ → “to give it sway”: the bare English infinitive conveys purpose, the recipient pronoun stands separately after “give”, and “sway” renders ‘dominion’.

> 37 Tu sai che fece in Alba sua dimora  
> 38 per trecent' anni ed oltre, infino al fine  
> 39 che i tre ai tre pugnar per lui ancora.  

Thou knowest that it made its sojourn in Alba for three hundred years and more, until the close, when three with three yet fought for it.

- *in Alba sua dimora* ‘in Alba its dwelling’ → “its sojourn in Alba”: “sojourn” renders the stay, and the place phrase follows rather than precedes the object.
- *per trecent' anni ed oltre* ‘for three hundred years and beyond’ → “for three hundred years and more”: “more” expresses the excess in years.
- *infino al fine che* ‘until the end that’ → “until the close, when”: “close” renders ‘end’, and the relative becomes an explicit expression of time.
- *i tre ai tre pugnar per lui ancora* ‘the three against the three fought for it again’ → “three with three yet fought for it”: both articles disappear; “with” expresses the relation between opponents. “Yet” takes the adverb in the sense of ‘still’ rather than ‘again’, and moves it before the verb.

> 40 E sai ch' ei fe' dal mal delle Sabine  
> 41 al dolor di Lucrezia in sette regi,  
> 42 vincendo intorno le genti vicine.  

And thou knowest what it wrought from the Sabine women’s wrong unto Lucretia’s woe, through seven kings, conquering around the neighbour folk.

- *ch' ei fe'* ‘what it did’ → “what it wrought”: “wrought” is a more marked verb than ‘did’.
- *dal mal delle Sabine* ‘from the harm of the Sabine women’ → “from the Sabine women’s wrong”: “wrong” specifies the kind of harm; the feminine plural is expressed by “women”, and the dependent noun phrase becomes a preceding possessive.
- *al dolor di Lucrezia* ‘to the grief of Lucretia’ → “unto Lucretia’s woe”: “unto” and “woe” render the preposition and noun, and the possessor comes first.
- *in sette regi* ‘in seven kings’ → “through seven kings”: “through” replaces ‘in’.
- *le genti vicine* ‘the peoples neighbouring’ → “the neighbour folk”: the plural noun becomes a collective, and the following adjective becomes an attributive noun placed first.

> 43 Sai quel ch' ei fe', portato dagli egregi  
> 44 Romani incontro a Brenno, incontro a Pirro,  
> 45 e contra gli altri principi e collegi:  

Thou knowest what it wrought, borne by the chosen Romans against Brennus, against Pyrrhus and against the rest, princes and governments;

- *quel ch' ei fe'* ‘that which it did’ → “what it wrought”: “what” combines antecedent and relative, and “wrought” again renders the general verb ‘did’.
- *egregi* ‘distinguished’ → “chosen”: distinction is expressed in terms of selection.
- *incontro a Brenno, incontro a Pirro* ‘to meet Brennus, to meet Pyrrhus’ → “against Brennus, against Pyrrhus”: the preposition of encounter is rendered in its hostile sense, by the same English preposition as the ensuing *contra*.
- *gli altri principi e collegi* ‘the other princes and corporate bodies’ → “the rest, princes and governments”: the adjective becomes the independent noun phrase “the rest”, with the two nouns in apposition; “governments” gives the collective bodies a specifically political sense.

## Lines 46–78 — The Roman Eagle From Torquatus to Cleopatra

> 46 onde Torquato, e Quinzio che dal cirro  
> 47 negletto fu nomato, i Deci, e' Fabi  
> 48 ebber la fama che volontier mirro.  

whence Torquatus and Quinctius, named from his neglected locks, the Decii and the Fabii, drew the fame which I rejoice in thus embalming.

- *che dal cirro negletto fu nomato* ‘who from the lock neglected was named’ → “named from his neglected locks”: the passive relative clause becomes a participial phrase, with “named” first; the single lock becomes plural, the article becomes a possessive, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *ebber* ‘had’ → “drew”: possession becomes acquisition from the source indicated by “whence”.
- *volontier mirro* ‘gladly I embalm’ → “I rejoice in thus embalming”: the adverb becomes the main verb, the finite verb becomes a gerund, and “thus” is supplied.

> 49 Esso atterrò l'orgoglio degli Arabi,  
> 50 che di retro ad Annibale passaro  
> 51 l' alpestre rocce, di che, Po, tu labi.  

It cast down the pride of the Arabs that followed Hannibal across the Alpine rocks, whence, Po, thou glidest.

- *di retro ad Annibale passaro l' alpestre rocce* ‘behind Hannibal crossed the Alpine rocks’ → “followed Hannibal across the Alpine rocks”: the phrase indicating whom they went behind becomes the main verb and its object; the crossing is expressed by a preposition.

> 52 Sott' esso giovinetti trionfaro  
> 53 Scipione e Pompeo, ed a quel colle,  
> 54 sotto il qual tu nascesti, parve amaro.  

Under it, Scipio and Pompey triumphed, yet in their youth, and bitter did it seem unto those hills beneath which thou wast born.

- *giovinetti trionfaro Scipione e Pompeo* ‘as youths triumphed Scipio and Pompey’ → “Scipio and Pompey triumphed, yet in their youth”: the names move before the verb; the plural term describing the two men becomes a phrase of time after the verb, with “yet” in the sense of ‘still’.
- *a quel colle, sotto il qual tu nascesti, parve amaro* ‘to that hill, beneath which thou wast born, it seemed bitter’ → “bitter did it seem unto those hills beneath which thou wast born”: the adjective comes first, with auxiliary “did” and inversion; the phrase governed by ‘seemed’ moves to the end, and one hill becomes several.

> 55 Poi, presso al tempo che tutto il ciel volle  
> 56 ridur lo mondo a suo modo sereno,  
> 57 Cesare, per voler di Roma, il tolle:  

Then, nigh the time when all heaven willed to bring the world to its own serene mood, Cæsar, at Rome’s behest, laid hold of it;

- *tutto il ciel* ‘all the heaven’ → “all heaven”: English uses “heaven” without the article.
- *ridur* ‘to bring back’ → “to bring”: the sense of return is not explicit.
- *a suo modo sereno* ‘to its manner serene’ → “to its own serene mood”: ‘manner’ becomes a state of feeling, “own” emphasizes the possessive, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *per voler di Roma* ‘by the will of Rome’ → “at Rome’s behest”: will is expressed as a bidding, with a different preposition and a preceding possessive.
- *il tolle* ‘it he takes up’ → “laid hold of it”: the historical present becomes a past tense; taking up becomes grasping, with the object after the verbal phrase.

> 58 e quel che fe'da Varo infino al Reno,  
> 59 Isara vide ed Era e vide Senna,  
> 60 ed ogni valle onde Rodano è pieno.  

and what it wrought from Var to Rhine knoweth Isère and Arar, knoweth Seine and every valley by which Rhone is filled.

- *da Varo infino al Reno* ‘from Var as far as the Rhine’ → “from Var to Rhine”: “to” does not separately express the emphasis on the limit, and the article before the second river is dropped.
- *Isara vide ed Era e vide Senna* ‘Isère saw and Arar and saw Seine’ → “knoweth Isère and Arar, knoweth Seine”: past seeing becomes present knowledge; the first verb moves before both its subjects, and the conjunction before the repeated verb is dropped.
- *onde Rodano è pieno* ‘from which Rhone is full’ → “by which Rhone is filled”: source becomes agency, and the adjective of state becomes a passive participle.

> 61 Quel che fe' poi ch' egli uscì di Ravenna,  
> 62 e saltò Rubicon, fu di tal volo  
> 63 che nol seguiteria lingua nè penna.  

What it then wrought when he issued forth of Ravenna and sprang the Rubicon, was of such flight that neither tongue nor pen might follow it.

- *poi ch' egli uscì di Ravenna* ‘after he went out of Ravenna’ → “then wrought when he issued forth of Ravenna”: the temporal connection is redistributed between “then”, attached to the preceding action, and “when”; ‘went out’ becomes “issued forth”.
- *saltò Rubicon* ‘leapt Rubicon’ → “sprang the Rubicon”: the verb of leaping changes and the river takes an article.
- *nol seguiteria lingua nè penna* ‘would not follow it tongue nor pen’ → “neither tongue nor pen might follow it”: the negation moves to the paired subjects, which precede the verb; the conditional becomes a modal of possibility, and the object follows the verb.

> 64 In ver la Spagna rivolse lo stuolo;  

Towards Spain it wheeled the host,

- *In ver la Spagna* ‘towards the Spain’ → “Towards Spain”: the country name loses its article.
- *rivolse lo stuolo* ‘turned the host’ → “wheeled the host”: the verb specifies a wheeling movement.

> 65 poi ver Durazzo, e Farsalia percosse  
> 66 sì ch' al Nil caldo si sentì del duolo.  

then towards Durazzo, and so smote Pharsalia that to hot Nile was felt the woe.

- *Farsalia percosse sì* ‘Pharsalia it struck so’ → “so smote Pharsalia”: the adverb moves before the verb and the object after it; ‘struck’ becomes “smote”.
- *al Nil caldo* ‘to the Nile hot’ → “to hot Nile”: the adjective precedes the name and the article disappears.
- *si sentì del duolo* ‘one felt some of the grief’ → “was felt the woe”: the impersonal construction becomes a passive with “the woe” as its postposed subject; the partitive becomes definite.

> 67 Antandro e Simoenta, onde si mosse,  
> 68 rivide, e là dov' Ettore si cuba,  
> 69 e mal per Tolommeo poi si riscosse:  

Antandros and Simois, whence it first came, it saw once more, and saw the spot where Hector lieth couched; and then (alas for Ptolemy!) ruffled itself again;

- *onde si mosse* ‘whence it set out’ → “whence it first came”: departure is expressed as coming from a place, and “first” is supplied.
- *rivide* ‘it saw again’ → “it saw once more”: the prefix of repetition becomes a separate phrase.
- *e là dov' Ettore si cuba* ‘and there where Hector lies’ → “and saw the spot where Hector lieth couched”: the verb of seeing is repeated, the adverb of place becomes a noun with an article, and the single verb of reclining becomes “lieth couched”.
- *mal per Tolommeo poi si riscosse* ‘to Ptolemy’s hurt then it roused itself again’ → “then (alas for Ptolemy!) ruffled itself again”: “then” moves first; the expression of harm becomes an exclamation, and rousing oneself is rendered as ruffling oneself.

> 70 da indi scese folgorando a Iuba;  

thereafter swooped in lightning upon Juba,

- *da indi* ‘from there’ → “thereafter”: a point of departure in space becomes a point of succession in time.
- *scese folgorando a Iuba* ‘it descended flashing like lightning to Juba’ → “swooped in lightning upon Juba”: descent becomes a swoop, the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase, and “upon” replaces the preposition of destination.

> 71 poscia si volse nel vostro occidente,  
> 72 dove sentia la Pompeiana tuba.  

then wheeled to-wards your west, where it heard the Pompeian trumpet.

- *si volse* ‘it turned itself’ → “wheeled”: a reflexive turn becomes an intransitive wheeling movement.
- *sentia* ‘it was hearing’ → “it heard”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.

> 73 Di quel ch' ei fe' col baiulo seguente,  
> 74 Bruto con Cassio nello inferno latra,  
> 75 e Modena e Perugia fe' dolente.  

For what it wrought with the succeeding marshal Brutus and Cassius howl in hell; and Modena and Perugia it made doleful.

- *Di quel ch' ei fe'* ‘of that which it did’ → “For what it wrought”: “For” makes the causal relation explicit, the demonstrative and relative contract to “what”, and ‘did’ becomes “wrought”.
- *col baiulo seguente* ‘with the bearer following’ → “with the succeeding marshal”: the bearer is called a marshal, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *Bruto con Cassio nello inferno latra* ‘Brutus with Cassius in hell barks’ → “Brutus and Cassius howl in hell”: accompaniment becomes coordination, with a plural verb; barking becomes howling, and the phrase of place follows the verb and loses its article.

> 76 Piangene ancor la trista Cleopatra,  
> 77 che, fuggendogli innanzi, dal colubro  
> 78 la morte prese subitana ed atra.  

Yet doth wail for it the wretched Cleopatra, who, as she fled before it, caught from the viper sudden and black death.

- *Piangene ancor la trista Cleopatra* ‘weeps for it still the sad Cleopatra’ → “Yet doth wail for it the wretched Cleopatra”: the temporal adverb moves first; weeping becomes wailing, with auxiliary “doth”; the attached pronoun becomes “for it”, and sadness becomes wretchedness.
- *fuggendogli innanzi* ‘fleeing before it’ → “as she fled before it”: the gerund becomes a finite temporal clause.
- *dal colubro la morte prese subitana ed atra* ‘from the snake the death she took, sudden and black’ → “caught from the viper sudden and black death”: the verb moves before the source phrase and becomes “caught”; the snake is specified as a viper, and the adjectives move before “death”, which loses its article.

## Lines 79–111 — The Eagle’s Vengeance and the Rival Standards

> 79 Con costui corse infino al lito rubro;  

With him it coursed unto the Red-Sea shore,

- *Con costui* ‘with this man’ → “With him”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *infino al lito rubro* ‘as far as the shore red’ → “unto the Red-Sea shore”: the limit of the course is expressed by “unto”; the adjective following the noun becomes a compound naming the sea before “shore”.

> 80 con costui pose il mondo in tanta pace,  
> 81 che fu serrato a Jano il suo delubro.  

with him it set the world in so deep peace that Janus saw his temple barred upon him.

- *con costui* ‘with this man’ → “with him”: again the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *in tanta pace* ‘in so much peace’ → “in so deep peace”: depth takes the place of quantity.
- *fu serrato a Jano il suo delubro* ‘was shut to Janus his temple’ → “Janus saw his temple barred upon him”: the dative becomes the subject of the supplied verb “saw”; the subject of the Italian passive becomes its object, with “barred” as an object complement. “upon him” retains the sense that the closure affects Janus.

> 82 Ma ciò che il segno che parlar mi face  
> 83 fatto avea prima, e poi era fatturo,  
> 84 per lo regno mortal, ch' a lui soggiace,  

But what the ensign that doth make me speak had done before, what it was yet to do throughout the mortal realm subject unto it,

- *ciò che* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative are fused.
- *che parlar mi face* ‘that speak me makes’ → “that doth make me speak”: the finite verb comes first, with auxiliary “doth”, and the pronoun precedes the infinitive.
- *fatto avea prima* ‘done had before’ → “had done before”: the auxiliary precedes the participle.
- *e poi era fatturo* ‘and afterwards was going to do’ → “what it was yet to do”: the conjunction gives way to a repeated relative; the prospective participle becomes an infinitive, and “yet” looks forward where *poi* marks succession.
- *per lo regno mortal* ‘through the realm mortal’ → “throughout the mortal realm”: “throughout” stresses extent, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *ch' a lui soggiace* ‘which to it is subject’ → “subject unto it”: the finite relative clause becomes an adjectival phrase, with the prepositional complement last.

> 85 diventa in apparenza poco e scuro,  
> 86 se in mano al terzo Cesare si mira  
> 87 con occhio chiaro e con affetto puro;  

becometh small and dusky to behold, if it be looked upon in the third Cæsar’s hand with clear eye and pure heart;

- *in apparenza poco e scuro* ‘in appearance little and dark’ → “small and dusky to behold”: the phrase of appearance moves after the adjectives and becomes an infinitive of perception; “dusky” softens ‘dark’.
- *in mano al terzo Cesare si mira* ‘in the hand of the third Cæsar one looks at it’ → “it be looked upon in the third Cæsar’s hand”: the *si* construction becomes an English passive, with subjunctive “be” after the condition; the phrase of place moves after the verb, and the possessor before “hand”.
- *con occhio chiaro e con affetto puro* ‘with eye clear and with affection pure’ → “with clear eye and pure heart”: the preposition is not repeated, both adjectives move before their nouns, and “heart” stands for the inward disposition.

> 88 chè la viva giustizia che mi spira  
> 89 gli concedette, in mano a quel ch' io dico,  
> 90 gloria di far vendetta alla sua ira.  

for the living justice that inspireth me, granted it, in his hand of whom I speak, the glory of wreaking vengeance for his wrath.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the explanation is coordinated rather than subordinated.
- *che mi spira* ‘that me inspires’ → “that inspireth me”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *gli concedette* ‘to it granted’ → “granted it”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *in mano a quel ch' io dico* ‘in the hand of him whom I mention’ → “in his hand of whom I speak”: the possessor is anticipated by “his”, then identified by a relative with “of” dependent on “speak”.
- *gloria di far vendetta alla sua ira* ‘glory of doing vengeance to his wrath’ → “the glory of wreaking vengeance for his wrath”: an article is supplied, “wreaking” replaces the general verb, and “for” expresses the relation to the wrath.

> 91 Or qui t' ammira in ciò ch' io ti replico!  

Now find a marvel in the double thing I tell thee!

- *qui t' ammira* ‘here marvel’ → “find a marvel”: the local adverb drops out, and the reflexive verb of wondering becomes a verb with a noun as its object.
- *in ciò ch' io ti replico* ‘in that which I repeat to thee’ → “in the double thing I tell thee”: the idea of doubling in *replico* is transferred to the object as “double”; the verb itself becomes “tell”, with the recipient following it.

> 92 Poscia con Tito a far vendetta corse  
> 93 della vendetta del peccato antico.  

Thereafter, under Titus, to wreak vengeance on the vengeance on the ancient sin it rushed.

- *con Tito* ‘with Titus’ → “under Titus”: association is expressed as subordination.
- *a far vendetta corse* ‘to do vengeance it ran’ → “to wreak vengeance” … “it rushed”: the general verb for taking vengeance becomes “wreak”; the verb of motion is intensified and deferred until after the complements of “vengeance”.
- *della vendetta del peccato antico* ‘of the vengeance of the sin ancient’ → “on the vengeance on the ancient sin”: both genitives are interpreted as objects of vengeance, and the adjective moves before “sin”.

> 94 E quando il dente Longobardo morse  
> 95 la santa Chiesa, sotto alle sue ali  
> 96 Carlo Magno, vincendo, la soccorse.  

And when the Lombard tooth bit into Holy Church, under its wings did Charlemagne victorious succour her.

- *il dente Longobardo* ‘the tooth Lombard’ → “the Lombard tooth”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *morse la santa Chiesa* ‘bit the holy Church’ → “bit into Holy Church”: “into” is supplied, and the article drops out.
- *Carlo Magno, vincendo, la soccorse* ‘Charlemagne, conquering, her succoured’ → “did Charlemagne victorious succour her”: the gerund becomes an adjective describing the subject; “did” precedes the subject after the opening phrase of place, and the object pronoun moves after the main verb.

> 97 Omai puoi giudicar di quei cotali  
> 98 ch' io accusai di sopra, e di lor falli,  
> 99 che son cagion di tutti vostri mali.  

Now mayst thou judge of such as I accused but now, and of their sins, which are the cause of all your ills.

- *Omai puoi* ‘by now thou canst’ → “Now mayst thou”: the temporal expression is shortened, and the modal precedes its subject.
- *di quei cotali ch' io accusai di sopra* ‘of those such ones whom I accused above’ → “of such as I accused but now”: “such as” absorbs the demonstrative and relative, and a reference to an earlier place in the discourse becomes one to the recent past.
- *falli* ‘faults’ → “sins”: the English word gives the faults a more specifically moral sense.
- *son cagion* ‘are cause’ → “are the cause”: a definite article is supplied.

> 100 L' uno al pubblico segno i gigli gialli  
> 101 oppone, e l' altro appropria quello a parte,  
> 102 sì che forte a veder è chi più falli.  

The one opposeth to the public standard the yellow lilies, and the other doth annex it to a faction, so that ’tis hard to see which most offendeth.

- *al pubblico segno i gigli gialli oppone* ‘to the public standard the lilies yellow opposes’ → “opposeth to the public standard the yellow lilies”: the verb moves before both complements, and the colour adjective precedes its noun.
- *appropria quello a parte* ‘appropriates that to a party’ → “doth annex it to a faction”: “annex” with auxiliary “doth” replaces the simple verb; the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and “faction” makes the partisan sense explicit.
- *forte a veder è* ‘hard to see it is’ → “’tis hard to see”: the copula moves before its complement.
- *chi più falli* ‘who errs more’ → “which most offendeth”: “which” marks a choice between the two; the comparative becomes a superlative, and the Italian dependent subjunctive is rendered by an indicative.

> 103 Faccian li Ghibellin, faccian lor arte  
> 104 sott' altro segno; chè mal segue quello  
> 105 sempre chi la giustizia e lui diparte:  

Ply, ply the Ghibellines their arts under some other standard! for this he ever followeth ill who cleaveth justice from it;

- *Faccian li Ghibellin, faccian lor arte* ‘let the Ghibellines do, let them do their art’ → “Ply, ply the Ghibellines their arts”: the exhortative verbs are placed together before the named subject; “ply” replaces ‘do’, and the singular activity becomes plural.
- *sott' altro segno* ‘under another standard’ → “under some other standard”: “some” makes the indefiniteness explicit.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating explanation replaces the subordinate clause.
- *mal segue quello sempre chi* ‘ill follows that always whoever’ → “this he ever followeth ill who”: the demonstrative changes from ‘that’ to “this” and is fronted as object; the subject is anticipated by “he”, the adverb of frequency moves before the verb, and “ill” follows it.
- *la giustizia e lui diparte* ‘justice and it separates’ → “cleaveth justice from it”: the verb moves first, and the two coordinated objects become one object and a complement with “from”.

> 106 e non l' abbatta esto Carlo novello  
> 107 coi Guelfi suoi, ma tema degli artigli  
> 108 ch' a più alto leon trasser lo vello.  

and let not that new Charles down beat it with his Guelfs, but let him fear talons that have ripped its fell from mightier lion.

- *non l' abbatta esto Carlo novello* ‘let not this Charles new beat it down’ → “let not that new Charles down beat it”: the negative exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”; the named subject comes before the action, ‘this’ becomes “that”, and the adjective precedes the name. “down beat” expands the single verb, with its object following it.
- *ma tema degli artigli* ‘but let him fear the talons’ → “but let him fear talons”: the subjunctive is again expressed with “let”, and the Italian prepositional construction gives way to a bare direct object.
- *ch' a più alto leon trasser lo vello* ‘that from a higher lion drew the fleece’ → “that have ripped its fell from mightier lion”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, ‘drew’ is intensified to “ripped”, and “its fell” replaces the noun with definite article. The lion follows rather than precedes the verb, and height is interpreted as might.

> 109 Molte fiate già pianser li figli  
> 110 per la colpa del padre; e non si creda  
> 111 che Dio trasmuti l' arme per suoi gigli.  

Many a time ere now have children wailed for father’s fault, and let him not suppose God will change arms for those his lilies.

- *Molte fiate* ‘many times’ → “Many a time”: a distributive singular replaces the plural.
- *già pianser li figli* ‘already wept the children’ → “ere now have children wailed”: the time adverb is expanded, the simple past becomes a present perfect, and only its auxiliary precedes the subject; the article drops out and “wailed” intensifies ‘wept’.
- *la colpa del padre* ‘the fault of the father’ → “father’s fault”: a possessive before the noun replaces the following genitive, with neither noun retaining a separate article.
- *non si creda* ‘let it not be believed’ → “let him not suppose”: the impersonal injunction becomes personal, and ‘believe’ becomes “suppose”.
- *che Dio trasmuti l' arme* ‘that God change the arms’ → “God will change arms”: the conjunction and article drop out, and the dependent present subjunctive is interpreted as a future.
- *per suoi gigli* ‘for his lilies’ → “for those his lilies”: the demonstrative “those” is supplied.

## Lines 112–142 — Heavenly Harmony and Romeo’s Unrewarded Service

> 112 Questa picciola stella si correda  
> 113 dei buoni spirti, che son stati attivi  
> 114 perchè onore e fama li succeda;  

This little star adorneth her with good spirits who were active that honour and that fame might come to them;

- *si correda* ‘herself she equips’ → “adorneth her”: the verb takes the sense of adorning; the reflexive precedes the Italian verb, whereas “her”, used reflexively, follows the English verb.
- *dei buoni spirti* ‘of the good spirits’ → “with good spirits”: English uses “with” for that with which the star adorns herself and drops the definite article.
- *son stati attivi* ‘have been active’ → “were active”: the compound perfect becomes a simple past.
- *perchè onore e fama li succeda* ‘in order that honour and fame may follow them’ → “that honour and that fame might come to them”: “that” is repeated before “fame”; the singular present subjunctive becomes “might come” after a past tense, and the preverbal *li* becomes “to them” after the verb.

> 115 e quando li disiri poggian quivi  
> 116 sì disviando, pur convien che i raggi  
> 117 del vero amore in su poggin men vivi.  

and when hereon desire, thus swerving, leaneth, needs must the rays of the true love mount upward with less life.

- *li disiri poggian quivi sì disviando* ‘the desires lean there, thus swerving’ → “hereon desire, thus swerving, leaneth”: the definite plural becomes a bare singular; “hereon” expresses that **on** which desire leans and moves to the front, while the participial phrase moves before the verb.
- *pur convien che i raggi del vero amore in su poggin men vivi* ‘nevertheless it is necessary that the rays of the true love upward mount less lively’ → “needs must the rays of the true love mount upward with less life”: the force of *pur convien* is concentrated in “needs must”; the subject of the Italian subjunctive becomes the subject of “must … mount”. The direction follows “mount”, and the adjective describing the rays becomes a phrase with the noun “life”.

> 118 Ma, nel commensurar dei nostri gaggi  
> 119 col merto, è parte di nostra letizia,  
> 120 perchè non li vedem minor nè maggi.  

But in the commeasuring of our rewards to our desert is part of our joy, because we see them neither less nor more.

- *col merto* ‘with the desert’ → “to our desert”: English measures one thing “to” another and makes the possessive explicit in place of the article.
- *non li vedem minor nè maggi* ‘we do not see them smaller nor greater’ → “we see them neither less nor more”: the negative moves from the verb into the pair “neither … nor”, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 121 Quindi addolcisce la viva giustizia  
> 122 in noi l' affetto sì, che non si puote  
> 123 torcer giammai ad alcuna nequizia.  

Whereby the living justice so sweeteneth our affection that it may ne’er be warped to any malice.

- *Quindi addolcisce la viva giustizia* ‘hence sweetens the living justice’ → “Whereby the living justice so sweeteneth”: the connective is made relative, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *in noi l' affetto sì* ‘in us the affection so’ → “our affection”, “so”: the phrase locating the affection in us becomes a possessive; the degree word moves from after the object to before “sweeteneth”.
- *non si puote torcer giammai* ‘cannot turn aside ever’ → “it may ne’er be warped”: the negative on the modal and the later adverb are combined in “ne’er”; the Italian *si* construction is rendered by an explicit passive, with “warped” for ‘turned aside’.

> 124 Diverse voci fan giù dolci note;  
> 125 così diversi scanni in nostra vita  
> 126 rendon dolce armonia tra queste rote.  

Divers voices upon earth make sweet harmony, and so the divers seats in our life render sweet harmony amongst these wheels.

- *fan giù* ‘make down below’ → “upon earth make”: the place meant by ‘below’ is named, and the phrase moves before the verb.
- *dolci note* ‘sweet notes’ → “sweet harmony”: the plural notes become their collective effect, anticipating the wording of line 126.
- *così diversi scanni* ‘so divers seats’ → “and so the divers seats”: a coordinating conjunction and a definite article are supplied.

> 127 E dentro alla presente margarita  
> 128 luce la luce di Romeo, di cui  
> 129 fu l' opra bella e grande mal gradita.  

And within the present pearl shineth the light of Romeo, whose beauteous and great work was so ill answered.

- *alla presente margarita* ‘to the present pearl’ → “the present pearl”: *dentro* takes a complement with *a* here; “within” takes its complement directly.
- *di cui fu l' opra bella e grande mal gradita* ‘of whom was the work beautiful and great ill received’ → “whose beauteous and great work was so ill answered”: the possessive relative replaces ‘of whom’ and the article; the adjectives move before “work”, and “was” moves after the whole subject. ‘Ill received’ becomes “ill answered”, with “so” added.

> 130 Ma i Provenzali che fer contra lui  
> 131 non hanno riso, e però mal cammina  
> 132 qual si fa danno del ben fare altrui.  

But the Provençals who wrought against him have not the laugh; wherefore he taketh an ill path who maketh of another’s good work his own loss.

- *non hanno riso* ‘have not laughed’ → “have not the laugh”: English treats *riso* as a noun, with a supplied article, rather than as the participle of a compound perfect.
- *e però* ‘and therefore’ → “wherefore”: a single relative connective replaces the conjunction and adverb.
- *mal cammina qual* ‘walks ill whoever’ → “he taketh an ill path who”: the adverb becomes an adjective modifying the supplied noun “path”; the free relative is divided into an antecedent before the verb and a relative after it.
- *si fa danno del ben fare altrui* ‘makes harm for himself out of another’s doing well’ → “maketh of another’s good work his own loss”: the infinitival expression becomes “good work”, with the possessor moved before it; ‘harm for himself’ becomes the possessive phrase “his own loss”, placed last.

> 133 Quattro figlie ebbe, e ciascuna regina,  
> 134 Ramondo Beringhieri, e ciò gli fece  
> 135 Romeo persona umile e peregrina;  

Four daughters, and each one a queen, had Raymond Berengar; and this was wrought for him by Romeo, a lowly and an alien man;

- *ebbe, e ciascuna regina* ‘had, and each a queen’ → “and each one a queen, had”: the verb is postponed until after the parenthesis; “one” expands ‘each’, and English supplies an article before “queen”.
- *ciò gli fece Romeo* ‘this for him did Romeo’ → “this was wrought for him by Romeo”: the object becomes the subject of a passive, the Italian subject becomes its agent, and the beneficiary follows the verb.
- *persona umile e peregrina* ‘a person lowly and foreign’ → “a lowly and an alien man”: “man” specifies the more general ‘person’; the adjectives precede the noun, with an article before each.

> 136 e poi il mosser le parole biece  
> 137 a domandar ragione a questo giusto,  
> 138 che gli assegnò sette e cinque per diece;  

then words uttered askance moved him to demand account of this just man, who gave him five and seven for every ten;

- *e poi* ‘and then’ → “then”: the conjunction is omitted.
- *il mosser le parole biece* ‘him moved the crooked words’ → “words uttered askance moved him”: the subject moves before the verb and loses its article; the object moves after the verb. The adjective describing the words becomes a participle and an adverb describing their utterance.
- *domandar ragione a questo giusto* ‘demand an account from this just one’ → “demand account of this just man”: the Italian phrase has bare *ragione*, retained as bare “account”; the person addressed is introduced by “of”, and the substantivized adjective becomes an adjective with the noun “man”.
- *gli assegnò sette e cinque per diece* ‘to him assigned seven and five for ten’ → “gave him five and seven for every ten”: “gave” replaces ‘assigned’, the recipient follows the verb, the two addends change places, and “every” makes the ratio explicit.

> 139 indi partissi povero e vetusto;  

then took his way in poverty and age;

- *partissi* ‘he departed’ → “took his way”: a verb of departure becomes a phrase with a possessive object; the subject is left unexpressed in English as well.
- *povero e vetusto* ‘poor and old’ → “in poverty and age”: two adjectives predicated of the departing man become nouns governed by “in”.

> 140 e se il mondo sapesse il cor ch' egli ebbe  
> 141 mendicando sua vita a frusto a frusto,  
> 142 assai lo loda, e più lo loderebbe.”  

and might the world know the heart he had within him, begging his life by crust and crust, much as it praiseth, it would praise him more.”

- *se il mondo sapesse* ‘if the world knew’ → “might the world know”: inversion with a modal replaces *se* and the imperfect subjunctive.
- *ch' egli ebbe* ‘that he had’ → “he had within him”: the object relative is left unexpressed and “within him” is supplied.
- *a frusto a frusto* ‘crust by crust’ → “by crust and crust”: the preposition is placed before the pair, and the second occurrence of *a* is replaced by a conjunction.
- *assai lo loda, e più lo loderebbe* ‘much it praises him, and more it would praise him’ → “much as it praiseth, it would praise him more”: the first assertion becomes a concessive clause with “as” and no expressed object; the conjunction disappears, and in the conditional clause both the object and “more” follow the verb.

## Summary — Close Phrasing, Freer Constructions

Wicksteed often keeps the Italian phrasing even where its order is unusual in English: “Caesar I was” follows *Cesare fui* word for word. Elsewhere he moves objects after their verbs, adjectives before their nouns, and possessors before the things possessed: *al dolor di Lucrezia* becomes “unto Lucretia’s woe”. Two routine differences generally go unmarked in the comparison. English supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject by the verb ending, as in *fui* and “I was”; it also drops the articles that Italian uses before possessives, as in *al mio Bellisar* and “to my Belisarius”.

A change of construction often shifts the emphasis without changing the action. In *di retro ad Annibale passaro*, crossing is the action and ‘behind Hannibal’ tells how it was done; in “followed Hannibal across”, following becomes the action and “across” conveys the crossing. A gerund may become a clause, as *fuggendogli innanzi* becomes “as she fled before it”; adjectives may become nouns, as *povero e vetusto* becomes “in poverty and age”. Tenses, too, are flexible: *vide* (‘saw’) becomes “knoweth”, turning past perception into present knowledge.

The choice of words ranges from close equivalents to more specific or emphatic terms. *Segno* becomes “standard” or “ensign”; *trasser* (‘drew’) becomes “have ripped”. Wicksteed sometimes makes an implication explicit, adding “now” to *ch' io sento*, but need not give every word a separate equivalent: the second *con* in *con occhio chiaro e con affetto puro* disappears in “with clear eye and pure heart”. Such freedom coexists with close attention to repetition. The two occurrences of *con costui* are answered by the same English phrase, and the doubling of *vendetta* is preserved in “vengeance on the vengeance”.
