# Paradiso 25

## Lines 1–15 — The Poet’s Hoped-for Return

> 1 Se mai continga che il poema sacro,  
> 2 al quale ha posto mano e cielo e terra,  
> 3 sì che m' ha fatto per più anni macro,  

Should it e’er come to pass that the sacred poem to which both heaven and earth so have set hand, that it hath made me lean through many a year,

- *Se mai continga* ‘if ever it should happen’ → “Should it e’er come to pass”: inversion with “Should” replaces the conditional conjunction and subjunctive; “come to pass” renders a single verb.
- *il poema sacro* ‘the poem sacred’ → “the sacred poem”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *ha posto mano e cielo e terra* ‘has set hand both heaven and earth’ → “both heaven and earth so have set hand”: the compound subject precedes the verb, which is plural rather than singular.
- *sì che* ‘so that’ → “so” … “that”: the consecutive phrase is split, with the degree word moved back into the preceding clause.
- *m' ha fatto per più anni macro* ‘me it has made for several years lean’ → “it hath made me lean through many a year”: the object follows the verb, and its complement immediately follows it; the expression of duration comes last, with “through” for *per* and the distributive singular “many a year” for the plural.

> 4 vinca la crudeltà, che fuor mi serra  
> 5 del bello ovil, dov' io dormii agnello  
> 6 nimico ai lupi, che gli danno guerra;  

should overcome the cruelty which doth bar me forth from the fair sheepfold wherein I used to sleep, a lamb, foe to the wolves which war upon it;

- *vinca* ‘may overcome’ → “should overcome”: an auxiliary renders the subjunctive dependent on the opening condition.
- *fuor mi serra del bello ovil* ‘out me shuts from the fair sheepfold’ → “doth bar me forth from the fair sheepfold”: “doth bar” renders the simple present; the object and the adverb both follow the verb, in the reverse of their Italian order.
- *dov' io dormii* ‘where I slept’ → “wherein I used to sleep”: the relative adverb gains an explicit locative preposition, and the simple past is interpreted as habitual.
- *agnello* ‘lamb’ → “a lamb”: the predicate noun receives an indefinite article.
- *gli danno guerra* ‘to it give war’ → “war upon it”: the noun becomes the verb, and the preceding indirect object becomes a following prepositional phrase.

> 7 con altra voce omai, con altro vello  
> 8 ritornerò poeta, ed in sul fonte  
> 9 del mio battesmo prenderò il cappello;  

with changed voice now, and with changed fleece shall I return, a poet, and at the font of my baptism shall I assume the chaplet;

- *con altra voce omai, con altro vello* ‘with another voice now, with another fleece’ → “with changed voice now, and with changed fleece”: difference is expressed as change in both phrases, and a conjunction is supplied between them.
- *ritornerò poeta* ‘I shall return poet’ → “shall I return, a poet”: the auxiliary precedes the subject after the introductory phrases, and the predicate noun receives an article.
- *in sul fonte* ‘upon the font’ → “at the font”: the compound preposition is rendered by a simple preposition of place.
- *prenderò il cappello* ‘I shall take the cap’ → “shall I assume the chaplet”: the auxiliary again precedes the subject; “assume” gives *prenderò* the sense of putting on, and “chaplet” specifies the kind of headwear intended.

> 10 però che nella Fede, che fa conte  
> 11 l' anime a Dio, quivi entra' io, e poi  
> 12 Pietro per lei sì mi girò la fronte.  

because into the Faith which maketh souls known of God, ’twas there I entered; and afterward Peter, for its sake, circled thus my brow.

- *nella Fede* ‘in the Faith’ → “into the Faith”: the preposition makes the direction of entry explicit.
- *fa conte l' anime a Dio* ‘makes known the souls to God’ → “maketh souls known of God”: the object precedes its adjectival complement and loses its definite article; “of” expresses the relation to the one who knows.
- *quivi entra' io* ‘there entered I’ → “’twas there I entered”: a cleft construction emphasizes the adverb, and the subject precedes the verb.
- *per lei* ‘for her’ → “for its sake”: a neuter possessive in a phrase of cause replaces the feminine personal pronoun.
- *sì mi girò la fronte* ‘thus to me he circled the brow’ → “circled thus my brow”: the adverb follows the verb, and a possessive modifying the body part replaces the personal dative and definite article.

> 13 Indi si mosse un lume verso noi  
> 14 di quella spera, ond' uscì la primizia  
> 15 che lasciò Cristo dei vicari suoi.  

Thereafter moved a light toward us from out that sphere whence issued forth the first fruits of his vicars left by Christ.

- *si mosse* ‘set itself in motion’ → “moved”: an intransitive verb replaces the pronominal construction.
- *di quella spera* ‘from that sphere’ → “from out that sphere”: “out” makes emergence from within explicit.
- *la primizia* ‘the first fruit’ → “the first fruits”: the singular becomes plural.
- *che lasciò Cristo dei vicari suoi* ‘that Christ left of his vicars’ → “of his vicars left by Christ”: the partitive phrase is brought forward, with the possessive before its noun; the active relative clause becomes a passive participial phrase, with Christ expressed as its agent.

## Lines 16–48 — The Greeting and the Questions on Hope

> 16 E la mia donna piena di letizia  
> 17 mi disse: “Mira, mira, ecco il Barone,  
> 18 per cui laggiù si visita Galizia.”  

And my Lady, full of gladness, said to me, “Look! look! behold the Baron for whose sake, down below, they seek Galicia.”

- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *per cui* ‘for whom’ → “for whose sake”: the preposition is expanded into a phrase expressing motive.
- *si visita Galizia* ‘one visits Galicia’ → “they seek Galicia”: impersonal *si* becomes an indefinite plural subject, and visiting a place becomes seeking it.

> 19 Sì come quando il colombo si pone  
> 20 presso al compagno, e l' uno all' altro pande,  
> 21 girando e mormorando, l' affezione,  

As when a dove taketh his place near his companion, and the one poureth out his love for the other, circling round and murmuring,

- *Sì come quando* ‘just as when’ → “As when”: the intensifying *sì* is not separately rendered.
- *il colombo* ‘the dove’ → “a dove”: the generic definite article becomes indefinite.
- *si pone* ‘places itself’ → “taketh his place”: the reflexive construction becomes a verb with a possessive noun phrase as its object.
- *presso al compagno* ‘near the companion’ → “near his companion”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *l' uno all' altro pande, girando e mormorando, l' affezione* ‘the one to the other shows, circling and murmuring, the affection’ → “the one poureth out his love for the other, circling round and murmuring”: showing affection to the other becomes pouring out love for the other. The article becomes a possessive; the direct object comes immediately after the verb, the phrase for the other follows it, and the two participles come last. “round” makes the turning motion explicit.

> 22 così vid' io l' un dall' altro grande  
> 23 principe glorioso essere accolto,  
> 24 laudando il cibo che lassù li prande.  

so did I see one great chieftain glorious received by the other, praising the food which there above doth feast them.

- *vid' io* ‘saw I’ → “did I see”: the inverted preterite becomes an inverted construction with auxiliary “did”.
- *l' un dall' altro grande principe glorioso essere accolto* ‘the one by the other great glorious prince to be welcomed’ → “one great chieftain glorious received by the other”: the translation takes the title with the one received, bringing “by the other” to the end. The article of *l' un* drops out, *principe* becomes “chieftain”, and a bare passive participle replaces the passive infinitive.
- *li prande* ‘feeds them’ → “doth feast them”: feeding becomes feasting, the simple present takes an auxiliary, and the object follows the verb.

> 25 Ma poi che il gratular si fu assolto,  
> 26 tacito *coram me* ciascun s' affisse,  
> 27 ignito sì che vinceva il mio volto.  

But when the greeting was fulfilled, silent before me each one fixed himself, so kindled it subdued my countenance.

- *poi che* ‘after’ → “when”: the temporal conjunction no longer expressly marks succession.
- *il gratular si fu assolto* ‘the act of rejoicing was brought to an end’ → “the greeting was fulfilled”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun for the greeting itself; the pronominal construction denoting completion becomes a passive with “fulfilled”.
- *s' affisse* ‘himself fixed’ → “fixed himself”: the reflexive object follows the verb.
- *ignito sì* ‘kindled so’ → “so kindled”: the degree adverb precedes the participle.
- *che vinceva il mio volto* ‘that it was overpowering my face’ → “it subdued my countenance”: the result conjunction is left implicit; the imperfect becomes a simple past, and *volto* takes the more formal “countenance”.

> 28 Ridendo allora Beatrice disse:  
> 29 “Inclita vita, per cui la larghezza  
> 30 della nostra basilica si scrisse,  

Smiling then Beatrice said: “Illustrious life, by whom the generosity of our court was chronicled,

- *Ridendo* ‘laughing’ → “Smiling”: the participle denotes a quieter expression.
- *basilica* ‘basilica’ → “court”: the institutional sense replaces the architectural term.
- *si scrisse* ‘was written’ → “was chronicled”: the Italian *si*-passive becomes an English verbal passive, and writing is specified as making a record.

> 31 fa risonar la speme in questa altezza;  

make hope be sounded in this height;

- *fa risonar la speme* ‘make hope resound’ → “make hope be sounded”: the active infinitive becomes passive, with “hope” before it rather than after it.

> 32 tu sai, che tante volte la figuri,  
> 33 quanto Jesù ai tre fe' più chiarezza.”  

thou knowest that all those times thou figurest it when Jesus gave more light unto the three.”

- *che tante volte la figuri, quanto* ‘that as many times thou figurest it as’ → “that all those times thou figurest it when”: the correlation of number, *tante … quanto*, becomes a temporal construction with “all those times” and “when”; the object pronoun follows its verb.
- *Jesù ai tre fe' più chiarezza* ‘Jesus to the three made greater clarity’ → “Jesus gave more light unto the three”: making clarity becomes giving light, and the recipients move from before the verb to the end.

> 34 “Leva la testa, e fa che t' assicuri;  

“Uplift thy head, and see thou reassure thee,

- *la testa* ‘the head’ → “thy head”: English makes the possessor of the body part explicit.
- *fa che t' assicuri* ‘see to it that thou make thyself secure’ → “see thou reassure thee”: the imperative and subjunctive construction is compressed into “see” with an unintroduced clause; the reflexive pronoun follows the verb, which is rendered in the sense of restoring confidence.

> 35 chè ciò che vien quassù dal mortal mondo,  
> 36 convien ch' ai nostri raggi si maturi.”  

for whatso cometh from the mortal world up hither, behoves it ripen in our rays.”

- *ciò che* ‘that which’ → “whatso”: the antecedent and relative are combined in a single indefinite relative.
- *quassù dal mortal mondo* ‘up here from the mortal world’ → “from the mortal world up hither”: the source precedes the destination, and “hither” gives the latter a directional sense.
- *convien ch' ai nostri raggi si maturi* ‘it is needful that in our rays it come to maturity’ → “behoves it ripen in our rays”: the impersonal verb and subjunctive clause give way to “behoves” with no expressed introductory subject or conjunction. The pronominal verb becomes intransitive “ripen”, and the phrase about the rays follows it.

> 37 Questo conforto dal foco secondo  
> 38 mi venne; ond' io levai gli occhi ai monti,  
> 39 che gl' incurvaron pria col troppo pondo.  

Such exhortation from the second flame came to me; whereat I lifted up mine eyes unto the mountains, which had before down-bowed them with excess of weight.

- *Questo conforto* ‘this encouragement’ → “Such exhortation”: the demonstrative identifying the encouragement becomes “Such”, and encouragement is rendered as an urging to act.
- *dal foco secondo* ‘from the fire second’ → “from the second flame”: “flame” replaces ‘fire’, and the ordinal precedes the noun.
- *mi venne* ‘to me came’ → “came to me”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *ond' io* ‘whence I’ → “whereat I”: the connective presents the preceding words as the occasion for the action.
- *levai gli occhi* ‘I raised the eyes’ → “I lifted up mine eyes”: a phrasal verb renders the simple verb, and a possessive replaces the article.
- *gl' incurvaron pria* ‘them bent down before’ → “had before down-bowed them”: the preterite becomes a past perfect; the adverb comes between auxiliary and participle, and the object follows the participle.
- *col troppo pondo* ‘with the excessive weight’ → “with excess of weight”: the adjective becomes the governing noun, with “weight” dependent on it, and the article drops out.

> 40 “Poichè, per grazia, vuol che tu t' affronti  
> 41 lo nostro Imperadore, anzi la morte,  
> 42 nell' aula più segreta, co' suoi Conti;  

“Since of his grace our Emperor willeth that ere thy death thou be confronted with his Counts in his most secret hall;

- *per grazia* ‘by grace’ → “of his grace”: the preposition changes and the possessor is specified.
- *vuol che tu t' affronti lo nostro Imperadore* ‘wills that thou come face to face, our Emperor’ → “our Emperor willeth that ere thy death thou be confronted”: the subject of ‘wills’ is moved from after the subordinate verb to before the main verb; the reflexive subjunctive becomes passive.
- *anzi la morte* ‘before death’ → “ere thy death”: the possessor is supplied, and the time phrase moves inside the clause before its subject.
- *nell' aula più segreta, co' suoi Conti* ‘in the hall most secret, with his Counts’ → “with his Counts in his most secret hall”: the two phrases exchange places, the superlative precedes its noun, and the hall acquires a possessive in place of the article.

> 43 sì che, veduto il ver di questa corte,  
> 44 la speme che laggiù bene innamora  
> 45 in te ed in altrui di ciò conforte:  

that, having seen the truth of this court, thou mayst thereby strengthen in thyself and mo’ the hope that upon earth enamoureth folk of good;

- *sì che* ‘so that’ → “that”: purpose is expressed without a separate equivalent for *sì*.
- *veduto il ver* ‘the truth seen’ → “having seen the truth”: the absolute participial construction becomes an active perfect participle with the observer as its understood subject.
- *la speme che laggiù bene innamora* ‘the hope that down there well enamours’ → “the hope that upon earth enamoureth folk of good”: the object and its relative clause move after the verb of strengthening. “upon earth” specifies the place indicated by *laggiù*; the translation takes *bene* substantivally as the good that inspires love rather than adverbially as ‘well’, and supplies “folk” as those moved to love.
- *in te ed in altrui* ‘in thee and in others’ → “in thyself and mo’”: the first pronoun is made expressly reflexive, the repeated preposition drops out, and ‘others’ becomes ‘more’.
- *di ciò conforte* ‘by this thou strengthen’ → “thou mayst thereby strengthen”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb, and the subjunctive is expressed with “mayst”; the verb now precedes the phrase naming those in whom hope is strengthened.

> 46 di' quel che ell' è, e come se ne infiora  
> 47 la mente tua, e di' onde a te venne”;  

say what thing it is, and how thy mind is therewith enflowered, and say whence unto thee it cometh”;

- *quel che ell' è* ‘that which it is’ → “what thing it is”: an indirect question replaces the antecedent and relative, with “thing” supplying a noun.
- *come se ne infiora la mente tua* ‘how with it flowers thy mind’ → “how thy mind is therewith enflowered”: the subject moves before the verb and the possessive before the noun; the pronominal verb becomes passive, with *ne* rendered by the adverb between auxiliary and participle.
- *venne* ‘came’ → “it cometh”: the preterite becomes present.

> 48 così seguì 'l secondo lume ancora.  

so followed on the second light.

- *seguì … ancora* ‘continued … further’ → “followed on”: the verb and the final adverb of continuation are drawn together into a phrasal verb before the subject.

## Lines 49–81 — Dante Answers on Hope

> 49 E quella pia, che guidò le penne  
> 50 delle mie ali a così alto volo,  
> 51 alla risposta così mi prevenne:  

And that tender one who guided the feathers of my wings to so lofty flight, thus foreran me in answer:

- *quella pia* ‘that compassionate one’ → “that tender one”: “tender” gives the sense of the adjective rather than its usual equivalent ‘pious’ or ‘compassionate’; “one” lets the adjective stand for a person in English.
- *alla risposta così mi prevenne* ‘in the answer thus me anticipated’ → “thus foreran me in answer”: the phrase about answering moves from first to last and loses its definite article; the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 52 “La Chiesa militante alcun figliuolo  
> 53 non ha con più speranza, com' è scritto  
> 54 nel sol che raggia tutto nostro stuolo;  

“Church militant hath not a child richer in hope, as is written in the sun who o’errayeth all our host;

- *La Chiesa militante* ‘the Church militant’ → “Church militant”: the definite article is dropped.
- *alcun figliuolo non ha* ‘any child she has not’ → “hath not a child”: the object follows the negated verb, and the indefinite *alcun* becomes an indefinite article.
- *con più speranza* ‘with more hope’ → “richer in hope”: the comparison of quantities becomes an adjective describing the child.
- *che raggia* ‘that irradiates’ → “who o’errayeth”: the relative is explicitly personal, and the verb conveys the image of casting rays over something.

> 55 però gli è conceduto che d' Egitto  
> 56 venga in Jerusalemme per vedere,  
> 57 anzi che il militar gli sia prescritto.  

therefore was it granted him to come from Egypt to Jerusalem, to look on her, e’er the prescribed limit of his soldiery.

- *gli è conceduto* ‘to him it is granted’ → “was it granted him”: the present passive becomes past, with the recipient placed last.
- *che d' Egitto venga in Jerusalemme* ‘that from Egypt he come into Jerusalem’ → “to come from Egypt to Jerusalem”: the subjunctive clause becomes an infinitive; the phrase of origin follows the verb, and ‘into’ becomes “to”.
- *per vedere* ‘in order to see’ → “to look on her”: the infinitive alone marks purpose; “look on” replaces ‘see’, and “her” makes the unexpressed object explicit.
- *anzi che il militar gli sia prescritto* ‘before his serving as a soldier be bounded for him’ → “e’er the prescribed limit of his soldiery”: the temporal clause becomes a noun phrase after “e’er”. The passive subjunctive gives way to an adjective modifying the supplied noun “limit”; the substantivized infinitive becomes “soldiery”, with the dative represented by “his”.

> 58 Gli altri due punti, che, non per sapere  
> 59 son domandati, ma perchè rapporti  
> 60 quanto questa virtù t' è in piacere,  

Those two other points—asked not that thou mayst learn, but that he may bear back word how much this virtue is held in pleasure by thee,—

- *Gli altri due punti* ‘the other two points’ → “Those two other points”: a demonstrative replaces the article, and the numeral precedes “other”.
- *che, non per sapere son domandati* ‘which, not in order to know, are asked’ → “asked not that thou mayst learn”: the finite passive relative becomes a participle preceding the purpose phrase; the infinitive becomes a finite clause with an explicit knower, and ‘know’ is rendered as acquiring knowledge.
- *ma perchè rapporti* ‘but that he may report’ → “but that he may bear back word”: the single verb is expanded into a phrase that brings out the sense of reporting back.
- *quanto questa virtù t' è in piacere* ‘how much this virtue is to thee a pleasure’ → “how much this virtue is held in pleasure by thee”: “held” gives the expression passive form, and the person experiencing the pleasure becomes the complement of “by”.

> 61 a lui lasc' io; chè non gli saran forti,  
> 62 nè di iattanza: ed egli a ciò risponda,  
> 63 e la grazia di Dio ciò gli comporti.”  

to him I leave; for they will not be hard, nor boastful matter, to him; so let him thereto answer, and may the grace of God concede this to him.”

- *a lui lasc' io* ‘to him leave I’ → “to him I leave”: the emphatically postposed subject moves before the verb.
- *chè non gli saran forti* ‘for not to him will they be difficult’ → “for they will not be hard” … “to him”: the negation moves inside the English verb phrase, and the dative is held back until after both predicates.
- *nè di iattanza* ‘nor of boasting’ → “nor boastful matter”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adjective with a supplied noun.
- *ed egli a ciò risponda* ‘and let him to this answer’ → “so let him thereto answer”: “so” gives the connective consequential force; “let” with an object expresses the third-person subjunctive, and “thereto” contracts the prepositional phrase.
- *ciò gli comporti* ‘this to him may it allow’ → “may the grace of God concede this to him”: the optative is expressed by “may” before the subject; “concede” renders ‘allow’, and both objects follow the main verb.

> 64 Come discente ch' a dottor seconda,  
> 65 pronto e libente, in quello ch' egli è sperto,  
> 66 perchè la sua bontà si disasconda:  

As the pupil who followeth the teacher, eager and glad, in that wherein he is expert, in order that his excellence may be revealed;

- *discente ch' a dottor seconda* ‘a learner who seconds a teacher’ → “the pupil who followeth the teacher”: both nouns receive definite articles; the verb with a dative becomes “followeth” with a direct object after it.
- *pronto e libente* ‘ready and willing’ → “eager and glad”: the adjectives of readiness and willingness are given a more affective turn.
- *in quello ch' egli è sperto* ‘in that in which he is experienced’ → “in that wherein he is expert”: “wherein” compresses the relative construction, and “expert” stresses proficiency.
- *perchè la sua bontà si disasconda* ‘so that his worth may unhide itself’ → “in order that his excellence may be revealed”: “excellence” specifies the sense of worth, and a passive replaces the reflexive verb.

> 67 “Speme, diss' io, è uno attender certo  
> 68 della gloria futura, il qual produce  
> 69 grazia divina e precedente merto.  

“Hope,” said I, “is a certain expectation of future glory, the product of divine grace and precedent merit.

- *uno attender certo* ‘a waiting certain’ → “a certain expectation”: an abstract noun replaces the substantivized infinitive, and the adjective moves before it.
- *della gloria futura* ‘of the glory future’ → “of future glory”: the article disappears, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *il qual produce* ‘which … produces’ → “the product of”: the relative pronoun is the object of the Italian verb, whose subject follows. English makes the expectation itself “the product” in apposition and introduces its causes with “of”.
- *grazia divina* ‘grace divine’ → “divine grace”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 70 Da molte stelle mi vien questa luce;  
> 71 ma quei la distillò nel mio cor pria,  
> 72 che fu sommo cantor del sommo duce.  

From many stars cometh this light to me; but he first distilled it into my heart who was the supreme singer of the supreme leader.

- *mi vien questa luce* ‘to me comes this light’ → “cometh this light to me”: the recipient moves from before the verb to after the subject.
- *quei la distillò nel mio cor pria* ‘he it distilled in my heart first’ → “he first distilled it into my heart”: “first” moves before the verb and the object after it; “into” makes the heart the destination of the distilling.
- *sommo cantor* ‘supreme singer’ → “the supreme singer”: the predicate noun receives a definite article.

> 73 *Sperino in te*, nella sua teodia  
> 74 dice, *color che sanno il nome tuo*:  
> 75 e chi nol sa, s' egli ha la fede mia?  

*Let them hope in thee*, in his divine song he saith, *who know thy name*; and who knoweth it not, having my faith?

- *Sperino* ‘may they hope’ → “Let them hope”: the third-person exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”; “them” anticipates the delayed subject.
- *nella sua teodia* ‘in his theody’ → “in his divine song”: the compound noun is unpacked into adjective and noun.
- *color che* ‘those who’ → “who”: the separate demonstrative is not repeated with the relative.
- *chi nol sa* ‘who does not know it’ → “who knoweth it not”: the negative and object both move after the verb, with the object first.
- *s' egli ha la fede mia* ‘if he has the faith of mine’ → “having my faith”: a participial phrase replaces the conditional clause, and the postposed possessive moves before the noun.

> 76 Tu mi stillasti con lo stillar suo  
> 77 nell' epistola poi, sì ch' io son pieno,  
> 78 ed in altrui vostra pioggia repluo.”  

Thou then didst drop it on me with his dropping, in thine Epistle, so that I am full and pour again your shower upon others.”

- *mi stillasti* ‘to me thou didst distil’ → “didst drop it on me”: “drop” retains the liquid image; the object “it” is supplied, and the recipient follows it in a prepositional phrase.
- *con lo stillar suo* ‘with the dripping of his’ → “with his dropping”: the possessive moves before the verbal noun.
- *poi* ‘afterwards’ → “then”: the adverb moves from after the mention of the epistle to before the main verb.
- *ed in altrui vostra pioggia repluo* ‘and on others your rain I rain back’ → “and pour again your shower upon others”: the verb moves before its object and the phrase of destination moves to the end. “pour again” separates the sense of the prefix from that of the verb; “shower” and “pour” do not repeat the rain image in the same words.

> 79 Mentr' io diceva, dentro al vivo seno  
> 80 di quello incendio tremolava un lampo  
> 81 subito e spesso, a guisa di baleno.  

Whilst I was speaking, within the living bosom of that flame trembled a flash sudden and dense like unto lightning.

- *diceva* ‘I was saying’ → “I was speaking”: the progressive conveys the imperfect, but the activity of speaking replaces that of saying.
- *vivo seno* ‘living breast’ → “living bosom”: “bosom” is the more specific choice for the interior evoked here.
- *di quello incendio* ‘of that blaze’ → “of that flame”: “flame” renders the fire rather than the conflagration.
- *spesso* ‘frequent’ → “dense”: Wicksteed takes the adjective in its sense of thickness or density rather than frequency of recurrence.
- *a guisa di baleno* ‘in the manner of a lightning flash’ → “like unto lightning”: a direct comparison replaces the phrase of manner, and the individual flash becomes the mass noun “lightning”.

## Lines 82–114 — Hope’s Promise and the Third Light

> 82 Indi spirò: “L' amore ond' io avvampo  
> 83 ancor ver la virtù, che mi seguette  
> 84 infin la palma, ed all' uscir del campo,  

Then breathed forth: “The love whence I am still a-flame to-ward that virtue which followed me even to the palm and issuing from the field,

- *spirò* ‘breathed’ → “breathed forth”: the English adds a particle of outward motion.
- *io avvampo ancor* ‘I blaze still’ → “I am still a-flame”: the verb becomes a form of ‘be’ with a predicate adjective, and the adverb moves before the adjective.
- *la virtù* ‘the virtue’ → “that virtue”: a demonstrative replaces the definite article.
- *mi seguette* ‘me followed’ → “followed me”: the object moves after the verb.
- *ed all’ uscir del campo* ‘and to the going out of the field’ → “and issuing from the field”: the English lets the preceding “to” govern the second term as well, renders the substantivized infinitive by an *-ing* form without an article, and expresses departure by “from”.

> 85 vuol ch' io respiri a te, che ti dilette  
> 86 di lei; ed emmi a grato che tu diche  
> 87 quello che la speranza ti promette.”  

willeth that I breathe on thee who dost delight thee in her; and further, ’tis my pleasure that thou tell the thing which hope doth promise thee.”

- *respiri a te* ‘breathe in turn to thee’ → “breathe on thee”: the English does not mark the answering force of the prefixed verb and changes the preposition.
- *ti dilette* ‘thyself delightest’ → “dost delight thee”: the verb is expanded with an auxiliary and the reflexive pronoun placed after it.
- *ed emmi a grato* ‘and it is to me agreeable’ → “and further, ’tis my pleasure”: “further” is added, and a possessive with a noun replaces the dative pronoun and predicative phrase.
- *quello che* ‘that which’ → “the thing which”: the demonstrative is expanded into a noun phrase.
- *la speranza ti promette* ‘the hope to thee promises’ → “hope doth promise thee”: the abstract noun loses its article, the verb takes an auxiliary, and the indirect object follows it.

> 88 Ed io: “Le nuove e le scritture antiche  
> 89 pongono il segno, ed esso lo mi addita.  

And I: “The new and the ancient scriptures set down the symbol, which again doth point me to the thing itself.

- *Le nuove e le scritture antiche* ‘the new and the scriptures ancient’ → “The new and the ancient scriptures”: the second adjective moves before the noun.
- *pongono il segno* ‘set the sign’ → “set down the symbol”: the particle makes the setting a setting in writing, and “symbol” interprets the sign as standing for something else.
- *ed esso lo mi addita* ‘and it points it out to me’ → “which again doth point me to the thing itself”: a relative replaces the conjunction and subject pronoun; “again” is added. The person addressed becomes the direct object of ‘point’, while the Italian direct object is expanded into the object of “to”; the simple verb also takes an auxiliary.

> 90 Dell' anime che Dio s' ha fatte amiche.  
> 91 Dice Isaia che ciascuna vestita  
> 92 nella sua terra fia di doppia vesta,  
> 93 e la sua terra è questa dolce vita.  

Of the souls which God hath made his friends Isaiah saith that each one shall be clad with double garb in its own land, and its own land is this sweet life.

- *Dio s’ ha fatte amiche* ‘God has made friends to himself’ → “God hath made his friends”: the reflexive dative becomes a possessive modifying “friends”.
- *Dice Isaia* ‘says Isaiah’ → “Isaiah saith”: subject and verb exchange places.
- *vestita nella sua terra fia di doppia vesta* ‘clothed in its land shall be in double raiment’ → “shall be clad with double garb in its own land”: the auxiliary comes before the participle, the clothing phrase comes next, with “with” for *di*, and the place phrase comes last. “Own” reinforces the possessive.
- *la sua terra è questa dolce vita* ‘its land is this sweet life’ → “its own land is this sweet life”: “own” again reinforces the possessive.

> 94 E il tuo fratello assai vie più digesta,  
> 95 là dove tratta delle bianche stole,  
> 96 questa rivelazion ci manifesta.”  

And more worked out by far, doth thy brother, where he treateth of the white robes, set forth this revelation to us.”

- *il tuo fratello assai vie più digesta* ‘thy brother, by far more fully elaborated’ → “more worked out by far, doth thy brother”: the comparative phrase, which qualifies the revelation, moves ahead of the subject; “by far” comes after “worked out”, and the English puts an auxiliary before the subject.
- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “where”: the separate adverb of place is dropped.
- *tratta delle bianche stole* ‘treats of the white stoles’ → “he treateth of the white robes”: “robes” is a broader garment term.
- *questa rivelazion ci manifesta* ‘this revelation to us makes manifest’ → “set forth this revelation to us”: a phrasal verb replaces the simple verb and precedes both its objects.

> 97 E prima, appresso al fin d' este parole,  
> 98 *Sperent in te*, di sopra noi s' udì,  
> 99 a che risposer tutte le carole;  

And, close upon the ending of these words, first rang above us, *Let them hope in thee*, whereunto all the carols answered;

- *prima, appresso al fin d’ este parole* ‘first, close upon the end of these words’ → “close upon the ending of these words, first”: the time phrase moves ahead of “first”, with “ending” for the noun ‘end’.
- *Sperent in te* ‘may they hope in thee’ → “*Let them hope in thee*”: the Latin subjunctive is rendered by a construction with “Let”, and the words heard are placed after the verb of sounding.
- *di sopra noi s’ udì* ‘above us was heard’ → “rang above us”: the passive construction becomes an active verb of sounding, followed rather than preceded by the place phrase.
- *risposer tutte le carole* ‘answered all the carols’ → “all the carols answered”: the subject comes before the verb.

> 100 poscia tra esse un lume si schiarì,  
> 101 sì che, se il Cancro avesse un tal cristallo,  
> 102 l' inverno avrebbe un mese d' un sol dì.  

then, from amongst themselves, a light flashed out, in fashion such that if the Crab contained a crystal like it winter would have a month of one unbroken day.

- *tra esse* ‘among them’ → “from amongst themselves”: the English adds a sense of emergence and uses an emphatic reflexive form.
- *si schiarì* ‘grew bright’ → “flashed out”: a change of brightness becomes a sudden outward flash.
- *sì che* ‘so that’ → “in fashion such that”: the adverb is expanded into a phrase with a noun.
- *avesse un tal cristallo* ‘had such a crystal’ → “contained a crystal like it”: possession becomes containment, and the modifying ‘such’ becomes an explicit comparison after the noun.
- *d’ un sol dì* ‘of one sole day’ → “of one unbroken day”: the adjective of singleness is interpreted as indicating continuity.

> 103 E come surge, e va, ed entra in ballo  
> 104 vergine lieta, sol per fare onore  
> 105 alla novizia, e non per alcun fallo,  

And as doth rise and go her way and enter on the dance a joyous virgin, only to do honour to the bride, and not for any failing,

- *surge, e va, ed entra in ballo* ‘rises, and goes, and enters into dance’ → “doth rise and go her way and enter on the dance”: one auxiliary serves all three verbs; “her way” is added, and the last verb takes “on” and a definite article.
- *vergine lieta* ‘virgin joyous’ → “a joyous virgin”: the English supplies an indefinite article and puts the adjective first.
- *alla novizia* ‘to the newlywed’ → “to the bride”: the English uses the name of the bridal role rather than a term marking newness.
- *per alcun fallo* ‘for any fault’ → “for any failing”: “failing” gives the fault the sense of a shortcoming.

> 106 così vid' io lo schiarato splendore  
> 107 venire ai due, che si volgeano a rota,  
> 108 qual conveniasi al loro ardente amore.  

so did I see the illumined splendour join the other two, who were wheeling round in such guise as their burning love befitted.

- *vid’ io* ‘saw I’ → “did I see”: the inversion is retained by using an auxiliary and an infinitive instead of the simple past.
- *venire ai due* ‘come to the two’ → “join the other two”: a transitive verb replaces the verb of motion with its preposition, and “other” is supplied.
- *si volgeano a rota* ‘were turning in a wheel’ → “were wheeling round”: the noun supplies the English verb, with an adverb of circular motion replacing the prepositional phrase.
- *qual conveniasi al loro ardente amore* ‘as was fitting to their burning love’ → “in such guise as their burning love befitted”: the English supplies a noun of manner and makes the love the subject of a transitive verb rather than the complement of ‘was fitting’.

> 109 Misesi lì nel canto e nella nota;  

There it launched itself into their music and their words;

- *Misesi lì* ‘put itself there’ → “There it launched itself”: the place adverb is fronted, and “launched” gives the reflexive verb more impetus.
- *nel canto e nella nota* ‘into the song and into the tune’ → “into their music and their words”: the English distinguishes the musical and verbal elements, putting the music first rather than rendering ‘tune’ last. It uses a possessive for each article but gives the preposition only once.

> 110 e la mia donna in lor tenne l' aspetto,  
> 111 pur come sposa, tacita ed immota.  

and my Lady held her look upon them just like a bride, silent and unmoving.

- *in lor tenne l’ aspetto* ‘on them held the gaze’ → “held her look upon them”: the prepositional phrase moves after verb and object, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *come sposa* ‘like bride’ → “like a bride”: English supplies the indefinite article.

> 112 “Questi è colui che giacque sopra il petto  
> 113 del nostro Pellicano, e questi fue  
> 114 d' in su la croce al grande offizio eletto.”  

“This is he who lay upon the breast of our Pelican, and this was he chosen from upon the cross for the great office.”

- *e questi fue … eletto* ‘and this one was … chosen’ → “and this was he chosen”: the English adds “he”, echoing the identification at the start of the sentence, and brings the participle forward from the end, before “from upon the cross”.
- *al grande offizio* ‘to the great office’ → “for the great office”: the English uses “for” for the office to which he was chosen.

## Lines 115–139 — The Last Flame and Dante’s Loss of Sight

> 115 La donna mia così; nè però piùe  
> 116 mosse la vista sua di stare attenta  
> 117 poscia, che prima, alle parole sue.  

My Lady thus; but no more after than before her words moved she her eyes from their fixed intent.

- *La donna mia* ‘the lady mine’ → “My Lady”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *nè però piùe* ‘nor for that any more’ → “but no more”: the negative passes from the conjunction to the comparative, and the force of *però* (‘for that’, ‘on that account’) is not separately expressed.
- *mosse la vista sua* ‘moved her sight’ → “moved she her eyes”: the singular faculty of sight becomes the plural organs of sight; the verb and its object follow the comparison of times instead of preceding it.
- *di stare attenta* ‘from remaining attentive’ → “from their fixed intent”: the infinitive and adjective become a noun phrase, with “their” referring back to “her eyes”.
- *poscia, che prima, alle parole sue* ‘afterwards, than before, in relation to her words’ → “after than before her words”: “her words” serves as the complement of both temporal prepositions; the separate relation marked by *alle* disappears, and the possessive precedes the noun.

> 118 Quale è colui ch' adocchia, e s' argomenta  
> 119 di vedere eclissar lo sole un poco,  
> 120 che per veder non vedente diventa;  

As who doth gaze and strain to see the sun eclipsed a space, who by looking grows bereft of sight;

- *Quale è colui ch’* ‘such as is he who’ → “As who”: the copula and demonstrative antecedent are left out.
- *adocchia, e s’ argomenta* ‘eyes and bestirs himself’ → “doth gaze and strain”: “doth” is supplied, and the effort expressed reflexively in Italian becomes an intransitive verb.
- *di vedere eclissar lo sole* ‘to see go into eclipse the sun’ → “to see the sun eclipsed”: the sun comes immediately after “see”, and the infinitive becomes a participle.
- *un poco* ‘a little’ → “a space”: a phrase of extent or degree is rendered as one of duration.
- *per veder non vedente diventa* ‘through seeing, non-seeing he becomes’ → “by looking grows bereft of sight”: “looking” distinguishes the act from its result; the verb of becoming precedes its complement, and the negative participle becomes a phrase of deprivation.

> 121 tal mi fec' io a quell' ultimo foco,  
> 122 mentre che detto fu: “Perchè t' abbagli  
> 123 per veder cosa, che qui non ha loco?  

so did I to this last flame till a word came: “Wherefore dost dazzle thee to see that which hath here no place?

- *tal mi fec’ io* ‘such did I make myself’ → “so did I”: “so” takes up the comparison, and the reflexive verb of becoming gives way to “did”, referring back to the act of gazing.
- *a quell’ ultimo foco* ‘at that last fire’ → “to this last flame”: the preposition is rendered directionally, the demonstrative changes from distant to near, and ‘fire’ becomes “flame”.
- *mentre che detto fu* ‘while it was said’ → “till a word came”: simultaneity becomes a limit in time, and the impersonal passive becomes an active clause with a new subject.
- *t’ abbagli* ‘thyself thou dazzlest’ → “dost dazzle thee”: the question takes the auxiliary “dost”, and the reflexive object follows the verb.
- *cosa, che* ‘a thing that’ → “that which”: a demonstrative replaces the noun as antecedent of the relative.
- *qui non ha loco* ‘here has not place’ → “hath here no place”: the adverb follows the verb, and the negative qualifies the noun.

> 124 In terra è terra il mio corpo, e saragli  
> 125 tanto con gli altri che il numero nostro  
> 126 con l' eterno proposito s' agguagli.  

Earth in the earth my body is, and there it shall be, with the rest, until our number equalleth the eternal purpose.

- *In terra è terra il mio corpo* ‘in earth is earth my body’ → “Earth in the earth my body is”: the predicate “Earth” comes first, the locative gains an article, and the verb comes after the subject.
- *e saragli tanto … che* ‘and it will be there so long … that’ → “and there it shall be, with the rest, until”: “there” is detached from the future verb and placed before it; the duration and its limit are expressed together by “until”.
- *con gli altri* ‘with the others’ → “with the rest”: a collective expression replaces the plural.
- *il numero nostro* ‘the number ours’ → “our number”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *con l’ eterno proposito s’ agguagli* ‘with the eternal purpose may make itself equal’ → “equalleth the eternal purpose”: the reflexive subjunctive becomes a transitive indicative, with what was the complement of a preposition now the direct object after the verb.

> 127 Con le due stole nel beato chiostro  
> 128 son le due luci sole che saliro;  
> 129 e questo apporterai nel mondo vostro.”  

With the two robes in the blessed cloister are the two lights alone which rose; and this thou shalt take back into your world.”

- *le due luci sole* ‘the two sole lights’ → “the two lights alone”: the restrictive adjective is rendered by postposed “alone”.
- *apporterai* ‘thou wilt bring’ → “thou shalt take back”: “take” gives the direction from the speaker rather than towards the destination, and “back” makes the return explicit.
- *nel mondo vostro* ‘into the world yours’ → “into your world”: the possessive precedes the noun.

> 130 A questa voce l' infiammato giro  
> 131 si quietò con esso il dolce mischio,  
> 132 che si facea del suon del trino spiro,  

At this voice the flamed circle stilled itself, together with the sweet interlacing made by the sound of the threefold breath,

- *l’ infiammato giro* ‘the enkindled round’ → “the flamed circle”: the participle is rendered by the less usual “flamed”, and “circle” gives the spatial sense of *giro*.
- *con esso il dolce mischio* ‘along with the sweet mingling’ → “together with the sweet interlacing”: “together” conveys the reinforcement of *con* by *esso*; “interlacing” gives the mingling a more specific form.
- *che si facea del suon* ‘which was being made of the sound’ → “made by the sound”: a participle replaces the relative clause and its imperfect verb; the sound is treated as producing the interlacing rather than as its material.

> 133 sì come, per cessar fatica o rischio,  
> 134 li remi, pria nell' acqua ripercossi,  
> 135 tutti si posan al sonar d' un fischio.  

as, to avert or weariness or peril, the oars till now smitten upon the water, all pause at a whistle’s sound.

- *sì come* ‘just as’ → “as”: the reinforcement supplied by *sì* is left out.
- *per cessar fatica o rischio* ‘to put a stop to toil or risk’ → “to avert or weariness or peril”: ending becomes averting, “weariness” gives the effect of exertion, and the single disjunction becomes the paired “or … or”.
- *pria nell’ acqua ripercossi* ‘previously struck back in the water’ → “till now smitten upon the water”: “till now” marks a continuing interval; the participle precedes the prepositional phrase and does not separately express the ‘back’ or ‘again’ of *ri-*; “upon” replaces ‘in’.
- *si posan* ‘come to rest’ → “pause”: the pronominal verb becomes a simple intransitive.
- *al sonar d’ un fischio* ‘at the sounding of a whistle’ → “at a whistle’s sound”: the nominal infinitive becomes a noun, with a preceding possessive instead of a following prepositional phrase.

> 136 Ahi quanto nella mente mi commossi,  
> 137 quando mi volsi per veder Beatrice,  
> 138 per non poter vedere, ben ch' io fossi  
> 139 presso di lei, e nel mondo felice!  

Ah! how was I stirred in my mind, turning to look on Beatrice, for that I might not see her, albeit I was nigh to her and in the world of bliss!

- *quanto nella mente mi commossi* ‘how greatly in the mind I was moved’ → “how was I stirred in my mind”: the degree is expressed by “how” alone; the pronominal preterite is rendered as a passive, and the phrase locating the emotion comes last, with a possessive instead of the article.
- *quando mi volsi* ‘when I turned’ → “turning”: a participle replaces the temporal conjunction and finite reflexive verb.
- *per veder* ‘to see’ → “to look on”: the translation specifies the directed act rather than the perception.
- *per non poter vedere* ‘for not being able to see’ → “for that I might not see her”: the causal infinitive becomes a finite clause, inability is expressed by the modal “might not”, and the object “her” is supplied.
- *ben ch’ io fossi* ‘although I were’ → “albeit I was”: the Italian imperfect subjunctive after the concessive conjunction becomes an indicative.
- *nel mondo felice* ‘in the happy world’ → “in the world of bliss”: the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase with an abstract noun.

## Summary — Order, idiom, and emphasis

Wicksteed often keeps Dante’s sequence of clauses while rearranging the words within them. Object pronouns move after their verbs, adjectives and possessives commonly move before their nouns, and phrases separated in Italian are brought together: in *m’ ha fatto per più anni macro*, “made me lean” stays together and “through many a year” comes last. Yet Wicksteed does not consistently prefer ordinary English order. “Thereafter moved a light” preserves the delayed subject of *Indi si mosse un lume*, and “so did I see” retains the inversion of *così vid’ io* by means of an auxiliary.

A change of construction often matters more than a change of vocabulary. *Gli danno guerra*, ‘give it war’, becomes “war upon it”: the noun supplies the verb, and the indirect object becomes a prepositional phrase. Conversely, *uno attender certo*, ‘a sure waiting’, becomes “a certain expectation”, with an abstract noun in place of the infinitive. A finite clause may shrink to a participle, as *quando mi volsi*, ‘when I turned’, becomes “turning”; an infinitive may also expand into a clause, as *per non poter vedere* becomes “for that I might not see her”, with the object supplied.

Some departures specify a sense rather than reproduce a word: *cappello* becomes “chaplet”, *basilica* becomes “court”, and *laggiù* becomes “upon earth”. Small words may disappear, as the reinforcement in *sì come* does in “as”, or be supplied for emphasis, as “own” is in “its own land”. Two routine adjustments generally pass unremarked in the comparison: English supplies subject pronouns where the Italian verb ending indicates the subject, and omits the Italian article before a possessive, as in *la mia donna*, “my Lady”.

For all these adjustments, Wicksteed stays close to Dante’s images and verbal echoes. *Sommo cantor del sommo duce* remains “the supreme singer of the supreme leader”, with the same adjective repeated in the same relation; *terra* likewise sounds twice in “Earth in the earth”. Even where “with changed voice now, and with changed fleece” interprets *altra* and *altro* as change, it preserves the parallel phrasing and the two concrete nouns.
