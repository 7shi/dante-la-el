# Paradiso 31

## Lines 1–15 — The White Rose and the Angels

> 1 In forma dunque di candida rosa  
> 2 mi si mostrava la milizia santa,  
> 3 che nel suo sangue Cristo fece sposa;  

In form, then, of a white rose displayed itself to me that sacred soldiery which in his blood Christ made his spouse;

- *di candida rosa* ‘of white rose’ → “of a white rose”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *mi si mostrava* ‘to me itself was showing’ → “displayed itself to me”: the verb comes before the reflexive, the indirect object moves to the end, and the simple past does not mark the continuing aspect of the imperfect.
- *la milizia santa* ‘the soldiery holy’ → “that sacred soldiery”: the article becomes a demonstrative, and the adjective, rendered as “sacred”, precedes the noun.
- *fece sposa* ‘made spouse’ → “made his spouse”: the possessive is supplied.

> 4 ma l'altra, che volando vede e canta  
> 5 la gloria di colui che la innamora  
> 6 e la bontà che la fece cotanta,  

but the other, which as it flieth seeth and doth sing his glory who enamoureth it, and the excellence which hath made it what it is,

- *volando* ‘flying’ → “as it flieth”: the gerund becomes a finite clause of time.
- *canta* ‘sings’ → “doth sing”: the simple present is expressed with an auxiliary.
- *la gloria di colui che la innamora* ‘the glory of him who it enamours’ → “his glory who enamoureth it”: the possessor is expressed by “his” before the noun, leaving the relative “who” to follow “glory”; the object pronoun moves after its verb.
- *la bontà* ‘the goodness’ → “the excellence”: the noun is rendered in the sense of excellence rather than goodness.
- *la fece cotanta* ‘it made so great’ → “hath made it what it is”: the object follows the verb, the simple past becomes a present perfect, and the expression of degree becomes a clause without an explicit word for greatness.

> 7 sì come schiera d'api, che s' infiora  
> 8 una fiata ed una si ritorna  
> 9 là dove suo lavoro s' insapora,  

like to a swarm of bees which doth one while plunge into the flowers and another while wend back to where its toil is turned to sweetness,

- *sì come schiera d'api* ‘just as a troop of bees’ → “like to a swarm of bees”: the comparison becomes a prepositional phrase without a separate intensifier; an article is supplied, and “swarm” names the group specifically as bees.
- *s' infiora una fiata* ‘betakes itself among the flowers one time’ → “doth one while plunge into the flowers”: the reflexive verb is expanded into a verb of motion and a phrase naming its destination; the time expression becomes “one while” and moves between the auxiliary and the main verb.
- *ed una si ritorna* ‘and one [time] returns’ → “and another while wend back”: English makes the contrast and the understood time noun explicit; the pronominal verb of return becomes “wend back”, sharing the earlier auxiliary.
- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “to where”: the separate indication of place becomes a preposition of direction.
- *suo lavoro s' insapora* ‘its work takes on flavour’ → “its toil is turned to sweetness”: “toil” stresses the labour, and the pronominal verb of change becomes a passive phrase specifying sweetness rather than flavour generally.

> 10 nel gran fior discendeva, che s'adorna  
> 11 di tante foglie, e quindi risaliva  
> 12 là dove il suo amor sempre soggiorna.  

ever descended into the great flower adorned with so many leaves, and reascended thence to where its love doth ceaseless make sojourn.

- *nel gran fior discendeva* ‘into the great flower it was descending’ → “ever descended into the great flower”: the verb moves before the destination, and “ever” brings out the repeated action of the imperfect.
- *che s'adorna* ‘which adorns itself’ → “adorned”: the finite reflexive relative clause becomes a passive participle.
- *quindi risaliva* ‘thence it was going up again’ → “reascended thence”: the adverb moves after the verb, and the simple past continues the iterative sense established by “ever”.
- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “to where”: the demonstrative of place gives way to a preposition of direction.
- *sempre soggiorna* ‘always sojourns’ → “doth ceaseless make sojourn”: “ceaseless” expresses continuity in place of ‘always’, and the simple verb becomes an auxiliary and a verb–noun phrase.

> 13 Le facce tutte avean di fiamma viva,  
> 14 e l'ali d'oro, e l'altro tanto bianco  
> 15 che nulla neve a quel termine arriva.  

They had their faces all of living flame, and wings of gold, and the rest so white that never snow reacheth such limit.

- *Le facce tutte avean* ‘the faces all they had’ → “They had their faces all”: the verb moves ahead of its object, and the definite article with a body part becomes a possessive.
- *di fiamma viva* ‘of flame living’ → “of living flame”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *l'ali* ‘the wings’ → “wings”: the article is dropped.
- *l'altro* ‘the other [part]’ → “the rest”: the substantivized adjective becomes a noun denoting what remains.
- *nulla neve* ‘no snow’ → “never snow”: the negative determiner becomes a negative adverb of time.
- *a quel termine arriva* ‘at that limit arrives’ → “reacheth such limit”: the verb precedes the limit and takes it as a direct object rather than a prepositional complement; “such” expresses degree in place of the demonstrative ‘that’.

## Lines 16–48 — Looking Upon the Heavenly Flower

> 16 Quando scendean nel fior, di banco in banco  
> 17 porgevan della pace e dell'ardore,  
> 18 ch' egli acquistavan ventilando il fianco,  

When they descended into the flower, from rank to rank they proffered of the peace and of the ardour which they acquired as they fanned their sides,

- *di banco in banco* ‘from bench to bench’ → “from rank to rank”: the rows are named rather than the seats.
- *ventilando il fianco* ‘fanning the flank’ → “as they fanned their sides”: the gerund becomes a finite clause of time; the singular body part, understood of each separately, becomes plural with a possessive.

> 19 nè lo interporsi tra il di sopra e il fiore  
> 20 di tanta plenitudine volante  
> 21 impediva la vista e lo splendore;  

nor did the interposing of so great a flying multitude, betwixt the flower and that which was above, impede the vision nor the splendour;

- *nè … impediva* ‘nor … impeded’ → “nor did” … “impede”: the negative opening calls for inversion with an auxiliary in English.
- *lo interporsi* ‘the putting of itself between’ → “the interposing”: the verbal noun is retained without the reflexive pronoun.
- *tra il di sopra e il fiore* ‘between the above and the flower’ → “betwixt the flower and that which was above”: the terms change places, and the substantivized adverb is expanded into a relative construction.
- *di tanta plenitudine volante* ‘of so great a fullness flying’ → “of so great a flying multitude”: the abstract noun becomes a collective one, the modifier precedes it, and the whole phrase comes immediately after “the interposing”.
- *e lo splendore* ‘and the splendour’ → “nor the splendour”: the negative coordination is made explicit at the second object.

> 22 chè la luce divina è penetrante  
> 23 per l'universo, secondo ch' è degno,  
> 24 sì che nulla le puote essere ostante.  

for the divine light so penetrateth through the universe, in measure of its worthiness, that nought hath power to oppose it.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating explanation replaces the subordinate one.
- *la luce divina* ‘the light divine’ → “the divine light”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *è penetrante* ‘is penetrating’ → “penetrateth”: a simple verb replaces the copula and participial adjective.
- *secondo ch' è degno* ‘according as it is worthy’ → “in measure of its worthiness”: a clause becomes a prepositional phrase, with an abstract noun in place of the adjective.
- *sì che* ‘so that’ → “so” … “that”: the degree word moves before “penetrateth”, leaving “that” to introduce the result.
- *le puote essere ostante* ‘to it can be obstructive’ → “hath power to oppose it”: ability is expressed by a noun phrase, and a transitive infinitive with a following object replaces the predicative expression with a preceding dative.

> 25 Questo sicuro e gaudioso regno,  
> 26 frequente in gente antica ed in novella,  
> 27 viso ed amore avea tutto ad un segno.  

This realm, secure and gladsome, thronged with ancient folk and new, had look and love all turned unto one mark.

- *Questo sicuro e gaudioso regno* ‘this secure and gladsome realm’ → “This realm, secure and gladsome”: the adjectives follow the noun.
- *frequente in* ‘populous in’ → “thronged with”: a participial expression with a different preposition replaces the adjective.
- *gente antica ed in novella* ‘folk ancient and in new’ → “ancient folk and new”: the first adjective precedes the noun, and the preposition is not repeated.
- *viso ed amore avea* ‘gaze and love had’ → “had look and love”: the verb moves before its objects.
- *tutto ad un segno* ‘all at one mark’ → “all turned unto one mark”: “turned” is supplied to express direction.

> 28 O trina luce, che in unica stella  
> 29 scintillando a lor vista sì gli appaga,  
> 30 guarda quaggiù alla nostra procella.  

O threefold light, which in a single star, glinting upon their sight doth so content them, look down upon our storm!

- *in unica stella* ‘in single star’ → “in a single star”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *a lor vista* ‘to their sight’ → “upon their sight”: the preposition is adapted to “glinting”.
- *sì gli appaga* ‘so them contents’ → “doth so content them”: the auxiliary is supplied, and the object pronoun follows the main verb.
- *guarda quaggiù alla nostra procella* ‘look down here at our storm’ → “look down upon our storm”: the indication of place loses its explicit ‘here’, and “upon” introduces the object of the look.

> 31 Se i Barbari, venendo di tal plaga,  
> 32 che ciascun giorno d' Elice si copra,  
> 33 rotante col suo figlio ond' ell' è vaga,  

If the Barbarians coming from such region as every day is spanned by Helice, wheeling with her son towards whom she yearneth,

- *di tal plaga, che ciascun giorno d' Elice si copra* ‘from such a region as each day covers itself with Helice’ → “from such region as every day is spanned by Helice”: the reflexive subjunctive becomes an English passive indicative; covering is interpreted as spanning, and Helice becomes the agent.
- *ond' ell' è vaga* ‘of whom she is desirous’ → “towards whom she yearneth”: a verb of longing replaces the copula and adjective, and the relation is expressed as direction.

> 34 vedendo Roma e l'ardua sua opra  
> 35 stupefaciensi, quando Laterano  
> 36 alle cose mortali andò di sopra;  

on seeing Rome and her mighty works—what time the Lateran transcended mortal things—were stupified;

- *vedendo* ‘seeing’ → “on seeing”: the preposition makes the temporal relation explicit.
- *l'ardua sua opra* ‘her arduous work’ → “her mighty works”: the adjective expresses magnitude rather than difficulty, and the singular becomes plural.
- *stupefaciensi* ‘they grew stupefied’ → “were stupified”: the pronominal verb becomes a passive form, deferred until after the time clause.
- *quando* ‘when’ → “what time”: a temporal noun phrase serves as the conjunction.
- *alle cose mortali andò di sopra* ‘above mortal things went’ → “transcended mortal things”: the expression of motion becomes a transitive verb, and its object follows it.

> 37 io, che al divino dall'umano,  
> 38 all'eterno dal tempo era venuto,  
> 39 e di Fiorenza in popol giusto e sano,  

what then of me, who to the divine from the human, to the eternal from time had passed, and from Florence to a people just and sane,

- *io* ‘I’ → “what then of me”: the subject of the coming question becomes the object of a preposition in a supplied preliminary question.
- *era venuto* ‘had come’ → “had passed”: the pluperfect is retained, but passage replaces coming.
- *in popol giusto e sano* ‘into a people just and sound’ → “to a people just and sane”: “to” marks the destination, and “sane” narrows the sense of soundness.

> 40 di che stupor dovea esser compiuto!  

with what stupor must I needs be filled!

- *di che stupor … esser compiuto* ‘of what stupor … be full’ → “with what stupor” … “be filled”: the English participle takes “with” rather than ‘of’.
- *dovea* ‘I must have’ → “must I needs”: English inverts subject and modal in the question and reinforces necessity with “needs”, without a distinct past-tense form.

> 41 certo tra esso e il gaudio mi facea  
> 42 libito il non udire, e starmi muto.  

verily, what with it and what with joy, my will was to hear nought and to be dumb myself.

- *tra esso e il gaudio* ‘between it and the joy’ → “what with it and what with joy”: the repeated idiom makes the two feelings contributing causes, and the article before ‘joy’ disappears.
- *mi facea libito* ‘made it pleasing to me’ → “my will was”: the experiencer becomes a possessor, and a statement of inclination replaces the construction with ‘made’.
- *il non udire* ‘the not hearing’ → “to hear nought”: the substantivized infinitive loses its article, and the negation becomes the verb’s object.
- *starmi muto* ‘to stay mute’ → “to be dumb myself”: being replaces remaining, and the pronominal element is represented by an emphatic “myself”.

> 43 E quasi peregrin, che si ricrea  
> 44 nel tempio del suo voto riguardando,  
> 45 e spera già ridir com' ello stea,  

As the pilgrim who doth draw fresh life in the temple of his vow as he gazeth, and already hopeth to tell again how it be placed,

- *E quasi peregrin* ‘and like a pilgrim’ → “As the pilgrim”: the initial conjunction drops out, and the generic noun takes the definite article in English.
- *si ricrea* ‘refreshes himself’ → “doth draw fresh life”: an expression with a new object replaces the reflexive verb.
- *riguardando* ‘gazing’ → “as he gazeth”: the gerund becomes a finite temporal clause.
- *spera già* ‘hopes already’ → “already hopeth”: the adverb precedes the verb.
- *ridir* ‘to retell’ → “to tell again”: a separate adverb represents the verbal prefix.
- *com' ello stea* ‘how it stands’ → “how it be placed”: the subjunctive is retained, but the verb of standing becomes a passive expression of disposition.

> 46 sì per la viva luce passeggiando,  
> 47 menava io gli occhi per li gradi,  
> 48 mo su, mo giù, e mo ricirculando.  

so, traversing the living light, I led mine eyes along the ranks, now up, now down, and now round circling.

- *per la viva luce passeggiando* ‘through the living light walking’ → “traversing the living light”: the participle moves to the front, and its transitive construction absorbs the preposition.
- *menava io gli occhi* ‘led I the eyes’ → “I led mine eyes”: the expressed subject moves before the verb, and a possessive replaces the article.
- *per li gradi* ‘through the steps’ → “along the ranks”: the steps are rendered as rows, and “along” describes the course of the gaze.
- *ricirculando* ‘circling about’ → “round circling”: a separate adverb before the participle gives the circular motion.

## Lines 49–81 — Dante Looks Up to Beatrice

> 49 Vedea di carità visi suadi,  
> 50 d'altrui lume fregiati e del suo riso,  
> 51 ed atti ornati di tutte onestadi.  

I saw countenances suasive of love, adorned by another’s light and their own smile, and gestures graced with every dignity.

- *Vedea* ‘I was seeing’ → “I saw”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *di carità visi suadi* ‘of charity countenances suasive’ → “countenances suasive of love”: the complement of the adjective moves from before the noun to after the adjective; ‘charity’ is rendered more broadly as “love”.
- *d'altrui lume fregiati e del suo riso* ‘with another’s light adorned and with their smile’ → “adorned by another’s light and their own smile”: the participle comes before both complements; one “by” serves for both Italian prepositions, and “own” makes the contrast with “another’s” explicit.
- *ed atti ornati* ‘and acts adorned’ → “and gestures graced”: ‘acts’ is narrowed to visible gestures, and the participle is varied to “graced”.
- *di tutte onestadi* ‘with all dignities’ → “with every dignity”: the plural with ‘all’ becomes a distributive singular.

> 52 La forma general di Paradiso  
> 53 già tutta mio sguardo avea compresa,  
> 54 e in nulla parte ancor fermato il viso;  

The general form of Paradise my glance had already taken in, in its entirety, and on no part as yet had my sight paused;

- *La forma general di Paradiso* ‘the form general of Paradise’ → “The general form of Paradise”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *già tutta mio sguardo avea compresa* ‘already all of it my gaze had comprehended’ → “my glance had already taken in, in its entirety”: ‘gaze’ becomes “glance”; the adverb moves inside the verb phrase, ‘comprehended’ becomes the phrasal verb “taken in”, and the adjective agreeing with the object becomes a phrase after the verb.
- *e in nulla parte ancor* ‘and in no part yet’ → “and on no part as yet”: English uses “on” for the point where the gaze rests, and expands ‘yet’ to “as yet”.
- *fermato il viso* ‘fixed my gaze’ → “had my sight paused”: Italian carries forward the auxiliary and subject of ‘had comprehended’ and makes ‘gaze’ the object of ‘fixed’; English repeats the auxiliary and makes “my sight” the subject of an intransitive verb, inverted after the negative phrase.

> 55 e volgeami con voglia riaccesa  
> 56 per domandar la mia donna di cose,  
> 57 di che la mente mia era sospesa.  

and I turned me with rekindled will to question my Lady concerning things whereanent my mind was in suspense.

- *e volgeami* ‘and I was turning myself’ → “and I turned me”: the imperfect becomes a simple past; “me” serves as the reflexive.
- *con voglia riaccesa* ‘with desire rekindled’ → “with rekindled will”: ‘desire’ becomes “will”, and the participle precedes its noun.
- *per domandar la mia donna di cose* ‘in order to ask my lady about things’ → “to question my Lady concerning things”: a plain infinitive expresses purpose, and ‘ask … about’ becomes “question … concerning”.
- *di che* ‘about which’ → “whereanent”: preposition and relative pronoun are compressed into a relative adverb.
- *era sospesa* ‘was suspended’ → “was in suspense”: the participial predicate becomes a prepositional phrase.

> 58 Uno intendea, ed altro mi rispose;  

One thing I purposed, and another answered me;

- *Uno intendea* ‘one I intended’ → “One thing I purposed”: “thing” supplies a noun for the substantival numeral; ‘intended’ becomes “purposed”, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.

> 59 credea veder Beatrice, e vidi un sene  
> 60 vestito con le genti gloriose.  

I thought to see Beatrice, and I saw an elder clad like the folk in glory.

- *credea veder* ‘I was expecting to see’ → “I thought to see”: the expectation is expressed by “thought” with an infinitive, and the imperfect by a simple past.
- *un sene* ‘an old man’ → “an elder”: a single English noun conveys the description.
- *vestito con le genti gloriose* ‘clad with the glorious folk’ → “clad like the folk in glory”: “like” interprets the association as likeness of dress, and the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.

> 61 Diffuso era per gli occhi e per le gene  
> 62 di benigna letizia, in atto pio,  
> 63 quale a tenero padre si conviene.  

His eyes and cheeks were overpoured with benign gladness, in kindly gesture as befits a tender father.

- *Diffuso era per gli occhi e per le gene* ‘overspread was he, over the eyes and over the cheeks’ → “His eyes and cheeks were overpoured”: the man is the Italian subject, the eyes and cheeks complements of place; English makes the features the subject, marks their possessor once, drops the repeated preposition, and puts the participle after the auxiliary.
- *in atto pio* ‘in an attitude compassionate’ → “in kindly gesture”: “gesture” replaces ‘attitude’, and the adjective, rendered “kindly”, precedes it.
- *quale a tenero padre si conviene* ‘such as to a tender father is fitting’ → “as befits a tender father”: “as” links the comparison without an explicit ‘such’; a transitive verb replaces the pronominal verb with its prepositional complement, and the father follows the verb.

> 64 Ed: “Ella ov' è?” di subito diss' io;  

And: “Where is she?” all sudden I exclaimed;

- *Ella ov' è?* ‘she where is?’ → “Where is she?”: the interrogative comes first and the subject follows the verb.
- *di subito* ‘suddenly’ → “all sudden”: an adverbial idiom with “all” replaces the Italian prepositional idiom.
- *diss' io* ‘said I’ → “I exclaimed”: the subject precedes the verb, and ‘said’ becomes the more emphatic “exclaimed”.

> 65 ond'egli: “A terminar lo tuo disiro  
> 66 mosse Beatrice me del loco mio;  

whereunto he: “To bring thy desire to its goal Beatrice moved me from my place;

- *A terminar lo tuo disiro* ‘to bring to completion thy desire’ → “To bring thy desire to its goal”: the single infinitive is expanded into a verb followed by its object and a goal phrase; “its” is supplied.
- *mosse Beatrice me* ‘moved Beatrice me’ → “Beatrice moved me”: the subject is moved before the verb.

> 67 e se riguardi su nel terzo giro  
> 68 del sommo grado, tu la rivedrai  
> 69 nel trono che i suoi merti le sortiro.”  

and if thou look up to the circle third from the highest rank, thou shalt re-behold her, on the throne her merits have assigned to her.”

- *e se riguardi su nel terzo giro* ‘and if thou look up in the third circle’ → “and if thou look up to the circle third”: “to” marks the direction of the look rather than the place in which to look; the ordinal follows the noun.
- *del sommo grado* ‘of the highest rank’ → “from the highest rank”: “from” interprets the genitive as giving the point from which to count.
- *tu la rivedrai* ‘thou her wilt see again’ → “thou shalt re-behold her”: the object follows the verb, and ‘see again’ is conveyed by prefixing “re-” to the more formal “behold”.
- *nel trono* ‘in the throne’ → “on the throne”: the English idiom changes the preposition.
- *che i suoi merti le sortiro* ‘which her merits to her allotted’ → “her merits have assigned to her”: the object relative is left unexpressed; the simple past becomes a present perfect, ‘allotted’ becomes “assigned”, and the indirect object follows the verb as a prepositional phrase.

> 70 Senza risponder gli occhi su levai,  
> 71 e vidi lei che si facea corona,  
> 72 riflettendo da sè gli eterni rai.  

Without answering I lifted up mine eyes and saw her, making to herself a crown as she reflected from her the eternal rays.

- *Senza risponder* ‘without to answer’ → “Without answering”: English uses the gerund after the preposition in place of the infinitive.
- *gli occhi su levai* ‘the eyes up I lifted’ → “I lifted up mine eyes”: the verb comes before the particle and object, and the possessive replaces the article.
- *che si facea corona* ‘who was making herself a crown’ → “making to herself a crown”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase, and “to” makes the reflexive’s indirect-object function explicit.
- *riflettendo da sè* ‘reflecting from herself’ → “as she reflected from her”: the Italian participle becomes a finite clause with “as”, and an ordinary personal pronoun replaces the reflexive.

> 73 Da quella region, che più su tuona,  
> 74 occhio mortale alcun tanto non dista,  
> 75 qualunque in mare più giù s'abbandona,  
> 76 quanto lì da Beatrice la mia vista;  

From that region which thundereth most high, no mortal eye is so far distant, though plunged most deep within the sea, as there from Beatrice was my sight;

- *che più su tuona* ‘which highest up thunders’ → “which thundereth most high”: the expression of height follows the verb.
- *occhio mortale alcun tanto non dista* ‘eye mortal any so much does not stand distant’ → “no mortal eye is so far distant”: the negative verb with ‘any’ becomes an affirmative predicate with “no” on the subject; the adjective precedes the noun, and the single verb of distance becomes “is so far distant”.
- *qualunque in mare più giù s'abbandona* ‘whichever in the sea furthest down lets itself go’ → “though plunged most deep within the sea”: the indefinite relative clause becomes a concessive participial phrase, the reflexive gives way to a passive, and the phrase of place follows the expression of depth.
- *quanto lì da Beatrice la mia vista* ‘as much as there from Beatrice my sight’ → “as there from Beatrice was my sight”: “as” completes the comparison with “so far”; “was” supplies a verb where Italian leaves the predicate understood.

> 77 ma nulla mi facea, chè sua effige  
> 78 non discendeva a me per mezzo mista.  

but that wrought not upon me, for her image descended not to me mingled with any medium.

- *ma nulla mi facea* ‘but nothing it did to me’ → “but that wrought not upon me”: “that” supplies an explicit subject, the negative object becomes a negation of the verb, and the clitic becomes a phrase with “upon”; the imperfect is rendered by a simple past.
- *chè sua effige non discendeva a me* ‘because her image was not descending to me’ → “for her image descended not to me”: the causal subordinator becomes “for”, the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the negative follows the verb.
- *per mezzo mista* ‘through a medium mingled’ → “mingled with any medium”: the participle comes first; “with” treats the medium as that with which the image would be mingled rather than that through which it would pass, and “any” makes the scope of the negative explicit.

> 79 “O donna, in cui la mia speranza vige,  
> 80 e che soffristi per la mia salute  
> 81 in Inferno lasciar le tue vestige;  

“O Lady, in whom my hope hath vigour, and who for my salvation didst endure to leave in Hell thy footprints;

- *vige* ‘flourishes’ → “hath vigour”: a single verb becomes a verb with a noun as its object.
- *e che soffristi per la mia salute* ‘and who didst suffer for my salvation’ → “and who for my salvation didst endure”: the purpose phrase is moved before the verb, and ‘suffer’ is rendered “endure”.
- *in Inferno lasciar* ‘in Hell to leave’ → “to leave in Hell”: the infinitive precedes the phrase of place.
- *le tue vestige* ‘thy traces’ → “thy footprints”: the broader noun is given a specifically bodily sense.

## Lines 82–114 — Dante’s Prayer to Beatrice and Bernard’s Guidance

> 82 di tante cose, quante io ho vedute,  
> 83 dal tuo potere e dalla tua bontate  
> 84 riconosco la grazia e la virtute.  

of all the things which I have seen I recognise the grace and might, by thy power and by thine excellence.

- *di tante cose, quante io ho vedute* ‘of as many things as I have seen’ → “of all the things which I have seen”: the correlation of quantity becomes “all” with a relative clause.
- *dal tuo potere e dalla tua bontate* ‘from thy power and from thy goodness’ → “by thy power and by thine excellence”: “by” marks agency rather than source, “excellence” renders ‘goodness’, and the phrase moves from before the verb to the end of the clause.
- *la grazia e la virtute* ‘the grace and the might’ → “the grace and might”: the two nouns share one article in English.

> 85 Tu m' hai di servo tratto a libertate  
> 86 per tutte quelle vie, per tutti i modi,  
> 87 che di ciò fare avei la potestate.  

Thou hast drawn me from a slave to liberty by all those paths, by all those methods by which thou hadst the power so to do.

- *Tu m' hai di servo tratto a libertate* ‘thou me hast from a slave drawn to liberty’ → “Thou hast drawn me from a slave to liberty”: the object moves after the participle, and the phrase of departure no longer separates auxiliary and participle.
- *per tutti i modi* ‘by all the ways’ → “by all those methods”: the demonstrative replaces the article, echoing “those paths”; “methods” specifies ways of acting.
- *che di ciò fare avei la potestate* ‘that to do this thou hadst the power’ → “by which thou hadst the power so to do”: the relative makes the instrumental relation explicit; the infinitive follows “power”, and its object ‘this’ becomes the adverb “so”.

> 88 La tua magnificenza in me custodi  
> 89 sì che l'anima mia, che fatta hai sana,  
> 90 piacente a te dal corpo si disnodi.”  

Preserve thy munificence in me, so that my soul which thou hast made sound, may unloose it from the body, pleasing unto thee.”

- *La tua magnificenza in me custodi* ‘thy magnificence in me preserve’ → “Preserve thy munificence in me”: the petition puts the verb first, and “munificence” gives ‘magnificence’ the particular sense of generosity.
- *che fatta hai sana* ‘which made thou hast sound’ → “which thou hast made sound”: the auxiliary precedes the participle.
- *piacente a te* ‘pleasing to thee’ → “pleasing unto thee”: the phrase moves from before the verb to the end of the clause.
- *dal corpo si disnodi* ‘from the body it unbind itself’ → “may unloose it from the body”: “may” conveys the subjunctive; the reflexive is rendered by the object “it”, and the phrase of separation follows the verb.

> 91 Così orai; ed ella sì lontana,  
> 92 come parea, sorrise, e riguardommi;  
> 93 poi si tornò all'eterna fontana.  

So did I pray; and she, so distant as she seemed, smiled and looked on me, then turned her to the eternal fountain.

- *Così orai* ‘so I prayed’ → “So did I pray”: the simple past becomes an inverted construction with “did”.
- *sì lontana, come parea* ‘so far away, as she appeared’ → “so distant as she seemed”: the adjective “distant” renders the expression of distance.
- *riguardommi* ‘looked at me’ → “looked on me”: English uses a prepositional phrase for the attached object pronoun.
- *si tornò* ‘turned herself back’ → “turned her”: the reflexive follows the verb as “her”, and the sense of turning back is left implicit.

> 94 E il santo sene: “Acciocchè tu assommi  
> 95 perfettamente, disse, il tuo cammino,  
> 96 a che prego ed amor santo mandommi,  

And the holy elder said: “That thou mayest consummate thy journey perfectly—whereto prayer and holy love dispatched me,—

- *E il santo sene: “Acciocchè tu assommi perfettamente, disse, il tuo cammino* ‘and the holy elder: In order that thou consummate perfectly, said he, thy journey’ → “And the holy elder said: “That thou mayest consummate thy journey perfectly”: the reporting verb moves from inside the speech to the introductory clause, “mayest” conveys the subjunctive of purpose, and the adverb follows the object.
- *a che* ‘to which’ → “whereto”: a single relative adverb renders the preposition and relative pronoun.
- *amor santo* ‘love holy’ → “holy love”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 97 vola con gli occhi per questo giardino;  
> 98 chè veder lui t'acconcerà lo sguardo  
> 99 più al montar per lo raggio divino.  

fly with thine eyes throughout this garden; for gazing on it will equip thy glance better to mount through the divine ray.

- *per questo giardino* ‘through this garden’ → “throughout this garden”: “throughout” stresses the extent of the movement.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating conjunction replaces the subordinating one.
- *veder lui* ‘to see it’ → “gazing on it”: the subject infinitive becomes an *-ing* form, and simple seeing becomes sustained looking.
- *t'acconcerà lo sguardo più* ‘will better fit the gaze for thee’ → “will equip thy glance better”: the personal pronoun becomes a possessive modifying “glance”; “better” gives the comparative force of ‘more’.
- *al montar* ‘for the mounting’ → “to mount”: the preposition and substantivized infinitive become an English infinitive.
- *per lo raggio divino* ‘through the ray divine’ → “through the divine ray”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 100 E la Regina del cielo, ond' i' ardo  
> 101 tutto d'amor, ne farà ogni grazia,  
> 102 però ch' io sono il suo fedel Bernardo.”  

And the Queen of heaven for whom I am all burning with love, will grant us every grace, because I am her faithful Bernard.”

- *ond' i' ardo tutto d'amor* ‘for whom I burn wholly with love’ → “for whom I am all burning with love”: the simple present becomes progressive, and the intensifier moves before “burning”.
- *ne farà ogni grazia* ‘to us will do every grace’ → “will grant us every grace”: the idiom of doing a grace becomes one of granting it, with the recipient after the verb.
- *però ch'* ‘for the reason that’ → “because”: a single conjunction renders the connective phrase.

> 103 Quale è colui, che forse di Croazia  
> 104 viene a veder la Veronica nostra,  
> 105 che per l'antica fama non si sazia,  

As is he who perchance from Croatia cometh to look on our Veronica and because of ancient fame is sated not,

- *viene a veder* ‘comes to see’ → “cometh to look on”: “look on” makes the act of looking more explicit.
- *la Veronica nostra* ‘the Veronica of ours’ → “our Veronica”: the postposed possessive becomes a determiner before the noun.
- *che per l'antica fama* ‘who because of the ancient fame’ → “and because of ancient fame”: coordination replaces the renewed relative clause, and the article before ‘fame’ drops out.
- *non si sazia* ‘does not sate himself’ → “is sated not”: the reflexive construction becomes passive, with the negative following the participle.

> 106 ma dice nel pensier, fin che si mostra:  
> 107 “Signor mio Gesù Cristo, Dio verace,  
> 108 or fu sì fatta la sembianza vostra?”  

but saith in thought, so long as it be shown; “My Lord Jesus Christ, true God, and was this, then, the fashion of thy semblance?”

- *nel pensier* ‘in the thought’ → “in thought”: English uses the phrase without an article.
- *fin che si mostra* ‘as long as it shows itself’ → “so long as it be shown”: the Italian pronominal construction is read passively, and the indicative becomes an English subjunctive.
- *Signor mio* ‘Lord of mine’ → “My Lord”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *Dio verace* ‘God truthful’ → “true God”: “true” renders ‘truthful’ and precedes the noun.
- *or fu sì fatta la sembianza vostra?* ‘now was thy semblance thus fashioned?’ → “and was this, then, the fashion of thy semblance?”: “and” is supplied, and “then” renders the question-opening particle. The description ‘thus fashioned’ is recast with the subject “this” and the noun “fashion”; the possessive precedes “semblance”.

> 109 tale era io mirando la vivace  
> 110 carità di colui, che in questo mondo,  
> 111 contemplando, gustò di quella pace.  

such was I, gazing upon the living love of him who in this world by contemplation tasted of that peace.

- *mirando* ‘beholding’ → “gazing upon”: a verb taking a direct object becomes one taking a preposition.
- *la vivace carità* ‘the living charity’ → “the living love”: “love” renders the more specific ‘charity’.
- *contemplando* ‘contemplating’ → “by contemplation”: the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase expressing means.

> 112 “Figliuol di grazia, questo esser giocondo,  
> 113 cominciò egli, non ti sarà noto  
> 114 tenendo gli occhi pur quaggiù al fondo;  

“Son of grace! this joyous being,” he began, “will not become known to thee by holding thine eyes only here down at the base;

- *questo esser giocondo* ‘this being joyous’ → “this joyous being”: the adjective modifying the substantivized infinitive comes before it.
- *cominciò egli* ‘began he’ → “he began”: the subject precedes the reporting verb.
- *non ti sarà noto* ‘will not be known to thee’ → “will not become known to thee”: “become” makes a change of state explicit, and the personal pronoun follows “known” in a prepositional phrase.
- *tenendo gli occhi* ‘holding the eyes’ → “by holding thine eyes”: “by” marks means, and a possessive replaces the article before the part of the body.

## Lines 115–142 — The Queen Amid the Angels

> 115 ma guarda i cerchi fino al più remoto,  
> 116 tanto che veggi seder la Regina,  
> 117 cui questo regno è suddito e devoto.”  

but look upon the circles, even to the remotest, until thou seest enthroned the Queen to whom this realm is subject and devoted.”

- *guarda i cerchi* ‘look at the circles’ → “look upon the circles”: the Italian verb takes a direct object; English uses a preposition.
- *fino al più remoto* ‘as far as the most remote’ → “even to the remotest”: “even” emphasizes the limit; the superlative is expressed by an ending rather than a separate word.
- *tanto che veggi* ‘so long that thou mayest see’ → “until thou seest”: the phrase expressing the limit of the looking becomes a single conjunction of time; the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *seder la Regina* ‘sit the Queen’ → “enthroned the Queen”: the infinitive after the verb of seeing becomes a participle, and the throne is specified rather than implied.

> 118 Io levai gli occhi; e come da mattina  
> 119 le parti oriental dell' orizzonte  
> 120 soperchian quella dove il sol declina,  

I lifted up mine eyes, and as at morn the oriental regions of the horizon overcome that where the sun declineth,

- *Io levai gli occhi* ‘I raised the eyes’ → “I lifted up mine eyes”: the verb is expanded with “up”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *le parti oriental* ‘the parts eastern’ → “the oriental regions”: “regions” replaces ‘parts’, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *soperchian* ‘surpass’ → “overcome”: a verb of prevailing over replaces one of exceeding.

> 121 così, quasi di valle andando a monte,  
> 122 con gli occhi vidi parte nello estremo  
> 123 vincer di lume tutta l'altra fronte.  

so, as from the valley rising to the mountain; with mine eyes I saw a region at the boundary surpass all the remaining ridge in light.

- *quasi di valle andando a monte* ‘as if from valley going to mountain’ → “as from the valley rising to the mountain”: “as” introduces the comparison without an explicit ‘if’; “rising” gives the direction of the going, and both nouns acquire articles.
- *con gli occhi* ‘with the eyes’ → “with mine eyes”: the possessive replaces the definite article.
- *parte nello estremo* ‘part at the extremity’ → “a region at the boundary”: the bare noun acquires an article and becomes “region”; the extreme limit is called a “boundary”.
- *vincer di lume tutta l'altra fronte* ‘outdo in light all the other front’ → “surpass all the remaining ridge in light”: the specification “in light” moves after the object; “remaining” interprets ‘other’ as the rest, and “ridge” gives ‘front’ a topographical sense.

> 124 E come quivi, ove s' aspetta il temo  
> 125 che mal guidò Fetonte, più s' infiamma,  
> 126 e quinci e quindi il lume è fatto scemo;  

And as with us that place where we await the chariot pole that Phaëton guided ill, is most aglow, and on this side and on that the light is shorn away;

- *quivi, ove* ‘there, where’ → “with us that place where”: the adverb of place is recast as a noun phrase that can serve as subject, and “with us” is supplied.
- *s' aspetta il temo* ‘one awaits the pole’ → “we await the chariot pole”: the impersonal construction becomes first-person plural, and “chariot” specifies the kind of pole.
- *che mal guidò Fetonte* ‘that ill guided Phaëton’ → “that Phaëton guided ill”: the subject moves before the verb and the adverb after it.
- *più s' infiamma* ‘more it kindles’ → “is most aglow”: the comparative is taken in a superlative sense, and a change of brightness becomes a state.
- *quinci e quindi* ‘hereabouts and thereabouts’ → “on this side and on that”: the paired adverbs become prepositional phrases specifying sides.
- *è fatto scemo* ‘is made less’ → “is shorn away”: verb and adjective become a passive verb suggesting subtraction.

> 127 così quella pacifica oriafiamma  
> 128 nel mezzo s' avvivava, e d' ogni parte  
> 129 per egual modo allentava la fiamma.  

so was that pacific oriflame quickened in the midst, on either side in equal measure tempering its flame.

- *quella pacifica oriafiamma nel mezzo s' avvivava* ‘that pacific oriflame in the middle grew more vivid’ → “was that pacific oriflame quickened in the midst”: the pronominal imperfect becomes a passive, with the auxiliary before the subject and the phrase of place after the participle.
- *e d' ogni parte* ‘and on every side’ → “on either side”: the conjunction drops out, and “either” presents the sides as two.
- *per egual modo* ‘in equal manner’ → “in equal measure”: degree replaces manner.
- *allentava la fiamma* ‘it slackened the flame’ → “tempering its flame”: the second finite imperfect becomes a participle, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 130 Ed a quel mezzo, con le penne sparte,  
> 131 vidi più di mille Angeli festanti,  
> 132 ciascun distinto e di fulgore e d'arte.  

And at that mid point, with outstretched wings, I saw more than a thousand Angels making festival, each one distinct in glow and art.

- *a quel mezzo* ‘at that middle’ → “at that mid point”: “point” gives the substantivized ‘middle’ an explicit noun.
- *con le penne sparte* ‘with the feathers spread’ → “with outstretched wings”: the feathers stand for the whole wings; the article disappears and the participle precedes the noun.
- *festanti* ‘rejoicing’ → “making festival”: a single participial adjective becomes a participial phrase.
- *ciascun* ‘each’ → “each one”: “one” makes the pronominal use explicit.
- *e di fulgore e d'arte* ‘both in brilliance and in art’ → “in glow and art”: the first member loses its introductory conjunction, one preposition serves both nouns, and “glow” renders ‘brilliance’.

> 133 Vidi quivi ai lor giochi ed ai lor canti  
> 134 ridere una bellezza, che letizia  
> 135 era negli occhi a tutti gli altri santi.  

I saw there, smiling to their sports and to their songs, a beauty which was gladness in the eyes of all the other saints.

- *ai lor giochi ed ai lor canti ridere* ‘at their games and at their songs smile’ → “smiling to their sports and to their songs”: the infinitive dependent on the verb of seeing becomes a participle and moves before its complements; “to” presents the smile as a response, and “sports” renders ‘games’.
- *letizia era* ‘gladness was’ → “was gladness”: the predicate noun moves after the verb.
- *negli occhi a tutti gli altri santi* ‘in the eyes, to all the other saints’ → “in the eyes of all the other saints”: the Italian dative of the possessors becomes an English phrase with “of”.

> 136 E s' io avessi in dir tanta divizia,  
> 137 quanto ad imaginar, non ardirei  
> 138 lo minimo tentar di sua delizia.  

And had I equal wealth in speech as in conception, yet dared I not attempt the smallest part of her delightsomeness.

- *E s' io avessi* ‘and if I had’ → “And had I”: inversion expresses the condition without ‘if’.
- *in dir tanta divizia, quanto ad imaginar* ‘in speaking as much wealth as in imagining’ → “equal wealth in speech as in conception”: “equal” expresses the comparison of amount; the wealth comes before its first qualification, and nouns replace both infinitives.
- *non ardirei* ‘I would not dare’ → “yet dared I not”: “yet” is supplied, and an inverted form of ‘dare’ replaces the explicit conditional.
- *lo minimo tentar* ‘the least to attempt’ → “attempt the smallest part”: the object moves after the verb, and “part” supplies a noun for the substantivized superlative.

> 139 Bernardo, come vide gli occhi miei  
> 140 nel caldo suo calor fissi ed attenti,  
> 141 li suoi con tanto affetto volse a lei,  
> 142 che i miei di rimirar fe' più ardenti.  

Bernard, when he saw mine eyes fixed and eager towards the glowing source of his own glow, turned his eyes to her, with so much love that he made mine more ardent to re-gaze.

- *gli occhi miei* ‘the eyes of mine’ → “mine eyes”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *nel caldo suo calor* ‘in his warm heat’ → “towards the glowing source of his own glow”: location becomes direction; the translation distinguishes a “source” from the “glow” it produces, supplies the emphasis of “own”, and places the phrase after the description of the eyes.
- *fissi ed attenti* ‘fixed and attentive’ → “fixed and eager”: “eager” gives attentiveness the force of desire.
- *li suoi con tanto affetto volse a lei* ‘his own with such affection he turned to her’ → “turned his eyes to her, with so much love”: “eyes” supplies the noun understood with the possessive; the verb precedes its object, and the phrase of affection moves to the end, with “love” for ‘affection’.
- *che i miei di rimirar fe' più ardenti* ‘that mine, to gaze again, he made more ardent’ → “that he made mine more ardent to re-gaze”: the verb moves before its object, and the infinitive, introduced in Italian by *di*, follows the adjective it qualifies.

## Summary — Italian order and English idiom

Wicksteed often brings the verb before its object, as in *viso ed amore avea* ‘look and love it had’ → “had look and love”, and puts a descriptive adjective before its noun, as in *fiamma viva* → “living flame”. Yet he keeps the object first in “The general form of Paradise my glance had already taken in”. He also follows the Italian closely in the sequence “to the divine from the human, to the eternal from time”, preserving both the balance of the phrases and the order of destination and source.

Changes of construction often make a relation explicit. The gerund *volando* ‘flying’ becomes a clause of time, “as it flieth”, whereas *contemplando* ‘contemplating’ becomes a phrase of means, “by contemplation”. The reflexive relative clause *che s'adorna* ‘which adorns itself’ contracts to the participle “adorned”. An imperfect often becomes a simple past, as in *Vedea* → “I saw”; in “ever descended”, by contrast, the added adverb brings out the repeated action of *discendeva*.

Word choice ranges from close correspondence to a more specific interpretation. In *di banco in banco* ‘from bench to bench’, “from rank to rank” names the rows rather than the seats but keeps the repetition. For *s’insapora* ‘takes on flavour’, “is turned to sweetness” specifies the flavour as well as changing the construction. Small additions and omissions likewise vary in effect: “the smallest part” supplies a noun for *lo minimo* ‘the least’, while “in glow and art” leaves out the first conjunction and the repeated preposition of *e di fulgore e d’arte*.

Two regular adjustments generally pass unremarked in the comparison. English supplies subject pronouns where the Italian verb ending identifies the subject, as in *orai* → “did I pray”. It also omits the article that Italian can place before a possessive: *la mia speranza* becomes “my hope”, and *il suo amor* becomes “its love”.
