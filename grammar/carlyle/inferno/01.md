# Inferno 1

## Lines 1–9 — Lost in a Dark Wood

> 1 Nel mezzo del cammin di nostra vita  
> 2 Mi ritrovai per una selva oscura,  
> 3 Chè la diritta via era smarrita.  

In the middle of the journey of our life, I found myself in a dark wood; for the straight way was lost.

- *Mi ritrovai* ‘myself I found’ → “I found myself”: the subject, expressed only by the Italian verb ending, is supplied explicitly, and the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.
- *per una selva oscura* ‘through a wood dark’ → “in a dark wood”: *per* (‘through’, ‘about’) becomes a preposition of rest, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.

> 4 Ahi quanto a dir qual era è cosa dura  
> 5 Questa selva selvaggia ed aspra e forte,  
> 6 Che nel pensier rinnova la paura!  

Ah! how hard a thing it is to tell what a wild, and rough, and stubborn wood this was, which in my thought renews the fear:

- *quanto ... è cosa dura* ‘how much ... is thing hard’ → “how hard a thing it is”: the adjective moves into the opening exclamatory phrase; English supplies the indefinite article and the anticipatory subject “it”.
- *a dir* ‘to say’ → “to tell”: the verb is rendered by a different English expression, and the infinitive moves after the main exclamatory phrase.
- *qual era ... Questa selva selvaggia ed aspra e forte* ‘of what kind was ... this wood wild and rough and strong’ → “what a wild, and rough, and stubborn wood this was”: the question of what the wood was like becomes an embedded exclamation introduced by “what a”. The demonstrative becomes the pronoun subject “this”, followed by “was”; the noun and its adjectives move into the preceding exclamatory phrase, with the adjectives before the noun. *Forte* is interpreted as “stubborn” rather than ‘strong’.
- *nel pensier* ‘in the thought’ → “in my thought”: the definite article is replaced by a possessive not explicit in the Italian.

> 7 Tanto è amara, che poco è più morte:  

so bitter is it, that scarcely more is death.

- *Tanto è amara* ‘so is bitter’ → “so bitter is it”: the adjective moves before the verb, and the subject “it” is supplied.
- *poco è più morte* ‘little is more death’ → “scarcely more is death”: the expression of degree becomes “scarcely more”, and “more” moves before the verb.

> 8 Ma per trattar del ben ch’ivi trovai,  
> 9 Dirò dell’ altre cose, ch’io v’ ho scorte.  

But to treat of the good that I there found, I will relate the other things that I discerned.

- *ch’ivi trovai* ‘that there I found’ → “that I there found”: the subject implicit in the verb ending is made explicit before the adverb.
- *Dirò* ‘I will say’ → “I will relate”: ‘say’ becomes “relate”, and the subject expressed by the Italian verb ending is supplied explicitly.
- *dell’ altre cose* ‘of the other things’ → “the other things”: the preposition is omitted, making the phrase the direct object of “relate”.
- *ch’io v’ ho scorte* ‘that I there have discerned’ → “that I discerned”: the locative *v’* (‘there’) is omitted, and the Italian compound perfect becomes an English simple past.

## Lines 10–21 — Fear Eased by Sunlight

> 10 Io non so ben ridir com’ io v’ entrai;  

I cannot rightly tell how I entered it,

- *Io non so ben ridir* ‘I do not know how to recount well’ → “I cannot rightly tell”: knowing how becomes ability expressed by “cannot”; ‘recount’ becomes “tell,” and ‘well’ becomes “rightly.”
- *com’ io v’ entrai* ‘how I there entered’ → “how I entered it”: the locative pronoun *v’* becomes the direct object “it,” placed after the verb.

> 11 Tant’ era pien di sonno in su quel punto,  
> 12 Che la verace via abbandonai.  

so full of sleep was I about the moment that I left the true way.

- *Tant’ era pien di sonno* ‘So much was I full of sleep’ → “so full of sleep was I”: the degree word is attached directly to “full,” the complement precedes the verb, and the implicit subject is expressed.
- *in su quel punto, Che* ‘at that moment when’ → “about the moment that”: “about” replaces ‘at,’ the demonstrative becomes a definite article, and “that” introduces the temporal clause. This reads *Che* as specifying the moment, rather than as introducing a consequence of being so full of sleep.
- *la verace via abbandonai* ‘the true way I abandoned’ → “I left the true way”: the object follows the verb, the implicit subject is expressed, and ‘abandoned’ becomes “left.”

> 13 Ma poi ch’ io fui al piè d’un colle giunto,  
> 14 Là ove terminava quella valle,  
> 15 Che m’ avea di paura il cor compunto,  

But after I had reached the foot of a Hill there, where that valley ended, which had pierced my heart with fear,

- *poi ch’ io fui al piè d’un colle giunto* ‘after I was at the foot of a hill arrived’ → “after I had reached the foot of a Hill”: the Italian anterior past, formed with ‘be,’ becomes an English past perfect with “had”; ‘arrived at’ becomes the transitive “reached,” and the participle moves before the destination.
- *Là ove terminava quella valle* ‘there where ended that valley’ → “there, where that valley ended”: the subject moves before the verb, and the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past.
- *Che m’ avea di paura il cor compunto* ‘which had for me with fear the heart pierced’ → “which had pierced my heart with fear”: the participle moves before the object, and the phrase expressing fear follows it. The personal pronoun *m’*, marking the person affected, and the definite article become the possessive “my.”

> 16 Guardai in alto, e vidi le sue spalle  
> 17 Vestite già de’ raggi del pianeta,  
> 18 Che mena dritto altrui per ogni calle.  

I looked up and saw its shoulders already clothed with the rays of the Planet that leads men straight on every road.

- *in alto* ‘on high’ → “up”: the prepositional phrase becomes a directional adverb.
- *le sue spalle* ‘the its shoulders’ → “its shoulders”: the article accompanying the Italian possessive is omitted.
- *Vestite già* ‘clothed already’ → “already clothed”: the adverb moves before the participle.
- *de’ raggi* ‘of the rays’ → “with the rays”: Italian *di* becomes the English preposition used for what something is clothed with.
- *Che mena dritto altrui* ‘that leads straight others’ → “that leads men straight”: the indefinite personal pronoun becomes “men,” and the object moves before “straight.”
- *per ogni calle* ‘through every path’ → “on every road”: ‘through’ becomes “on,” and ‘path’ becomes “road.”

> 19 Allor fu la paura un poco queta,  

Then the fear was somewhat calmed,

- *Allor fu la paura un poco queta* ‘Then was the fear a little quiet’ → “Then the fear was somewhat calmed”: the subject moves before the verb; ‘a little’ becomes “somewhat,” and the adjective ‘quiet’ becomes “calmed,” presenting the state as the result of calming.

> 20 Che nel lago del cor m’ era durata  
> 21 La notte, ch’ io passai con tanta pieta.  

which had continued in the lake of my heart the night that I passed so piteously.

- *Che nel lago del cor m’ era durata La notte* ‘which in the lake of the heart had lasted for me the night’ → “which had continued in the lake of my heart the night”: the verb moves before the location, and ‘lasted’ becomes “continued.” Italian ‘be’ as the perfect auxiliary becomes English “had”; *m’*, identifying the person affected, is absorbed into “my,” replacing the definite article before ‘heart.’
- *con tanta pieta* ‘with so much anguish’ → “so piteously”: a prepositional phrase containing a noun becomes an adverbial expression. The degree is retained in “so,” while ‘anguish’ becomes a description of manner suggesting pitiable suffering.

## Lines 22–33 — Looking Back and Encountering a Leopard

> 22 E come quei, che con lena affannata  
> 23 Uscito fuor del pelago alla riva,  
> 24 Si volge all’ acqua perigliosa, e guata;  

And as he, who with panting breath has escaped from the deep sea to the shore, turns to the dangerous water and gazes;

- *con lena affannata* ‘with breath laboured’ → “with panting breath”: the adjective precedes the noun, and laboured breathing is expressed more specifically as panting.
- *Uscito fuor* ‘having come out’ → “has escaped”: the past participle becomes a finite present-perfect verb within the English relative clause. Coming out is interpreted as escaping, with *fuor* absorbed into that verb.
- *pelago* ‘open sea’ → “deep sea”: the noun is rendered by a qualified English noun, emphasizing depth rather than openness.
- *Si volge* ‘turns himself’ → “turns”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb. In Italian this is the finite verb governed by the relative pronoun; in English it becomes the predicate of “he”, outside the relative clause.
- *all’ acqua perigliosa* ‘to the water dangerous’ → “to the dangerous water”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 25 Così l’ animo mio, che ancor fuggiva,  
> 26 Si volse indietro a rimirar lo passo,  
> 27 Che non lasciò giammai persona viva.  

so my mind, which still was fleeing, turned back to see the pass that no one ever left alive.

- *l’ animo mio* ‘the mind my’ → “my mind”: the possessive moves before the noun, and the Italian definite article is omitted.
- *che ancor fuggiva* ‘which still fled’ → “which still was fleeing”: the Italian imperfect is expressed by the English past progressive, making the continuing action explicit.
- *Si volse indietro* ‘turned itself back’ → “turned back”: the Italian reflexive is rendered by an intransitive English verb without a reflexive pronoun.
- *a rimirar* ‘to gaze at’ → “to see”: a verb of directed, attentive looking becomes the more general verb of seeing.
- *Che non lasciò giammai persona viva* ‘which never left any person alive’ → “that no one ever left alive”: the grammatical roles are reversed. The passage is the subject of the Italian verb but the object of the English verb; the person becomes the English subject. The negative moves from the verb to “no one”, and leaving someone alive becomes leaving a place alive.

> 28 Poi ch’ ebbi riposato il corpo lasso,  

After I had rested my wearied body,

- *ebbi riposato il corpo lasso* ‘had rested the body weary’ → “I had rested my wearied body”: English supplies the subject expressed by the Italian verb ending; the definite article becomes a possessive; and the adjective moves before the noun. “wearied” also uses a participial form where Italian has an adjective meaning weary.

> 29 Ripresi via per la piaggia diserta,  
> 30 Sì che il piè fermo sempre era il più basso.  

I took the way again along the desert strand, so that the firm foot always was the lower.

- *Ripresi via* ‘resumed way’ → “I took the way again”: the subject implicit in the verb ending is supplied, a definite article is added, and the single verb meaning resumed is expanded into “took” and “again”.
- *per la piaggia diserta* ‘along the slope deserted’ → “along the desert strand”: Carlyle takes *piaggia* in its shore or strand sense rather than its slope sense. The adjective also moves before the noun.
- *il piè fermo* ‘the foot firm’ → “the firm foot”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *il più basso* ‘the lowest’ → “the lower”: the Italian definite superlative form is expressed by an English comparative, appropriate to a comparison between two feet.

> 31 Ed ecco, quasi al cominciar dell’ erta,  
> 32 Una lonza leggiera e presta molto,  
> 33 Che di pel maculato era coverta.  

And behold, almost at the commencement of the steep, a Leopard, light and very nimble, which was covered with a spotted skin:

- *al cominciar* ‘at the beginning’ → “at the commencement”: the Italian nominalized infinitive is rendered by an English abstract noun.
- *lonza* ‘ounce’ → “Leopard”: the translation identifies the animal as a leopard rather than retaining the English cognate ounce.
- *presta molto* ‘nimble very’ → “very nimble”: the degree adverb moves before the adjective.
- *Che di pel maculato era coverta* ‘which with hair spotted was covered’ → “which was covered with a spotted skin”: the verb moves before the covering phrase; hair or fur becomes “skin”; an indefinite article is supplied; and the adjective moves before its noun.

## Lines 34–47 — Hope and Renewed Fear

> 34 E non mi si partia dinanzi al volto;  

and it went not from before my face;

- *non mi si partia dinanzi al volto* ‘not to me did it depart from before the face’ → “it went not from before my face”: the implicit subject becomes “it,” and the negative follows the verb. The pronominal verb *partirsi* becomes “went,” without a reflexive pronoun; the personal dative *mi* is expressed through the possessive “my” before “face,” replacing the Italian definite article.

> 35 Anzi impediva tanto il mio cammino,  
> 36 Ch’io fui per ritornar più volte vôlto.  

nay, so impeded my way, that I had often turned to go back.

- *Ch’io fui per ritornar più volte vôlto* ‘that I was, to return, several times turned’ → “that I had often turned to go back”: the simple-past construction ‘was turned’ becomes the active past perfect ‘had turned.’ The purpose phrase moves to the end, with ‘return’ expressed as “go back”; ‘several times’ becomes the less numerically explicit “often.”

> 37 Tempo era dal principio del mattino;  

The time was at the beginning of the morning;

- *Tempo era* ‘time was’ → “The time was”: a definite article is supplied.
- *dal principio* ‘from the beginning’ → “at the beginning”: the preposition expressing a starting point becomes one locating the time.

> 38 E il Sol montava in su con quelle stelle  
> 39 Ch’eran con lui, quando l’ Amor Divino  
> 40 Mosse da prima quelle cose belle;  

and the Sun was mounting up with those stars, which were with him when Divine Love first moved those fair things:

- *montava in su* ‘mounted upwards’ → “was mounting up”: the Italian imperfect is represented by an explicitly progressive construction.
- *l’Amor Divino* ‘the Love Divine’ → “Divine Love”: the article is omitted, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *Mosse da prima* ‘moved at first’ → “first moved”: the adverbial phrase is compressed to “first” and placed before the verb.
- *quelle cose belle* ‘those things fair’ → “those fair things”: the adjective precedes its noun.

> 41 Sì che a bene sperar m’ eran cagione  
> 42 Di quella fera la gaietta pelle,  
> 43 L’ ora del tempo, e la dolce stagione:  

so that the gay skin of that animal, the hour of time, and the sweet season, were causes to me of good hope;

- *a bene sperar* ‘to hope well’ → “of good hope”: the infinitive becomes a noun, its modifying adverb becomes an adjective, and the relation is expressed with “of” rather than ‘to.’ The phrase moves from before the predicate to its end.
- *m’eran cagione* ‘to me were cause’ → “were causes to me”: the singular predicate noun becomes plural, agreeing in number with the several subjects. The personal pronoun follows the predicate as “to me,” and the predicate follows the subjects rather than preceding them.
- *Di quella fera la gaietta pelle* ‘of that beast the gay little skin’ → “the gay skin of that animal”: the possessive phrase follows the noun instead of preceding it; ‘beast’ becomes the more general “animal,” and the diminutive force of *gaietta* is not separately expressed.

> 44 Ma non sì, che paura non mi desse  
> 45 La vista, che m’ apparve, d’un leone.  

yet not so, but that I feared at the sight, which appeared to me, of a Lion.

- *che paura non mi desse La vista* ‘that the sight should not give me fear’ → “but that I feared at the sight”: the negative subordinate construction becomes the exception-like “but that,” with an affirmative clause. The sight ceases to be the grammatical subject and becomes an “at” phrase; the recipient of fear becomes the subject “I.” ‘Give fear’ becomes the verb “feared,” and the imperfect subjunctive becomes a simple-past indicative.
- *che m’apparve* ‘which to me appeared’ → “which appeared to me”: the personal pronoun is expressed by a prepositional phrase following the verb.

> 46 Questi parea, che contra me venesse  
> 47 Con la testa alta, e con rabbiosa fame;  

He seemed coming upon me with head erect, and furious hunger;

- *Questi parea, che contra me venesse* ‘This one seemed that against me he were coming’ → “He seemed coming upon me”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun. The finite subordinate clause, with its imperfect subjunctive, becomes a participial complement to “seemed”; ‘against me’ becomes “upon me” and follows the expression of movement rather than preceding it.
- *Con la testa alta* ‘with the head high’ → “with head erect”: the definite article is omitted, and ‘high’ becomes “erect.”
- *e con rabbiosa fame* ‘and with furious hunger’ → “and furious hunger”: the repeated preposition is omitted, leaving the earlier “with” to govern both phrases.

## Lines 48–60 — The She-Wolf Drives Him Back

> 48 Sì che parea che l’aer ne tremesse.  

so that the air seemed to quake thereat.

- *parea che l’aer ne tremesse* ‘it seemed that the air trembled at it’ → “the air seemed to quake thereat”: the impersonal construction with a subordinate subjunctive becomes a personal construction, with “the air” as the subject of “seemed” and an infinitive replacing the subordinate verb.

> 49 Ed una lupa, che di tutte brame  
> 50 Sembiava carca nella sua magrezza,  
> 51 E molte genti fe’ già viver grame.  

And a She-wolf, that looked full of all cravings in her leanness; and has ere now made many live in sorrow:

- *che di tutte brame Sembiava carca* ‘that with all cravings seemed laden’ → “that looked full of all cravings”: “looked” renders seeming as visual appearance; ‘laden with’ becomes “full of”; and the complement describing the cravings moves after the verb and adjective.
- *molte genti* ‘many people’ → “many”: the noun is left implicit, and the object moves from before the causative verb to after it.
- *fe’ già* ‘made already’ → “has ere now made”: the simple past becomes a present perfect; *già* is rendered by “ere now” and placed before the participle.
- *grame* ‘wretched’ → “in sorrow”: an adjective describing the people becomes a prepositional phrase modifying how they live.

> 52 Questa mi porse tanto di gravezza  
> 53 Con la paura, che uscia di sua vista,  
> 54 Ch’ io perdei la speranza dell’altezza.  

She brought such heaviness upon me with the terror of her aspect, that I lost the hope of ascending.

- *Questa* ‘this one’ → “She”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *mi porse tanto di gravezza* ‘to me gave so much of heaviness’ → “brought such heaviness upon me”: giving becomes bringing something upon someone; the recipient pronoun moves after the object and becomes a prepositional complement; and the quantitative construction ‘so much of’ becomes “such”.
- *Con la paura, che uscia di sua vista* ‘with the fear that came out of her appearance’ → “with the terror of her aspect”: “terror” gives a stronger term for fear; the relative clause, including its imperfect verb, is compressed into “of her aspect”, leaving the source relationship implicit in “of”.
- *dell’altezza* ‘of the height’ → “of ascending”: the noun naming the height, with its definite article, becomes a gerund naming the action of rising toward it.

> 55 E quale è quei, che volentieri acquista,  
> 56 E giugne il tempo che perder lo face,  
> 57 Che in tutti i suoi pensier piange e s’ attrista;  

And as one who is eager in gaining, and, when the time arrives that makes him lose, weeps and afflicts himself in all his thoughts;

- *quale è quei* ‘as is that one’ → “as one”: the copula is omitted, and the demonstrative expression becomes indefinite.
- *che volentieri acquista* ‘who willingly gains’ → “who is eager in gaining”: the adverb becomes the predicate “is eager”, giving willingness the stronger sense of eagerness; the finite verb becomes a gerund governed by “in”.
- *E giugne il tempo* ‘and arrives the time’ → “and, when the time arrives”: “when” is supplied, turning the arrival into a temporal subordinate clause, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *che perder lo face* ‘that lose him makes’ → “that makes him lose”: the causative verb moves before both the object pronoun and the infinitive, following normal English order.
- *Che in tutti i suoi pensier piange e s’ attrista* ‘who in all his thoughts weeps and grows sad’ → “weeps and afflicts himself in all his thoughts”: the resumptive relative pronoun is omitted; the phrase about his thoughts moves after the verbs; and the pronominal verb meaning ‘grows sad’ becomes the explicitly reflexive “afflicts himself”.

> 58 Tal mi fece la bestia senza pace,  
> 59 Che, venendomi incontro, a poco a poco  
> 60 Mi ripingeva là dove il Sol tace.  

such that restless beast made me, which coming against me, by little and little drove me back to where the Sun is silent.

- *mi fece* ‘me made’ → “made me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *la bestia senza pace* ‘the beast without peace’ → “that restless beast”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative; the post-nominal prepositional phrase becomes a preceding adjective; and the subject moves before the verb.
- *venendomi incontro* ‘coming towards me’ → “coming against me”: “against” gives the approach an explicitly opposing sense; the attached Italian pronoun becomes a separate pronoun after the preposition.
- *a poco a poco* ‘little by little’ → “by little and little”: the distributive phrase is rendered by a different English idiom, joining the repeated word with “and”.
- *Mi ripingeva* ‘me was driving back’ → “drove me back”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, without explicitly marking the action as ongoing, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “to where”: the locative expression becomes directional, with “to” replacing an explicit equivalent of ‘there’.

## Lines 61–72 — Dante Appeals to a Shade

> 61 Mentre ch’ io rovinava in basso loco,  
> 62 Dinanzi agli occhi mi si fu offerto  
> 63 Chi per lungo silenzio parea fioco.  

Whilst I was rushing downwards, there appeared before my eyes one who seemed hoarse from long silence.

- *io rovinava* ‘I was falling headlong’ → “I was rushing”: the verb of headlong falling is rendered as one of rapid movement.
- *in basso loco* ‘into a low place’ → “downwards”: a phrase naming a low place becomes an adverb of direction; the noun *loco* is not separately rendered.
- *Dinanzi agli occhi mi si fu offerto* ‘before the eyes to me had presented himself’ → “there appeared before my eyes”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and the Italian anterior past form becomes a simple past. The opening phrase moves after the verb; the dative *mi* becomes the possessive “my”, and introductory “there” is supplied.
- *Chi* ‘who’ → “one who”: the antecedent implicit in the Italian relative pronoun is made explicit.
- *per lungo silenzio parea fioco* ‘from long silence seemed faint’ → “seemed hoarse from long silence”: the causal phrase moves after the predicate, and *fioco* is interpreted specifically as hoarseness of voice.

> 64 Quando vidi costui nel gran diserto,  
> 65 Miserere di me, gridai a lui,  
> 66 Qual che tu sie, od ombra, od uomo certo.  

When I saw him in the great desert, I cried: “Have pity on me, whate’er thou be, whether shade or veritable man!”

- *vidi costui* ‘I saw this one’ → “I saw him”: the subject implicit in the verb ending is expressed, and the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *gridai a lui* ‘I cried to him’ → “I cried”: the reporting clause moves before the quoted speech instead of interrupting it; *a lui* is omitted, and the subject implicit in the verb ending is expressed.
- *od ombra, od uomo certo* ‘or shade, or man real’ → “whether shade or veritable man”: the repeated alternatives become a “whether”–“or” construction, and the adjective qualifying *uomo* moves before its noun.

> 67 Risposemi: Non uomo, uomo già fui,  

He answered me: “Not man, a man I once was;

- *Risposemi* ‘answered me’ → “He answered me”: the subject implicit in the verb ending is supplied, and the attached object pronoun becomes a separate word.
- *uomo già fui* ‘man once I was’ → “a man I once was”: an indefinite article is supplied, the subject implicit in *fui* is expressed, and “once” follows that subject rather than preceding it.

> 68 E li parenti miei furon Lombardi,  
> 69 E Mantovani per patria ambedui.  

and my parents were Lombards, and both of Mantua by country.

- *li parenti miei* ‘the parents my’ → “my parents”: the definite article is omitted, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *Mantovani per patria ambedui* ‘Mantuans by homeland both’ → “both of Mantua by country”: the demonym becomes the prepositional phrase “of Mantua”, *ambedui* moves to the beginning, and *patria* is rendered as “country”.

> 70 Nacqui sub Julio, ancorchè fosse tardi,  
> 71 E vissi a Roma sotto il buono Augusto,  
> 72 Al tempo degli Dei falsi e bugiardi.  

I was born sub Julio, though it was late; and lived at Rome under the good Augustus, in the time of the false and lying gods.

- *Nacqui* ‘was born’ → “I was born”: the subject implicit in the ending is expressed; the active Italian verb of birth is rendered by the English passive construction.
- *ancorchè fosse tardi* ‘although it were late’ → “though it was late”: the Italian imperfect subjunctive becomes an English indicative, and English supplies the impersonal subject “it”.
- *Al tempo degli Dei falsi e bugiardi* ‘at the time of the gods false and lying’ → “in the time of the false and lying gods”: the temporal preposition becomes “in”, and both adjectives move before their noun.

## Lines 73–85 — Virgil’s Identity and Dante’s Reverence

> 73 Poeta fui, e cantai di quel giusto  
> 74 Figliuol d’ Anchise, che venne da Troia,  
> 75 Poi che il superbo Ilion fu combusto.  

A Poet I was; and sang of that just son of Anchises, who came from Troy after proud Ilium was burnt.

- *Poeta fui* ‘poet I was’ → “A Poet I was”: the indefinite article is supplied, and the subject implicit in the Italian verb ending is expressed as “I”.
- *il superbo Ilion* ‘the proud Ilium’ → “proud Ilium”: the definite article before the qualified proper name is omitted.

> 76 Ma tu, perchè ritorni a tanta noia?  

But thou, why returnest thou to such disquiet?

- *tu, perchè ritorni* ‘thou, why returnest’ → “thou, why returnest thou”: the initial pronoun is repeated after the verb to form the English question.
- *tanta noia* ‘so much distress’ → “such disquiet”: the expression of quantity becomes one of kind or degree, and distress is rendered as “disquiet”.

> 77 Perchè non sali il dilettoso monte,  
> 78 Ch’ è principio e cagion di tutta gioia?  

why ascendest not the delectable mountain, which is the beginning and the cause of all gladness?”

- *è principio e cagion* ‘is beginning and cause’ → “is the beginning and the cause”: a definite article is supplied before each predicate noun.

> 79 Or se’ tu quel Virgilio, e quella fonte,  
> 80 Che spande di parlar sì largo fiume?  
> 81 Risposi lui con vergognosa fronte.  

“Art thou then that Virgil, and that fountain which pours abroad so rich a stream of speech?” I answered him, with bashful front.

- *Or se’ tu* ‘now art thou’ → “Art thou then”: *Or* is interpreted as inferential rather than temporal, and its English equivalent follows the verb and subject.
- *spande* ‘spreads’ → “pours abroad”: the single verb becomes a verb with an adverb expressing outward movement, with “pours” making the liquid imagery explicit.
- *di parlar sì largo fiume* ‘of speaking so broad river’ → “so rich a stream of speech”: the modifying phrase moves after the noun; the substantival infinitive becomes “speech”; breadth becomes richness; “stream” replaces ‘river’; and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *Risposi lui* ‘I replied to him’ → “I answered him”: the subject implicit in the verb ending is expressed, and the Italian indirect object, used here without a preposition, becomes the direct object of “answered”.

> 82 O degli altri poeti onore e lume,  

“O glory, and light of other poets!

- *degli altri poeti* ‘of the other poets’ → “of other poets”: the phrase moves from before the two nouns it qualifies to after them, and the definite article is omitted.
- *onore* ‘honour’ → “glory”: a different noun of praise is used.

> 83 Vagliami il lungo studio e il grande amore,  
> 84 Che m’ han fatto cercar lo tuo volume.  

May the long zeal avail me, and the great love, that made me search thy volume.

- *Vagliami il lungo studio* ‘may avail me the long study’ → “May the long zeal avail me”: the optative subjunctive is expressed with the modal “May”, and the subject moves between that modal and the main verb. The attached object pronoun becomes a separate word, while “zeal” interprets *studio* as devoted application rather than simply study.
- *Che m’ han fatto cercar* ‘that have made me seek’ → “that made me search”: the present perfect becomes a simple past, and the object pronoun moves from before the auxiliary to after “made”. “Search” interprets the activity as examining the volume rather than seeking it out.
- *lo tuo volume* ‘the thy volume’ → “thy volume”: the definite article accompanying the possessive is omitted.

> 85 Tu se’ lo mio maestro, e il mio autore:  

Thou art my master and my author.

- *lo mio maestro, e il mio autore* ‘the my master, and the my author’ → “my master and my author”: both definite articles accompanying possessives are omitted.

## Lines 86–100 — An Appeal and Another Road

> 86 Tu se’ solo colui, da cui io tolsi  
> 87 Lo bello stile, che m’ ha fatto onore.  

Thou alone art he from whom I took the good style that hath done me honour.

- *Tu se’ solo* ‘Thou art alone’ → “Thou alone art”: “alone” moves before the verb.
- *colui* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *bello* ‘beautiful’ → “good”: aesthetic praise becomes a more general commendation.
- *che m’ha fatto onore* ‘that to me has made honour’ → “that hath done me honour”: the idiom uses “done” rather than ‘made’, and the indirect object follows the verb instead of preceding it.

> 88 Vedi la bestia, per cui io mi volsi:  

See the beast from which I turned back.

- *per cui* ‘because of which’ → “from which”: the causal expression becomes an expression of movement away.
- *io mi volsi* ‘I myself turned’ → “I turned back”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and “back” is supplied.

> 89 Aiutami da lei, famoso saggio,  
> 90 Ch’ ella mi fa tremar le vene e i polsi.  

Help me from her, thou famous sage; for she makes my veins and pulses tremble.”

- *famoso saggio* ‘famous sage’ → “thou famous sage”: “thou” is supplied in the address.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *ella mi fa tremar le vene e i polsi* ‘she makes tremble to me the veins and the pulses’ → “she makes my veins and pulses tremble”: the possessive dative becomes “my”, replacing the Italian definite articles; the nouns move before the infinitive whose subject they are.

> 91 A te convien tenere altro viaggio,  
> 92 Rispose, poi che lagrimar mi vide,  
> 93 Se vuoi campar d’ esto loco selvaggio:  

“Thou must take another road,” he answered, when he saw me weeping, “if thou desirest to escape from this wild place;

- *A te convien* ‘To thee it is necessary’ → “Thou must”: an impersonal construction with a dative becomes a personal subject with a modal verb.
- *tenere altro viaggio* ‘hold another journey’ → “take another road”: the Italian verbal expression is recast with “take”, and the journey becomes the road travelled.
- *Rispose* ‘answered’ → “he answered”: the subject, implicit in the Italian verb, is supplied.
- *poi che* ‘after that’ → “when”: the translation makes the temporal connection less explicitly sequential.
- *lagrimar mi vide* ‘weep me he saw’ → “he saw me weeping”: the main verb precedes its object and the action perceived, rather than following them; the infinitive becomes a present participle, and the subject is explicitly supplied.
- *loco selvaggio* ‘place wild’ → “wild place”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 94 Chè questa bestia, per la qual tu gride,  
> 95 Non lascia altrui passar per la sua via,  
> 96 Ma tanto lo impedisce, che l’ uccide:  

because this beast, for which thou criest, lets not men pass her way, but so entangles that she slays them;

- *Non lascia* ‘not lets’ → “lets not”: the negative follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *altrui* ‘another’ → “men”: an indefinite pronoun becomes an explicit plural noun.
- *per la sua via* ‘along her way’ → “her way”: the preposition is omitted, as is the article accompanying the Italian possessive.
- *lo impedisce* ‘him hinders’ → “entangles”: hindering is rendered more concretely as entangling, and the object pronoun is omitted.
- *che l’uccide* ‘that him she kills’ → “that she slays them”: the subject is explicitly supplied, the object moves after the verb, and the singular object becomes plural to agree with “men”.

> 97 E ha natura sì malvagia e ria,  
> 98 Che mai non empie la bramosa voglia,  
> 99 E dopo il pasto ha più fame che pria.  

and has a nature so perverse and vicious, that she never satiates her craving appetite; and after feeding, she is hungrier than before.

- *natura* ‘nature’ → “a nature”: the indefinite article is supplied.
- *malvagia e ria* ‘wicked and evil’ → “perverse and vicious”: the two general terms of moral condemnation are rendered with terms suggesting perversity and vice.
- *Che mai non empie* ‘that never not fills’ → “that she never satiates”: the subject is supplied, Italian negative concord becomes the single English negative “never”, and the image of filling is rendered as satiating.
- *la bramosa voglia* ‘the craving desire’ → “her craving appetite”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the general word for desire becomes the more specifically alimentary “appetite”.
- *il pasto* ‘the meal’ → “feeding”: a definite noun naming a meal becomes an articleless verbal noun naming the activity.
- *ha più fame* ‘has more hunger’ → “she is hungrier”: the construction with ‘have’ and a noun becomes one with “is” and a comparative adjective; the subject is explicitly supplied.

> 100 Molti son gli animali, a cui s’ ammoglia,  

The animals to which she weds herself are many;

- *Molti son gli animali, a cui s’ammoglia* ‘Many are the animals to which herself she weds’ → “The animals to which she weds herself are many”: the subject and its relative clause move before the copula and predicate; within the relative clause, the subject is explicitly supplied and the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.

## Lines 101–105 — The Greyhound’s Coming and Qualities

> 101 E più saranno ancora, infin che il Veltro  
> 102 Verrà, che la farà morir di doglia.  

and will yet be more, until the Greyhound comes, that will make her die with pain.

- *più saranno ancora* ‘more will be still’ → “will yet be more”: “more” moves after the verb, while “yet” renders *ancora* and moves between the auxiliary and “be”.
- *infin che il Veltro Verrà* ‘until the Greyhound will come’ → “until the Greyhound comes”: the Italian future becomes an English present, as usual in a future-time clause introduced by “until”.
- *la farà morir* ‘her will make die’ → “will make her die”: the object pronoun moves from before the finite verb to after “make”.
- *di doglia* ‘of pain’ → “with pain”: the preposition expressing the cause of death is rendered by “with” rather than ‘of’.

> 103 Questi non ciberà terra nè peltro,  
> 104 Ma sapienza, e amore, e virtute;  

He will not feed on land or pelf, but on wisdom, and love, and manfulness;

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “He”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *non ciberà* ‘will not eat’ → “will not feed on”: a verb taking a direct object becomes the construction “feed on”.
- *nè peltro* ‘nor pewter’ → “or pelf”: English uses “or” after “not”; the metal is rendered interpretively as wealth or possessions, with the disparaging term “pelf”.
- *sapienza* ‘wisdom’ → “on wisdom”: the preposition is repeated to continue the English construction “feed on”, whereas the Italian noun is a direct object.
- *virtute* ‘virtue’ → “manfulness”: the translation selects the sense of manly strength or courage rather than the broader sense of moral excellence.

> 105 E sua nazion sarà tra Feltro e Feltro.  

and his nation shall be between Feltro and Feltro.

- *nazion* ‘birth’ or ‘birthplace’ → “nation”: the Italian term for birth or origin is represented by its English cognate rather than by an explicit reference to birth or birthplace.

## Lines 106–120 — Salvation and a Guided Journey

> 106 Di quell’ umile Italia fia salute,  
> 107 Per cui morì la vergine Cammilla,  
> 108 Eurialo, e Turno, e Niso di ferute:  

He shall be the salvation of that low Italy, for which Camilla the virgin, Euryalus, and Turnus, and Nisus, died of wounds.

- *Di quell’ umile Italia fia salute* ‘of that humble Italy he shall be salvation’ → “He shall be the salvation of that low Italy”: the possessive phrase moves after “salvation”; the subject implicit in *fia* is supplied, as is the article before “salvation”; *umile* becomes “low” rather than ‘humble’.
- *la vergine Cammilla* ‘the virgin Camilla’ → “Camilla the virgin”: the name precedes rather than follows its descriptive title.

> 109 Questi la caccerà per ogni villa,  
> 110 Fin che l’ avrà rimessa nell’ Inferno,  
> 111 Là onde invidia prima dipartilla.  

He shall chase her through every city, till he have put her into Hell again; from which envy first set her loose.

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “He”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *la caccerà* ‘her shall chase’ → “shall chase her”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *per ogni villa* ‘through every town’ → “through every city”: “city” replaces ‘town’.
- *l’ avrà rimessa nell’ Inferno* ‘her he will have put back in Hell’ → “he have put her into Hell again”: the subject is supplied and the object pronoun follows the verb; the Italian future perfect becomes an English perfect subjunctive after “till”; the sense of returning conveyed by *rimessa* is expressed through “again”, and ‘in’ becomes the directional “into”.
- *Là onde* ‘there whence’ → “from which”: the explicit ‘there’ disappears, and the expression becomes a relative phrase referring directly to “Hell”.
- *dipartilla* ‘sent her forth’ → “set her loose”: departure or separation is rendered as release.

> 112 Ond’ io per lo tuo me’ penso e discerno,  
> 113 Che tu mi segui, ed io sarò tua guida,  
> 114 E trarrotti di qui per luogo eterno,  

Wherefore I think and discern this for thy best, that thou follow me. And I will be thy guide, and lead thee hence through an eternal place,

- *io per lo tuo me’ penso e discerno* ‘I for thy better think and discern’ → “I think and discern this for thy best”: the phrase expressing benefit moves after the verbs; ‘better’ becomes the superlative “best”; “this” is supplied to anticipate the following clause.
- *tu mi segui* ‘thou me follow’ → “thou follow me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *trarrotti* ‘I shall draw thee’ → “lead thee”: ‘draw’ becomes “lead”; the subject and future auxiliary are shared with the preceding clause rather than expressed again.
- *di qui* ‘from here’ → “hence”: the prepositional phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *per luogo eterno* ‘through place eternal’ → “through an eternal place”: an indefinite article is supplied, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 115 Ove udirai le disperate strida,  
> 116 Vedrai gli antichi spiriti dolenti,  
> 117 Che la seconda morte ciascun grida:  

where thou shalt hear the hopeless shrieks, shalt see the ancient spirits in pain; so that each calls for second death.

- *udirai* ‘shalt hear’ → “thou shalt hear”: the subject implicit in the verb ending is supplied.
- *dolenti* ‘suffering’ → “in pain”: an adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *Che* ‘who’ → “so that”: the translation takes the connection as consequential rather than as a relative clause referring to the spirits.
- *la seconda morte ciascun grida* ‘the second death each cries out’ → “each calls for second death”: the object moves from the beginning to the end of the clause, its definite article is omitted, and ‘cries out’ becomes “calls for”, interpreting the cry as a request.

> 118 E vederai color, che son contenti  
> 119 Nel fuoco, perchè speran di venire,  
> 120 Quando che sia, alle beate genti:  

And thou shalt see those who are contented in the fire; for they hope to come, whensoever it be, amongst the blessed.

- *vederai* ‘shalt see’ → “thou shalt see”: the subject implicit in the verb ending is supplied.
- *perchè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *speran* ‘hope’ → “they hope”: the subject implicit in the verb ending is supplied.
- *Quando che sia* ‘when that it be’ → “whensoever it be”: the indefinite temporal construction is condensed into “whensoever”.
- *alle beate genti* ‘to the blessed peoples’ → “amongst the blessed”: “amongst” replaces ‘to’; the noun *genti* is omitted, leaving “the blessed” as a substantive adjective.

## Lines 121–136 — A Worthier Guide and Departure

> 121 Alle quai poi se tu vorrai salire,  
> 122 Anima fia a ciò di me più degna:  

Then to these, if thou desirest to ascend, there shall be a Spirit worthier than I to guide thee.

- *Alle quai* ‘to which’ → “to these”: the relative pronoun becomes a demonstrative.
- *vorrai* ‘wilt wish’ → “desirest”: the future becomes a present tense.
- *Anima fia* ‘a soul shall be’ → “there shall be a Spirit”: “there” is supplied to form an existential construction, the subject follows the verb, and ‘soul’ becomes “Spirit”.
- *a ciò* ‘for this’ → “to guide thee”: the general reference becomes an explicit infinitive phrase, placed after the comparison.
- *di me più degna* ‘than me more worthy’ → “worthier than I”: the adjective moves before the comparison, the comparative becomes a single word, and the object-form pronoun becomes a subject-form pronoun.

> 123 Con lei ti lascierò nel mio partire;  

With her will I leave thee at my parting.

- *ti lascierò* ‘thee I shall leave’ → “will I leave thee”: the subject implicit in the verb is supplied, the auxiliary precedes it, and the object follows the verb.
- *nel mio partire* ‘in the my departing’ → “at my parting”: ‘in’ becomes “at”, the definite article is omitted before the possessive, and the substantivized infinitive becomes an English verbal noun.

> 124 Chè quello Imperador, che lassù regna,  
> 125 Perch’ io fui ribellante alla sua legge,  
> 126 Non vuol che in sua città per me si vegna.  

For that Emperor who reigns above, because I was rebellious to his law, wills not that I come into his city.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “For”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *lassù regna* ‘up there reigns’ → “reigns above”: the adverb follows the verb, and “above” renders the more explicitly locative ‘up there’.
- *alla sua* ‘to the his’ → “to his”: the definite article is omitted before the possessive.
- *che in sua città per me si vegna* ‘that into his city through me one should come’ → “that I come into his city”: the impersonal subjunctive construction becomes a personal clause with “I” as subject, and the destination follows the verb. The translation takes *per me* as identifying the person entering, rather than the person through whose agency someone enters.

> 127 In tutte parti impera, e quivi regge:  

In all parts he rules; and there he dwells.

- *impera* ‘rules’ → “he rules”: the subject implicit in the verb is supplied.
- *quivi regge* ‘there governs’ → “there he dwells”: the subject is supplied, and governing is rendered as dwelling.

> 128 Quivi è la sua cittade, e l’ alto seggio:  

There is his city, and his high seat.

- *la sua* ‘the his’ → “his”: the definite article is omitted before the possessive.
- *l’ alto seggio* ‘the high seat’ → “his high seat”: the definite article becomes a possessive, extending the preceding possessive construction to this noun.

> 129 O felice colui, cui ivi elegge!  

O happy whom he chooses for it!”

- *colui, cui* ‘he whom’ → “whom”: the demonstrative antecedent is omitted.
- *ivi* ‘there’ → “for it”: the locative adverb becomes a prepositional phrase expressing what the person is chosen for, and moves after the verb.
- *elegge* ‘chooses’ → “he chooses”: the subject implicit in the verb is supplied.

> 130 Ed io a lui: Poeta, io ti richieggio  
> 131 Per quello Iddio, che tu non conoscesti,  
> 132 A ciò ch’ io fugga questo male e peggio,  

And I to him: “Poet, I beseech thee by that God whom thou knewest not: in order that I may escape this ill and worse,

- *io ti richieggio* ‘I thee beseech’ → “I beseech thee”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *tu non conoscesti* ‘thou not knewest’ → “thou knewest not”: the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *fugga* ‘escape’ → “may escape”: the Italian subjunctive is represented by a modal auxiliary and infinitive.

> 133 Che tu mi meni là dov’ or dicesti,  
> 134 Sì ch’ io vegga la porta di San Pietro,  
> 135 E color, che tu fai cotanto mesti.  

lead me where thou now hast said, so that I may see the Gate of St. Peter, and those whom thou makest so sad.”

- *Che tu mi meni* ‘that thou me lead’ → “lead me”: the subordinate subjunctive request becomes an imperative; the conjunction and explicit subject are omitted, and the object follows the verb.
- *là dov’* ‘there where’ → “where”: the correlative ‘there’ is omitted.
- *or dicesti* ‘now thou saidst’ → “thou now hast said”: the subject implicit in the verb is supplied before the adverb, and the simple past becomes a present perfect.
- *vegga* ‘see’ → “may see”: the Italian subjunctive is represented by a modal auxiliary and infinitive.

> 136 Allor si mosse; ed io li tenni dietro.  

Then he moved; and I kept on behind him.

- *si mosse* ‘moved himself’ → “he moved”: the pronominal construction becomes an intransitive English verb, with an explicit subject and no reflexive pronoun.
- *io li tenni dietro* ‘I to him kept behind’ → “I kept on behind him”: the preverbal dative becomes the complement of “behind”, and “on” is supplied to express continuation.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Constructions

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s sequence of expression and often preserves its distinctive imagery and phrasing: “the lake of my heart,” “where the Sun is silent,” and “my master and my author.” He also retains some marked word orders, such as “A Poet I was” and “With her will I leave thee.” Yet this closeness is not consistently word-for-word: the prose repeatedly rearranges and expands Italian expressions to accommodate English grammar.

The most frequent changes concern word order and words supplied or omitted. Adjectives generally move before their nouns, objects follow their verbs, and subjects implicit in Italian verb endings become explicit pronouns. Articles accompanying possessives disappear, while English articles are supplied elsewhere. Italian personal datives often become possessives: *mi* with “the face,” for example, yields “my face.” Reflexive or pronominal verbs frequently become plain intransitive verbs, as *si mosse* becomes “he moved.” Small elements may also disappear, including the locative *v’* in “that I discerned” and the repeated preposition in “with head erect, and furious hunger.”

Changes of construction sometimes go beyond these grammatical adjustments. Knowing how becomes ability in “I cannot rightly tell”; “with so much anguish” becomes the adverbial “so piteously”; and “the hope of the height” becomes “the hope of ascending.” Italian imperfects may become English progressives, compound pasts may become simple pasts, and subjunctives may be represented by modal auxiliaries. More substantial recastings alter grammatical roles: the passage that “never left any person alive” becomes the pass “that no one ever left alive,” while the impersonal construction with *per me* becomes the personal “that I come into his city.”

Word choice likewise ranges from close equivalents to interpretive substitutions. *Forte* becomes “stubborn,” *bello* becomes “good,” and *virtute* becomes “manfulness”; *peltro*, literally pewter, is rendered as “pelf.” Elsewhere Carlyle expands a single word into an English phrase, as *ripresi* becomes “took … again,” or compresses a clause, as the fear issuing from an appearance becomes “the terror of her aspect.” The overall result combines considerable verbal fidelity with recurrent grammatical reshaping and occasional choices that narrow, strengthen, or redirect the Italian expression.
