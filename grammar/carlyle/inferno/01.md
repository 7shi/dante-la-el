# Inferno 1

## Lines 1–9 — Lost in a Dark Wood

> 1 Nel mezzo del cammin di nostra vita  
> 2 Mi ritrovai per una selva oscura,  
> 3 Chè la diritta via era smarrita.  

In the middle of the journey of our life, I found myself in a dark wood; for the straight way was lost.

- *Mi ritrovai* ‘I found myself again’ → “I found myself”: the prefix *ri-* is not separately rendered; here, however, the Italian need not imply finding oneself a second time. The reflexive pronoun moves after the verb, and English makes the subject explicit.
- *per una selva oscura* ‘through a wood dark’ → “in a dark wood”: *per* is rendered by a preposition of location rather than movement or extension; the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a causal subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction introducing an explanation.

> 4 Ahi quanto a dir qual era è cosa dura  
> 5 Questa selva selvaggia ed aspra e forte,  
> 6 Che nel pensier rinnova la paura!  

Ah! how hard a thing it is to tell what a wild, and rough, and stubborn wood this was, which in my thought renews the fear:

- *quanto … è cosa dura* ‘how much … is a hard thing’ → “how hard a thing it is”: the degree word is attached directly to the adjective, which moves before the noun. English supplies the anticipatory subject “it” and places the infinitive phrase after the main construction.
- *a dir* ‘to say’ → “to tell”: the verb is rendered by a different English verb suited to describing something.
- *qual era … Questa selva selvaggia ed aspra e forte* ‘of what kind was this wood, wild and rough and strong’ → “what a wild, and rough, and stubborn wood this was”: the description is recast as an exclamatory “what a” construction. The adjectives move before the noun; *questa*, a demonstrative modifying *selva*, becomes the independent pronoun “this”; and the verb moves to the end of the clause.
- *forte* ‘strong’ → “stubborn”: Carlyle chooses a sense suggesting resistance or intractability rather than translating the adjective by its most direct English equivalent.
- *nel pensier* ‘in the thought’ → “in my thought”: the definite article is replaced by a possessive, making the thinker explicit.

> 7 Tanto è amara, che poco è più morte:  

so bitter is it, that scarcely more is death.

- *Tanto è amara* ‘So much is bitter’ → “so bitter is it”: the degree word and adjective are brought together before the verb, and the subject, implicit in Italian, is expressed by “it”.
- *poco* ‘little’ → “scarcely”: a small degree of difference is expressed as a difference that barely exists.
- *è più morte* ‘is more death’ → “more is death”: the comparative word moves before the verb; the subject remains after it.

> 8 Ma per trattar del ben ch’ivi trovai,  
> 9 Dirò dell’ altre cose, ch’io v’ ho scorte.  

But to treat of the good that I there found, I will relate the other things that I discerned.

- *per trattar* ‘in order to treat’ → “to treat”: the purpose expressed by *per* and an infinitive is conveyed by the English infinitive alone.
- *ch’ivi trovai* ‘that there I found’ → “that I there found”: English supplies the subject pronoun implicit in *trovai* and places it before the locative adverb.
- *Dirò* ‘I shall say’ → “I will relate”: the general verb of saying becomes a verb of recounting; the subject implicit in the Italian verb is expressed separately.
- *dell’ altre cose* ‘of the other things’ → “the other things”: the prepositional complement becomes the direct object of “relate”, with no separate equivalent for *di*.
- *ch’io v’ ho scorte* ‘that I have discerned there’ → “that I discerned”: the compound perfect becomes an English simple past, and the locative *v’*, ‘there’, is omitted.

## Lines 10–21 — Uncertain Entry and Easing Fear

> 10 Io non so ben ridir com’ io v’ entrai;  

I cannot rightly tell how I entered it,

- *Io non so ben ridir* ‘I do not know how to recount well’ → “I cannot rightly tell”: knowing how is expressed as ability; *ridir* becomes “tell” rather than ‘recount’, and *ben* becomes “rightly” rather than ‘well’.
- *v’ entrai* ‘I entered there’ → “I entered it”: the locative adverb *v’* is replaced by the direct object “it”.

> 11 Tant’ era pien di sonno in su quel punto,  
> 12 Che la verace via abbandonai.  

so full of sleep was I about the moment that I left the true way.

- *Tant’ era pien di sonno* ‘So much was I full of sleep’ → “so full of sleep was I”: the entire adjective phrase precedes the verb, whereas Italian places *era* between *Tant’* and *pien*. English supplies the subject pronoun, implicit in *era*.
- *in su quel punto* ‘at that point’ → “about the moment”: the prepositional expression becomes “about”; *punto* is rendered temporally as “moment”, and the demonstrative *quel* becomes the definite article “the”.
- *Che* ‘that’ → “that”: the English construction treats this as a temporal relative attached to “the moment”, rather than as a result clause dependent on *Tant’*.
- *la verace via abbandonai* ‘the true way I abandoned’ → “I left the true way”: the object moves after the verb, the implicit subject is supplied, and the stronger ‘abandoned’ becomes “left”.

> 13 Ma poi ch’ io fui al piè d’un colle giunto,  
> 14 Là ove terminava quella valle,  
> 15 Che m’ avea di paura il cor compunto,  

But after I had reached the foot of a Hill there, where that valley ended, which had pierced my heart with fear,

- *poi ch’* ‘after that’ → “after”: the Italian conjunctional expression is represented by a single English conjunction.
- *io fui al piè d’un colle giunto* ‘I had arrived at the foot of a hill’ → “I had reached the foot of a Hill”: the intransitive construction with *giungere* and *a* becomes transitive “reached”, without a preposition before its object. The participle moves from after the destination phrase to before it; “Hill” is capitalized although *colle* is not.
- *Là ove terminava quella valle* ‘There where ended that valley’ → “there, where that valley ended”: the subject moves before the verb. The Italian imperfect *terminava* is represented by the English simple past.
- *m’ avea di paura il cor compunto* ‘had pricked the heart for me with fear’ → “had pierced my heart with fear”: the affected-person pronoun *m’* and definite article *il* become possessive “my”; *compunto* becomes “pierced” rather than ‘pricked’. English places the participle immediately after the auxiliary and moves the fear phrase after the object.

> 16 Guardai in alto, e vidi le sue spalle  
> 17 Vestite già de’ raggi del pianeta,  
> 18 Che mena dritto altrui per ogni calle.  

I looked up and saw its shoulders already clothed with the rays of the Planet that leads men straight on every road.

- *Guardai in alto* ‘I looked on high’ → “I looked up”: the prepositional phrase becomes the directional adverb “up”; English supplies the subject implicit in *Guardai*.
- *le sue spalle* ‘the its shoulders’ → “its shoulders”: English omits the definite article used with the Italian possessive.
- *Vestite già* ‘Clothed already’ → “already clothed”: the adverb moves before the participle.
- *pianeta* ‘planet’ → “Planet”: the English capitalizes the noun.
- *mena dritto altrui* ‘leads straight others’ → “leads men straight”: the indefinite human pronoun *altrui* becomes the noun “men”, and “straight” moves after the object.
- *per ogni calle* ‘through every path’ → “on every road”: *per* becomes “on” rather than ‘through’, and *calle* becomes “road” rather than ‘path’.

> 19 Allor fu la paura un poco queta,  

Then the fear was somewhat calmed,

- *fu la paura un poco queta* ‘was the fear a little quiet’ → “the fear was somewhat calmed”: the subject moves before the verb; “somewhat” replaces ‘a little’, and the adjective *queta* is rendered by the participle “calmed”, expressing the attainment of quiet rather than simply describing a quiet state.

> 20 Che nel lago del cor m’ era durata  
> 21 La notte, ch’ io passai con tanta pieta.  

which had continued in the lake of my heart the night that I passed so piteously.

- *nel lago del cor m’ era durata* ‘in the lake of the heart had lasted for me’ → “had continued in the lake of my heart”: *durata* becomes “continued” rather than ‘lasted’. The affected-person pronoun *m’* is represented by possessive “my”, replacing the definite article attached to *cor*. The verb moves before the phrase specifying where the fear persisted.
- *con tanta pieta* ‘with so much anguish’ → “so piteously”: the prepositional phrase containing a noun becomes an adverbial expression. *Tanta* becomes the degree modifier “so”, while the anguish expressed by *pieta* is conveyed as a piteous manner of passing the night.

## Lines 22–33 — Escape, Ascent, and a Leopard

> 22 E come quei, che con lena affannata  
> 23 Uscito fuor del pelago alla riva,  
> 24 Si volge all’ acqua perigliosa, e guata;  

And as he, who with panting breath has escaped from the deep sea to the shore, turns to the dangerous water and gazes;

- *quei* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *lena affannata* ‘laboured breath’ → “panting breath”: the adjective becomes a participial modifier describing the breathing, and precedes rather than follows the noun.
- *Uscito fuor* ‘having come out’ → “has escaped”: the Italian participial construction becomes a finite present-perfect verb. Coming out is rendered more specifically as escaping, and *fuor* is not separately expressed.
- *pelago* ‘open sea’ → “deep sea”: the expansive or offshore sense of the noun is rendered in terms of depth.
- *Si volge* ‘turns himself’ → “turns”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an English intransitive verb.
- *acqua perigliosa* ‘water dangerous’ → “dangerous water”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 25 Così l’ animo mio, che ancor fuggiva,  
> 26 Si volse indietro a rimirar lo passo,  
> 27 Che non lasciò giammai persona viva.  

so my mind, which still was fleeing, turned back to see the pass that no one ever left alive.

- *l’animo mio* ‘my spirit’ → “my mind”: *animo* is given a specifically mental rendering. The possessive moves before the noun, and the Italian definite article is not separately represented.
- *Si volse indietro* ‘turned itself back’ → “turned back”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *a rimirar* ‘to gaze at’ → “to see”: the verb of directed, attentive looking becomes the more general verb of seeing.
- *Che non lasciò giammai persona viva* ‘which never left any person alive’ → “that no one ever left alive”: the translation takes the relative pronoun as the object of “left” and the person as its subject, rather than taking the passage as the subject that left nobody alive. The negation is consequently attached to “no one,” while *giammai* becomes “ever.”
- *lo passo, / Che* ‘the passage, which’ → “the pass that”: the comma introducing the Italian relative clause is omitted, producing an explicitly restrictive English construction.

> 28 Poi ch’ ebbi riposato il corpo lasso,  

After I had rested my wearied body,

- *Poi ch’* ‘after that’ → “After”: the two-part Italian conjunction is rendered by a single English conjunction.
- *il corpo lasso* ‘the weary body’ → “my wearied body”: the definite article becomes a possessive identifying whose body it is. The adjective moves before the noun, and the condition of weariness is expressed with the participial adjective “wearied.”

> 29 Ripresi via per la piaggia diserta,  
> 30 Sì che il piè fermo sempre era il più basso.  

I took the way again along the desert strand, so that the firm foot always was the lower.

- *Ripresi via* ‘I resumed the way’ → “I took the way again”: the sense of resumption contained in *ripresi* is distributed between “took” and “again.” English also supplies “the” before the noun, which has no article in Italian.
- *per la piaggia diserta* ‘along the deserted slope’ → “along the desert strand”: the translation selects the shore-related sense of *piaggia*, rather than its sense of a slope or hillside. The adjective precedes the noun rather than following it.
- *il piè fermo* ‘the stationary foot’ → “the firm foot”: *fermo* is rendered as firmness rather than explicitly as remaining stationary or planted. The adjective moves before the noun.
- *il più basso* ‘the lowest’ → “the lower”: the Italian relative-superlative construction becomes an English comparative, appropriate to a comparison between two feet.

> 31 Ed ecco, quasi al cominciar dell’ erta,  
> 32 Una lonza leggiera e presta molto,  
> 33 Che di pel maculato era coverta.  

And behold, almost at the commencement of the steep, a Leopard, light and very nimble, which was covered with a spotted skin:

- *al cominciar* ‘at the beginning’ → “at the commencement”: the substantivized Italian infinitive is rendered by an English abstract noun.
- *dell’erta* ‘of the ascent’ → “of the steep”: the noun denoting an upward slope is rendered with the less usual English noun “steep.”
- *lonza* ‘leopard’ → “Leopard”: the English animal name is capitalized although the Italian is not.
- *presta molto* ‘nimble very’ → “very nimble”: the degree modifier moves before the adjective.
- *Che di pel maculato era coverta* ‘which with spotted hair was covered’ → “which was covered with a spotted skin”: the covering phrase moves from before the verb to after it. *Pel*, referring to hair or fur, becomes “skin”; English supplies the indefinite article “a,” and the adjective moves before its noun. The Italian sentence-ending period is replaced by a colon.

## Lines 34–47 — Blocked progress and renewed fear

> 34 E non mi si partia dinanzi al volto;  

and it went not from before my face;

- *mi si partia* ‘it departed from me’ → “it went”: the pronominal verb is rendered by “went,” without a separate equivalent for *si*; *mi* is represented by “my” with “face.”
- *dinanzi al volto* ‘from before the face’ → “from before my face”: the definite article becomes a possessive, incorporating *mi*.

> 35 Anzi impediva tanto il mio cammino,  
> 36 Ch’io fui per ritornar più volte vôlto.  

nay, so impeded my way, that I had often turned to go back.

- *Anzi* ‘rather’ → “nay”: the corrective conjunction is rendered by an English expression of correction or intensification.
- *impediva tanto il mio cammino* ‘impeded my way so much’ → “so impeded my way”: *tanto* becomes “so” and moves before the verb.
- *Ch’io fui per ritornar più volte vôlto* ‘that I was several times turned toward returning’ → “that I had often turned to go back”: the simple-past construction *fui … vôlto* becomes an active pluperfect; the phrase expressing the direction of the turning moves to the end.
- *più volte* ‘several times’ → “often”: an expression counting repeated occasions becomes an adverb of frequency.
- *per ritornar* ‘toward returning’ → “to go back”: the orientation expressed by *per* becomes an infinitive of purpose, and *ritornar* is rendered by a phrasal expression.

> 37 Tempo era dal principio del mattino;  

The time was at the beginning of the morning;

- *Tempo era* ‘time was’ → “The time was”: the definite article is supplied.
- *dal principio* ‘from the beginning’ → “at the beginning”: the Italian preposition is rendered as a temporal location rather than a starting point.

> 38 E il Sol montava in su con quelle stelle  
> 39 Ch’eran con lui, quando l’ Amor Divino  
> 40 Mosse da prima quelle cose belle;  

and the Sun was mounting up with those stars, which were with him when Divine Love first moved those fair things:

- *montava* ‘mounted’ → “was mounting”: the Italian imperfect becomes an explicitly progressive English past.
- *l’ Amor Divino* ‘the Love Divine’ → “Divine Love”: the article is omitted, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Mosse da prima* ‘moved at first’ → “first moved”: the adverbial phrase becomes a single adverb placed before the verb.
- *quelle cose belle* ‘those things beautiful’ → “those fair things”: the adjective moves before the noun, and *belle* is rendered by “fair.”

> 41 Sì che a bene sperar m’ eran cagione  
> 42 Di quella fera la gaietta pelle,  
> 43 L’ ora del tempo, e la dolce stagione:  

so that the gay skin of that animal, the hour of time, and the sweet season, were causes to me of good hope;

- *a bene sperar m’ eran cagione* ‘were to me a cause for hoping well’ → “were causes to me of good hope”: the infinitive *sperar* becomes the noun “hope,” the adverb *bene* becomes the adjective “good,” and singular *cagione* becomes plural “causes.”
- *m’ eran cagione* ‘to me were a cause’ → “were causes to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb and its complement.
- *Sì che a bene sperar m’ eran cagione* ‘so that for hoping well were to me a cause’ → “so that”: the predicate following *Sì che* is postponed until after the three subjects listed in lines 42–43.
- *Di quella fera la gaietta pelle* ‘of that beast the gay little skin’ → “the gay skin of that animal”: the possessive phrase moves after “skin”; *fera* becomes the less specific “animal,” and the diminutive nuance of *gaietta* is not separately expressed.

> 44 Ma non sì, che paura non mi desse  
> 45 La vista, che m’ apparve, d’un leone.  

yet not so, but that I feared at the sight, which appeared to me, of a Lion.

- *Ma* ‘but’ → “yet”: the contrast is expressed with a different conjunction.
- *che paura non mi desse La vista* ‘that the sight should not give me fear’ → “but that I feared at the sight”: the negative subjunctive construction becomes an affirmative indicative clause introduced by “but that.” The sight changes from the subject giving fear to a prepositional complement; *mi*, the recipient of fear, becomes the subject “I.”
- *leone* ‘lion’ → “Lion”: the noun is capitalized.

> 46 Questi parea, che contra me venesse  
> 47 Con la testa alta, e con rabbiosa fame;  

He seemed coming upon me with head erect, and furious hunger;

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “He”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *parea, che contra me venesse* ‘seemed that he was coming against me’ → “seemed coming upon me”: the finite clause with subjunctive *venesse* becomes a participial construction; *contra me* moves after the expression of coming, and “upon” replaces ‘against.’
- *Con la testa alta* ‘with the head high’ → “with head erect”: the definite article is omitted, and “erect” renders height as upright posture.
- *e con rabbiosa fame* ‘and with raging hunger’ → “and furious hunger”: the repeated preposition *con* is omitted, and *rabbiosa* is rendered by “furious.”

## Lines 48–60 — The She-wolf Drives Dante Back

> 48 Sì che parea che l’aer ne tremesse.  

so that the air seemed to quake thereat.

- *parea che l’aer ne tremesse* ‘it seemed that the air trembled at it’ → “the air seemed to quake thereat”: the Italian subordinate subject becomes the subject of “seemed,” and the finite imperfect subjunctive becomes an infinitive. *Tremesse* is rendered as “quake” rather than ‘tremble’; *ne*, placed before the Italian verb, becomes the final adverb “thereat.”

> 49 Ed una lupa, che di tutte brame  
> 50 Sembiava carca nella sua magrezza,  
> 51 E molte genti fe’ già viver grame.  

And a She-wolf, that looked full of all cravings in her leanness; and has ere now made many live in sorrow:

- *di tutte brame / Sembiava carca* ‘of all cravings seemed laden’ → “looked full of all cravings”: the complement preceding the Italian verb is moved after “full.” ‘Seemed’ becomes the more specifically visual “looked,” and ‘laden’ becomes “full.”
- *sua* ‘her’ → “her”: the Italian possessive does not specify the possessor’s gender; English makes it explicit.
- *molte genti* ‘many people’ → “many”: the noun is omitted, leaving “many” to stand for the people. This object moves from before *fe’* to after “made.”
- *fe’ già* ‘already made’ → “has ere now made”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, and *già* is expanded into “ere now.”
- *viver grame* ‘live sorrowful’ → “live in sorrow”: the adjective describing the people becomes a prepositional phrase.

> 52 Questa mi porse tanto di gravezza  
> 53 Con la paura, che uscia di sua vista,  
> 54 Ch’ io perdei la speranza dell’altezza.  

She brought such heaviness upon me with the terror of her aspect, that I lost the hope of ascending.

- *Questa* ‘this one’ → “She”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *mi porse* ‘offered me’ → “brought such heaviness upon me”: the verb of offering or giving becomes “brought,” while the preceding indirect-object pronoun becomes the following phrase “upon me.”
- *tanto di gravezza* ‘so much heaviness’ → “such heaviness”: the Italian quantitative construction becomes an English construction expressing degree or quality.
- *la paura, che uscia di sua vista* ‘the fear that issued from her appearance’ → “the terror of her aspect”: “terror” strengthens ‘fear,’ and the relative clause expressing emergence is compressed into “of.” *Vista* is interpreted as her appearance, rendered by “aspect.”
- *dell’altezza* ‘of the height’ → “of ascending”: a noun naming height becomes a gerund naming the action of rising.

> 55 E quale è quei, che volentieri acquista,  
> 56 E giugne il tempo che perder lo face,  
> 57 Che in tutti i suoi pensier piange e s’ attrista;  

And as one who is eager in gaining, and, when the time arrives that makes him lose, weeps and afflicts himself in all his thoughts;

- *quale è quei* ‘as is that one’ → “as one”: the explicit “is” of the Italian comparison is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes the indefinite “one.”
- *volentieri acquista* ‘willingly gains’ → “is eager in gaining”: the adverb becomes a predicative adjective expressing eagerness, with “is” supplied; the finite verb becomes a gerund.
- *E giugne il tempo* ‘and the time comes’ → “and, when the time arrives”: “when” is supplied, turning the arrival of the time into a subordinate temporal clause. “And” consequently coordinates the person’s gaining and weeping rather than directly introducing the time’s arrival. The Italian verb–subject order is reversed.
- *perder lo face* ‘lose him makes’ → “makes him lose”: the causative construction is reordered into normal English verb–object–infinitive order.
- *Che in tutti i suoi pensier piange e s’attrista* ‘who in all his thoughts weeps and saddens himself’ → “weeps and afflicts himself in all his thoughts”: the renewed *Che* is not separately rendered, and the phrase about his thoughts moves from before the verbs to after them.
- *s’attrista* ‘saddens himself’ → “afflicts himself”: the translation uses a broader term for causing distress rather than the specific verb of becoming sad.

> 58 Tal mi fece la bestia senza pace,  
> 59 Che, venendomi incontro, a poco a poco  
> 60 Mi ripingeva là dove il Sol tace.  

such that restless beast made me, which coming against me, by little and little drove me back to where the Sun is silent.

- *Tal mi fece la bestia senza pace* ‘such me made the beast without peace’ → “such that restless beast made me”: “such” retains its initial position, but the subject moves before the verb and the object pronoun after it.
- *la bestia senza pace* ‘the beast without peace’ → “that restless beast”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative, and the following prepositional phrase becomes the preceding adjective “restless.”
- *venendomi incontro* ‘coming toward me’ → “coming against me”: “against” gives the approach an explicitly opposing direction; the attached Italian pronoun becomes a separate English pronoun after the preposition.
- *a poco a poco* ‘little by little’ → “by little and little”: the repeated Italian prepositional construction becomes an English phrase coordinating the two occurrences of “little.”
- *Mi ripingeva* ‘was pushing me back’ → “drove me back”: “drove” replaces ‘pushed,’ and the imperfect is rendered by a simple past rather than an explicitly continuing construction. The object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “to where”: the explicit locative ‘there’ is not retained; “to” instead marks the destination of the backward movement.
- *tace* ‘is silent’ → “is silent”: the single Italian verb becomes an English copula and adjective.

## Lines 61–72 — A stranger identifies himself

> 61 Mentre ch’ io rovinava in basso loco,  
> 62 Dinanzi agli occhi mi si fu offerto  
> 63 Chi per lungo silenzio parea fioco.  

Whilst I was rushing downwards, there appeared before my eyes one who seemed hoarse from long silence.

- *Mentre ch’ io* ‘while that I’ → “Whilst I”: the compound conjunction *mentre che* becomes a single English conjunction.
- *rovinava* ‘was falling headlong’ → “was rushing”: falling or tumbling is rendered as rapid movement.
- *in basso loco* ‘into a low place’ → “downwards”: a phrase naming a low place becomes an adverb of direction; *loco* has no separate equivalent.
- *Dinanzi agli occhi mi* ‘before the eyes, to me’ → “before my eyes”: the personal dative *mi* becomes the possessive “my”; the phrase is moved after the verb.
- *si fu offerto* ‘had presented himself’ → “there appeared”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, the compound past becomes a simple past, and presentational “there” is supplied.
- *Chi* ‘who’ → “one who”: the independent relative pronoun is expanded into an antecedent and a relative pronoun.
- *parea fioco* ‘seemed faint’ → “seemed hoarse”: the translation chooses a specifically vocal interpretation of *fioco*, rather than general faintness or weakness.
- *per lungo silenzio* ‘through long silence’ → “from long silence”: causal *per* becomes “from”; the phrase moves from before the predicate to after it.

> 64 Quando vidi costui nel gran diserto,  
> 65 Miserere di me, gridai a lui,  
> 66 Qual che tu sie, od ombra, od uomo certo.  

When I saw him in the great desert, I cried: “Have pity on me, whate’er thou be, whether shade or veritable man!”

- *vidi costui* ‘I saw this one’ → “I saw him”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun; English explicitly expresses the subject encoded in *vidi*.
- *Miserere di me* ‘have mercy on me’ → “Have pity on me”: the Latin imperative *Miserere* is rendered by an English verbal phrase, with pity rather than mercy.
- *gridai a lui* ‘I cried to him’ → “I cried”: *a lui* is omitted, and the reporting clause moves from within the speech to before it.
- *od ombra, od uomo certo* ‘either shade, or actual man’ → “whether shade or veritable man”: the paired alternatives become a “whether … or” construction.
- *uomo certo* ‘actual man’ → “veritable man”: the adjective moves before the noun; “veritable” expresses *certo* in the sense of real or actual, rather than certain.

> 67 Risposemi: Non uomo, uomo già fui,  

He answered me: “Not man, a man I once was;

- *Risposemi* ‘answered me’ → “He answered me”: English supplies an explicit subject and renders the attached pronoun *-mi* as a separate word.
- *uomo già fui* ‘man formerly I was’ → “a man I once was”: English supplies the indefinite article and explicitly expresses the subject encoded in *fui*; *già* becomes “once,” placed after “I.”

> 68 E li parenti miei furon Lombardi,  
> 69 E Mantovani per patria ambedui.  

and my parents were Lombards, and both of Mantua by country.

- *li parenti miei* ‘the parents my’ → “my parents”: the possessive moves before the noun, replacing the Italian combination of definite article and possessive.
- *Mantovani* ‘Mantuans’ → “of Mantua”: the plural demonym becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *per patria ambedui* ‘by homeland both’ → “both of Mantua by country”: “both” moves before the description of origin, while *patria* is rendered as “country.”

> 70 Nacqui sub Julio, ancorchè fosse tardi,  

I was born sub Julio, though it was late;

- *Nacqui* ‘I was born’ → “I was born”: the Italian simple active verb is rendered by the English passive construction required by this idiom, with an explicit subject.
- *ancorchè fosse tardi* ‘although it were late’ → “though it was late”: the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative; English also requires the impersonal subject “it.”

> 71 E vissi a Roma sotto il buono Augusto,  
> 72 Al tempo degli Dei falsi e bugiardi.  

and lived at Rome under the good Augustus, in the time of the false and lying gods.

- *E vissi* ‘and I lived’ → “and lived”: the first-person subject encoded in *vissi* is carried over from the preceding English clause rather than repeated.
- *Al tempo* ‘at the time’ → “in the time”: the temporal preposition changes.
- *degli Dei falsi e bugiardi* ‘of the gods false and lying’ → “of the false and lying gods”: both adjectives move before the noun; the capital initial of *Dei* is not retained.

## Lines 73–85 — Virgil’s Identity and Dante’s Tribute

> 73 Poeta fui, e cantai di quel giusto  
> 74 Figliuol d’ Anchise, che venne da Troia,  
> 75 Poi che il superbo Ilion fu combusto.  

A Poet I was; and sang of that just son of Anchises, who came from Troy after proud Ilium was burnt.

- *Poeta fui* ‘Poet I was’ → “A Poet I was”: English supplies the indefinite article and makes explicit the subject implicit in *fui*.
- *il superbo Ilion* ‘the proud Ilium’ → “proud Ilium”: the definite article before the qualified proper name is omitted.

> 76 Ma tu, perchè ritorni a tanta noia?  

But thou, why returnest thou to such disquiet?

- *tu, perchè ritorni* ‘thou, why returnest’ → “thou, why returnest thou”: English repeats the subject pronoun after the verb to form the question.
- *tanta noia* ‘so much trouble’ → “such disquiet”: the expression of quantity or degree becomes one with “such”; *noia* is rendered as inward uneasiness.

> 77 Perchè non sali il dilettoso monte,  
> 78 Ch’ è principio e cagion di tutta gioia?  

why ascendest not the delectable mountain, which is the beginning and the cause of all gladness?”

- *Perchè non sali* ‘why not ascendest’ → “why ascendest not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *dilettoso* ‘delightful’ → “delectable”: Carlyle uses an alternative adjective for something affording delight.
- *è principio e cagion* ‘is beginning and cause’ → “is the beginning and the cause”: English supplies a definite article before each predicate noun.
- *gioia* ‘joy’ → “gladness”: an alternative noun is used.

> 79 Or se’ tu quel Virgilio, e quella fonte,  
> 80 Che spande di parlar sì largo fiume?  
> 81 Risposi lui con vergognosa fronte.  

“Art thou then that Virgil, and that fountain which pours abroad so rich a stream of speech?” I answered him, with bashful front.

- *Or se’ tu* ‘Now art thou’ → “Art thou then”: *or* is interpreted inferentially rather than temporally, and its equivalent moves after the subject.
- *spande* ‘spreads’ → “pours abroad”: a single verb becomes a verb with an adverb, retaining the sense of outward diffusion.
- *di parlar* ‘of speaking’ → “of speech”: the substantival infinitive becomes a noun, and the phrase moves from before *sì largo fiume* to after its English equivalent.
- *sì largo fiume* ‘so wide a river’ → “so rich a stream”: physical breadth becomes richness or abundance, while *fiume* is rendered by “stream” rather than ‘river’.
- *Risposi lui* ‘I replied to him’ → “I answered him”: the Italian dative *lui* becomes the direct object of “answered”; English also states the subject implicit in *risposi*.
- *vergognosa fronte* ‘ashamed brow’ → “bashful front”: *vergognosa* is interpreted as bashfulness; “front” is an archaic equivalent for the brow or countenance.

> 82 O degli altri poeti onore e lume,  

“O glory, and light of other poets!

- *degli altri poeti onore e lume* ‘of the other poets honour and light’ → “glory, and light of other poets”: the dependent phrase moves after the nouns it qualifies, and the definite article in *degli* is omitted.
- *onore* ‘honour’ → “glory”: an alternative noun of distinction or praise is used.

> 83 Vagliami il lungo studio e il grande amore,  
> 84 Che m’ han fatto cercar lo tuo volume.  

May the long zeal avail me, and the great love, that made me search thy volume.

- *Vagliami il lungo studio* ‘May the long study avail me’ → “May the long zeal avail me”: the Italian optative subjunctive is expressed with “May”; the attached pronoun *-mi* becomes the separate object “me”. The first subject moves from after the Italian verb to between the English auxiliary and main verb.
- *studio* ‘study’ → “zeal”: Carlyle selects the sense of devoted application rather than study itself.
- *m’ han fatto* ‘have made me’ → “made me”: the Italian compound past becomes an English simple past.
- *lo tuo volume* ‘the thy volume’ → “thy volume”: the definite article accompanying the possessive is omitted.

> 85 Tu se’ lo mio maestro, e il mio autore:  

Thou art my master and my author.

- *lo mio maestro, e il mio autore* ‘the my master, and the my author’ → “my master and my author”: both definite articles accompanying possessives are omitted.

## Lines 86–100 — Praise and Another Road

> 86 Tu se’ solo colui, da cui io tolsi  
> 87 Lo bello stile, che m’ ha fatto onore.  

Thou alone art he from whom I took the good style that hath done me honour.

- *Tu se’ solo colui* ‘Thou art alone he’ → “Thou alone art he”: “alone” moves before the verb.
- *bello* ‘beautiful’ → “good”: aesthetic praise becomes a more general expression of excellence.
- *che m’ ha fatto onore* ‘that to me has made honour’ → “that hath done me honour”: *fare onore* becomes the English idiom “done me honour”; the pronoun moves from before the auxiliary to after the participle.

> 88 Vedi la bestia, per cui io mi volsi:  

See the beast from which I turned back.

- *per cui* ‘because of which’ → “from which”: the Italian gives the cause of the turning; the translation presents the beast as what the speaker turned away from.
- *io mi volsi* ‘I turned myself’ → “I turned back”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb; “back” makes the reversal of direction explicit.

> 89 Aiutami da lei, famoso saggio,  
> 90 Ch’ ella mi fa tremar le vene e i polsi.  

Help me from her, thou famous sage; for she makes my veins and pulses tremble.”

- *famoso saggio* ‘famous sage’ → “thou famous sage”: “thou” is supplied in the address.
- *mi fa tremar le vene e i polsi* ‘makes the veins and the pulses tremble for me’ → “makes my veins and pulses tremble”: the affected-person pronoun *mi* becomes possessive “my”; the definite articles are replaced by a single possessive governing both nouns.
- *fa tremar le vene e i polsi* ‘makes tremble the veins and the pulses’ → “makes my veins and pulses tremble”: the nouns move between the causative verb and its infinitive, as English requires.

> 91 A te convien tenere altro viaggio,  
> 92 Rispose, poi che lagrimar mi vide,  
> 93 Se vuoi campar d’ esto loco selvaggio:  

“Thou must take another road,” he answered, when he saw me weeping, “if thou desirest to escape from this wild place;

- *A te convien* ‘For thee it is necessary’ → “Thou must”: an impersonal construction with *a te* becomes a personal subject with a modal verb.
- *tenere altro viaggio* ‘keep to another journey’ → “take another road”: *tenere* becomes “take,” and *viaggio*, a journey or course, becomes the road travelled.
- *Rispose* ‘answered’ → “he answered”: English supplies the subject pronoun implicit in the Italian verb.
- *poi che* ‘after that’ → “when”: the translation expresses the time of the response without explicitly retaining the sense of temporal succession.
- *lagrimar mi vide* ‘to weep me he saw’ → “he saw me weeping”: the infinitive becomes a participle and moves after “me”; the subject pronoun, implicit in *vide*, is supplied.
- *vuoi* ‘wishest’ → “desirest”: the translation chooses a more formal expression of wishing.

> 94 Chè questa bestia, per la qual tu gride,  
> 95 Non lascia altrui passar per la sua via,  
> 96 Ma tanto lo impedisce, che l’ uccide:  

because this beast, for which thou criest, lets not men pass her way, but so entangles that she slays them;

- *Non lascia* ‘not lets’ → “lets not”: the negative moves from before the verb to after it.
- *altrui* ‘another’ or ‘others’ → “men”: an indefinite reference to other people becomes an explicitly plural noun.
- *passar per la sua via* ‘pass along her way’ → “pass her way”: the preposition *per* is not separately expressed; the Italian article before the possessive is also absent.
- *tanto lo impedisce* ‘so much impedes him’ → “so entangles”: “entangles” gives a more specific manner of obstruction than *impedisce*; “so” renders the degree expressed by *tanto*, and the object pronoun *lo* is omitted.
- *che l’ uccide* ‘that kills him’ → “that she slays them”: “she” supplies the implicit subject; singular *l’* becomes plural “them,” agreeing with “men”; “slays” is a more elevated equivalent of ‘kills’.

> 97 E ha natura sì malvagia e ria,  
> 98 Che mai non empie la bramosa voglia,  
> 99 E dopo il pasto ha più fame che pria.  

and has a nature so perverse and vicious, that she never satiates her craving appetite; and after feeding, she is hungrier than before.

- *ha natura* ‘has nature’ → “has a nature”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *malvagia e ria* ‘evil and wicked’ → “perverse and vicious”: the translation chooses different terms of condemnation, with “perverse” particularly emphasizing a distorted or contrary disposition.
- *mai non* ‘never not’ → “never”: Italian negative concord is expressed by a single English negative.
- *empie* ‘fills’ → “she never satiates”: “satiates” interprets filling as satisfying appetite; “she” supplies the implicit subject, while “never” renders the preceding *mai non*.
- *la bramosa voglia* ‘the craving desire’ → “her craving appetite”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the general *voglia* is narrowed to “appetite.”
- *dopo il pasto* ‘after the meal’ → “after feeding”: a noun naming the meal becomes a gerund naming the activity; the article is omitted.
- *ha più fame* ‘has more hunger’ → “she is hungrier”: possession of a greater quantity of hunger becomes a state expressed by a comparative adjective; the implicit subject is supplied.

> 100 Molti son gli animali, a cui s’ ammoglia,  

The animals to which she weds herself are many;

- *Molti son gli animali, a cui s’ ammoglia* ‘Many are the animals to which she marries herself’ → “The animals to which she weds herself are many;”: the predicate “many” moves from the beginning to the end, while the relative clause moves with the subject before “are.”
- *s’ ammoglia* ‘marries’ → “she weds herself”: the Italian pronominal verb is rendered with an explicit reflexive object, “herself,” and its implicit subject is supplied as “she.”

## Lines 101–105 — The Greyhound’s Coming

> 101 E più saranno ancora, infin che il Veltro  
> 102 Verrà, che la farà morir di doglia.  

and will yet be more, until the Greyhound comes, that will make her die with pain.

- *più saranno ancora* ‘more will be still’ → “will yet be more”: *più* moves after the verb, while *ancora*, rendered “yet”, moves before “be”.
- *infin che il Veltro Verrà* ‘until the Greyhound will come’ → “until the Greyhound comes”: the Italian future becomes an English present, as normally required in a future-time clause introduced by “until”.
- *di doglia* ‘of pain’ → “with pain”: the preposition expressing the cause of dying changes from *di* to “with”.

> 103 Questi non ciberà terra nè peltro,  
> 104 Ma sapienza, e amore, e virtute;  

He will not feed on land or pelf, but on wisdom, and love, and manfulness;

- *Questi* ‘This one’ → “He”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *non ciberà* ‘will not eat’ → “will not feed on”: the Italian transitive verb becomes an English verb with a prepositional complement.
- *terra* ‘earth’ → “land”: the translation selects the sense of land rather than earth as material.
- *nè* ‘nor’ → “or”: English places the coordination under the preceding “not” rather than repeating the negative in the conjunction.
- *peltro* ‘pewter’ → “pelf”: the metal is rendered by a word meaning wealth or riches, making its figurative sense explicit.
- *Ma sapienza* ‘But wisdom’ → “but on wisdom”: “on” is supplied again to continue the English construction “feed on”.
- *virtute* ‘virtue’ → “manfulness”: the translation chooses a sense emphasizing courage or strength of character rather than the broader ‘virtue’.

> 105 E sua nazion sarà tra Feltro e Feltro.  

and his nation shall be between Feltro and Feltro.

- *nazion* ‘birth’ or ‘origin’ → “nation”: the translation retains the cognate English noun rather than explicitly rendering the Italian sense of birth or origin.

## Lines 106–120 — Salvation and the promised journey

> 106 Di quell’ umile Italia fia salute,  
> 107 Per cui morì la vergine Cammilla,  
> 108 Eurialo, e Turno, e Niso di ferute:  

He shall be the salvation of that low Italy, for which Camilla the virgin, Euryalus, and Turnus, and Nisus, died of wounds.

- *Di quell’ umile Italia fia salute* ‘Of that humble Italy shall be salvation’ → “He shall be the salvation of that low Italy”: the initial *di* phrase moves after “salvation”; “He” makes the understood subject explicit, and “the” is supplied before “salvation.”
- *umile* ‘humble’ → “low”: the translation adopts the word’s spatial sense rather than its sense of humility or lowliness.
- *la vergine Cammilla* ‘the virgin Cammilla’ → “Camilla the virgin”: the name precedes its descriptive title, and its spelling is normalized.
- *Eurialo*, *Turno*, *Niso* ‘Eurialo, Turno, Niso’ → “Euryalus”, “Turnus”, “Nisus”: the Italian names are replaced by their conventional English forms.

> 109 Questi la caccerà per ogni villa,  
> 110 Fin che l’ avrà rimessa nell’ Inferno,  
> 111 Là onde invidia prima dipartilla.  

He shall chase her through every city, till he have put her into Hell again; from which envy first set her loose.

- *Questi* ‘This one’ → “He”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *villa* ‘town’ → “city”: the translation chooses a more specific designation for the settlement.
- *l’ avrà rimessa* ‘he will have put her back’ → “he have put her into Hell again”: the Italian future perfect becomes an English perfect subjunctive after “till”; the return expressed by *ri-* is rendered by “again,” placed after the destination.
- *nell’ Inferno* ‘in Hell’ → “into Hell”: English explicitly marks motion towards the destination.
- *Là onde* ‘There whence’ → “from which”: ‘There’ is omitted, and the phrase becomes a relative expression referring directly to “Hell.”
- *dipartilla* ‘sent her away’ → “set her loose”: the translation interprets the sending away as a release, rather than reproducing the verb’s sense of separation or departure.

> 112 Ond’ io per lo tuo me’ penso e discerno,  
> 113 Che tu mi segui, ed io sarò tua guida,  
> 114 E trarrotti di qui per luogo eterno,  

Wherefore I think and discern this for thy best, that thou follow me. And I will be thy guide, and lead thee hence through an eternal place,

- *io per lo tuo me’ penso e discerno* ‘I for your better think and discern’ → “I think and discern this for thy best”: the phrase expressing benefit moves after the verbs, and “this” is supplied to anticipate the following clause.
- *lo tuo me’* ‘your better’ → “thy best”: the substantival comparative *me’*, shortened from *meglio*, is rendered by a superlative.
- *Che tu mi segui* ‘that you me follow’ → “that thou follow me”: the Italian object pronoun before the verb moves after it in English.
- *ed io sarò tua guida* ‘and I shall be your guide’ → “And I will be thy guide”: the coordination is retained, but a new sentence begins.
- *E trarrotti* ‘And I shall draw you’ → “and lead thee”: “lead” renders *trarre* less literally than ‘draw’; the subject and future auxiliary are shared with the preceding clause rather than repeated.
- *di qui* ‘from here’ → “hence”: the prepositional phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *per luogo eterno* ‘through eternal place’ → “through an eternal place”: “an” is supplied, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 115 Ove udirai le disperate strida,  
> 116 Vedrai gli antichi spiriti dolenti,  
> 117 Che la seconda morte ciascun grida:  

where thou shalt hear the hopeless shrieks, shalt see the ancient spirits in pain; so that each calls for second death.

- *le disperate strida* ‘the despairing shrieks’ → “the hopeless shrieks”: the adjective is rendered as absence of hope rather than as an expression of despair.
- *gli antichi spiriti dolenti* ‘the ancient suffering spirits’ → “the ancient spirits in pain”: *dolenti*, an adjective, becomes the prepositional phrase “in pain.”
- *Che* ‘who / that’ → “so that”: the translation takes the connective as introducing a consequence rather than as a relative referring to the spirits.
- *la seconda morte ciascun grida* ‘the second death each cries’ → “each calls for second death”: the object moves from before the subject to after the verb; “calls for” interprets the crying as an appeal, supplying “for”; and the definite article before “second death” is omitted.

> 118 E vederai color, che son contenti  
> 119 Nel fuoco, perchè speran di venire,  
> 120 Quando che sia, alle beate genti:  

And thou shalt see those who are contented in the fire; for they hope to come, whensoever it be, amongst the blessed.

- *perchè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating explanation, separated from the preceding clause by a semicolon.
- *alle beate genti* ‘to the blessed peoples’ → “amongst the blessed”: “amongst” replaces the directional “to”; the explicit plural noun *genti* is not separately rendered, and “the blessed” functions substantivally.

## Lines 121–136 — Virgil’s Offer and Dante’s Request

> 121 Alle quai poi se tu vorrai salire,  
> 122 Anima fia a ciò di me più degna:  

Then to these, if thou desirest to ascend, there shall be a Spirit worthier than I to guide thee.

- *Alle quai poi* ‘to which then’ → “Then to these”: the relative pronoun becomes a demonstrative, and the adverb moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *se tu vorrai salire* ‘if thou wilt wish to ascend’ → “if thou desirest to ascend”: the future *vorrai* becomes a present tense.
- *Anima fia* ‘a soul shall be’ → “there shall be a Spirit”: the subject moves after the verb in an existential construction with introductory “there”; ‘soul’ becomes the capitalized “Spirit.”
- *a ciò* ‘for this’ → “to guide thee”: the general expression of purpose becomes an explicit infinitive phrase, supplying the action and its object.
- *a ciò di me più degna* ‘for this more worthy than me’ → “worthier than I to guide thee”: the purpose phrase moves after the comparison; ‘more worthy’ becomes “worthier,” and the comparison uses the subject form “I.”

> 123 Con lei ti lascierò nel mio partire;  

With her will I leave thee at my parting.

- *Con lei ti lascierò* ‘with her I shall leave thee’ → “With her will I leave thee”: the auxiliary precedes the subject, while the object pronoun moves from before the Italian verb to after the English verb.
- *nel mio partire* ‘in my departing’ → “at my parting”: “at” replaces ‘in’ in the temporal phrase.

> 124 Chè quello Imperador, che lassù regna,  
> 125 Perch’ io fui ribellante alla sua legge,  
> 126 Non vuol che in sua città per me si vegna.  

For that Emperor who reigns above, because I was rebellious to his law, wills not that I come into his city.

- *che lassù regna* ‘who up there reigns’ → “who reigns above”: the adverb follows the verb, and “above” renders the more explicitly locative *lassù*.
- *che in sua città per me si vegna* ‘that into his city one should come through me’ → “that I come into his city”: the impersonal *si vegna* becomes a first-person clause. Carlyle interprets the restriction as concerning the speaker’s own entrance rather than entrance through his agency; *per me* is consequently not separately rendered.
- *in sua città … si vegna* ‘into his city … one should come’ → “I come into his city”: the destination moves from before the verb to after it.

> 127 In tutte parti impera, e quivi regge:  

In all parts he rules; and there he dwells.

- *impera* ‘he exercises imperial power’ → “he rules”: the explicitly imperial verb becomes the more general “rules.”
- *quivi regge* ‘there he rules’ → “there he dwells”: Carlyle interprets the verb as residence rather than government.

> 128 Quivi è la sua cittade, e l’ alto seggio:  

There is his city, and his high seat.

- *l’alto seggio* ‘the high seat’ → “his high seat”: the definite article becomes a possessive, extending the preceding *sua* to this second noun.

> 129 O felice colui, cui ivi elegge!  

O happy whom he chooses for it!”

- *colui, cui* ‘that one whom’ → “whom”: the demonstrative antecedent is left unexpressed, producing a compressed English construction.
- *cui ivi elegge* ‘whom there he chooses’ → “whom he chooses for it”: the locative adverb becomes a prepositional phrase expressing destination or appointment, and moves after the verb.

> 130 Ed io a lui: Poeta, io ti richieggio  
> 131 Per quello Iddio, che tu non conoscesti,  
> 132 A ciò ch’ io fugga questo male e peggio,  
> 133 Che tu mi meni là dov’ or dicesti,  

And I to him: “Poet, I beseech thee by that God whom thou knewest not: in order that I may escape this ill and worse, lead me where thou now hast said,

- *io ti richieggio* ‘I thee beseech’ → “I beseech thee”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *io fugga* ‘I flee’ → “I may escape”: the Italian present subjunctive is rendered with “may,” and ‘flee’ becomes “escape.”
- *Che tu mi meni* ‘that thou lead me’ → “lead me”: the subordinate request introduced by *Che* becomes an imperative; the conjunction and explicit subject are omitted, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *là dov’* ‘there where’ → “where”: the separate demonstrative adverb *là* is omitted.
- *or dicesti* ‘now thou saidst’ → “thou now hast said”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, and the expressed English subject precedes “now.”

> 134 Sì ch’ io vegga la porta di San Pietro,  
> 135 E color, che tu fai cotanto mesti.  

so that I may see the Gate of St. Peter, and those whom thou makest so sad.”

- *io vegga* ‘I see’ → “I may see”: the present subjunctive is rendered with the modal “may.”
- *la porta di San Pietro* ‘the gate of Saint Peter’ → “the Gate of St. Peter”: the common noun is capitalized, and the saint’s title is abbreviated.

> 136 Allor si mosse; ed io li tenni dietro.  

Then he moved; and I kept on behind him.

- *si mosse* ‘he moved himself’ → “he moved”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive English verb without an expressed reflexive pronoun.
- *li tenni dietro* ‘to him I held behind’ → “I kept on behind him”: the Italian idiom becomes an English expression of continued following. “Kept on” renders *tenni*, and the preverbal indirect-object pronoun becomes “him” after “behind.”

## Summary — Close Echoes and Selective Recasting

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s sequence of expression, often preserving its repetitions, images, and unusual phrasing. “The lake of my heart,” “where the Sun is silent,” and “my veins and pulses tremble” retain distinctive features rather than replacing them with more ordinary English. Inversions such as “so bitter is it” and “A Poet I was,” together with archaic pronouns and verb forms, also keep the prose from becoming a wholly idiomatic modern paraphrase.

Many departures arise from differences in grammar and word order. Adjectives generally move before their nouns, object pronouns follow their verbs, and subjects precede verbs where Italian places them afterwards. Carlyle regularly supplies subject pronouns that Italian verb endings make unnecessary, drops articles accompanying possessives, and turns personal datives into possessives: *m’ … il cor* becomes “my heart.” More substantial recasting changes *A te convien* into “Thou must,” *ha più fame* into “she is hungrier,” and *con tanta pieta* into “so piteously.” Italian tense and mood distinctions are sometimes expressed through English auxiliaries, sometimes reduced: compound pasts become simple pasts, while future verbs in temporal clauses can become presents.

His vocabulary alternates between close equivalents and interpretive choices. “Stubborn” for *forte*, “zeal” for *studio*, and “manfulness” for *virtute* select particular shades of meaning rather than the most immediate English cognates. “Pelf” for *peltro* makes a figurative meaning explicit, whereas “strand” for *piaggia* chooses a different geographical sense from “slope.” Such choices can narrow, strengthen, or redirect the Italian without greatly expanding the translation.

Omissions and additions are usually small: a repeated preposition disappears, a locative such as *v’* goes unrepresented, or an article or pronoun is supplied. Occasionally, however, compression or clarification changes the construction more noticeably. The relative clause describing fear issuing from an appearance becomes “the terror of her aspect,” while *a ciò* is expanded to “to guide thee.” Elsewhere, interpretation affects agency: *per me si vegna* becomes “I come,” replacing an impersonal expression of entrance through someone’s agency with that person’s own entrance. Overall, Carlyle preserves much of the Italian’s verbal texture, but combines that closeness with grammatical accommodation and selective interpretation.
