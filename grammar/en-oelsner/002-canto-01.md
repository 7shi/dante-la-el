# Inferno 1

## Lines 1–21 — Lost in a Dark Wood

> 1 Nel mezzo del cammin di nostra vita  
> 2 mi ritrovai per una selva oscura,  
> 3 che la diritta via era smarrita.  

In the middle of the journey of our life I [came to] myself in a dark wood [where] the straight way was lost.

- *mi ritrovai* ‘myself I found’ → “I [came to] myself”: finding oneself is rendered as coming to oneself; the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *per una selva oscura* ‘through a wood dark’ → “in a dark wood”: *per* is rendered by a preposition of location rather than movement, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *che* ‘that’ → “[where]”: the connective is interpreted as a locative relative referring to the wood, rather than as a causal conjunction, ‘because’.

> 4 Ahi quanto a dir qual era è cosa dura  
> 5 questa selva selvaggia ed aspra e forte,  
> 6 che nel pensier rinnova la paura!  

Ah! how hard a thing it is to tell what a wild, and rough, and stubborn wood this was, which in my thought renews the fear!

- *quanto a dir ... è cosa dura* ‘how much to tell ... is a thing hard’ → “how hard a thing it is to tell”: the degree word is attached directly to the fronted adjective; introductory “it” is supplied, and the infinitive follows the predicate instead of preceding it.
- *qual era ... questa selva selvaggia ed aspra e forte* ‘of what kind was ... this wood wild and rough and strong’ → “what a wild, and rough, and stubborn wood this was”: the question about the wood’s character becomes a construction with “what a”; “this” becomes the subject, while “wood” becomes part of the predicate. The adjectives precede the noun, and *forte* is rendered as “stubborn” rather than ‘strong’.
- *nel pensier* ‘in the thought’ → “in my thought”: the definite article is replaced by a possessive not explicit in the Italian.

> 7 Tanto è amara, che poco è più morte:  

So bitter is it, that scarcely more is death:

- *Tanto è amara* ‘So much is it bitter’ → “So bitter is it”: the adjective moves before the verb, immediately after its degree word.
- *che poco è più morte* ‘that little is more death’ → “that scarcely more is death”: *poco* is expressed by “scarcely”, and “more” moves before the verb.

> 8 ma per trattar del ben ch' i' vi trovai,  
> 9 dirò dell' altre cose, ch' io v' ho scorte.  

but to treat of the good that I there found, I will relate the other things that I discerned.

- *dirò dell' altre cose* ‘I shall speak of the other things’ → “I will relate the other things”: speaking ‘of’ something becomes relating it directly; the prepositional complement becomes a direct object.
- *ch' io v' ho scorte* ‘that I there have discerned’ → “that I discerned”: the locative *v’* is omitted, and the compound perfect becomes a simple past.

> 10 I' non so ben ridir com' io v' entrai;  

I cannot rightly tell how I entered it,

- *I' non so ben ridir* ‘I do not know well how to tell again’ → “I cannot rightly tell”: not knowing how is expressed as inability; *ben* becomes “rightly”, emphasizing accuracy, and the reiterative force of *ridir* is not separately expressed.
- *com' io v' entrai* ‘how I there entered’ → “how I entered it”: the locative pronoun becomes the direct object “it” and moves after the verb.

> 11 tant' era pien di sonno in su quel punto,  
> 12 che la verace via abbandonai.  

so full of sleep was I about the moment that I left the true way.

- *tant' era pien di sonno* ‘so much was I full of sleep’ → “so full of sleep was I”: the whole adjective phrase moves before the verb, immediately after its degree word.
- *in su quel punto* ‘at that point’ → “about the moment”: the prepositional expression becomes the less exact “about”; the demonstrative becomes a definite article, and *punto* is rendered explicitly as a moment in time.
- *che la verace via abbandonai* ‘that the true way I abandoned’ → “that I left the true way”: the object moves after the verb, and “left” replaces the stronger ‘abandoned’.

> 13 Ma poi ch' io fui al piè d' un colle giunto,  
> 14 là dove terminava quella valle,  
> 15 che m' avea di paura il cor compunto,  

But after I had reached the foot of a Hill there, where that valley ended, which had pierced my heart with fear,

- *io fui al piè d' un colle giunto* ‘I had at the foot of a hill arrived’ → “I had reached the foot of a Hill”: arriving ‘at’ a place becomes reaching it directly, so the preposition is absorbed into the transitive construction. The participle moves before the destination; the Italian past-anterior construction is rendered by the English past perfect.
- *là dove terminava quella valle* ‘there where ended that valley’ → “there, where that valley ended”: the subject moves before the verb; the Italian imperfect is rendered by an English simple past.
- *che m' avea di paura il cor compunto* ‘which to me had with fear the heart pierced’ → “which had pierced my heart with fear”: the personal dative *m’* becomes the possessive “my”; the participle moves next to its auxiliary, and the phrase expressing fear moves after the object.

> 16 guardai in alto, e vidi le sue spalle  
> 17 vestite già de' raggi del pianeta,  
> 18 che mena dritto altrui per ogni calle.  

I looked up and saw its shoulders already clothed with the rays of the Planet that leads men straight on every road.

- *guardai in alto* ‘I looked on high’ → “I looked up”: the prepositional phrase becomes a directional adverb.
- *vestite già* ‘clothed already’ → “already clothed”: the adverb moves before the participle.
- *de' raggi* ‘of the rays’ → “with the rays”: the Italian preposition introducing what clothes something is replaced by the English idiomatic “with”.
- *mena dritto altrui* ‘leads straight others’ → “leads men straight”: the indefinite pronoun *altrui* becomes the generic noun “men”, and the object moves before the adverb.
- *per ogni calle* ‘along every road’ → “on every road”: the preposition expressing a route becomes “on”.

> 19 Allor fu la paura un poco queta,  
> 20 che nel lago del cor m' era durata  
> 21 la notte, ch' i' passai con tanta pieta.  

Then the fear was somewhat calmed, which had continued in the lake of my heart the night that I passed so piteously.

- *Allor fu la paura un poco queta* ‘Then was the fear a little quiet’ → “Then the fear was somewhat calmed”: the subject moves before the verb; the adjective ‘quiet’ becomes the participle “calmed”, suggesting a resulting state, and the phrase ‘a little’ becomes the single adverb “somewhat”.
- *che nel lago del cor m' era durata* ‘which in the lake of the heart to me had lasted’ → “which had continued in the lake of my heart”: the verb moves before the locative phrase; the personal dative *m’* becomes the possessive “my”, and ‘had lasted’ becomes “had continued”.
- *con tanta pieta* ‘with so much distress’ → “so piteously”: a prepositional phrase containing a noun becomes an adverb of manner, with *tanta* expressed by “so”.

## Lines 22–54 — Escape and the Three Beasts

> 22 E come quei, che con lena affannata  
> 23 uscito fuor del pelago alla riva,  
> 24 si volge all' acqua perigliosa, e guata:  

And as he, who with panting breath has escaped from the deep sea to the shore, turns to the dangerous water and gazes:

- *con lena affannata* ‘with breath laboured’ → “with panting breath”: the adjective precedes the noun, and “panting” expresses the laboured breathing.
- *uscito fuor* ‘having come out’ → “has escaped”: the participial construction becomes the finite present perfect of the English relative clause; coming out is interpreted specifically as escaping.
- *del pelago* ‘from the sea’ → “from the deep sea”: “deep” makes explicit a connotation of *pelago*, without a separate Italian adjective.
- *si volge* ‘turns himself’ → “turns”: the reflexive pronoun disappears. This verb falls outside the English relative clause, whereas the Italian relative construction extends through it.
- *all' acqua perigliosa* ‘to the water dangerous’ → “to the dangerous water”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 25 così l' animo mio, che ancor fuggiva,  
> 26 si volse indietro a rimirar lo passo,  
> 27 che non lasciò giammai persona viva.  

so my mind, which still was fleeing, turned back to see the pass that no one ever left alive.

- *l' animo mio* ‘the mind my’ → “my mind”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *si volse indietro* ‘turned itself back’ → “turned back”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive.
- *a rimirar* ‘to look closely at’ → “to see”: the deliberate looking expressed by *rimirar* becomes the more general act of seeing.
- *che non lasciò giammai persona viva* ‘which never left a person alive’ → “that no one ever left alive”: the pass, originally the subject of *lasciò*, becomes the object of “left”; the person becomes the subject. The negative consequently shifts from the verb to “no one,” and leaving someone alive becomes leaving a place alive.

> 28 Poi ch' ei posato un poco il corpo lasso,  
> 29 ripresi via per la piaggia diserta,  
> 30 sì che il piè fermo sempre era il più basso.  

After I had rested my wearied body [a short while], I took the way again along the desert strand, so that the [right] foot always was the lower.

- *un poco* ‘a little’ → “[a short while]”: the expression is made explicitly temporal and moved after the object of “rested.”
- *il corpo lasso* ‘the body weary’ → “my wearied body”: the definite article becomes a possessive, the adjective precedes the noun, and “wearied” presents the weariness as a resulting condition.
- *ripresi via* ‘I resumed way’ → “I took the way again”: the renewed action expressed by *ripresi* is distributed between “took” and “again”; an article is supplied before “way.”
- *per la piaggia diserta* ‘along the deserted slope’ → “along the desert strand”: “strand” selects the shore sense of *piaggia* rather than the slope sense; “desert” replaces ‘deserted,’ and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *il piè fermo* ‘the foot planted’ → “the [right] foot”: the revision substitutes an identification of the foot for the description of it as planted or stationary; the modifier also moves before the noun.
- *il più basso* ‘the lowest’ → “the lower”: the Italian construction is rendered with an English comparative, appropriate to a comparison between two feet.

> 31 Ed ecco, quasi al cominciar dell' erta,  
> 32 una lonza leggiera e presta molto,  
> 33 che di pel maculato era coperta.  

And behold, almost at the commencement of the steep, a Leopard, light and very nimble, which was covered with spotted [hair].

- *al cominciar* ‘at the beginning’ → “at the commencement”: the substantivized infinitive becomes an ordinary noun.
- *presta molto* ‘nimble very’ → “very nimble”: the intensifier moves before the adjective.
- *di pel maculato* ‘with hair spotted’ → “with spotted [hair]”: the phrase moves from before the passive verb to after it, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 34 E non mi si partia dinanzi al volto;  
> 35 anzi impediva tanto il mio cammino,  
> 36 ch' io fui per ritornar più volte volto.  

And it went not from before my face; nay, so impeded my way, that I had often turned to go back.

- *non ... si partia* ‘not ... departed’ → “it went not”: the pronominal verb of departure becomes “went,” without a reflexive form; the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *mi ... dinanzi al volto* ‘to me ... from before the face’ → “from before my face”: the dative pronoun expressing the person affected becomes the possessive “my.”
- *anzi* ‘rather’ → “nay”: the corrective adverb becomes an English corrective interjection.
- *impediva tanto* ‘impeded so much’ → “so impeded”: the expression of degree is reduced to “so” and moved before the verb.
- *ch' io fui per ritornar più volte volto* ‘that I was several times turned towards returning’ → “that I had often turned to go back”: *fui ... volto* becomes active rather than passive, and the simple past becomes past perfect. “Often” replaces the countable repetition ‘several times,’ while the phrase expressing return moves after “turned.”

> 37 Tempo era dal principio del mattino;  
> 38 e il sol montava in su con quelle stelle  
> 39 ch' eran con lui, quando l' amor divino  
> 40 mosse da prima quelle cose belle;  

The time was at the beginning of the morning; and the sun was mounting up with those stars, which were with him when Divine Love first moved those fair things:

- *Tempo era* ‘time was’ → “The time was”: the definite article is supplied.
- *dal principio del mattino* ‘from the beginning of the morning’ → “at the beginning of the morning”: “at” replaces the Italian preposition ‘from.’
- *l' amor divino* ‘the love divine’ → “Divine Love”: the adjective precedes the noun, and the definite article is omitted.
- *da prima* ‘at first’ → “first”: the prepositional expression becomes a single adverb and moves before “moved.”
- *quelle cose belle* ‘those things beautiful’ → “those fair things”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 41 sì che a bene sperar m' eran cagione  
> 42 di quella fera alla gaietta pelle  
> 43 l' ora del tempo, e la dolce stagione:  

so that the hour of time and the sweet season [caused me to have good hope of that animal with the gay skin];

- *a bene sperar m' eran cagione* ‘for hoping well were to me a cause’ → “caused me to have good hope”: the construction ‘were a cause to me’ becomes a causative verb with “me” as its object. The infinitive ‘hope’ and adverb ‘well’ become the noun “hope” and adjective “good,” with “to have” supplied; the resulting hope follows rather than precedes its cause.
- *di quella fera* ‘of that wild beast’ → “of that animal”: “animal” is less specifically wild or fierce than *fera*.
- *alla gaietta pelle* ‘with the gayish skin’ → “with the gay skin”: the diminutive colouring of *gaietta* is not separately represented; the Italian descriptive construction with *a* is expressed by “with.”
- *l' ora del tempo, e la dolce stagione* ‘the hour of the time and the sweet season’ → “the hour of time and the sweet season”: the subjects move from the end of the clause to its beginning, before the verb; the article before ‘time’ is omitted.

> 44 ma non sì, che paura non mi desse  
> 45 la vista, che m' apparve, d' un leone.  

yet not so, but that I feared at the sight, which appeared to me, of a Lion.

- *ma non sì, che paura non mi desse la vista* ‘but not so that the sight did not give me fear’ → “yet not so, but that I feared at the sight”: the negative subordinate clause becomes the affirmative English construction “but that.” The sight ceases to be the subject giving fear: the recipient becomes the subject, *paura* becomes the verb “feared,” and the sight becomes a prepositional complement. The Italian subjunctive is replaced by an indicative.
- *che m' apparve* ‘which to me appeared’ → “which appeared to me”: the preverbal indirect-object pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase after the verb.

> 46 Questi parea che contra me venesse  
> 47 con la testa alta, e con rabbiosa fame,  
> 48 sì che parea che l' aer ne temesse;  

He seemed coming upon me with head erect, and furious hunger; so that the air seemed to [have fear] thereat;

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “He”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *parea che contra me venesse* ‘seemed that against me he came’ → “seemed coming upon me”: the finite subordinate clause, with subjunctive *venesse*, becomes a participial complement. “Upon” renders the hostile direction expressed by *contra*, and the phrase follows rather than precedes the verb of movement.
- *con la testa alta* ‘with the head high’ → “with head erect”: the article is omitted, and “erect” interprets the head’s being held high.
- *e con rabbiosa fame* ‘and with furious hunger’ → “and furious hunger”: the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *sì che parea che l' aer ne temesse* ‘so that it seemed that the air feared because of it’ → “so that the air seemed to [have fear] thereat”: the air becomes the subject of “seemed,” and the finite subjunctive clause becomes an infinitive complement. The verb ‘fear’ becomes the noun phrase “[have fear],” while the preverbal pronoun *ne* becomes the following adverb “thereat.”

> 49 ed una lupa, che di tutte brame  
> 50 sembiava carca nella sua magrezza,  
> 51 e molte genti fe' già viver grame.  

and a She-wolf, that looked full of all cravings in her leanness; and has ere now made many live in sorrow.

- *che di tutte brame sembiava carca* ‘that of all cravings seemed laden’ → “that looked full of all cravings”: “looked” gives the seeming a specifically visual expression, and “full” replaces the image of being laden. The phrase specifying the cravings moves after the predicate.
- *molte genti* ‘many people’ → “many”: the noun is omitted, leaving “many” substantival; the object moves from before the causative verb to after it.
- *fe' già* ‘made already’ → “has ere now made”: the simple past becomes present perfect; “ere now” renders *già* and precedes rather than follows the main verb.
- *viver grame* ‘live wretched’ → “live in sorrow”: the adjective describing the people’s condition becomes a prepositional phrase with an abstract noun.

> 52 Questa mi porse tanto di gravezza  
> 53 con la paura, che uscia di sua vista,  
> 54 ch' io perdei la speranza dell' altezza.  

She brought such heaviness upon me with the terror of her aspect, that I lost the hope of ascending.

- *Questa* ‘this one’ → “She”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *mi porse tanto di gravezza* ‘to me offered so much of heaviness’ → “brought such heaviness upon me”: *porse* becomes “brought,” and the indirect object becomes “upon me,” following the direct object. The quantitative construction *tanto di* becomes the modifier “such.”
- *con la paura, che uscia di sua vista* ‘with the fear that issued from her appearance’ → “with the terror of her aspect”: “terror” strengthens ‘fear’; the relative clause expressing the fear’s emergence is compressed into “of her aspect,” leaving its verb unrepresented.
- *dell' altezza* ‘of the height’ → “of ascending”: the noun denoting height becomes a gerund denoting upward movement, and its article disappears.

## Lines 55–87 — Dante Meets His Master

> 55 E quale è quei, che volentieri acquista,  
> 56 e giugne il tempo che perder lo face,  
> 57 che in tutti i suoi pensier piange e s'attrista:  

And as one who is eager in gaining, and, when the time arrives that makes him lose, weeps and afflicts himself in all his thoughts:

- *quale è quei* ‘as is he’ → “as one”: the verb is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *che volentieri acquista* ‘who willingly gains’ → “who is eager in gaining”: the adverb becomes a predicative adjective, and the finite verb becomes a gerund governed by a preposition.
- *e giugne il tempo* ‘and arrives the time’ → “and, when the time arrives”: a temporal conjunction is supplied, turning the coordinated clause into a subordinate one; the subject moves before the verb.
- *che perder lo face* ‘that to lose him makes’ → “that makes him lose”: the causative verb comes first, followed by its object and the infinitive.
- *che in tutti i suoi pensier piange e s'attrista* ‘who in all his thoughts weeps and saddens himself’ → “weeps and afflicts himself in all his thoughts”: the resumptive relative pronoun is omitted; the prepositional phrase moves after the verbs; becoming sad is rendered as afflicting oneself.

> 58 tal mi fece la bestia senza pace,  
> 59 che, venendomi incontro, a poco a poco  
> 60 mi ripingeva là dove il sol tace.  

such that restless beast made me, which coming against me, by little and little drove me back to where the Sun is silent.

- *tal mi fece la bestia senza pace* ‘such me made the beast without peace’ → “such that restless beast made me”: the subject moves before the verb and the object after it; the definite article becomes a demonstrative; the phrase describing the beast becomes an adjective placed before its noun.
- *venendomi incontro* ‘coming to meet me’ → “coming against me”: the combination of a pronominal complement and *incontro* becomes a prepositional phrase that explicitly expresses opposition.
- *a poco a poco* ‘little by little’ → “by little and little”: the repeated Italian phrase is rendered by a differently constructed English idiom.
- *mi ripingeva* ‘me was pushing back’ → “drove me back”: pushing becomes driving, the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the object follows the verb.
- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “to where”: the demonstrative adverb is omitted, while a preposition explicitly marks the destination.
- *tace* ‘keeps silent’ → “is silent”: the verb becomes a copula with an adjective.

> 61 Mentre ch' io rovinava in basso loco,  
> 62 dinanzi agli occhi mi si fu offerto  
> 63 chi per lungo silenzio parea fioco.  

Whilst I was rushing downwards, there appeared before my eyes one who seemed hoarse from long silence.

- *rovinava in basso loco* ‘was tumbling into a low place’ → “was rushing downwards”: tumbling is rendered as rushing, and the phrase naming a low place becomes an adverb of direction.
- *dinanzi agli occhi mi si fu offerto* ‘before the eyes to me had offered himself’ → “there appeared before my eyes”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and the past perfect becomes a simple past; presentational “there” is supplied. The reference to the speaker becomes the possessive “my”, and the phrase locating the appearance moves after the verb.
- *chi* ‘who’ → “one who”: the independent relative pronoun is expanded into an antecedent and a relative pronoun.
- *per lungo silenzio parea fioco* ‘through long silence seemed faint’ → “seemed hoarse from long silence”: the causal phrase moves after the predicate; *fioco* is interpreted specifically as hoarseness rather than more generally as faintness or weakness.

> 64 Quand' io vidi costui nel gran diserto,  
> 65 “Miserere di me,” gridai a lui,  
> 66 “qual che tu sii, od ombra, od uomo certo.”  

When I saw him in the great desert, I cried: “Have pity on me, whate’er thou be, whether shade or veritable man!”

- *costui* ‘this man’ → “him”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *Miserere di me* ‘Have mercy on me’ → “Have pity on me”: the Latin imperative is translated into an English verb-and-noun phrase, with pity in place of mercy.
- *gridai a lui* ‘I cried to him’ → “I cried”: the reporting clause moves before the direct speech, and its explicit addressee is omitted.
- *od ombra, od uomo certo* ‘either shade or man real’ → “whether shade or veritable man”: the first alternative is introduced by “whether” rather than ‘either’, and the adjective precedes its noun.

> 67 Risposemi : “Non uomo, uomo già fui,  
> 68 e li parenti miei furon Lombardi,  
> 69 Mantovani per patria ambo e dui.  

He answered me: “Not man, a man I once was; and my parents were Lombards, and both of Mantua by country.

- *uomo già fui* ‘man once I was’ → “a man I once was”: an indefinite article is supplied, and the adverb follows the expressed subject.
- *li parenti miei* ‘the parents mine’ → “my parents”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *Mantovani per patria ambo e dui* ‘Mantuans by native land both and two’ → “and both of Mantua by country”: a coordinating conjunction is supplied; the demonym becomes a prepositional phrase; the emphatic expression *ambo e dui* is reduced to “both” and moved before that phrase. *Patria* is rendered as “country” rather than specifically as native land.

> 70 Nacqui sub Julio, ancorchè fosse tardi,  
> 71 e vissi a Roma sotto il buono Augusto,  
> 72 al tempo degli Dei falsi e bugiardi.  

I was born *sub Julio*, though it was late; and lived at Rome under the good Augustus, in the time of the false and lying Gods.

- *ancorchè fosse tardi* ‘although it were late’ → “though it was late”: the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *al tempo* ‘at the time’ → “in the time”: the temporal preposition changes.
- *degli Dei falsi e bugiardi* ‘of the Gods false and lying’ → “of the false and lying Gods”: both adjectives move before the noun.

> 73 Poeta fui, e cantai di quel giusto  
> 74 figliuol d' Anchise, che venne da Troia,  
> 75 poi che il superbo Ilion fu combusto.  

A poet I was; and sang of that just son of Anchises, who came from Troy after proud Ilium was burnt.

- *Poeta fui* ‘poet I was’ → “A poet I was”: an indefinite article is supplied before the predicate noun.
- *il superbo Ilion* ‘the proud Ilium’ → “proud Ilium”: the definite article before the qualified proper name is omitted.

> 76 Ma tu, perchè ritorni a tanta noia?  

But thou, why returnest thou to such disquiet?

- *tu, perchè ritorni* ‘thou, why returnest’ → “thou, why returnest thou”: the explicitly stated subject is repeated within the question.
- *a tanta noia* ‘to so much distress’ → “to such disquiet”: the expression of quantity or degree becomes one with “such”, and distress is rendered more specifically as disquiet.

> 77 perchè non sali il dilettoso monte,  
> 78 ch' è principio e cagion di tutta gioia?”  

why ascendest not the delectable mountain, which is the beginning and the cause of all gladness?”

- *perchè non sali* ‘why not ascendest’ → “why ascendest not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *è principio e cagion* ‘is beginning and cause’ → “is the beginning and the cause”: a definite article is supplied before each predicate noun.

> 79 “Or se' tu quel Virgilio, e quella fonte,  
> 80 che spande di parlar sì largo fiume?”  
> 81 risposi lui con vergognosa fronte.  

“Art thou then that Virgil, and that fountain which pours abroad so rich a stream of speech?” I answered him, with bashful front.

- *Or se' tu* ‘Now art thou’ → “Art thou then”: the introductory adverb is rendered as inferential “then” and moves after the subject.
- *spande* ‘pours out’ → “pours abroad”: the verb is rendered by a phrase emphasizing outward diffusion.
- *di parlar sì largo fiume* ‘of speaking so broad a river’ → “so rich a stream of speech”: the dependent phrase moves after its noun; the substantival infinitive becomes a noun; breadth becomes richness, and a river becomes a stream.
- *vergognosa* ‘ashamed’ → “bashful”: the adjective is interpreted as expressing bashfulness rather than shame.

> 82 “O degli altri poeti onore e lume,  

“O glory, and light of other poets!

- *degli altri poeti onore e lume* ‘of the other poets honour and light’ → “glory, and light of other poets”: the dependent phrase moves after the nouns it qualifies, its definite article is omitted, and honour becomes glory.

> 83 vagliami il lungo studio e il grande amore,  
> 84 che m' ha fatto cercar lo tuo volume.  

May the long zeal avail me, and the great love, that made me search thy volume.

- *vagliami il lungo studio* ‘avail me the long study’ → “May the long zeal avail me”: the optative subjunctive is expressed with “May”, the subject moves before the main verb, and study is interpreted as zeal.
- *che m' ha fatto cercar* ‘that me has made seek’ → “that made me search”: the compound perfect becomes a simple past, and the object pronoun moves after the causative verb. Seeking is rendered as searching, presenting the volume as something examined rather than merely sought.

> 85 Tu se' lo mio maestro, e il mio autore;  

Thou art my master and my author;

- Literal.

> 86 tu se' solo colui, da cui io tolsi  
> 87 lo bello stile, che m' ha fatto onore.  

thou alone art he from whom I took the good style that hath done me honour.

- *tu se' solo colui* ‘thou art only he’ → “thou alone art he”: the restrictive word moves from after the verb to immediately after the subject.
- *lo bello stile* ‘the beautiful style’ → “the good style”: aesthetic beauty is rendered as more general excellence.
- *che m' ha fatto onore* ‘that to me has made honour’ → “that hath done me honour”: the Italian idiom with ‘make’ becomes the English idiom with “done”, and the indirect object follows the verb.

## Lines 88–120 — The Beast and the Other Road

> 88 Vedi la bestia, per cui io mi volsi;  

See the beast from which I turned back;

- *per cui io mi volsi* ‘because of which I turned myself’ → “from which I turned back”: the causal relation becomes one of movement away; the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, with “back” added.

> 89 aiutami da lei, famoso saggio,  
> 90 ch'ella mi fa tremar le vene e i polsi."  

help me from her, thou famous sage; for she makes my veins and pulses tremble."

- *famoso saggio* ‘famous sage’ → “thou famous sage”: “thou” is added to the vocative.
- *ch'* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *mi fa tremar le vene e i polsi* ‘makes tremble to me the veins and the pulses’ → “makes my veins and pulses tremble”: the bodily parts precede the infinitive in the English causative construction; the dative *mi* becomes the possessive “my”, and the repeated articles disappear.

> 91 "A te convien tenere altro viaggio,"  
> 92 rispose, poi che lagrimar mi vide,  
> 93 "se vuoi campar d'esto loco selvaggio:  

"Thou must take another road," he answered, when he saw me weeping, "if thou desirest to escape from this wild place:

- *A te convien* ‘To thee it behoves’ → “Thou must”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal subject with a modal verb expressing necessity.
- *tenere altro viaggio* ‘to keep another journey’ → “take another road”: the expression for pursuing a journey becomes one for taking a route; the infinitive loses “to” after “must”.
- *poi che lagrimar mi vide* ‘after he saw me weep’ → “when he saw me weeping”: temporal succession becomes a temporal coincidence; the infinitive becomes a participle, and the Italian order, with *lagrimar* before *mi vide*, is reversed.
- *loco selvaggio* ‘place wild’ → “wild place”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 94 chè questa bestia, per la qual tu gride,  
> 95 non lascia altrui passar per la sua via,  
> 96 ma tanto lo impedisce, che l' uccide;  

because this beast, for which thou criest, lets not men pass her way; but so entangles that she slays them;

- *non lascia altrui passar* ‘does not let others pass’ → “lets not men pass”: the indefinite pronoun *altrui* becomes the generic noun “men”; the negative follows the finite verb.
- *per la sua via* ‘along her way’ → “her way”: the preposition is omitted, leaving the route directly after “pass”.
- *tanto lo impedisce, che l' uccide* ‘so much hinders him that she kills him’ → “so entangles that she slays them”: hindering becomes entangling; the first object pronoun is omitted, and the second changes from singular to plural to agree with “men”.

> 97 ed ha natura sì malvagia e ria,  
> 98 che mai non empie la bramosa voglia,  
> 99 e dopo il pasto ha più fame che pria.  

and has a nature so perverse and vicious, that she never satiates her craving appetite; and after feeding, she is hungrier than before.

- *natura* ‘nature’ → “a nature”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *mai non empie* ‘never not fills’ → “never satiates”: Italian negative concord becomes a single English negative; filling is expressed as satiating.
- *la bramosa voglia* ‘the craving desire’ → “her craving appetite”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the general word for desire becomes the more specifically appetitive “appetite”.
- *dopo il pasto* ‘after the meal’ → “after feeding”: a noun naming the meal becomes a gerund naming the activity.
- *ha più fame* ‘has more hunger’ → “is hungrier”: possession of a greater quantity becomes a state expressed by a comparative adjective.

> 100 Molti son gli animali, a cui s' ammoglia,  
> 101 e più saranno ancora, infin che il Veltro  
> 102 verrà, che la farà morir con doglia.  

The animals to which she weds herself are many; and will yet be more, until the Greyhound comes, that will make her die with pain.

- *Molti son gli animali, a cui s' ammoglia* ‘Many are the animals to which she weds herself’ → “The animals to which she weds herself are many”: the predicate “many” moves from the beginning to the end, after the subject and its relative clause.
- *e più saranno ancora* ‘and more will they be yet’ → “and will yet be more”: the comparative moves after the verb, while “yet” moves before “be”.
- *infin che il Veltro verrà* ‘until the Greyhound will come’ → “until the Greyhound comes”: the Italian future becomes an English present in a future-time subordinate clause.

> 103 Questi non ciberà terra nè peltro,  
> 104 ma sapienza, e amore, e virtute;  

He will not feed on land or pelf, but on wisdom, and love, and manfulness;

- *Questi* ‘This one’ → “He”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *non ciberà* ‘will not eat’ → “will not feed on”: a transitive verb becomes a verb with a prepositional complement.
- *nè peltro* ‘nor pewter’ → “or pelf”: ‘nor’ becomes “or” under the preceding negative; the named metal is rendered figuratively as money or wealth.
- *ma sapienza* ‘but wisdom’ → “but on wisdom”: “on” is repeated to continue the English construction “feed on”.
- *virtute* ‘virtue’ → “manfulness”: the translation selects the sense of courage or manly excellence rather than the broader sense of virtue.

> 105 e sua nazion sarà tra Feltro e Feltro.  

and his nation shall be between Feltro and Feltro.

- *nazion* ‘birth’ → “nation”: the translation takes the word as a collective or territorial designation rather than as birth or natal origin.

> 106 Di quell' umile Italia fia salute,  
> 107 per cui morì la vergine Cammilla,  
> 108 Eurialo, e Turno, e Niso di ferute;  

He shall be the salvation of that low Italy, for which Camilla the virgin, Euryalus, and Turnus, and Nisus, died of wounds;

- *Di quell' umile Italia fia salute* ‘Of that low Italy he will be salvation’ → “He shall be the salvation of that low Italy”: the initial prepositional phrase moves after “salvation”, and a definite article is supplied before that noun.
- *per cui morì la vergine Cammilla, Eurialo, e Turno, e Niso di ferute* ‘for which died the virgin Camilla, Euryalus, and Turnus, and Nisus of wounds’ → “for which Camilla the virgin, Euryalus, and Turnus, and Nisus, died of wounds”: the verb moves after the complete list of subjects, and “the virgin” follows rather than precedes Camilla’s name.

> 109 questi la caccerà per ogni villa,  
> 110 fin che l' avrà rimessa nell' Inferno,  
> 111 là onde invidia prima dipartilla.  

he shall chase her through every city, till he have put her into Hell again; from which envy first set her loose.

- *questi* ‘this one’ → “he”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *fin che l' avrà rimessa nell' Inferno* ‘until he will have put her back into the Hell’ → “till he have put her into Hell again”: the future perfect becomes an English perfect subjunctive after “till”; the restorative prefix of *rimessa* is expressed separately by “again”, and the article before *Inferno* is omitted.
- *là onde* ‘there whence’ → “from which”: the paired adverbs become a relative prepositional phrase referring directly to Hell.
- *dipartilla* ‘parted her’ → “set her loose”: separation or removal is rendered as release.

> 112 Ond' io per lo tuo me' penso e discerno,  
> 113 che tu mi segui, ed io sarò tua guida,  
> 114 e trarrotti di qui per luogo eterno,  

Wherefore I think and discern *this* for thy best, that thou follow me; and I will be thy guide, and lead thee hence through an eternal place,

- *Ond' io per lo tuo me' penso e discerno* ‘Wherefore I for thy better think and discern’ → “Wherefore I think and discern *this* for thy best”: the phrase expressing benefit moves after the verbs; comparative *me’* becomes superlative “best”, and “*this*” is supplied to anticipate the following clause.
- *e trarrotti di qui* ‘and I shall draw thee from here’ → “and lead thee hence”: drawing becomes leading; the future auxiliary is shared with the preceding “will”, and the prepositional phrase becomes the single adverb “hence”.
- *per luogo eterno* ‘through place eternal’ → “through an eternal place”: an indefinite article is supplied, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 115 ove udirai le disperate strida,  
> 116 vedrai gli antichi spiriti dolenti,  
> 117 che la seconda morte ciascun grida;  

where thou shalt hear the hopeless shrieks, shalt see the ancient spirits in pain, so that each calls for a second death;

- *dolenti* ‘suffering’ → “in pain”: an adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *che* ‘who’ → “so that”: the translation reads the connection as consecutive, expressing a result, rather than as a relative clause describing the spirits.
- *la seconda morte ciascun grida* ‘the second death each cries’ → “each calls for a second death”: the fronted object moves after the subject and verb; transitive crying becomes “calls for”, and the definite article becomes indefinite.

> 118 e poi vedrai color, che son contenti  
> 119 nel fuoco, perchè speran di venire,  
> 120 quando che sia, alle beate genti;  

and [then] thou shalt see those who are contented in the fire: for they hope to come, whensoever it be, amongst the blessed;

- *perchè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *alle beate genti* ‘to the blessed peoples’ → “amongst the blessed”: movement “to” becomes movement “amongst”; the noun *genti* is omitted, leaving the adjective to designate the people collectively.

## Lines 121–136 — A Worthier Guide and Dante’s Request

> 121 alle qua' poi se tu vorrai salire,  
> 122 anima fia a ciò di me più degna;  

then to these, if thou desirest to ascend, there shall be a spirit worthier than I to guide thee;

- *alle qua' poi* ‘to whom then’ → “then to these”: the relative pronoun becomes a demonstrative, and “then” moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *se tu vorrai* ‘if thou shalt wish’ → “if thou desirest”: the future becomes a present.
- *anima fia* ‘a soul shall be’ → “there shall be a spirit”: “spirit” replaces ‘soul’, and the copular construction becomes an existential construction with “there”.
- *a ciò* ‘for this’ → “to guide thee”: the general reference is made explicit as an infinitive naming the task.
- *di me più degna* ‘than me more worthy’ → “worthier than I”: the comparative precedes its complement; “worthier” replaces the two-word comparative, and “I” replaces the oblique pronoun.

> 123 con lei ti lascerò nel mio partire:  

with her will I leave thee at my parting:

- *con lei ti lascerò* ‘with her thee I shall leave’ → “with her will I leave thee”: the object pronoun follows the verb, and the supplied subject stands after the auxiliary.
- *nel mio partire* ‘in my departing’ → “at my parting”: “at” replaces ‘in’, and the substantival infinitive is rendered by an English verbal noun.

> 124 chè quello Imperador, che lassù regna,  
> 125 perch' io fui ribellante alla sua legge,  
> 126 non vuol che in sua città per me si vegna.  

for that Emperor who reigns above, because I was rebellious to his law, wills not that I come into his city.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *che lassù regna* ‘who up there reigns’ → “who reigns above”: the spatial expression follows the verb, and “above” replaces ‘up there’.
- *non vuol* ‘not wills’ → “wills not”: the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *che in sua città per me si vegna* ‘that into his city through me one should come’ → “that I come into his city”: the impersonal construction becomes first-person and personal. The translation interprets the restriction as applying to the speaker’s own entry, rather than entry through his agency; the phrase naming the destination moves after the verb.

> 127 In tutte parti impera, e quivi regge,  
> 128 quivi è la sua città, e l' alto seggio:  
> 129 o felice colui, cui ivi elegge!"  

In all parts he rules and there [holds sway]; there is his city, and his high seat: o happy whom he chooses for it!”

- *regge* ‘governs’ → “[holds sway]”: a single verb becomes a verbal phrase.
- *e l' alto seggio* ‘and the high seat’ → “and his high seat”: the definite article is replaced by a supplied possessive.
- *colui, cui* ‘that one whom’ → “whom”: the demonstrative antecedent is omitted, leaving “whom” to carry both functions.
- *ivi elegge* ‘there he chooses’ → “he chooses for it”: the locative adverb is interpreted as the destination of the choice and rendered by a prepositional phrase after the verb.

> 130 Ed io a lui: "Poeta, io ti richieggio  
> 131 per quello Dio, che tu non conoscesti,  

And I to him: “Poet, I beseech thee by that God whom thou knowest not:

- *io ti richieggio* ‘I thee beseech’ → “I beseech thee”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *che tu non conoscesti* ‘whom thou didst not know’ → “whom thou knowest not”: the past becomes a present, and the negative follows the verb.

> 132 acciocch' io fugga questo male e peggio,  

in order that I may escape this ill and worse,

- *acciocch' io fugga* ‘so that I flee’ → “in order that I may escape”: the purpose conjunction is expanded, the subjunctive is expressed with “may”, and ‘flee’ is rendered as “escape”.

> 133 che tu mi meni là dov' or dicesti,  
> 134 sì ch' io vegga la porta di San Pietro,  
> 135 e color cui tu fai cotanto mesti."  

lead me where thou now hast said, so that I may see the Gate of St Peter, and those whom thou makest so sad.”

- *che tu mi meni* ‘that thou me lead’ → “lead me”: the subordinate request becomes a direct imperative, with the conjunction and explicit subject omitted; the object follows the verb.
- *là dov' or dicesti* ‘there where now thou saidst’ → “where thou now hast said”: ‘there’ is omitted, and the simple past becomes a present perfect.
- *sì ch' io vegga* ‘so that I see’ → “so that I may see”: the Italian subjunctive is expressed with “may”.

> 136 Allor si mosse; ed io li tenni retro.  

Then he moved; and I kept on behind him.

- *si mosse* ‘he moved himself’ → “he moved”: the pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive without a reflexive pronoun.
- *ed io li tenni retro* ‘and I to him kept behind’ → “and I kept on behind him”: the dative pronoun becomes the complement of “behind”, following it; “on” is supplied to express continuation.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Reshaping

The translation stays close to the Italian in its sequence of clauses, repeated conjunctions, and many distinctive expressions, including “the lake of my heart” and “where the Sun is silent”. It also preserves some marked word orders, such as “A poet I was”. More often, however, it rearranges the Italian into English syntax: subjects precede their verbs, objects follow them, adjectives move before nouns, and participles stand beside their auxiliaries. Thus *di pel maculato era coperta* becomes “was covered with spotted [hair]”, while *Molti son gli animali* becomes “The animals” followed, after the relative clause, by “are many”.

Changes of construction frequently accompany this reordering. Reflexive verbs such as *si volse* become intransitive English verbs without a reflexive pronoun; personal datives become possessives, as *m’* does in “my heart”. Impersonal *A te convien* becomes “Thou must”, and *ha più fame*, ‘has more hunger’, becomes “is hungrier”. Nouns, adjectives, and verbs also exchange roles: *con tanta pieta* becomes “so piteously”, *dell’ altezza* becomes “of ascending”, and *viver grame* becomes “live in sorrow”. Italian subjunctives are variously represented by English indicatives, infinitives, or constructions with “may”; compound past tenses sometimes become simple pasts, while futures in temporal or conditional clauses become presents.

Vocabulary ranges from close equivalents to interpretive choices. *Forte* becomes “stubborn”, *studio* becomes “zeal”, and *virtute* becomes “manfulness”, each selecting a particular sense rather than reproducing the most immediate English equivalent. Some departures alter the relation expressed by a whole clause: *che non lasciò giammai persona viva*, ‘which never left a person alive’, becomes “that no one ever left alive”, reversing the grammatical roles of the pass and the person. Likewise, *per me si vegna*, ‘one should come through me’, becomes “I come”, replacing entry through someone’s agency with that person’s own entry.

Small omissions and additions help produce the English prose. Locative *v’* is left unrepresented in “that I discerned”; repeated prepositions and some explicit nouns disappear, as *genti* does in “the blessed”. Conversely, *a ciò*, ‘for this’, is expanded into “to guide thee”, and “deep” makes explicit a connotation of *pelago*. Oelsner’s bracketed revisions can also introduce interpretation: “[right]” identifies the foot where *fermo* describes it as planted or stationary. Throughout, two routine adjustments largely left unstated in the comparison further distinguish the English from a word-for-word rendering: subject pronouns are supplied where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and articles before possessives are omitted, as in *lo mio maestro* becoming “my master”. The result retains much of the Italian’s phrasing and rhetorical shape while repeatedly reshaping its grammar and occasionally narrowing or redirecting its meaning.
