# Inferno 22

## Lines 1–18 — Marching With the Demons

> 1 Io vidi già cavalier muover campo,  
> 2 e cominciare stormo, e far lor mostra,  
> 3 e talvolta partir per loro scampo;  

I have ere now seen horsemen moving camp, and commencing the assault, and holding their muster, and at times retiring to escape;

- *Io vidi già* ‘I saw already’ → “I have ere now seen”: the simple past becomes the present perfect; *già* is expanded into “ere now” and placed before the participle.
- *muover campo* ‘to move camp’ → “moving camp”: the infinitive dependent on the verb of seeing becomes a present participle.
- *e cominciare stormo* ‘and to begin battle’ → “and commencing the assault”: the infinitive becomes a participle; “assault” gives a more specific rendering of *stormo*, and the definite article is supplied.
- *e far lor mostra* ‘and to make their display’ → “and holding their muster”: the infinitive becomes a participle, and the military display is expressed idiomatically as holding a muster.
- *e talvolta partir* ‘and sometimes to depart’ → “and at times retiring”: the infinitive becomes a participle; “retiring” gives *partir* a specifically military sense, while *talvolta* becomes the phrase “at times”.
- *per loro scampo* ‘for their escape’ → “to escape”: the prepositional phrase containing a noun and possessive becomes an infinitive of purpose.

> 4 corridor vidi per la terra vostra,  
> 5 o Aretini, e vidi gir gualdane,  
> 6 ferir torneamenti, e correr giostra,  

coursers have I seen upon your land, O Aretines! and seen the march of foragers, the shock of tournaments and race of jousts,

- *corridor vidi* ‘raiding horsemen I saw’ → “coursers have I seen”: “coursers” does not explicitly convey the military role of the riders designated by *corridor*. The simple past becomes the present perfect, with the subject following the auxiliary.
- *per la terra vostra* ‘through the land your’ → “upon your land”: movement through territory becomes location upon it, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *e vidi gir gualdane* ‘and I saw raiding parties go’ → “and seen the march of foragers”: the repeated finite verb becomes a participle sharing the earlier auxiliary “have”. The infinitive *gir* becomes the noun “march”, and its subject, *gualdane*, becomes an “of” phrase; raiding parties are described as “foragers”.
- *ferir torneamenti* ‘to strike tournaments’ → “the shock of tournaments”: the infinitive becomes a noun with a supplied article, and its direct object becomes an “of” phrase.
- *e correr giostra* ‘and to run a joust’ → “and race of jousts”: the infinitive becomes the noun “race”, its object becomes an “of” phrase, and singular *giostra* becomes plural “jousts”.

> 7 quando con trombe, e quando con campane,  
> 8 con tamburi e con cenni di castella,  
> 9 e con cose nostrali e con istrane:  

now with trumpets, and now with bells, with drums and castle-signals, and with native things and foreign:

- *quando con trombe, e quando con campane* ‘when with trumpets, and when with bells’ → “now with trumpets, and now with bells”: the paired *quando* clauses are rendered by the English expression of alternation “now … now”.
- *e con cenni di castella* ‘and with signals of castles’ → “and castle-signals”: the repeated preposition is omitted, and the ‘of’ construction becomes a compound noun, with plural *castella* represented by singular attributive “castle”.
- *e con cose nostrali e con istrane* ‘and with things native and with foreign ones’ → “and with native things and foreign”: “native” moves before “things”, and the second *con* is omitted.

> 10 nè già con sì diversa cennamella  
> 11 cavalier vidi muover, nè pedoni,  
> 12 nè nave a segno di terra o di stella.  

but never yet to so uncouth a cornet saw I cavaliers nor footmen move, nor ship by mark of land or star.

- *nè già* ‘nor indeed’ → “but never yet”: negative coordination becomes an explicit contrast, and the emphatic expression receives a temporal formulation.
- *con sì diversa cennamella* ‘with so different a shawm’ → “to so uncouth a cornet”: ‘with’ becomes “to”, expressing movement in response to a signal; *diversa* is interpreted as “uncouth”, rather than merely different or unusual, and the named instrument changes from a shawm to a cornet.
- *cavalier vidi muover, nè pedoni* ‘cavaliers I saw move, nor footmen’ → “saw I cavaliers nor footmen move”: the first object moves after the verb of seeing, while “move” follows both coordinated objects. The English also uses verb–subject inversion after the opening negative expression.
- *a segno di terra o di stella* ‘at a signal of land or of star’ → “by mark of land or star”: the phrase is construed as guidance by a navigational mark; *a* becomes “by”, and the second *di* is omitted.

> 13 Noi andavam con li dieci dimoni;  

We went with the ten Demons:

- *Noi andavam* ‘We were going’ → “We went”: the imperfect, presenting an ongoing action, becomes the English simple past.

> 14 ahi fiera compagnia! ma nella chiesa  
> 15 coi santi ed in taverna coi ghiottoni.  

ah, hideous company! but, ‘In church with saints, and with guzzlers in the tavern.’

- *fiera compagnia* ‘savage company’ → “hideous company”: the adjective shifts from savagery or fierceness to repulsiveness.
- *nella chiesa* ‘in the church’ → “In church”: the definite article is omitted in the English institutional expression.
- *coi santi* ‘with the saints’ → “with saints”: the definite article is omitted before the generic plural.
- *ed in taverna coi ghiottoni* ‘and in tavern with the gluttons’ → “and with guzzlers in the tavern”: the two prepositional phrases exchange positions; an article is supplied before “tavern” and omitted before “guzzlers”. “Guzzlers” emphasizes excessive drinking rather than gluttony more generally.

> 16 Pure alla pegola era la mia intesa,  
> 17 per veder della bolgia ogni contegno,  
> 18 e della gente ch’ entro v’ era incesa.  

Yet my intent was on the pitch, to see each habit of the chasm and of the people that were burning in it.

- *alla pegola era la mia intesa* ‘to the pitch was my attention’ → “my intent was on the pitch”: the subject moves before the verb and the prepositional phrase moves after it; *a* becomes “on”, and attentive concentration is expressed by “intent”.
- *della bolgia ogni contegno* ‘of the ditch every aspect’ → “each habit of the chasm”: the dependent “of” phrase moves after the noun it qualifies. The more specific *bolgia* becomes “chasm”, and *contegno*, denoting aspect or condition, is rendered as “habit”.
- *e della gente ch’ entro v’ era incesa* ‘and of the people that within there was kindled’ → “and of the people that were burning in it”: singular agreement with collective *gente* becomes plural agreement with “people”. The construction with the past participle *incesa* becomes the progressive “were burning”; *entro v’* is consolidated into “in it” and moved after the predicate.

## Lines 19–51 — Sinners Surface and One Is Seized

> 19 Come i delfini, quando fanno segno  
> 20 ai marinar con l' arco della schiena,  
> 21 che s' argomentin di campar lor legno:  

As dolphins, when with the arch of the back they make sign to mariners that they may prepare to save their ship:

- *i delfini* ‘the dolphins’ → “dolphins”: the generic definite article is omitted.
- *fanno segno … con l' arco della schiena* ‘they make sign … with the arch of the back’ → “with the arch of the back they make sign”: the prepositional phrase moves before the verb and its indirect object.
- *ai marinar* ‘to the mariners’ → “to mariners”: the definite article is omitted.
- *che s' argomentin* ‘that they exert themselves’ → “that they may prepare”: the pronominal verb becomes “prepare”, and the subjunctive is represented by “may”.
- *legno* ‘wood’ → “ship”: the translation gives the object designated by the Italian metonymy.

> 22 talor così ad alleggiar la pena  
> 23 mostrava alcun dei peccatori il dosso,  
> 24 e nascondeva in men che non balena.  

so now and then, to ease the punishment, some sinner showed his back and hid in less time than it lightens.

- *talor così* ‘sometimes thus’ → “so now and then”: the two adverbs exchange places, and “now and then” replaces the single-word adverb.
- *mostrava alcun dei peccatori il dosso* ‘showed one of the sinners the back’ → “some sinner showed his back”: the subject moves before the verb; the partitive construction becomes “some sinner”; and the definite article before the body part becomes a possessive.
- *in men che non balena* ‘in less than it flashes’ → “in less time than it lightens”: “time” is supplied after “less”, and the redundant comparative *non* is not represented.

> 25 E come all' orlo dell' acqua d' un fosso  
> 26 stanno i ranocchi pur col muso fuori,  
> 27 sì che celano i piedi e l' altro grosso :  

And as at the edge of the water of a ditch, the frogs stand only with their muzzles out, so that they hide their feet and other bulk:

- *stanno i ranocchi* ‘stand the frogs’ → “the frogs stand”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *col muso* ‘with the muzzle’ → “with their muzzles”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the distributive singular becomes plural.
- *i piedi e l' altro grosso* ‘the feet and the other bulk’ → “their feet and other bulk”: the two definite articles are replaced by a possessive governing the coordinated phrase.

> 28 sì stavan d' ogni parte i peccatori;  
> 29 ma come s' appressava Barbariccia,  
> 30 così si ritraean sotto i bollori.  

thus stood on every hand the sinners; but as Barbariccia approached, they instantly retired beneath the seething.

- *d' ogni parte* ‘on every side’ → “on every hand”: one spatial idiom replaces another.
- *come s' appressava Barbariccia* ‘as drew near Barbariccia’ → “as Barbariccia approached”: the subject moves before the verb, and the Italian pronominal verb becomes the simple intransitive “approached”.
- *così si ritraean* ‘so they drew themselves back’ → “they instantly retired”: “instantly” expresses the immediate response conveyed by the paired *come … così* construction; the reflexive verb becomes an intransitive verb.
- *sotto i bollori* ‘under the boilings’ → “beneath the seething”: the plural noun becomes a singular verbal noun.

> 31 Io vidi, ed anco il cor me n' accapriccia,  
> 32 uno aspettar così, com' egli incontra  
> 33 che una rana rimane ed altra spiccia;  

I saw, and my heart still shudders thereat, one linger so, as it will happen that one frog remains while the other spouts away;

- *ed anco il cor me n' accapriccia* ‘and still the heart shudders to me at it’ → “and my heart still shudders thereat”: the possessive dative becomes “my”; “still” moves after the subject; and the preverbal *ne* becomes the following adverb “thereat”.
- *aspettar* ‘wait’ → “linger”: the translation substitutes a verb emphasizing delayed departure.
- *com' egli incontra* ‘as it happens’ → “as it will happen”: the simple present becomes “will happen”, with “will” expressing characteristic occurrence.
- *ed altra spiccia* ‘and another darts away’ → “while the other spouts away”: coordination becomes a clause introduced by “while”; ‘another’ becomes the definite “the other”; and “spouts away” gives the verb a liquid-motion colouring rather than simply expressing a sudden departure.

> 34 e Graffiacan, che gli era più d' incontra,  
> 35 gli arroncigliò le impegolate chiome,  
> 36 e trassel su, che mi parve una lontra.  

and Graffiacane, who was nearest to him, hooked his pitchy locks and haled him up, so that to me he seemed an otter.

- *che gli era più d' incontra* ‘who was more directly opposite him’ → “who was nearest to him”: relative position opposite someone becomes proximity, and the comparative expression is rendered as a superlative.
- *gli arroncigliò le impegolate chiome* ‘to him hooked the pitch-covered locks’ → “hooked his pitchy locks”: the possessive dative and definite article become “his”; the participial adjective becomes “pitchy”.
- *trassel su* ‘drew him up’ → “haled him up”: “haled” substitutes a more forceful verb of dragging or hauling.
- *che mi parve una lontra* ‘that he seemed to me an otter’ → “so that to me he seemed an otter”: “so” makes the result construction explicit, and the experiencer phrase is placed before the subject and verb.

> 37 Io sapea già di tutti e quanti il nome,  
> 38 sì li notai quando furono eletti,  
> 39 e poi che si chiamaro attesi come.  

I already knew the name of every one, so well I noted them as they were chosen, and when they called each other, listened how.

- *Io sapea già* ‘I knew already’ → “I already knew”: the adverb moves before the verb.
- *di tutti e quanti il nome* ‘of all and every one the name’ → “the name of every one”: the noun moves before its dependent phrase, and the emphatic double expression of totality is reduced to “every one”.
- *sì li notai* ‘so I noted them’ → “so well I noted them”: “well” is supplied to specify the degree of attentive observation.
- *quando* ‘when’ → “as”: a different temporal conjunction is used.
- *e poi che* ‘and then, when’ → “and when”: the explicit indication of subsequent time in *poi* is omitted.
- *si chiamaro* ‘one another they called’ → “they called each other”: the reciprocal clitic is expanded into a phrase following the verb.
- *attesi come* ‘paid attention how’ → “listened how”: the general verb of attending becomes specifically auditory.

> 40 " O Rubicante, fa che tu gli metti  
> 41 gli unghioni addosso sì che tu lo scuoi,"  
> 42 gridavan tutti insieme i maledetti.  

“O Rubicante, see thou plant thy clutches on him, and flay him!” shouted together all the accursed crew.

- *fa che tu* ‘make [it] that you’ → “see thou”: the Italian injunction becomes an English construction with “see”, without an expressed conjunction.
- *gli metti gli unghioni addosso* ‘to him put the great claws upon [him]’ → “plant thy clutches on him”: ‘put’ becomes the stronger “plant”; the augmentative noun becomes “clutches”, without an explicit indication of size; the article becomes “thy”; and the dative pronoun and *addosso* are combined in the following phrase “on him”.
- *sì che tu lo scuoi* ‘so that you flay him’ → “and flay him”: the subordinate result clause becomes a coordinated command.
- *gridavan tutti insieme i maledetti* ‘shouted all together the accursed ones’ → “shouted together all the accursed crew”: “all” is moved from its association with “together” to the subject phrase; the substantivized plural adjective is expanded into an adjective modifying the collective noun “crew”.

> 43 Ed io: " Maestro mio, fa, se tu puoi,  
> 44 che tu sappi chi è lo sciagurato  
> 45 venuto a man degli avversari suoi."  

And I: “Master, learn if thou canst, who is that piteous wight, fallen into the hand of his adversaries.”

- *Maestro mio* ‘Master mine’ → “Master”: the possessive is omitted.
- *fa … che tu sappi* ‘make [it] … that you may know’ → “learn”: the periphrastic injunction is compressed into one imperative, and knowing becomes acquiring knowledge. The intervening conditional clause consequently follows the complete command rather than interrupting its construction.
- *lo sciagurato* ‘the unfortunate one’ → “that piteous wight”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative, and the substantivized adjective becomes an adjective and noun.
- *venuto a man* ‘come to hand’ → “fallen into the hand”: ‘come’ becomes “fallen”, and the Italian idiom is recast with “into” and a definite article.
- *degli avversari suoi* ‘of the adversaries his’ → “of his adversaries”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 46 Lo duca mio gli s' accostò allato,  
> 47 domandollo ond' ei fosse, e quei rispose:  
> 48 " Io fui del regno di Navarra nato.  

My Guide drew close to [his side] and asked him whence he came; and he replied: “I was born in the kingdom of Navarre.

- *Lo duca mio* ‘the leader my’ → “My Guide”: ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *gli s' accostò allato* ‘to him drew himself near at the side’ → “drew close to [his side]”: the pronominal verb becomes intransitive; the dative becomes the possessive “his”; and the adverbial *allato* is expanded into a prepositional phrase containing “side”.
- *domandollo* ‘asked him’ → “and asked him”: a coordinating conjunction is supplied.
- *ond' ei fosse* ‘whence he was’ → “whence he came”: being from somewhere becomes coming from somewhere, and the Italian subjunctive of the indirect question becomes an English indicative.
- *e quei* ‘and that one’ → “and he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *Io fui del regno di Navarra nato* ‘I was of the kingdom of Navarre born’ → “I was born in the kingdom of Navarre”: the participle moves next to the auxiliary, and the preposition expressing origin becomes the locative “in”.

> 49 Mia madre a servo d' un signor mi pose,  
> 50 chè m' avea generato d' un ribaldo  
> 51 distruggitor di sè e di sue cose.  

My mother placed me as servant of a lord; for she had borne me to a ribald waster of himself and of his substance.

- *a servo d' un signor mi pose* ‘as servant of a lord me placed’ → “placed me as servant of a lord”: the verb comes first, followed by its object and then the role assigned to that object.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *m' avea generato* ‘me had begotten’ → “she had borne me”: ‘begotten’ becomes the maternally specific “borne”, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *d' un ribaldo distruggitor* ‘by a scoundrel, destroyer’ → “to a ribald waster”: paternal origin expressed by *di* becomes “to”; “ribald” functions adjectivally before “waster”, rather than as a noun followed by an appositional description; and ‘destroyer’ becomes “waster”.
- *di sue cose* ‘of his things’ → “of his substance”: the plural general noun becomes a singular collective noun meaning possessions or wealth.

## Lines 52–84 — The Barrator Questioned and Tormented

> 52 Poi fui famiglio del buon re Tebaldo;  
> 53 quivi mi misi a far baratteria,  
> 54 di che io rendo ragione in questo caldo.”  

Then I was domestic with the good king Thibault; here I set myself to doing barratry, of which I render reckoning in this heat."

- *del buon re* ‘of the good king’ → “with the good king”: the relationship expressed by ‘of’ becomes one expressed by “with”.
- *quivi* ‘there’ → “here”: the locative changes from ‘there’ to “here”.
- *mi misi a far* ‘myself I put to do’ → “I set myself to doing”: the reflexive follows the verb, and the infinitive becomes a gerund after “to”.

> 55 E Ciriatto, a cui di bocca uscia  
> 56 d’ ogni parte una sanna come a porco,  
> 57 gli fe’ sentir come l’ una sdrucia.  

And Ciriatto, from whose mouth on either side came forth a tusk as from a hog, made him feel how one of them did rip.

- *a cui di bocca* ‘to whom out of the mouth’ → “from whose mouth”: the possessive dative becomes a possessive relative construction.
- *uscia d’ ogni parte* ‘came out on every side’ → “on either side came forth”: the expression of place precedes the verb, and ‘every’ becomes “either”.
- *come a porco* ‘as to a hog’ → “as from a hog”: the elliptical Italian construction expresses possession with a dative; English instead repeats the relationship of source.
- *gli fe’ sentir* ‘to him made feel’ → “made him feel”: the dative pronoun becomes the object of “made” and follows it.
- *l’ una sdrucia* ‘the one rips’ → “one of them did rip”: the definite expression becomes an explicit partitive, and the present tense becomes a past tense formed with “did”.

> 58 Tra male gatte era venuto il sorco;  

Amongst evil cats the mouse had come;

- *male gatte* ‘evil she-cats’ → “evil cats”: the explicitly feminine noun becomes a noun without an expressed sex.
- *era venuto il sorco* ‘had come the mouse’ → “the mouse had come”: the subject moves before the verb.

> 59 ma Barbariccia il chiuse con le braccia,  
> 60 e disse: “State in là, mentr’ io lo inforco.”  

but Barbariccia locked him in his arms, and said: "Stand off whilst I enfork him!"

- *il chiuse con le braccia* ‘him enclosed with the arms’ → “locked him in his arms”: the object follows the verb; ‘enclosed’ becomes “locked”; and ‘with the arms’ becomes “in his arms”, with possession made explicit.
- *State in là* ‘stay over there’ → “Stand off”: the command to remain at a distance becomes an English idiomatic command.
- *io lo inforco* ‘I him enfork’ → “I enfork him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 61 Ed al maestro mio volse la faccia:  
> 62 “Domanda,” disse, “ancor se più desii  
> 63 saper da lui, prima ch’ altri il disfaccia.”  

And turning his face to my Master: "Ask on," he said, "if thou wouldst learn more from him, before some other undo him."

- *Ed al maestro mio volse la faccia* ‘And to the master mine he turned the face’ → “And turning his face to my Master”: the finite verb becomes a participle attached to the following speech attribution; the destination follows the verb and its object; the possessive precedes “Master”; and ‘the face’ becomes “his face”.
- *Domanda … ancor* ‘Ask further’ → “Ask on”: the adverb becomes the continuative particle “on” and moves before the intervening speech attribution.
- *se più desii saper da lui* ‘if more thou desirest to know from him’ → “if thou wouldst learn more from him”: the verb of desire becomes “wouldst”, ‘know’ becomes “learn”, and “more” moves after that verb.
- *altri il disfaccia* ‘another him undo’ → “some other undo him”: the indefinite pronoun becomes “some other”, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 64 Lo duca dunque: “Or di’, degli altri rii  
> 65 conosci tu alcun che sia Latino  
> 66 sotto la pece?” E quegli: “Io mi partii  
> 67 poco è da un, che fu di là vicino;  

The Guide therefore: "Now say, of the other sinners knowest thou any that is a Latian, beneath the pitch?" And he: "I parted just now from one who was a neighbour of theirs [on the other side];

- *rii* ‘wicked ones’ → “sinners”: a substantivized adjective becomes a noun.
- *che sia Latino* ‘who be Latin’ → “that is a Latian”: the subjunctive becomes indicative, and ‘Latin’ is rendered as “a Latian”, with an indefinite article.
- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *mi partii* ‘myself I parted’ → “I parted”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes a non-reflexive English verb.
- *poco è* ‘a little time it is’ → “just now”: a finite expression of elapsed time becomes an adverbial phrase.
- *che fu di là vicino* ‘who was over there a neighbour’ → “who was a neighbour of theirs [on the other side]”: “of theirs” supplies a relationship not explicitly stated in the Italian; the locative moves after “neighbour” and is expanded in the bracketed wording.

> 68 così foss’ io ancor con lui coperto,  
> 69 ch’ io non temerei unghia nè uncino.”  

would I still were covered with him, for I should not fear claw nor hook!"

- *così foss’ io ancor con lui coperto* ‘so were I still with him covered’ → “would I still were covered with him”: the optative construction becomes one introduced by “would”; the subject precedes “were”, and “covered” precedes “with him”.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.

> 70 E Libicocco: “Troppo avem sofferto,”  
> 71 disse, e presegli il braccio col ronciglio,  
> 72 sì che, stracciando, ne portò un lacerto.  

And Libicocco cried: "Too much have we endured!" and with the hook seized his arm, and mangling carried off a part of brawn.

- *disse* ‘said’ → “cried”: the reporting verb becomes more forceful and moves before the direct speech.
- *e presegli il braccio col ronciglio* ‘and seized for him the arm with the hook’ → “and with the hook seized his arm”: the instrumental phrase moves before the verb, and the possessive dative becomes “his”.
- *sì che* ‘so that’ → “and”: the explicit relationship of consequence becomes simple coordination.
- *ne portò un lacerto* ‘of it carried a piece of flesh’ → “carried off a part of brawn”: the pronominal reference to the source is not separately expressed; “off” makes removal explicit; and the noun for a piece of flesh is rendered by “a part of brawn”.

> 73 Draghignazzo anco i volle dar di piglio  
> 74 giuso alle gambe; onde il decurio loro  
> 75 si volse intorno intorno con mal piglio.  

Draghignazzo, he too, wished to have a catch at the legs below; whereat their Decurion wheeled around around with evil aspect.

- *Draghignazzo anco* ‘Draghignazzo also’ → “Draghignazzo, he too”: English adds a pronoun repeating the named subject.
- *i volle dar di piglio giuso alle gambe* ‘to him wished to give a grip down at the legs’ → “wished to have a catch at the legs below”: the dative pronoun is omitted; the idiom built on ‘give’ becomes “have a catch”; and the downward specification moves after “legs”.
- *onde* ‘whence’ → “whereat”: the connective presents the following action as a response to the preceding action rather than literally as proceeding from it.
- *il decurio loro* ‘the decurion their’ → “their Decurion”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *si volse intorno intorno* ‘himself turned around around’ → “wheeled around around”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, with “wheeled” replacing ‘turned’.

> 76 Quand’ elli un poco rappaciati foro,  
> 77 a lui che ancor mirava sua ferita,  
> 78 domandò il duca mio senza dimoro:  

When they were somewhat pacified, my Guide without delay asked him that still kept gazing on his wound:

- *Quand’ elli un poco rappaciati foro* ‘When they a little pacified were’ → “When they were somewhat pacified”: the auxiliary moves before the participle and its modifier, while ‘a little’ becomes “somewhat”.
- *a lui* ‘to him’ → “him”: the indirect object becomes the direct object of “asked” and moves after that verb.
- *che ancor mirava sua ferita* ‘who still was looking at his wound’ → “that still kept gazing on his wound”: the imperfect becomes “kept gazing”, making continued attention explicit.
- *domandò il duca mio senza dimoro* ‘asked the guide mine without delay’ → “my Guide without delay asked”: the subject moves before the verb, the possessive precedes its noun, and the adverbial phrase moves before “asked”.

> 79 “Chi fu colui, da cui mala partita  
> 80 di’ che facesti per venire a proda?”  

"Who was he, from whom thou sayest that thou madest an ill departure to come ashore?"

- *Chi fu colui* ‘Who was that one’ → “Who was he”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *da cui mala partita di’ che facesti* ‘from whom an ill departure thou sayest that thou madest’ → “from whom thou sayest that thou madest an ill departure”: the object ‘ill departure’, placed before ‘thou sayest’ in Italian, moves after “madest”; English also supplies its indefinite article.
- *a proda* ‘to the shore’ → “ashore”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.

> 81 Ed ei rispose: “Fu frate Gomita,  
> 82 quel di Gallura, vasel d’ ogni froda,  
> 83 ch’ ebbe i nimici di suo donno in mano,  
> 84 e fe’ sì lor, che ciascun se ne loda:  

And he answered: "It was Friar Gomita, he of Gallura, vessel of every fraud, who had his master's enemies in hand, and did so to them that they all praise him for it:

- *quel di Gallura* ‘that one of Gallura’ → “he of Gallura”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *i nimici di suo donno* ‘the enemies of his lord’ → “his master's enemies”: the possessive relationship becomes an English possessive placed before “enemies”, and ‘lord’ is rendered as “master”.
- *che ciascun se ne loda* ‘that each one praises himself for it’ → “that they all praise him for it”: the singular distributive subject becomes plural “they all”; the reflexive expression, idiomatically conveying satisfaction, becomes praise directed at “him”, an object not explicitly present in the Italian.

## Lines 85–117 — A Bargain and a Challenge

> 85 denar si tolse, e lasciolli di piano,  
> 86 sì com’ei dice; e negli altri uffici anche  
> 87 barattier fu non picciol, ma soprano.  

money took he for himself, and dismissed them smoothly, as he says; and in his other offices besides, he was no petty but a sovereign barrator.

- *si tolse* ‘for himself took’ → “took he for himself”: the reflexive phrase follows the verb, and the supplied subject stands between them.
- *lasciolli* ‘left them’ → “dismissed them”: leaving or releasing is expressed more specifically as dismissal.
- *di piano* ‘readily, without difficulty’ → “smoothly”: the adverbial phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *sì com’ei dice* ‘just as he says’ → “as he says”: the reinforcing *sì* is omitted.
- *negli altri uffici anche* ‘in the other offices also’ → “in his other offices besides”: “his” replaces the definite article.
- *barattier fu non picciol, ma soprano* ‘a barrator he was, not small, but supreme’ → “he was no petty but a sovereign barrator”: the predicate noun moves after its adjectives; “no” replaces ‘not’; and “petty” and “sovereign” express figuratively the degrees conveyed by *picciol* and *soprano*.

> 88 Usa con esso donno Michel Zanche  
> 89 di Logodoro; ed a dir di Sardigna  
> 90 le lingue lor non si sentono stanche.  

With him keeps company Don Michel Zanche of Logodoro; and in speaking of Sardinia the tongues of them do not feel weary.

- *Usa con esso* ‘associates with him’ → “With him keeps company”: the prepositional phrase moves before the verb, and a verbal phrase replaces *usa*.
- *a dir di Sardigna* ‘at speaking of Sardinia’ → “in speaking of Sardinia”: “in” replaces *a*, and the Italian infinitive becomes an English gerund.
- *le lingue lor* ‘the tongues their’ → “the tongues of them”: the postposed possessive becomes an “of” phrase.
- *non si sentono stanche* ‘do not feel themselves weary’ → “do not feel weary”: the reflexive pronoun is omitted.

> 91 O me! vedete l’altro che digrigna;  
> 92 io direi anco; ma io temo ch’ello  
> 93 non s’apparecchi a grattarmi la tigna.”  

Oh me! see that other grinning; I would say more; but fear he is preparing to claw my scurf.”

- *l’altro* ‘the other’ → “that other”: a demonstrative replaces the definite article.
- *che digrigna* ‘who bares his teeth’ → “grinning”: the finite relative clause becomes a participle, and the action is expressed as grinning.
- *anco* ‘still, also’ → “more”: continued speech is expressed as an additional quantity of speech.
- *ma io temo* ‘but I fear’ → “but fear”: the explicitly stated subject is omitted.
- *ch’ello non s’apparecchi* ‘that he may not prepare himself’ → “he is preparing”: the conjunction and reflexive pronoun disappear, and the subjunctive becomes a progressive indicative. The *non*, expletive after a verb of fearing, is not translated as a negative.
- *a grattarmi la tigna* ‘to scratch the scurf for me’ → “to claw my scurf”: “claw” replaces ‘scratch,’ and the personal dative becomes a possessive modifying the noun.

> 94 E il gran proposto, volto a Farfarello  
> 95 che stralunava gli occhi per ferire,  
> 96 disse: “Fatti in costà, malvagio uccello.”  

And their great Marshal, turning to Farfarello, who rolled his eyes to strike, said: “Off with thee, villanous bird!”

- *il gran proposto* ‘the great chief’ → “their great Marshal”: “their” replaces the definite article, and “Marshal” gives a more specific title to *proposto*.
- *volto a Farfarello* ‘turned toward Farfarello’ → “turning to Farfarello”: the past participle becomes a present participle, presenting the turn as an accompanying action rather than a completed one.
- *gli occhi* ‘the eyes’ → “his eyes”: English makes the possessor explicit.
- *Fatti in costà* ‘put thyself over there’ → “Off with thee”: the reflexive imperative becomes an idiomatic verbless command, emphasizing departure rather than the indicated destination.

> 97 “Se voi volete vedere o udire,”  
> 98 ricominciò lo spaurato appresso,  
> 99 “Toschi o Lombardi, io ne farò venire.  

"If you wish to see or hear Tuscans or Lombards,” the frightened sinner then resumed, “I will make them come.

- *ricominciò lo spaurato appresso* ‘resumed the frightened one afterward’ → “the frightened sinner then resumed”: the subject moves before the verb; the substantivized adjective receives the supplied noun “sinner”; and the temporal adverb moves before the verb.
- *ne farò venire* ‘of them I shall make come’ → “will make them come”: the partitive pronoun becomes the direct object “them,” placed between the causative verb and the infinitive.

> 100 Ma stien le male branche un poco in cesso,  
> 101 sì ch’ei non teman delle lor vendette;  

But let the [evil claws hold back] a little, that they may not fear their vengeance;

- *stien le male branche un poco in cesso* ‘let the evil claws stand a little aside’ → “let the [evil claws hold back] a little”: the Italian subjunctive command becomes a construction with “let”; the subject precedes “hold”; “hold back” replaces standing aside; and “a little” follows the complete verbal phrase.
- *sì ch’ei* ‘so that they’ → “that they”: *sì* is not separately represented.
- *delle lor vendette* ‘of their revenges’ → “their vengeance”: the preposition required by Italian *temere di* disappears with English transitive “fear,” and the plural becomes singular.

> 102 ed io, sedendo in questo loco stesso,  
> 103 per un ch’io son, ne farò venir sette,  
> 104 quand’ io sufolerò, com’è nostr’ uso  
> 105 di fare allor che fuori alcun si mette.”  

and I, sitting in this same place, for one that I am, will make seven come, on whistling as is our wont to do when any of us gets out.”

- *in questo loco stesso* ‘in this place itself’ → “in this same place”: the postposed intensifier becomes “same,” placed before the noun.
- *ne farò venir sette* ‘of them I shall make come seven’ → “will make seven come”: the partitive pronoun is omitted, and “seven” moves before the infinitive.
- *quand’ io sufolerò* ‘when I shall whistle’ → “on whistling”: the finite future clause becomes a prepositional phrase with a gerund, without an expressed subject.
- *allor che* ‘then when’ → “when”: the temporal expression is compressed into a single conjunction.
- *fuori alcun si mette* ‘outside someone puts himself’ → “any of us gets out”: “of us” is supplied; the reflexive verb becomes intransitive “gets”; and the initial directional adverb moves to the end.

> 106 Cagnazzo a cotal motto levò il muso,  
> 107 crollando il capo, e disse: “Odi malizia  
> 108 ch’ egli ha pensata per gittarsi giuso.”  

Cagnazzo at these words raised his snout, shaking his head, and said: “Hear the malice he has contrived, to throw himself down!”

- *a cotal motto* ‘at such a saying’ → “at these words”: “these” replaces ‘such,’ and the singular noun becomes plural.
- *il muso* ‘the snout’ → “his snout”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *il capo* ‘the head’ → “his head”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *malizia* ‘malice’ → “the malice”: the definite article is supplied.
- *ch’egli ha pensata* ‘that he has thought out’ → “he has contrived”: the relative pronoun is omitted, and “contrived” renders thinking something out as devising it.

> 109 Ond’ei, ch’avea lacciuoli a gran divizia,  
> 110 rispose: “Malizioso son io troppo,  
> 111 quand’io procuro a’ miei maggior tristizia.”  

Whereat he, who had artifices in great store, replied: “Too malicious indeed! when I contrive for my companions greater sorrow.”

- *Ond’ei* ‘whence he’ → “Whereat he”: the connective is expressed as a response to the preceding words rather than literally as something proceeding from them.
- *lacciuoli* ‘little snares’ → “artifices”: the concrete metaphor becomes an abstract term, and the diminutive is not represented.
- *Malizioso son io troppo* ‘malicious am I too much’ → “Too malicious indeed”: the verb and explicit subject are omitted; the degree modifier moves before the adjective; and “indeed” is supplied for emphasis.
- *procuro* ‘procure, bring about’ → “contrive”: the English emphasizes devising rather than bringing about.
- *a’ miei* ‘for my own’ → “for my companions”: “companions” supplies a noun for the substantivized possessive.

> 112 Alichin non si tenne, e di rintoppo  
> 113 agli altri, disse a lui: “Se tu ti cali,  
> 114 io non ti verrò dietro di galoppo,  

Alichino held in no longer, and in opposition to the others said to him: “If thou stoop, I will not follow thee at gallop,

- *non si tenne* ‘did not restrain himself’ → “held in no longer”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verbal phrase, and “no longer” adds a temporal sense not separately expressed by *non*.
- *ti cali* ‘lower thyself’ → “stoop”: a reflexive verb becomes an intransitive verb.
- *io non ti verrò dietro* ‘I shall not come behind thee’ → “I will not follow thee”: the verb and directional adverb become the single transitive verb “follow,” with the pronoun placed after it.

> 115 ma batterò sopra la pece l’ali;  
> 116 lascisi il colle, e sia la ripa scudo  
> 117 a veder se tu sol più di noi vali.”  

but beat my wings above the pitch; let the height be left, and be the bank a screen, to see if thou alone prevailest over us.”

- *ma batterò* ‘but I shall beat’ → “but beat”: the finite future becomes a coordinated bare verb sharing the preceding ‘I will’.
- *sopra la pece l’ali* ‘above the pitch the wings’ → “my wings above the pitch”: the object moves before the prepositional phrase, and “my” replaces the definite article.
- *il colle* ‘the hill’ → “the height”: the landform is named by its elevation rather than directly as a hill.
- *scudo* ‘a shield’ → “a screen”: the protective object is rendered as something providing concealment.
- *più di noi vali* ‘more than we art worth’ → “prevailest over us”: the comparative expression of worth or power becomes a verb of superiority, with “over us” replacing the comparison introduced by *di*.

## Lines 118–151 — The Escape and the Devils’ Brawl

> 118 O tu che leggi, udirai nuovo ludo!  

O Reader, thou shalt hear new sport!

- *O tu che leggi* ‘O thou who readest’ → “O Reader”: the pronoun and relative clause become a noun of address.
- *udirai* ‘thou wilt hear’ → “thou shalt hear”: the Italian future is expressed with “shalt” rather than ‘wilt’.

> 119 Ciascun dall’ altra costa gli occhi volse;  
> 120 quei prima, ch’ a ciò fare era più crudo.  

All turned their eyes toward the other side, he first who had been most unripe for doing it.

- *Ciascun* ‘each’ → “All”: the distributive singular becomes a collective plural.
- *dall’ altra costa gli occhi volse* ‘toward the other side the eyes turned’ → “turned their eyes toward the other side”: the verb and object precede the directional phrase, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *quei prima* ‘that one first’ → “he first”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *ch’ a ciò fare era più crudo* ‘who for doing this was more unyielding’ → “who had been most unripe for doing it”: the imperfect becomes a past perfect, the comparative becomes a superlative, and the phrase qualifying the adjective moves after it. “Unripe” adopts another sense of *crudo*, rather than directly expressing reluctance; “it” replaces the demonstrative *ciò*.

> 121 Lo Navarrese ben suo tempo colse,  
> 122 fermò le piante a terra, ed in un punto  
> 123 saltò, e dal proposto lor si sciolse.  

The Navarrese chose well his time; planted his soles upon the ground, and in an instant leapt and from their purpose freed himself.

- *ben suo tempo colse* ‘well his time seized’ → “chose well his time”: the verb moves before its adverb and object; ‘seizing’ the occasion becomes ‘choosing’ the time.
- *fermò le piante a terra* ‘fixed the soles on the ground’ → “planted his soles upon the ground”: “planted” substitutes a more specific action for ‘fixed’, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *in un punto* ‘in a point’ → “in an instant”: the temporal sense of *punto* is made explicit.
- *e dal proposto lor si sciolse* ‘and from the purpose of them himself freed’ → “and from their purpose freed himself”: the possessive precedes its noun, and the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.

> 124 Di che ciascun di colpa fu compunto,  
> 125 ma quei più che cagion fu del difetto;  

Thereat each was stung [with guilt]; but he most who had been cause of the mistake;

- *Di che* ‘at which’ → “Thereat”: a relative connection becomes a demonstrative adverb.
- *di colpa fu compunto* ‘with guilt was pricked’ → “was stung [with guilt]”: the complement moves after the verb, and ‘pricked’ becomes “stung”. The bracketed words render *di colpa*.
- *ma quei più* ‘but that one more’ → “but he most”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the comparative becomes a superlative.
- *che cagion fu del difetto* ‘who cause was of the fault’ → “who had been cause of the mistake”: the predicate noun follows the verb, the simple past becomes a past perfect, and *difetto* is interpreted as a “mistake”.

> 126 però si mosse, e gridò: “Tu se’ giunto!”  

he therefore started forth, and shouted: “Thou’rt caught!”

- *si mosse* ‘moved himself’ → “he therefore started forth”: the reflexive verb becomes an intransitive expression, with “started forth” suggesting a sudden movement outward.
- *Tu se’ giunto* ‘thou art overtaken’ → “Thou’rt caught!”: *giunto* is interpreted as successful capture rather than merely being overtaken.

> 127 Ma poco i valse, chè l’ ale al sospetto  
> 128 non potero avanzar; quegli andò sotto,  
> 129 e quei drizzò, volando suso, il petto:  

But little it availed [him]; for wings could not outspeed the terror; the sinner went under; and he, flying, raised up his breast:

- *Ma poco i valse* ‘but little to him it availed’ → “But little it availed [him]”: the indirect-object pronoun moves after the verb; the bracketed “him” renders *i*.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *l’ ale al sospetto non potero avanzar* ‘the wings the apprehension could not outstrip’ → “wings could not outspeed the terror”: the definite article is omitted, the object moves after the verb, and *sospetto* becomes the stronger “terror”. “Outspeed” makes the comparison specifically one of speed.
- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “the sinner”: the demonstrative is replaced by an explicit identification.
- *e quei drizzò, volando suso, il petto* ‘and that one raised, flying upward, the breast’ → “and he, flying, raised up his breast”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, the participial phrase moves before the finite verb, and “up” is attached to “raised” rather than to “flying”. The definite article becomes a possessive.

> 130 non altrimenti l’ anitra di botto,  
> 131 quando il falcon s’ appressa, giù s’ attuffa,  
> 132 ed ei ritorna su crucciato e rotto.  

not otherwise the duck suddenly dives down, when the falcon approaches, and he returns up angry and defeated.

- *l’ anitra di botto, quando il falcon s’ appressa, giù s’ attuffa* ‘the duck at a stroke, when the falcon draws itself near, downward plunges itself’ → “the duck suddenly dives down, when the falcon approaches”: the main verb moves before the temporal clause, and the directional adverb follows it. *Di botto* becomes the single adverb “suddenly”; both reflexive verbs become English intransitives.
- *rotto* ‘broken’ → “defeated”: the participle is rendered in its figurative sense.

> 133 Irato Calcabrina della buffa,  
> 134 volando dietro gli tenne, invaghito  
> 135 che quei campasse per aver la zuffa.  

Calcabrina, furious at the trick, kept flying after him, desirous that the sinner might escape, to have a quarrel.

- *Irato Calcabrina della buffa* ‘angry Calcabrina at the trick’ → “Calcabrina, furious at the trick”: the adjective moves after the name and is strengthened to “furious”.
- *volando dietro gli tenne* ‘flying behind him he kept’ → “kept flying after him”: the idiom *tenere dietro* means ‘follow’; the translation redistributes its words into “kept flying” and “after him”.
- *invaghito che quei campasse* ‘enamoured that that one should escape’ → “desirous that the sinner might escape”: the participial adjective becomes “desirous”, the demonstrative is replaced by an explicit noun, and the imperfect subjunctive is expressed through “might”.
- *la zuffa* ‘the fight’ → “a quarrel”: the definite article becomes indefinite, and “quarrel” is less specifically physical than *zuffa*.

> 136 E come il barattier fu disparito,  
> 137 così volse gli artigli al suo compagno,  
> 138 e fu con lui sopra il fosso ghermito.  

And, when the barrator had disappeared, he turned his talons on his fellow, and was clutched with him above the ditch.

- *E come … così* ‘and as … so’ → “And, when”: the paired temporal construction becomes a single conjunction; *così* has no separate equivalent.
- *fu disparito* ‘was disappeared’ → “had disappeared”: the Italian past anterior, formed with *essere*, becomes an English past perfect formed with “had”.
- *volse gli artigli al suo compagno* ‘turned the talons toward his companion’ → “he turned his talons on his fellow”: the definite article becomes a possessive, “on” replaces the directional ‘toward’, and “fellow” renders ‘companion’.
- *fu con lui sopra il fosso ghermito* ‘was with him above the ditch clutched’ → “was clutched with him above the ditch”: the participle moves immediately after its auxiliary.

> 139 Ma l’ altro fu bene sparvier grifagno  
> 140 ad artigliar ben lui, e ambo e due  
> 141 cadder nel mezzo del bollente stagno.  

But the other was indeed a sparrowhawk to claw him well; and both dropt down into the middle of the boiling pond.

- *bene* ‘well’ → “indeed”: the adverb is interpreted as an emphatic affirmation.
- *sparvier grifagno* ‘a fierce sparrowhawk’ → “a sparrowhawk”: *grifagno*, qualifying the bird as fierce or powerful in seizing, has no separate equivalent; English supplies the indefinite article.
- *ad artigliar ben lui* ‘to claw well him’ → “to claw him well”: the object moves before the adverb.
- *e ambo e due* ‘and both and two’ → “and both”: the emphatic double expression is reduced to one word.
- *cadder nel mezzo* ‘fell in the middle’ → “dropt down into the middle”: “dropt down” replaces ‘fell’, and “into” explicitly marks motion into a destination.

> 142 Lo caldo sghermitor subito fue;  
> 143 ma però di levarsi era niente,  
> 144 sì aveano inviscate l’ ale sue.  

The heat at once unclutched them; but rise they could not, their wings were so beglued.

- *Lo caldo sghermitor subito fue* ‘the heat an unclutcher immediately was’ → “The heat at once unclutched them”: the predicate agent noun becomes a finite verb, *fue* loses its separate equivalent, and “them” is supplied as the object.
- *ma però di levarsi era niente* ‘but nevertheless of raising themselves there was nothing’ → “but rise they could not”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal statement of inability, with “could” supplied. The reflexive infinitive becomes intransitive “rise”, placed emphatically first; concessive *però* is not separately rendered.
- *sì aveano inviscate l’ ale sue* ‘so they had their wings limed’ → “their wings were so beglued”: the construction with ‘have’ and an object qualified by a participle becomes a passive construction with “wings” as subject. “Beglued” renders *inviscate* without explicitly identifying birdlime.

> 145 Barbariccia, con gli altri suoi dolente,  
> 146 quattro ne fe’ volar dall’ altra costa  
> 147 con tutti i raffi, ed assai prestamente  
> 148 di qua, di là discesero alla posta;  

Barbariccia with the rest lamenting, made four of them fly over to the other coast with all their drags; and most rapidly on this side, on that, they descended to the stand;

- *con gli altri suoi* ‘with his others’ → “with the rest”: “the rest” replaces ‘the others’, and the possessive has no equivalent.
- *dolente* ‘sorrowful’ → “lamenting”: an adjective expressing grief becomes a participle suggesting its expression.
- *quattro ne fe’ volar* ‘four of them made fly’ → “made four of them fly”: the causative verb moves before the object.
- *dall’ altra costa* ‘to the other side’ → “over to the other coast”: “over” is supplied, and “coast” replaces ‘side’.
- *con tutti i raffi* ‘with all the hooks’ → “with all their drags”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “drags” names the hooks as grappling implements.
- *assai prestamente* ‘very swiftly’ → “most rapidly”: “most” serves as an intensive equivalent of *assai*, rather than as a comparative superlative.

> 149 porser gli uncini verso gl’ impaniati,  
> 150 ch’ eran già cotti dentro dalla crosta;  

they stretched their hooks towards the limed *pair*, who were already scalded within the crust;

- *porser gli uncini* ‘extended the hooks’ → “they stretched their hooks”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *gl’ impaniati* ‘the limed ones’ → “the limed *pair*”: the substantivized plural participle becomes an adjective qualifying the supplied noun “pair”, which explicitly specifies two.
- *cotti* ‘cooked’ → “scalded”: the translation substitutes a more specific kind of injury by heat.

> 151 e noi lasciammo lor così impacciati.  

and we left them thus embroiled.

- *impacciati* ‘encumbered’ → “embroiled”: the translation chooses a term suggesting entanglement in difficulty rather than simply obstruction or impediment.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Recasting

Carlyle’s translation remains close to the Italian’s sequence of expressions, often preserving its repetitions, concrete vocabulary, and unusual turns of phrase. “around around” follows *intorno intorno*, while “the tongues of them” retains the postposed possession of *le lingue lor*. Such formulations coexist with frequent rearrangement into more customary English order: subjects precede verbs, objects follow them, and qualifying phrases move after their nouns. Thus *della bolgia ogni contegno* becomes “each habit of the chasm”. The English nevertheless retains conspicuous inversions, including “coursers have I seen” and “money took he for himself”.

Changes of construction are especially frequent. Infinitives governed by verbs of seeing become participles, as *muover campo* becomes “moving camp”, or nouns, as *gir gualdane* becomes “the march of foragers”. Pronominal verbs commonly become English intransitives, while possessive datives and definite articles before body parts become possessives: *gli arroncigliò le impegolate chiome* becomes “hooked his pitchy locks”. More extensive recasting turns *Lo caldo sghermitor subito fue*, ‘the heat immediately was an unclutcher’, into “The heat at once unclutched them”. Subordination sometimes gives way to coordination, notably when *sì che* becomes “and”; elsewhere, a finite clause is compressed, as *quand’ io sufolerò* becomes “on whistling”. Italian imperfects often receive simple-past renderings, though “kept gazing” explicitly conveys continued action.

Vocabulary ranges from close lexical echoes to interpretation, intensification, and shifts of emphasis. *Legno*, ‘wood’, becomes “ship”, replacing the metonymy with its referent; *lacciuoli*, ‘little snares’, becomes the abstract “artifices”. “haled” gives *trassel* greater force, while “hideous” for *fiera* shifts the emphasis from fierceness to repulsiveness. Some choices alter the designation more substantially: *cennamella*, ‘shawm’, becomes “cornet”, and *più d’incontra*, ‘more directly opposite’, becomes “nearest”. Conversely, expressions such as “vessel of every fraud” and “claw my scurf” closely reproduce the Italian imagery rather than smoothing it away.

Omissions and additions generally accompany these grammatical and idiomatic adjustments. Repeated prepositions, emphatic doubling such as *ambo e due*, and expletive negatives are reduced or left unrepresented; *grifagno* likewise has no separate equivalent beside “sparrowhawk”. Added nouns clarify substantivized expressions: *lo spaurato* becomes “the frightened sinner”, and *a’ miei* becomes “for my companions”. The bracketed “[him]” and “[with guilt]” explicitly render elements present in the Italian. Throughout, English also supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits the Italian articles before possessives. These routine changes help make the prose grammatically English without effacing its recurrent closeness to Italian phrasing.
