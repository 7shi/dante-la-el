# Inferno 16

## Lines 1–21 — Three Shades Request a Halt

> 1 Già era in loco ove s' udia il rimbombo  
> 2 dell' acqua che cadea nell' altro giro,  
> 3 simile a quel che l' arnie fanno rombo ;  

Already I was in a place where the resounding of the water, that fell into the other circle, was heard like the hum which bee-hives make;

- *ove s'udia il rimbombo* ‘where was heard the resounding’ → “where the resounding” … “was heard”: the passive construction with *si* becomes an English passive; the subject precedes the verb, which is delayed until after the description of the water.
- *cadea* ‘was falling’ → “fell”: the Italian imperfect becomes a simple past, without explicitly marking the continuing action.
- *nell'altro giro* ‘into the other circuit’ → “into the other circle”: *giro* is rendered as a spatial circle.
- *simile a quel che l'arnie fanno rombo* ‘similar to that hum which the bee-hives make’ → “like the hum which bee-hives make”: the adjective and preposition become “like”; the demonstrative becomes a definite article; *rombo*, postponed in Italian until after the relative clause, precedes it in English; and the article before *arnie* is omitted.

> 4 quando tre ombre insieme si partiro,  
> 5 correndo, d' una torma che passava  
> 6 sotto la pioggia dell' aspro martiro.  

when three shades together, running, quitted a troop that passed beneath the rain of the sharp torment.

- *si partiro, / correndo, d'una torma* ‘departed, running, from a troop’ → “running, quitted a troop”: the pronominal, intransitive verb with a phrase introduced by *di* becomes the transitive “quitted”; “running” moves before the finite verb.
- *passava* ‘was passing’ → “passed”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *aspro martiro* ‘harsh torment’ → “sharp torment”: the adjective of harshness is rendered by one suggesting acuteness.

> 7 Venian ver noi, e ciascuna gridava :  
> 8 " Sostati tu, che all' abito ne sembri  
> 9 essere alcun di nostra terra prava."  

They came towards us, and each cried: “Stay thee, thou who by thy dress to us appearest to be some one from our perverse country.”

- *Venian* ‘were coming’ → “came”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *gridava* ‘was crying’ → “cried”: the imperfect again becomes a simple past.
- *all'abito* ‘by the dress’ → “by thy dress”: the definite article becomes an explicit possessive.
- *di nostra terra prava* ‘of our country perverse’ → “from our perverse country”: *di* is expressed as origin by “from”, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 10 Ahi me, che piaghe vidi ne' lor membri  
> 11 recenti e vecchie dalle fiamme incese !  

Ah me! what wounds I saw upon their limbs, recent and old, by the flames burnt in.

- *ne' lor membri* ‘in their limbs’ → “upon their limbs”: the preposition locating the wounds within the limbs becomes one locating them on their surface.
- *incese* ‘burnt’ → “burnt in”: “in” is supplied, presenting the burning as an imprinting of the wounds.

> 12 Ancor men duol, pur ch' io me ne rimembri.  

It pains me yet, when I but think thereof.

- *Ancor men duol* ‘still it pains me on that account’ → “It pains me yet”: the initial adverb moves after the verb and object; the *ne* contained in *men*, referring back to what causes the pain, has no separate English equivalent.
- *pur ch'io me ne rimembri* ‘if only I recall it to myself’ → “when I but think thereof”: the conditional construction becomes temporal, while the restriction expressed by *pur* becomes “but”. Reflexive remembering becomes non-reflexive thinking, and the Italian subjunctive is rendered by an English indicative.

> 13 Alle lor grida il mio dottor s' attese,  
> 14 volse il viso ver me, e : “ Ora aspetta,”  
> 15 disse, “ a costor si vuole esser cortese ;  

To their cries my Teacher listened; turned his face toward me, and said: “Now wait: to these courtesy is due;

- *s'attese* ‘gave heed’ → “listened”: the pronominal expression of attention becomes a non-pronominal verb specifically denoting auditory attention.
- *il viso* ‘the face’ → “his face”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *e … Ora aspetta … disse* ‘and … Now wait … said’ → “and said: “Now wait:”: the reporting verb moves from after the first quoted command to before the quotation.
- *a costor si vuole esser cortese* ‘to these one ought to be courteous’ → “to these courtesy is due”: the impersonal construction expressing obligation is recast with “courtesy” as its subject; the adjective *cortese* becomes a noun, and the obligation is expressed by “is due”.

> 16 e se non fosse il foco che saetta  
> 17 la natura del loco, io dicerei  
> 18 che meglio stesse a te, che a lor, la fretta.”  

and were there not the fire, which the nature of the place darts, I should say the haste befitted thee more than them.”

- *se non fosse il foco* ‘if there were not the fire’ → “were there not the fire”: the conditional conjunction is omitted, and inversion expresses the condition instead.
- *che saetta / la natura del loco* ‘which darts the nature of the place’ → “which the nature of the place darts”: the Italian subject follows its verb; English places it before the verb, making explicit that the nature of the place does the darting.
- *che meglio stesse a te, che a lor, la fretta* ‘that better were fitting for you, than for them, the haste’ → “the haste befitted thee more than them”: the introductory *che* is omitted, and the postponed subject moves before the verb. The construction *stare a* becomes transitive “befitted”, while *meglio*, ‘better’, becomes the comparison of degree “more”.

> 19 Ricominciar, come noi ristemmo, ei  
> 20 l' antico verso ; e quando a noi fur giunti,  
> 21 fenno una rota di sè tutti e trei.  

They recommenced, as we stood still, their ancient wail; and when they had reached us, all the three made of themselves a wheel.

- *Ricominciar … ei* ‘recommenced … they’ → “They recommenced”: the subject, postponed in Italian until after the intervening clause, moves before the verb.
- *come noi ristemmo* ‘as we stopped’ → “as we stood still”: the act of stopping is rendered as the resulting state of standing still.
- *l'antico verso* ‘the former cry’ → “their ancient wail”: the definite article becomes a possessive. The translation interprets *verso* specifically as a lamenting cry and renders *antico*, referring to what they had been doing before, as “ancient”.
- *quando a noi fur giunti* ‘when to us they had arrived’ → “when they had reached us”: the intransitive verb with a preceding prepositional phrase becomes transitive “reached”, with its object following it.
- *fenno una rota di sè tutti e trei* ‘made a wheel of themselves all and three’ → “all the three made of themselves a wheel”: the postponed subject moves to the beginning, and “of themselves” moves before “a wheel”. The idiomatic *tutti e trei* becomes “all the three”, without a separate equivalent for *e* and with an added definite article.

## Lines 22–54 — Fame, Torment, and Compassion

> 22 Qual soleano i campion far nudi ed unti,  
> 23 avvisando lor presa e lor vantaggio,  
> 24 prima che sien tra lor battuti e punti:  

As champions, naked and anointed, were wont to do, spying their grasp and vantage, ere they came to blows and thrusts at one another:

- *soleano i campion far nudi ed unti* ‘were wont the champions to do naked and anointed’ → “champions, naked and anointed, were wont to do”: the subject precedes the verb, and its descriptive adjectives move forward with it; the generic definite article before *campion* is omitted.
- *lor presa e lor vantaggio* ‘their grasp and their vantage’ → “their grasp and vantage”: the second possessive is omitted.
- *prima che sien tra lor battuti e punti* ‘before they are struck and pricked among themselves’ → “ere they came to blows and thrusts at one another”: the present subjunctive passive becomes a past-tense active construction. The participles become the nouns “blows and thrusts,” and *tra lor* becomes the explicitly reciprocal “at one another.”

> 25 così, rotando, ciascuno il visaggio  
> 26 drizzava a me, sì che in contrario il collo  
> 27 faceva a' piè continuo viaggio.  

thus, wheeling, each directed his visage toward me, so that the neck kept travelling in a direction contrary to the feet.

- *il visaggio drizzava a me* ‘the visage directed toward me’ → “directed his visage toward me”: the object follows the verb rather than preceding it, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *in contrario il collo faceva a' piè continuo viaggio* ‘contrary to the feet the neck made a continuous journey’ → “the neck kept travelling in a direction contrary to the feet”: the interrupted expression *in contrario … a' piè* is brought together after the verb. Making a continuous journey becomes “kept travelling,” with the adjective’s meaning expressed through the continuing-action construction; “a direction” is supplied.

> 28 “Eh, se miseria d'esto loco sollo  
> 29 rende in dispetto noi e nostri preghi,”  
> 30 cominciò l' uno, “e il tinto aspetto e brollo,  

And one of them began: “If the misery of this loose place, and our [stained] and scorched aspect, bring us and our prayers into contempt,

- *Eh, se* ‘Ah, if’ → “If”: the opening interjection is omitted.
- *miseria* ‘misery’ → “the misery”: a definite article is supplied.
- *d'esto loco sollo* ‘of this place loose’ → “of this loose place”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *rende in dispetto noi e nostri preghi* ‘renders in contempt us and our prayers’ → “bring us and our prayers into contempt”: the object moves before the prepositional expression, and *rendere in dispetto* is recast as bringing into contempt. The Italian singular verb becomes plural, agreeing with both coordinated subjects.
- *cominciò l' uno* ‘began the one’ → “And one of them began”: the reporting clause moves from inside the speech to its beginning, and the subject precedes the verb. “And” and “of them” are supplied, while the definite expression becomes indefinite.
- *e il tinto aspetto e brollo* ‘and the stained aspect and scorched’ → “and our [stained] and scorched aspect”: the delayed second subject is moved alongside the first, before the verb. The definite article becomes “our,” and both adjectives precede the noun.

> 31 la fama nostra il tuo animo pieghi  
> 32 a dirne chi tu se', che i vivi piedi  
> 33 così sicuro per lo inferno freghi.  

let our fame incline thy mind to tell us who thou art, that thus securely movest thy living feet through Hell.

- *la fama nostra il tuo animo pieghi* ‘may our fame bend thy mind’ → “let our fame incline thy mind”: the independent subjunctive expressing a wish or appeal becomes a construction with “let.” The verb moves before its object, the postposed *nostra* moves before its noun, and metaphorical bending becomes inclining.
- *i vivi piedi* ‘the living feet’ → “thy living feet”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the object moves after the verb.
- *così sicuro* ‘so secure’ → “thus securely”: the adjective describing the person becomes an adverb describing his movement.
- *per lo inferno* ‘through the hell’ → “through Hell”: the definite article is omitted as the destination is treated as a proper name.
- *freghi* ‘rubbest’ → “movest”: the more specific action of rubbing is generalized to movement; the clause-final verb moves before its object and the phrase describing the route.

> 34 Questi, l' orme di cui pestar mi vedi,  
> 35 tutto che nudo e dipelato vada,  
> 36 fu di grado maggior che tu non credi.  

He in whose footsteps thou seest me tread, all naked and peeled though he be, was higher in degree than thou believest.

- *Questi* ‘This one’ → “He”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *l' orme di cui pestar mi vedi* ‘the footprints of whom to tread thou seest me’ → “in whose footsteps thou seest me tread”: the relative possessive is recast as “whose,” and the infinitive follows the verb of perception. The footprints, directly governed by *pestar*, become a prepositional expression with the supplied “in.”
- *tutto che nudo e dipelato vada* ‘although naked and peeled he go’ → “all naked and peeled though he be”: the concessive *tutto che* is expressed by “though,” while “all” supplies emphasis to the description. The adjectives precede the conjunction, and going in this condition becomes being in it.
- *fu di grado maggior* ‘was of greater degree’ → “was higher in degree”: the comparative adjective modifying *grado* becomes the predicate “higher,” with “in degree” specifying the respect in which it applies.
- *che tu non credi* ‘than thou dost not believe’ → “than thou believest”: the non-negative *non* used after a comparative is omitted.

> 37 Nepote fu della buona Gualdrada;  
> 38 Guido Guerra ebbe nome, ed in sua vita  
> 39 fece col senno assai e con la spada.  

Grandson of the good Gualdrada, his name was Guido Guerra; and in his lifetime he did much with counsel and with sword.

- *Nepote fu della buona Gualdrada; Guido Guerra ebbe nome* ‘Grandson he was of the good Gualdrada; Guido Guerra he had as name’ → “Grandson of the good Gualdrada, his name was Guido Guerra”: the first independent predication becomes an introductory noun phrase without a verb. The second changes from having a name to “his name” being something, placing the name after the verb.
- *in sua vita* ‘in his life’ → “in his lifetime”: “lifetime” explicitly presents the life as a span of time.
- *col senno assai* ‘with the judgement much’ → “much with counsel”: personal judgement or wisdom is rendered as counsel; the definite article is omitted, and “much” moves before the instrumental phrase.
- *con la spada* ‘with the sword’ → “with sword”: the definite article is omitted.

> 40 L' altro che appresso me l' arena trita,  
> 41 è Tegghiaio Aldobrandi, la cui voce  
> 42 nel mondo su dovria esser gradita.  

The other, that treads the sand behind me, is Tegghiaio Aldobrandi, whose fame should be grateful up in the world.

- *appresso me* ‘after me’ → “behind me”: the relation is expressed spatially, and the phrase moves from before the object to the end of the relative clause.
- *trita* ‘grinds’ → “treads”: the verb is rendered as trampling or treading rather than grinding, and moves before its object.
- *la cui voce* ‘whose voice’ → “whose fame”: the translation takes *voce* in the sense of reputation rather than voice.
- *nel mondo su* ‘in the world above’ → “up in the world”: the directional word moves before the prepositional phrase, and the whole expression moves after the predicate.
- *dovria esser gradita* ‘should be welcomed’ → “should be grateful”: *gradita* is rendered by “grateful” in its older sense of pleasing or agreeable, rather than by a participle expressing favourable reception.

> 43 Ed io, che posto son con loro in croce,  
> 44 Jacopo Rusticucci fui; e certo  
> 45 la fiera moglie più ch' altro mi nuoce.”  

And I, who am placed with them in torment, was Jacopo Rusticucci; and certainly, more than aught else, my savage wife injures me.”

- *posto son* ‘placed am’ → “am placed”: the auxiliary precedes the participle.
- *in croce* ‘on the cross’ → “in torment”: the figurative expression is rendered by its sense rather than its image.
- *Jacopo Rusticucci fui* ‘Jacopo Rusticucci I was’ → “was Jacopo Rusticucci”: the name moves from before the verb to after it.
- *la fiera moglie* ‘the savage wife’ → “my savage wife”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *più ch' altro* ‘more than anything else’ → “more than aught else”: the comparative phrase moves before the subject rather than following it.
- *mi nuoce* ‘me harms’ → “injures me”: the object pronoun follows the verb rather than preceding it.

> 46 S' io fussi stato dal foco coperto,  
> 47 gittato mi sarei tra lor di sotto,  
> 48 e credo che il dottor l' avria sofferto.  

Had I been sheltered from the fire, I should have thrown myself amid them below, and I believe my Teacher would have permitted it.

- *S' io fussi stato dal foco coperto* ‘If I had been from the fire covered’ → “Had I been sheltered from the fire”: conditional inversion replaces the clause introduced by *se*. The participle moves before its accompanying prepositional phrase, and covering is expressed as sheltering.
- *gittato mi sarei* ‘thrown myself I should have’ → “I should have thrown myself”: the auxiliary moves before the participle, and the reflexive pronoun follows it.
- *e credo che* ‘and I believe that’ → “and I believe”: the conjunction introducing the dependent statement is omitted.
- *il dottor* ‘the teacher’ → “my Teacher”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *l' avria sofferto* ‘it would have suffered’ → “would have permitted it”: *soffrire* is rendered in its sense of allowing or tolerating, rather than suffering; the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 49 Ma perch' io mi sarei bruciato e cotto,  
> 50 vinse paura la mia buona voglia,  
> 51 che di loro abbracciar mi facea ghiotto.  

But as I should have burnt and baked myself, fear overcame the good will which made me greedy to embrace them.

- *mi sarei bruciato e cotto* ‘myself I should have burnt and cooked’ → “I should have burnt and baked myself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after both participles, and the general *cotto* becomes the more specific “baked.”
- *vinse paura la mia buona voglia* ‘overcame fear my good will’ → “fear overcame the good will”: the subject moves before the verb. The possessive *mia* is omitted, leaving the definite article.
- *che di loro abbracciar mi facea ghiotto* ‘which of them embracing made me greedy’ → “which made me greedy to embrace them”: the infinitival expression moves from before the verb to after the adjective and is introduced by “to” rather than a preposition corresponding to *di*. Both object pronouns move after the verbs that govern them.

> 52 Poi cominciai : “Non dispetto, ma doglia  
> 53 la vostra condizion dentro mi fisse  
> 54 tanto che tardi tutta si dispoglia,  

Then I began: “Not contempt, but sorrow, your condition fixed within me, so deeply that it will not leave me soon,

- *la vostra condizion dentro mi fisse* ‘your condition within me fixed’ → “your condition fixed within me”: the verb moves before the expression of inward location, and *dentro* and *mi* are combined as “within me.”
- *tanto che* ‘so much that’ → “so deeply that”: degree is expressed specifically as depth, extending the spatial force of something being fixed within.
- *tardi tutta si dispoglia* ‘late it wholly strips itself off’ → “it will not leave me soon”: the present reflexive construction becomes a future active one, and the image of stripping off becomes departure. Affirmative lateness is recast as “not … soon”; *tutta*, expressing completeness, is omitted, and “me” is supplied as the object of “leave.”

## Lines 55–87 — Florence’s Decline and a Request for Remembrance

> 55 tosto che questo mio signor mi disse  
> 56 parole, per le quali io mi pensai,  
> 57 che qual voi siete, tal gente venisse.  

when this my Lord spake words to me, by which I felt that such men as you are might be coming.

- *tosto che* ‘as soon as’ → “when”: the explicit immediacy of *tosto* is omitted.
- *mi disse parole* ‘to me said words’ → “spake words to me”: “spake” replaces ‘said’, and the indirect object moves after the verb and its object.
- *per le quali* ‘because of which’ → “by which”: the words are presented as the means of apprehension rather than its cause.
- *io mi pensai* ‘I thought to myself’ → “I felt”: thought becomes a feeling or impression, and the reflexive expression is omitted.
- *che qual voi siete, tal gente venisse* ‘that as you are, such people should come’ → “that such men as you are might be coming”: the correlative construction *qual … tal* is reorganized as “such men as”; *gente* becomes “men”; and the imperfect subjunctive is rendered by a modal progressive.

> 58 Di vostra terra sono; e sempre mai  
> 59 l' opre di voi e gli onorati nomi  
> 60 con affezion ritrassi ed ascoltai.  

Of your city am I, and always with affection have I rehearsed and heard your deeds and honoured names.

- *terra* ‘land’ → “city”: the noun is rendered in its specifically civic sense.
- *sempre mai* ‘always ever’ → “always”: the emphatic combination is reduced to one adverb.
- *l' opre di voi e gli onorati nomi* ‘the deeds of you and the honoured names’ → “your deeds and honoured names”: the objects move from before the verbs to after them; *di voi* becomes the possessive “your”, which also governs “honoured names”.
- *ritrassi ed ascoltai* ‘I recounted and listened to’ → “have I rehearsed and heard”: the simple past becomes the present perfect, with inversion after the opening adverbial expressions. “Rehearsed” renders recounting, while “heard” replaces the more active ‘listened to’.

> 61 Lascio lo fele, e vo per dolci pomi  
> 62 promessi a me per lo verace duca;  
> 63 ma fino al centro pria convien ch' io tomi.”  

I leave the gall, and go for *the sweet* apples promised me by my veracious Guide; but to the centre it behoves me first to fall."

- *dolci pomi* ‘sweet apples’ → “*the sweet* apples”: the definite article is supplied.
- *a me* ‘to me’ → “me”: the preposition is omitted in the English indirect-object construction.
- *lo verace duca* ‘the veracious leader’ → “my veracious Guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”.
- *fino al centro* ‘as far as the centre’ → “to the centre”: the explicit expression of extent is reduced to the preposition of destination.
- *pria convien ch' io tomi* ‘first it is necessary that I tumble’ → “it behoves me first to fall”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an infinitive governed by “behoves me”; “first” moves after “me”; and “fall” is less specific than ‘tumble’.

> 64 “Se lungamente l' anima conduca  
> 65 le membra tue,” rispose quegli allora,  
> 66 “e se la fama tua dopo te luca,  

"So may the soul long animate thy members," he then replied, "and so thy fame shine after thee;

- *Se* ‘if’ → “So may”: the conditional opening of a wish is made explicitly optative in English.
- *lungamente l' anima conduca* ‘long the soul lead’ → “the soul long animate”: the adverb moves after the subject, and the soul’s ‘leading’ the body is interpreted as animating it.
- *le membra tue* ‘the members thine’ → “thy members”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *rispose quegli allora* ‘replied that one then’ → “he then replied”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun, and both subject and adverb precede the verb.
- *e se* ‘and if’ → “and so”: the second conditional formula likewise becomes a wish, carrying forward the force of “may”.
- *la fama tua dopo te luca* ‘the fame thine after thee shine’ → “thy fame shine after thee”: the possessive moves before its noun, and the temporal phrase moves after the verb.

> 67 cortesia e valor di' se dimora  
> 68 nella nostra città sì come suole,  
> 69 o se del tutto se n'è gita fuora?  

tell, if courtesy and valour abide within our city as they were wont, or have gone quite out of it?

- *cortesia e valor di' se dimora* ‘courtesy and valour tell if dwells’ → “tell, if courtesy and valour abide”: the imperative and conjunction move before the subject; the Italian singular verb becomes plural.
- *nella nostra città* ‘in our city’ → “within our city”: “within” replaces the simple preposition ‘in’.
- *sì come suole* ‘so as it is wont’ → “as they were wont”: the comparison is compressed into “as”, and the present singular verb becomes past and plural.
- *o se del tutto se n'è gita fuora* ‘or if altogether it has gone away out of there’ → “or have gone quite out of it”: the repeated ‘if’ is omitted, singular agreement becomes plural, and the pronominal expression is recast as “gone … out of it”. The adverbial *del tutto* becomes “quite” and moves after the verb.

> 70 chè Guglielmo Borsiere, il qual si duole  
> 71 con noi per poco, e va là coi compagni,  
> 72 assai ne cruccia con le sue parole.”  

For Guglielmo Borsiere—who has been short time in pain with us, and yonder goes with our companions—greatly torments us with his words."

- *chè* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating explanatory conjunction.
- *si duole con noi per poco* ‘suffers with us for a short time’ → “has been short time in pain with us”: the present becomes the present perfect, and the verb of suffering becomes “been … in pain”. The duration moves before “in pain”, with no expressed equivalent of *per*.
- *e va là coi compagni* ‘and goes there with the companions’ → “and yonder goes with our companions”: the adverb moves before the verb, and “our” replaces the definite article.
- *assai ne cruccia* ‘greatly us torments’ → “greatly torments us”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 73 “La gente nuova e i subiti guadagni  
> 74 orgoglio e dismisura han generata,  
> 75 Fiorenza, in te, sì che tu già ten piagni.”  

“The upstart people and the sudden gains, O Florence, have engendered in thee pride and excess, so that thou already weepest thereat.”

- *La gente nuova* ‘the new people’ → “The upstart people”: “upstart” gives ‘new’ a more explicitly disparaging social sense, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *orgoglio e dismisura han generata … in te* ‘pride and lack of measure have engendered … in thee’ → “have engendered in thee pride and excess”: *dismisura* is rendered as “excess”, and the objects move from before the verb to after both the verb and “in thee”.
- *Fiorenza* ‘Florence’ → “O Florence”: “O” is supplied, and the address moves from after the verb to between subject and verb.
- *tu già ten piagni* ‘thou already weepest to thyself over it’ → “thou already weepest thereat”: the reflexive *te* in *ten* is not separately expressed; *ne* becomes “thereat” and moves after the verb.

> 76 Così gridai con la faccia levata;  
> 77 e i tre, che ciò inteser per risposta,  
> 78 guatar l'un l'altro, come al ver si guata.  

Thus I cried with face uplifted; and the three, who understood this as an answer, looked at one another as men look when truth is told.

- *con la faccia levata* ‘with the face uplifted’ → “with face uplifted”: the definite article is omitted.
- *che ciò inteser per risposta* ‘who this understood for answer’ → “who understood this as an answer”: the object moves after the verb, *per* becomes “as”, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *guatar l'un l'altro* ‘looked the one at the other’ → “looked at one another”: the articulated reciprocal expression becomes the English reciprocal pronoun.
- *come al ver si guata* ‘as at the truth one looks’ → “as men look when truth is told”: impersonal *si* becomes “men”; the phrase ‘at the truth’ becomes a temporal clause, with “is told” supplied; and the verb moves before that clause.

> 79 “Se l' altre volte sì poco ti costa,”  
> 80 risposer tutti, “il satisfare altrui,  
> 81 felice te, se sì parli a tua posta!  

“If otherwhile it costs thee so little to satisfy others,” they all replied, “happy thou, [if] thus [thou] speakest at thy will!

- *Se l' altre volte* ‘if the other times’ → “If otherwhile”: the noun phrase expressing other occasions becomes an adverb.
- *sì poco ti costa … il satisfare altrui* ‘so little thee costs … the satisfying of others’ → “it costs thee so little to satisfy others”: introductory “it” is supplied; the substantival infinitive becomes a postponed infinitive clause; and both “thee” and “so little” follow the verb.
- *risposer tutti* ‘replied all’ → “they all replied”: the subject precedes the verb, with *tutti* expressed as “they all”; the reporting clause moves from within the conditional clause to its end.
- *felice te* ‘happy thee’ → “happy thou”: the disjunctive pronoun is rendered by the nominative form, as the subject of an implied verb.

> 82 Però, se campi d' esti lochi bui  
> 83 e torni a riveder le belle stelle,  

Therefore, if thou escape out of these gloomy regions, and return to see again the beauteous stars;

- *d' esti lochi bui* ‘from these dark places’ → “out of these gloomy regions”: “out of” replaces ‘from’; “regions” broadens ‘places’; and “gloomy” renders ‘dark’, with the adjective moved before the noun.

> 84 quando ti gioverà dicere: 'Io fui,'  
> 85 fa che di noi alla gente favelle.”  

when thou shalt rejoice to say, ‘I was,’ see that thou speak of us to men.”

- *quando ti gioverà dicere* ‘when it will please thee to say’ → “when thou shalt rejoice to say”: the construction with an indirect object becomes a personal construction in which “thou” is the subject of “rejoice”.
- *fa che* ‘make that’ → “see that”: the causative imperative is rendered by an English idiom of ensuring.
- *di noi alla gente favelle* ‘of us to the people thou speak’ → “thou speak of us to men”: the verb moves before both prepositional phrases, and the definite collective singular becomes the indefinite plural “men”.

> 86 Indi rupper la rota, ed a fuggirsi  
> 87 ale sembiar le gambe loro snelle.  

Then they broke their wheel; and, as they fled, their nimble legs seemed wings.

- *la rota* ‘the wheel’ → “their wheel”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *ed a fuggirsi* ‘and at fleeing away’ → “and, as they fled”: the preposition and pronominal infinitive become a finite temporal clause; the departure conveyed by *fuggirsi* is not separately expressed as ‘away’.
- *ale sembiar le gambe loro snelle* ‘wings seemed the legs their nimble’ → “their nimble legs seemed wings”: the subject moves before the verb and the predicative noun after it; both possessive and adjective move before “legs”.

## Lines 88–120 — The Waterfall and the Cord

> 88 Un ammen non saria potuto dirsi  
> 89 tosto così, com' ei furo spariti:  
> 90 per che al maestro parve di partirsi.  

An "Amen" could not have been said so quickly as they vanished: wherefore it pleased my Master to depart.

- *non saria potuto dirsi* ‘would not have been able to be said’ → “could not have been said”: the Italian conditional construction with *potere* becomes an English modal perfect passive.
- *tosto così* ‘quickly so’ → “so quickly”: the adverb and its modifier change places.
- *ei furo spariti* ‘they had vanished’ → “they vanished”: the compound past becomes a simple past.
- *al maestro parve di partirsi* ‘to the master it seemed fitting to depart’ → “it pleased my Master to depart”: seeming fitting becomes pleasing; the person affected follows the verb, and “my” replaces the definite article.

> 91 Io lo seguiva, e poco eravam iti,  
> 92 che il suon dell' acqua n' era sì vicino  
> 93 che, per parlar, saremmo appena uditi.  

I followed him; and we had gone but little, when the sound of the water was so near us, that in speaking we should scarce have heard each other.

- *Io lo seguiva* ‘I him followed’ → “I followed him”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *e poco eravam iti* ‘and little we had gone’ → “and we had gone but little”: the expression of distance follows the verb, and “but” is supplied.
- *che* ‘that’ → “when”: the conjunction introducing what happened after their short progress becomes explicitly temporal.
- *n' era sì vicino* ‘to us was so near’ → “was so near us”: the pronoun expressing proximity moves after “near”.
- *per parlar* ‘for speaking’ → “in speaking”: the infinitive becomes a gerund, and the preposition presents speaking as the circumstance.
- *saremmo appena uditi* ‘we should scarcely be heard’ → “we should scarce have heard each other”: the passive becomes an active reciprocal construction, “each other” is supplied, and the conditional becomes perfect.

> 94 Come quel fiume, che ha proprio cammino  
> 95 prima da Monte Veso in ver levante  
> 96 dalla sinistra costa d' Apennino,  

As that river—which first has a path of its own from Monte Veso toward the east, on the left skirt of the Apennine;

- *che ha proprio cammino prima* ‘which has its own path first’ → “which first has a path of its own”: “first” moves before the verb, and the possessive adjective becomes the phrase “of its own” after an indefinite noun.
- *dalla sinistra costa d' Apennino* ‘from the left flank of the Apennine’ → “on the left skirt of the Apennine”: “on” replaces the preposition of origin, and “skirt” replaces the bodily metaphor ‘flank’.

> 97 che si chiama Acquacheta suso, avante  
> 98 che si divalli giù nel basso letto,  
> 99 ed a Forlì di quel nome è vacante,  

which is called Acquacheta above, ere it descends to its low bed, and is vacant of that name at Forli—

- *si chiama* ‘calls itself’ → “is called”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes an English passive.
- *avante che* ‘before’ → “ere”: the two-word conjunction becomes a single archaic English conjunction.
- *si divalli giù nel basso letto* ‘it descends down into the low bed’ → “it descends to its low bed”: the pronominal verb becomes an ordinary intransitive verb; the separate ‘down’ is omitted, ‘into’ becomes “to”, and “its” replaces the definite article.
- *ed a Forlì di quel nome è vacante* ‘and at Forlì of that name is vacant’ → “and is vacant of that name at Forli”: the verb precedes its complement, and the place phrase moves to the end.

> 100 rimbomba là sopra San Benedetto  
> 101 dell' alpe, per cadere ad una scesa,  
> 102 ove dovea per mille esser ricetto:  

resounds from the mountain, there above San Benedetto, in falling at a descent, where for a thousand there should be refuge:

- *dell' alpe* ‘of the mountain’ → “from the mountain”: the translation treats the phrase as the source of the resounding rather than as a qualification of San Benedetto, and moves it before “there above San Benedetto”.
- *per cadere* ‘because of falling’ → “in falling”: the causal construction becomes circumstantial, and the infinitive becomes a gerund.
- *ove dovea per mille esser ricetto* ‘where ought for a thousand to be refuge’ → “where for a thousand there should be refuge”: the imperfect expression of obligation becomes “should be”, without an explicit past reference; “for a thousand” moves before the modal, and existential “there” is supplied.

> 103 così, giù d' una ripa discoscesa,  
> 104 trovammo risonar quell' acqua tinta,  
> 105 sì che in poc' ora avria l' orecchie offesa.  

thus down from a steep bank we found that tainted water re-echoing, so that in little time it would have stunned the ear.

- *una ripa discoscesa* ‘a bank steep’ → “a steep bank”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *trovammo risonar quell' acqua tinta* ‘we found resound that water stained’ → “we found that tainted water re-echoing”: the object precedes the verb describing its sound, the infinitive becomes a participle, and the adjective precedes its noun; ‘stained’ is rendered as “tainted”.
- *avria l' orecchie offesa* ‘would have the ears hurt’ → “it would have stunned the ear”: the object follows the completed verb phrase, plural ‘ears’ becomes singular “ear”, and the general ‘hurt’ becomes the more specific “stunned”.

> 106 Io aveva una corda intorno cinta,  
> 107 e con essa pensai alcuna volta  
> 108 prender la lonza alla pelle dipinta.  

I had a cord girt round me; and with it I thought some time to catch the Leopard of the painted skin.

- *intorno cinta* ‘around girt’ → “girt round me”: the participle precedes the expression of position, and “me” is supplied.
- *prender* ‘take’ → “to catch”: the general verb of taking is rendered by the more specific “catch”.
- *alla pelle dipinta* ‘with the skin painted’ → “of the painted skin”: the descriptive construction with *a* becomes an English phrase with “of”, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 109 Poscia che l'ebbi tutta da me sciolta,  
> 110 sì come il duca m' avea comandato,  
> 111 porsila a lui aggroppata e ravvolta.  

After I had quite unloosed it from me, as my Guide commanded me, I held it out to him coiled and wound up.

- *Poscia che* ‘after that’ → “After”: the Italian conjunction is reduced to its single-word English equivalent.
- *l'ebbi tutta da me sciolta* ‘it I had all from me unloosed’ → “I had quite unloosed it from me”: the object pronoun and “from me” follow the completed verb; *tutta*, agreeing with the cord, becomes the adverb “quite”, modifying the unloosening.
- *sì come* ‘so as’ → “as”: the reinforcing *sì* is omitted.
- *il duca* ‘the leader’ → “my Guide”: “Guide” replaces ‘leader’, and “my” replaces the definite article.
- *m' avea comandato* ‘to me had commanded’ → “commanded me”: the pluperfect becomes a simple past, and the pronoun follows the verb.
- *porsila* ‘I offered it’ → “I held it out”: a single Italian verb becomes an English verbal phrase describing the gesture.
- *aggroppata* ‘knotted’ → “coiled”: the translation interprets the cord as gathered into coils rather than tied into knots.

> 112 Ond' ei si volse in ver lo destro lato,  
> 113 e alquanto di lungi dalla sponda  
> 114 la gittò giuso in quell' alto burrato.  

Then he bent himself toward the right side, and threw it, some distance from the edge, down into that steep abyss.

- *Ond'* ‘whence’ → “Then”: the connective is rendered as temporal succession rather than consequence.
- *si volse* ‘turned himself’ → “bent himself”: turning becomes bending.
- *alquanto di lungi dalla sponda* ‘somewhat far from the edge’ → “some distance from the edge”: an adverbial expression becomes a noun phrase, and the phrase moves after the throwing and its object.
- *la gittò* ‘it he threw’ → “threw it”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *alto burrato* ‘deep chasm’ → “steep abyss”: “steep” substitutes inclination for depth, while “abyss” renders ‘chasm’.

> 115 “E pur convien che novità risponda,”  
> 116 dicea fra me medesmo, “al nuovo cenno  
> 117 che il maestro con l' occhio sì seconda.”  

"Surely," said I within myself, "something new must answer this new signal, which my Master thus follows with his eye."

- *E pur* ‘And indeed’ → “Surely”: ‘And’ is omitted, and the remaining emphasis becomes an assertion of certainty.
- *convien che novità risponda* ‘it is necessary that novelty answer’ → “something new must answer”: the impersonal construction with a subjunctive clause becomes a personal construction with “must”; the abstract noun becomes “something new”.
- *dicea fra me medesmo* ‘I was saying within my own self’ → “said I within myself”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, the reporting verb precedes its subject, and the reporting clause moves earlier in the speech; the separate emphasis of *medesmo* is absorbed into “myself”.
- *al nuovo cenno* ‘to the new signal’ → “this new signal”: English “answer” takes a direct object rather than a complement with ‘to’, and “this” replaces the definite article.
- *che il maestro con l' occhio sì seconda* ‘which the master with the eye thus seconds’ → “which my Master thus follows with his eye”: “follows” renders the verb of seconding or attending; the phrase about the eye moves after the verb, and “my” and “his” replace the two definite articles.

> 118 Ahi quanto cauti gli uomini esser denno  
> 119 presso a color, che non veggon pur l' opra,  
> 120 ma per entro i pensier miran col senno!  

Ah! how cautious ought men to be with those who see not only the deed, but with their sense look through into the thoughts!

- *quanto cauti gli uomini esser denno* ‘how cautious men to be ought’ → “how cautious ought men to be”: the modal moves before the subject, and the infinitive follows it; the definite article before the generic plural ‘men’ is omitted.
- *presso a color* ‘near those’ → “with those”: physical proximity is rendered as association.
- *per entro i pensier miran* ‘through within the thoughts they look’ → “look through into the thoughts”: the verb precedes the phrase describing its reach, and “into” gives directional force to ‘within’.
- *col senno* ‘with understanding’ → “with their sense”: “sense” renders intellectual understanding, “their” replaces the definite article, and the phrase moves before the verb.

## Lines 121–136 — A Figure Swims Upwards

> 121 Ei disse a me: “Tosto verrà di sopra  
> 122 ciò ch'io attendo, e che il tuo pensier sogna  
> 123 tosto convien ch' al tuo viso si scopra.”  

He said to me: “What I expect will soon come up; and what thy thought dreams of, soon must be discovered to thy view.”

- *Tosto verrà di sopra ciò ch'io attendo* ‘Soon will come up above what I expect’ → “What I expect will soon come up”: the subject moves before the verb, “soon” follows the auxiliary, and *di sopra* is compressed into “up”.
- *che il tuo pensier sogna* ‘what thy thought dreams’ → “what thy thought dreams of”: English adds “of” where Italian uses *sogna* transitively.
- *tosto convien ch'al tuo viso si scopra* ‘soon it is necessary that to thy sight it reveal itself’ → “soon must be discovered to thy view”: the impersonal construction *convien che* becomes the modal “must”; the reflexive verb becomes passive, and the phrase naming the recipient’s sight moves after it.

> 124 Sempre a quel ver ch' ha faccia di menzogna  
> 125 de' l' uom chiuder le labbra finch' ei puote,  
> 126 però che senza colpa fa vergogna;  

Always to that truth which has an air of falsehood, a man should close his lips, [so far as he is able], for, though blameless, he incurs reproach;

- *ha faccia di menzogna* ‘has a face of falsehood’ → “has an air of falsehood”: the image of a face becomes the idiomatic “air”, meaning an appearance.
- *de' l'uom chiuder le labbra* ‘ought the man to close the lips’ → “a man should close his lips”: the subject moves before the modal; the generic definite article becomes indefinite, and the article before “lips” becomes a possessive.
- *finch'ei puote* ‘as long as he can’ → “[so far as he is able]”: the temporal expression is interpreted as a limit of ability; ‘can’ becomes “is able”.
- *però che* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating explanatory conjunction.
- *senza colpa fa vergogna* ‘without fault causes shame’ → “though blameless, he incurs reproach”: the prepositional phrase becomes an explicitly concessive adjectival phrase. Rather than making the truth cause shame, the translation makes the man incur reproach.

> 127 ma qui tacer nol posso; e per le note  
> 128 di questa Commedìa, lettor, ti giuro,  
> 129 s' elle non sien di lunga grazia vote,  

but here keep silent I cannot; and, Reader, I swear to thee, by the notes of this my Comedy—so may they not be void of lasting favour—

- *tacer nol posso* ‘keep silent about it I cannot’ → “keep silent I cannot”: the object pronoun *lo*, contracted with *non* in *nol*, is omitted; the inverted order is retained.
- *per le note di questa Commedìa* ‘by the notes of this Comedy’ → “by the notes of this my Comedy”: “my” is supplied, and the phrase naming what the oath is sworn by moves after the declaration of swearing.
- *lettor, ti giuro* ‘reader, to thee I swear’ → “Reader, I swear to thee”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *s'elle non sien di lunga grazia vote* ‘if they be not of long favour void’ → “so may they not be void of lasting favour”: the conditional form is rendered as an optative wish with “so may”; the complement follows “void” rather than preceding it, and *lunga* is expressed as “lasting”.

> 130 ch' io vidi per quell' aer grosso e scuro  
> 131 venir nuotando una figura in suso,  
> 132 meravigliosa ad ogni cor sicuro,  

that I saw, through that air gross and dark, come swimming upwards, a figure marvellous to every steadfast heart;

- *venir nuotando una figura in suso* ‘come swimming a figure upwards’ → “come swimming upwards, a figure”: the directional phrase moves before the subject of the infinitive.
- *ad ogni cor sicuro* ‘to every heart secure’ → “to every steadfast heart”: *sicuro* is interpreted as “steadfast”, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 133 sì come torna colui che va giuso  
> 134 talora a solver ancora, ch' aggrappa  
> 135 o scoglio od altro che nel mare è chiuso,  
> 136 che in su si stende, e da piè si rattrappa.  

like as he returns, who on a time goes down to loose the anchor, which grapples a rock or other thing that in the sea is hid, who spreads the arms and gathers up the feet.

- *sì come* ‘so as’ → “like as”: the comparative formula is expressed with “like” rather than the corresponding ‘so’.
- *torna colui* ‘returns that one’ → “he returns”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun and moves before the verb.
- *che va giuso talora* ‘who goes down at times’ → “who on a time goes down”: the adverbial expression moves before the verb; “on a time” presents an indefinite occasion rather than the recurrent ‘at times’.
- *ancora* ‘anchor’ → “the anchor”: the definite article is supplied.
- *o scoglio od altro* ‘either rock or other’ → “a rock or other thing”: the first member of the paired *o … od* is omitted, an indefinite article is supplied before “rock”, and the substantival *altro* is expanded into “other thing”.
- *chiuso* ‘closed’ → “hid”: the translation expresses concealment rather than enclosure.
- *in su si stende* ‘upwards stretches himself’ → “spreads the arms”: the reflexive construction becomes transitive, with “the arms” supplied as its object; the explicit upward direction is omitted.
- *da piè si rattrappa* ‘at the feet draws himself in’ → “gathers up the feet”: the reflexive construction becomes transitive, and the phrase specifying the bodily location becomes the direct object.

## Summary — Close Echoes and Prose Recasting

Carlyle’s translation remains close to the Italian in its sequence of expressions, imagery, and often its vocabulary, but regularly rearranges them into English prose order. Subjects and objects move before or after their verbs, adjectives generally precede their nouns, and separated phrases are brought together. Reporting clauses also move to less interrupted positions. Yet the translation sometimes preserves conspicuous inversions, as in “Of your city am I” and “keep silent I cannot”, while archaic pronouns and verbal forms sustain its distance from ordinary English.

Changes of construction often accompany these rearrangements. Pronominal verbs become transitive expressions: *si partiro … d’una torma*, ‘departed from a troop’, becomes “quitted a troop”. Impersonal obligation is recast in *a costor si vuole esser cortese*, ‘to these one ought to be courteous’, as “to these courtesy is due”; elsewhere, *convien che* becomes “must”. Conditional wishes become formulas with “so may”, and reflexive constructions may become passives or active verbs with explicit objects. Particularly marked is *saremmo appena uditi*, ‘we should scarcely be heard’, rendered as “we should scarce have heard each other”. Italian imperfects frequently become English simple pasts, though continuing action is sometimes conveyed through expressions such as “kept travelling”.

Vocabulary ranges from close equivalents to explanatory or interpretive substitutions. “gall”, “wheel”, and “painted skin” retain the Italian images, whereas *in croce*, ‘on the cross’, becomes “in torment”. *Voce*, ‘voice’, is interpreted as “fame”; *gente nuova*, ‘new people’, becomes the more disparaging “upstart people”; and *freghi*, ‘rubbest’, is generalized to “movest”. Some strikingly literal choices remain, including “loose place” and “vacant of that name”. The result preserves much of the source’s verbal texture even where individual choices narrow, broaden, or redirect its meaning.

Omissions and additions further adapt the phrasing. Interjections, repeated conjunctions or possessives, and elements of emphatic combinations may disappear: *sempre mai* becomes simply “always”, and *tosto che*, ‘as soon as’, becomes “when”. Conversely, possessives replace definite articles, as in “his face” and “my Teacher”, while explanatory wording supplies what Italian leaves implicit: *come al ver si guata*, ‘as one looks at the truth’, becomes “as men look when truth is told”. Alongside these individually noted departures, two routine grammatical adjustments operate throughout: English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and omits Italian definite articles before possessives. These ordinary accommodations, together with the more selective recastings, make the translation readable as English prose without consistently smoothing away the Italian’s distinctive turns.
