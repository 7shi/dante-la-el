# Inferno 5

## Lines 1–24 — Minos Judges the Souls

> 1 Così discesi del cerchio primaio  
> 2 giù nel secondo, che men loco cinghia,  
> 3 e tanto più dolor, che pugne a guaio.  

Thus I descended from the first circle down into the second, which encompasses less space, and so much greater pain, that it stings to wailing.

- *cerchio primaio* ‘circle first’ → “first circle”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *men loco cinghia* ‘less space encompasses’ → “encompasses less space”: the verb moves before its object.
- *più dolor* ‘more pain’ → “greater pain”: the comparative of quantity becomes an adjective qualifying the pain.

> 4 Stavvi Minos orribilmente, e ringhia ;  
> 5 esamina le colpe nell' entrata,  
> 6 giudica e manda, secondo che avvinghia.  

There Minos sits horrific, and grins: examines the crimes upon the entrance; judges, and sends according as he girds himself.

- *Stavvi Minos orribilmente* ‘Stands there Minos horribly’ → “There Minos sits horrific”: the locative attached to the verb becomes an opening adverb; the subject precedes the verb; “sits” specifies a posture; and the adverb becomes an adjective describing Minos.
- *ringhia* ‘growls’ → “grins”: the translation uses a word that can suggest baring the teeth rather than directly naming the growling sound.
- *le colpe* ‘the faults’ → “the crimes”: the English noun gives the faults a more explicitly judicial character.
- *nell' entrata* ‘in the entrance’ → “upon the entrance”: the preposition changes.
- *avvinghia* ‘he entwines’ → “he girds himself”: the translation supplies a reflexive object and specifies encircling himself.

> 7 Dico, che quando l' anima mal nata  
> 8 li vien dinanzi, tutta si confessa ;  

I say, that when the ill-born spirit comes before him, it confesses all;

- *l' anima mal nata* ‘the soul ill-born’ → “the ill-born spirit”: “spirit” replaces ‘soul’, and the modifier moves before the noun.
- *li vien dinanzi* ‘to him comes before’ → “comes before him”: the pronoun preceding the verb becomes the complement of the following preposition.
- *tutta si confessa* ‘wholly confesses itself’ → “it confesses all”: the reflexive construction becomes a transitive one with “all” as its object; *tutta*, grammatically agreeing with the soul, is rendered as the totality confessed.

> 9 e quel conoscitor delle peccata  
> 10 vede qual loco d' inferno è da essa;  
> 11 cignesi colla coda tante volte,  
> 12 quantunque gradi vuol che giù sia messa.  

and that sin-discerner sees what place in hell is for it, and with his tail makes as many circles round himself as the degrees he will have it to descend.

- *quel conoscitor delle peccata* ‘that knower of the sins’ → “that sin-discerner”: the noun and its dependent phrase become a compound; plural ‘sins’ becomes singular attributive “sin”, the definite article disappears, and knowing is expressed as discerning.
- *d' inferno* ‘of hell’ → “in hell”: the dependent phrase becomes explicitly locative.
- *è da essa* ‘is suited to it’ → “is for it”: the idiomatic expression of suitability is compressed into “for”.
- *cignesi colla coda tante volte* ‘girds himself with the tail as many times’ → “and with his tail makes as many circles round himself”: “and” is supplied; the instrumental phrase moves first; the definite article becomes a possessive; and girding oneself a number of times becomes making a corresponding number of circles around oneself.
- *quantunque gradi vuol che giù sia messa* ‘as many degrees as he wills that it be put down’ → “as the degrees he will have it to descend”: the numerical correlation is recast with “as the degrees”; the finite passive subjunctive becomes an active infinitive after “will have”; and being put down becomes descending.

> 13 Sempre dinanzi a lui ne stanno molte :  
> 14 vanno a vicenda ciascuna al giudizio ;  
> 15 dicono e odono, e poi son giù volte.  

Always before him stands a crowd of them; they go each in its turn to judgment; they tell, and hear; and then are whirled down.

- *Sempre dinanzi a lui ne stanno molte* ‘Always before him stand many of them’ → “Always before him stands a crowd of them”: the plural quantity becomes a singular collective noun, with corresponding singular agreement; the preverbal *ne* is expressed after that noun as “of them”.
- *a vicenda ciascuna* ‘in turn each’ → “each in its turn”: the distributive pronoun moves before the phrase, and the possessive “its” is supplied.
- *al giudizio* ‘to the judgment’ → “to judgment”: the definite article is omitted.
- *dicono* ‘they say’ → “they tell”: the verb is rendered with “tell” rather than ‘say’.
- *son giù volte* ‘are down turned’ → “are whirled down”: “whirled” makes the movement more forceful and specific, and “down” follows the participle.

> 16 "O tu, che vieni al doloroso ospizio,"  
> 17 disse Minos a me, quando mi vide,  
> 18 lasciando l' atto di cotanto ufizio,  

"O thou who comest to the abode of pain!" said Minos to me, when he saw me leaving the act of that great office;

- *doloroso ospizio* ‘painful lodging’ → “abode of pain”: the adjective becomes a following noun phrase, and ‘lodging’ is rendered as “abode”.
- *lasciando l' atto di cotanto ufizio* ‘leaving the act of so great an office’ → “leaving the act of that great office”: *cotanto* becomes demonstrative “that great” rather than ‘so great’. The English construction naturally attaches “leaving” to “me” as the person seen leaving, whereas the Italian participle refers to Minos, the subject of *disse* and *vide*.

> 19 "guarda com' entri, e di cui tu ti fide ;  
> 20 non t' inganni l' ampiezza dell' entrare."  

"look how thou enterest, and in whom thou trustest; let not the wideness of the entrancy deceive thee."

- *di cui tu ti fide* ‘of whom thou trustest thyself’ → “in whom thou trustest”: the Italian reflexive construction with *di* becomes a non-reflexive English verb with “in”.
- *non t' inganni l' ampiezza dell' entrare* ‘may not thee deceive the wideness of the entering’ → “let not the wideness of the entrancy deceive thee”: the prohibitive subjunctive becomes a construction with “let not”; the subject moves before “deceive” and the object after it; and the substantivized infinitive becomes the noun “entrancy”.

> 21 E il duca mio a lui : "Perchè pur gride?  

And my guide to him: "Why criest thou too?

- *il duca mio* ‘the leader my’ → “my guide”: ‘leader’ is rendered as “guide”, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *Perchè pur gride* ‘Why still criest thou’ → “Why criest thou too”: the translation takes *pur* additively, as “too”, rather than continuatively as ‘still’ or emphatically as ‘indeed’; the adverb moves to the end.

> 22 Non impedir lo suo fatale andare :  
> 23 vuolsi così colà, dove si puote  
> 24 ciò che si vuole, e più non dimandare."  

Hinder not his fated going; thus it is willed there where what is willed can be done: and ask no more."

- *Non impedir* ‘not to hinder’ → “Hinder not”: the infinitive used for an Italian negative command becomes an English imperative, with the negative following the verb.
- *vuolsi così colà* ‘it is willed thus there’ → “thus it is willed there”: “thus” moves before the verb.
- *dove si puote ciò che si vuole* ‘where one can what one wills’ → “where what is willed can be done”: the impersonal constructions become English passives; “done” supplies the action left implicit after *puote*; and the relative clause moves before “can be done” as its subject.
- *e più non dimandare* ‘and more not to ask’ → “and ask no more”: the negative-command infinitive becomes an imperative, and the preceding adverb and negative become the following phrase “no more”.

## Lines 25–57 — The Storm of the Carnal Sinners

> 25 Ora incomincian le dolenti note  
> 26 a farmisi sentire; or son venuto  
> 27 là dove molto pianto mi percuote.  

Now begin the doleful notes to reach me; now am I come where much lamenting strikes me.

- *a farmisi sentire* ‘to make themselves heard by me’ → “to reach me”: the causative and reflexive construction becomes a simple active infinitive; hearing is expressed as the sounds reaching the listener.
- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “where”: the separate indication of place, *là*, is omitted.
- *molto pianto* ‘much weeping’ → “much lamenting”: weeping is rendered more generally as lamenting.

> 28 Io venni in loco d'ogni luce muto,  
> 29 che mugghia, come fa mar per tempesta,  
> 30 se da contrari venti è combattuto.  

I came into a place void of all light, which bellows like the sea in tempest, when it is combated by warring winds.

- *in loco* ‘in place’ → “into a place”: the preposition explicitly expresses movement into the place, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *d'ogni luce muto* ‘of every light mute’ → “void of all light”: the adjective moves before its complement; the metaphorical ‘mute’ becomes “void”, and ‘every’ becomes “all”.
- *come fa mar* ‘as does sea’ → “like the sea”: a comparative clause becomes a prepositional phrase; *fa* is omitted, and the definite article is supplied.
- *per tempesta* ‘because of a tempest’ → “in tempest”: the cause of the sea’s bellowing becomes the circumstances in which it bellows.
- *se* ‘if’ → “when”: a conditional conjunction becomes a temporal one.
- *da contrari venti è combattuto* ‘by contrary winds is combated’ → “it is combated by warring winds”: the passive verb precedes its agent, and ‘contrary’ or ‘opposing’ becomes “warring”.

> 31 La bufera infernal, che mai non resta,  
> 32 mena gli spirti con la sua rapina;  
> 33 voltando e percotendo li molesta.  

The hellish storm, which never rests, leads the spirits with its sweep; whirling, and smiting it vexes them.

- *La bufera infernal* ‘the storm infernal’ → “The hellish storm”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *mai non resta* ‘not ever stops’ → “never rests”: the negative construction is condensed into “never”, and stopping is expressed as resting.
- *rapina* ‘rapine’ → “sweep”: violent seizure or carrying off is rendered as the storm’s sweeping movement.
- *voltando* ‘turning’ → “whirling”: the movement is made more specifically rotational and rapid.
- *li molesta* ‘them it troubles’ → “it vexes them”: the object pronoun follows the verb rather than preceding it.

> 34 Quando giungon davanti alla ruina,  
> 35 quivi le strida, il compianto e il lamento;  
> 36 bestemmian quivi la virtù divina.  

When they arrive before the ruin, there the shrieks, the moanings, and the lamentation; there they blaspheme the divine power.

- *il compianto* ‘the lamenting’ → “the moanings”: a singular abstract noun becomes a plural noun denoting individual sounds.
- *bestemmian quivi* ‘they blaspheme there’ → “there they blaspheme”: the adverb moves before the subject and verb.
- *la virtù divina* ‘the power divine’ → “the divine power”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 37 Intesi, che a così fatto tormento  
> 38 enno dannati i peccator carnali,  
> 39 che la ragion sommettono al talento.  

I learnt that to such torment [are] doomed the carnal sinners, who subject reason to lust.

- *Intesi* ‘I understood’ → “I learnt”: understanding is expressed as acquiring knowledge.
- *così fatto* ‘so fashioned’ → “such”: a participial expression is condensed into a determiner.
- *i peccator carnali* ‘the sinners carnal’ → “the carnal sinners”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *la ragion sommettono* ‘the reason they subject’ → “subject reason”: the object follows the verb, and its definite article is omitted.
- *al talento* ‘to the desire’ → “to lust”: the general term for desire or inclination is given a specifically sexual sense, and the definite article is omitted.

> 40 E come gli stornei ne portan l'ali,  
> 41 nel freddo tempo, a schiera larga e piena:  
> 42 così quel fiato gli spiriti mali;  
> 43 di qua, di là, di giù, di su gli mena.  

And as their wings bear along the starlings, at the cold season, in large and crowded troop: so that blast, the evil spirits; hither, thither, down, up, it leads them.

- *gli stornei ne portan l'ali* ‘the starlings—away bear the wings’ → “their wings bear along the starlings”: the Italian’s postposed subject, ‘the wings’, moves before the verb, and its preposed object, ‘the starlings’, moves after it; “their” replaces the definite article, and directional *ne* is expressed by “along”.
- *nel freddo tempo* ‘in the cold time’ → “at the cold season”: “at” replaces ‘in’, and the general ‘time’ becomes “season”.
- *a schiera larga e piena* ‘in troop broad and full’ → “in large and crowded troop”: the adjectives precede the noun; breadth becomes general size, and fullness becomes crowding.
- *quel fiato* ‘that breath’ → “that blast”: the movement of air is expressed more forcefully.
- *gli spiriti mali* ‘the spirits evil’ → “the evil spirits”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *di qua, di là, di giù, di su* ‘this way, that way, downwards, upwards’ → “hither, thither, down, up”: the Italian prepositional adverbial expressions become single English adverbs.
- *gli mena* ‘them it leads’ → “it leads them”: the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 44 Nulla speranza gli conforta mai,  
> 45 non che di posa, ma di minor pena.  

No hope ever comforts them, not of rest but even of less pain.

- *gli conforta mai* ‘them comforts ever’ → “ever comforts them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the adverb moves before it.
- *non che … ma* ‘not to speak of … but’ → “not” … “but even”: the first expression is shortened, and “even” is supplied in the second to convey the contrast between the greater and lesser hopes.

> 46 E come i gru van cantando lor lai,  
> 47 facendo in aer di sè lunga riga;  
> 48 così vid' io venir, traendo guai,  
> 49 ombre portate dalla detta briga;  

And as the cranes go chanting their lays, making a long streak of themselves in the air: so I saw the shadows come, uttering wails, borne by that strife of winds;

- *facendo in aer di sè lunga riga* ‘making in air of themselves long line’ → “making a long streak of themselves in the air”: the object moves immediately after the participle, followed by its complement and then the indication of place; the articles “a” and “the” are supplied, and ‘line’ becomes “streak”.
- *così vid' io venir … ombre* ‘so saw I come … shadows’ → “so I saw the shadows come”: the subject precedes the finite verb; ‘shadows’ moves before the infinitive and the intervening participial phrase, and receives a definite article.
- *traendo guai* ‘drawing out laments’ → “uttering wails”: the Italian idiom is replaced by a direct expression of vocal utterance.
- *dalla detta briga* ‘by the said strife’ → “by that strife of winds”: ‘the said’ becomes the demonstrative “that”, and “of winds” is supplied.

> 50 per ch'io dissi: "Maestro, chi son quelle  
> 51 genti, che l'aura nera sì gastiga?"  

whereat I said: “Master who are those people, whom the black air thus lashes?”

- *per ch'* ‘for which’ → “whereat”: the connective is rendered as a relative adverb expressing the occasion for the speech.
- *l'aura nera* ‘the air black’ → “the black air”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *sì gastiga* ‘so chastises’ → “thus lashes”: the general act of punishment becomes the more specific physical action of lashing.

> 52 "La prima di color, di cui novelle  
> 53 tu vuoi saper," mi disse quegli allotta,  
> 54 "fu imperatrice di molte favelle.  

“The first of these concerning whom thou seekest to know,” he then replied, “was Empress of many tongues.

- *di color* ‘of those’ → “of these”: the demonstrative changes from more distant to nearer reference.
- *di cui novelle* ‘of whom news’ → “concerning whom”: the object *novelle*, ‘news’ or ‘reports’, is omitted, leaving the people themselves as the subject of inquiry.
- *tu vuoi saper* ‘thou wishest to know’ → “thou seekest to know”: wishing is expressed as seeking.
- *mi disse quegli allotta* ‘to me said that one then’ → “he then replied”: the indirect object is omitted, the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun, and the subject and adverb move before the verb; ‘said’ becomes “replied”.

> 55 A vizio di lussuria fu sì rotta,  
> 56 che libito fe' licito in sua legge  
> 57 per torre il biasmo, in che era condotta.  

With the vice of luxury she was so broken, that she made lust and law alike in her decree, to take away the blame she had incurred.

- *A vizio di lussuria* ‘to vice of lust’ → “With the vice of luxury”: “With” replaces the preposition marking surrender or subjection to the vice, and a definite article is supplied; “luxury” is used in its older sense of lust or lasciviousness.
- *che libito fe' licito* ‘that desire she made lawful’ → “that she made lust and law alike”: the object moves after the verb; ‘desire’ becomes specifically “lust”, while the predicate adjective ‘lawful’ becomes the coordinated noun “law” with the supplied predicate “alike”.
- *in sua legge* ‘in her law’ → “in her decree”: the broader term ‘law’ becomes the more specific “decree”.
- *in che era condotta* ‘into which she was brought’ → “she had incurred”: the prepositional relative construction disappears, and the passive imperfect becomes an active pluperfect; blame is now something she incurred rather than a condition into which she was brought.

## Lines 58–90 — The Lovers and Dante’s Appeal

> 58 Ell’ è Semiramis, di cui si legge,  
> 59 che succedette a Nino, e fu sua sposa;  

She is Semiramis, of whom we read that she succeeded Ninus, and was his spouse;

- *si legge* ‘one reads’ → “we read”: the impersonal construction becomes a verb with a first-person plural subject.
- *succedette a Nino* ‘succeeded to Ninus’ → “succeeded Ninus”: the Italian prepositional complement becomes a direct object.

> 60 tenne la terra, che il Soldan corregge.  

she held the land which the Soldan rules.

- Literal.

> 61 L’ altra è colei, che s’ ancise amorosa,  
> 62 e ruppe fede al cener di Sicheo;  

That other is she who slew herself in love, and broke faith to the ashes of Sichæus;

- *L’altra* ‘the other’ → “That other”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative.
- *s’ ancise amorosa* ‘herself slew, loving’ → “slew herself in love”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb, and the adjective describing the subject becomes the prepositional phrase “in love”.
- *al cener* ‘to the ash’ → “to the ashes”: the singular noun becomes plural.

> 63 poi è Cleopatras lussuriosa.  

next comes luxurious Cleopatra.

- *poi è* ‘then is’ → “next comes”: the temporal adverb marks the next item, and the copula becomes a verb of movement.
- *Cleopatras lussuriosa* ‘Cleopatra lustful’ → “luxurious Cleopatra”: the adjective precedes the name; “luxurious” is used in its older sense of lustful.

> 64 Elena vedi, per cui tanto reo  
> 65 tempo si volse; e vedi il grande Achille,  
> 66 che con amore al fine combatteo;  

Helena [see], for whom so long a time of ill revolved; and [see] the great Achilles, who fought at last with love;

- *tanto reo tempo* ‘so much evil time’ → “so long a time of ill”: quantity becomes explicit duration, while the adjective *reo* becomes the noun phrase “of ill” after “time”.
- *si volse* ‘turned itself’ → “revolved”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes a simple English intransitive.
- *con amore al fine combatteo* ‘with love at the end fought’ → “fought at last with love”: the verb moves before both modifying phrases, whose relative order is reversed; “at last” renders *al fine*.

> 67 vedi Paris, Tristano”; e più di mille  
> 68 ombre mostrommi, e nominommi a dito,  
> 69 ch’ amor di nostra vita dipartille.  

[see] Paris, Tristan”; and more than a thousand shades he shewed to me, and *pointing* with his finger, named [to me those] whom love had parted from our life.

- *nominommi a dito* ‘named to me by finger’ → “*pointing* with his finger, named [to me those]”: the phrase *a dito* becomes an explicit participial construction placed before the verb, with “his” supplied. The translation also supplies “those” as the antecedent of the following relative clause, rather than leaving that clause attached directly to the previously mentioned shades.
- *ch’ amor di nostra vita dipartille* ‘whom love from our life parted them’ → “whom love had parted from our life”: the verb precedes the prepositional phrase, the simple past becomes a pluperfect, and the resumptive object pronoun *-le*, repeating the relative object, is omitted.

> 70 Poscia ch’ io ebbi il mio dottore udito  
> 71 nomar le donne antiche e i cavalieri,  
> 72 pietà mi giunse, e fui quasi smarrito.  

After I had heard my teacher name the olden dames and cavaliers, pity [came over] me, and I was as if bewildered.

- *io ebbi il mio dottore udito nomar* ‘I had my teacher heard name’ → “I had heard my teacher name”: the object moves out from between the auxiliary and participle; the Italian past anterior is represented by the English past perfect.
- *le donne antiche e i cavalieri* ‘the women ancient and the knights’ → “the olden dames and cavaliers”: the adjective precedes its noun, “dames” and “cavaliers” give the nouns an archaizing register, and the second definite article is omitted.
- *pietà mi giunse* ‘pity reached me’ → “pity [came over] me”: a simple verb becomes a phrasal expression, and the object pronoun follows it.
- *fui quasi smarrito* ‘I was almost lost’ → “I was as if bewildered”: *quasi* is interpreted comparatively, as ‘as though’, rather than as a degree approaching a condition; *smarrito* is rendered in its psychological sense.

> 73 Io cominciai: “Poeta, volentieri  
> 74 parlerei a que’ duo, che insieme vanno,  
> 75 e paion sì al vento esser leggieri.”  

I began : “Poet, willingly would I speak with those two that go together, and seem so light upon the wind.”

- *volentieri parlerei a que’ duo* ‘willingly I would speak to those two’ → “willingly would I speak with those two”: the English auxiliary precedes the subject, and “with” replaces ‘to’.
- *che insieme vanno* ‘that together go’ → “that go together”: the adverb follows the verb.
- *e paion sì al vento esser leggieri* ‘and seem so to the wind to be light’ → “and seem so light upon the wind”: the infinitive *esser* is omitted, the adjective follows “so” directly, and the wind phrase moves to the end, with *al* rendered as “upon the”.

> 76 Ed egli a me: “Vedrai, quando saranno  
> 77 più presso a noi; e tu allor li prega  
> 78 per quell’ amor che i mena; e quei verranno.”  

And he to me: “Thou shalt see when they are nearer to us; and do thou then entreat them by that love, which leads them; and they will come.”

- *e tu allor li prega* ‘and thou then them entreat’ → “and do thou then entreat them”: “do” is supplied to form the English imperative, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *per quell’ amor* ‘for that love’ → “by that love”: “by” makes the phrase an invocation of the love as grounds for the entreaty.
- *che i mena* ‘which them leads’ → “which leads them”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *e quei verranno* ‘and those will come’ → “and they will come”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.

> 79 Sì tosto come il vento a noi li piega,  
> 80 mossi la voce: “O anime affannate,  
> 81 venite a noi parlar, s’ altri nol niega.”  

Soon as the wind bends them to us, I raise[d] my voice: “O wearied souls! come to speak with us, if none denies it.”

- *Sì tosto come* ‘so soon as’ → “Soon as”: the initial degree word is omitted.
- *a noi li piega* ‘to us them bends’ → “bends them to us”: the verb precedes both complements, and their order is reversed.
- *mossi la voce* ‘I moved the voice’ → “I raise[d] my voice”: the verbal expression becomes the English idiom for beginning to speak, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *O anime affannate* ‘O souls distressed’ → “O wearied souls!”: the adjective precedes the noun, and “wearied” emphasizes exhaustion rather than distress.
- *venite a noi parlar* ‘come to us to speak’ → “come to speak with us”: the phrase referring to the speakers moves after “speak” and becomes its complement, with “with” replacing ‘to’.
- *s’ altri nol niega* ‘if someone else does not deny it’ → “if none denies it”: the indefinite subject and verbal negation become a negative subject with an affirmative verb; ‘else’ is not separately represented, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 82 Quali colombe, dal disio chiamate,  
> 83 con l’ ali alzate e ferme al dolce nido  
> 84 vengon per l’ aer dal voler portate:  

As doves called by desire, with [raised] and steady wings [come] through the air to their loved nest, borne by their will:

- *dal disio chiamate* ‘by the desire called’ → “called by desire”: the participle precedes its complement, and the definite article is omitted.
- *con l’ ali alzate e ferme* ‘with the wings raised and steady’ → “with [raised] and steady wings”: the modifiers precede the noun, and the definite article is omitted.
- *al dolce nido* ‘to the sweet nest’ → “to their loved nest”: “loved” replaces ‘sweet’, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves after the verb and the phrase describing passage through the air.
- *dal voler portate* ‘by the will borne’ → “borne by their will”: the participle precedes its complement, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 85 cotali uscir della schiera ov’ è Dido,  
> 86 a noi venendo per l’ aer maligno,  
> 87 sì forte fu l’ affettuoso grido.  

so those spirits issued from the band where Dido is, coming to us through the malignant air; such was the force of my affectuous cry.

- *cotali uscir* ‘such came out’ → “so those spirits issued”: the pronominal *cotali*, answering the earlier comparison, becomes the adverb “so” with the supplied subject “those spirits”.
- *ov’ è Dido* ‘where is Dido’ → “where Dido is”: the subject precedes the verb.
- *a noi venendo* ‘to us coming’ → “coming to us”: the participle precedes its complement.
- *per l’ aer maligno* ‘through the air malignant’ → “through the malignant air”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *sì forte fu l’ affettuoso grido* ‘so strong was the affectionate cry’ → “such was the force of my affectuous cry”: the predicative adjective *forte* becomes the noun “force”, making the cry’s force, rather than the cry itself, the grammatical subject. “Such” replaces the degree expression, and the definite article before the cry becomes “my”.

> 88 “O animal grazioso e benigno,  
> 89 che visitando vai per l’ aer perso  
> 90 noi che tignemmo il mondo di sanguigno;  

“O living creature, gracious and benign! that goest through the black air, visiting us who stained the earth with blood;

- *animal* ‘animate being’ → “living creature”: the single Italian noun is expanded into an adjective and noun.
- *che visitando vai per l’ aer perso noi* ‘that visiting goest through the dark-blue air us’ → “that goest through the black air, visiting us”: the participle moves after the verb and the intervening prepositional phrase, bringing it beside its object. The Italian construction joining *andare* with a gerund becomes a movement verb followed by a supplementary participial phrase; *perso*, a very dark blue, becomes “black”, and the adjective precedes “air”.
- *che tignemmo il mondo di sanguigno* ‘who dyed the world blood-red’ → “who stained the earth with blood”: “stained” replaces ‘dyed’, “earth” replaces ‘world’, and the substantivized colour adjective *sanguigno* becomes the material “blood”, introduced by “with”.

## Lines 91–123 — Love, Death, and Remembered Happiness

> 91 se fosse amico il re dell' universo,  
> 92 noi pregheremmo lui per la tua pace,  
> 93 poi che hai pietà del nostro mal perverso.  

If the King of the Universe were our friend, we would pray him for thy peace; seeing that thou hast pity of our perverse misfortune.

- *fosse amico il re dell' universo* ‘were friend the King of the Universe’ → “the King of the Universe were our friend”: the subject moves before the verb, and “our” is supplied.
- *poi che* ‘since’ → “seeing that”: the causal conjunction becomes an explanatory phrase.
- *mal perverso* ‘evil perverse’ → “perverse misfortune”: *mal* is rendered as misfortune rather than evil, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 94 Di quel che udire e che parlar ti piace  
> 95 noi udiremo e parleremo a vui,  
> 96 mentrechè il vento, come fa, ci tace.  

Of that which it pleases thee to hear and to speak, we will hear and speak with you, whilst the wind, as now, is silent [for us].

- *quel che udire e che parlar ti piace* ‘that which to hear and which to speak pleases thee’ → “that which it pleases thee to hear and to speak”: anticipatory “it” is supplied, the infinitives move after “pleases thee,” and the repeated relative *che* is omitted.
- *a vui* ‘to you’ → “with you”: the preposition presents the exchange as conversation with the addressee.
- *come fa* ‘as it does’ → “as now”: the verb referring back to the wind’s silence is replaced by a temporal adverb.

> 97 Siede la terra, dove nata fui,  
> 98 su la marina dove il Po discende  
> 99 per aver pace co' seguaci sui.  

The town, where I was born, sits on the shore, where Po descends to rest with his attendant *streams*.

- *Siede la terra, dove nata fui* ‘Sits the town, where born I was’ → “The town, where I was born, sits”: the subject and its relative clause move before “sits”; within the relative clause, “born” moves after “I was.”
- *il Po* ‘the Po’ → “Po”: the article before the river’s name is omitted.
- *per aver pace* ‘to have peace’ → “to rest”: the verb-and-noun expression becomes a single verb.
- *co' seguaci sui* ‘with the followers his’ → “with his attendant *streams*”: the possessive moves before the noun; *seguaci*, a noun, becomes the adjective “attendant,” and “streams” is supplied to identify the followers.

> 100 Amor, che al cor gentil ratto s' apprende,  
> 101 prese costui della bella persona  
> 102 che mi fu tolta, e il modo ancor m' offende.  

Love, which is quickly caught in gentle heart, took him with the fair body of which I was bereft; and the manner still afflicts me.

- *al cor gentil ratto s' apprende* ‘to the heart gentle quickly attaches itself’ → “is quickly caught in gentle heart”: the reflexive construction becomes passive; attachment to the heart becomes being caught in it; the article is omitted, and the adjective moves before “heart.”
- *costui* ‘this man’ → “him”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *della bella persona* ‘of the fair body’ → “with the fair body”: “with” replaces *di* in expressing what occasioned love’s taking hold.
- *che mi fu tolta* ‘which was taken from me’ → “of which I was bereft”: the person deprived becomes the grammatical subject instead of the body taken away, and the relative pronoun becomes the complement of “of.”
- *m' offende* ‘me wounds’ → “afflicts me”: physical wounding becomes affliction, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 103 Amor, che a nullo amato amar perdona,  
> 104 mi prese del costui piacer sì forte,  
> 105 che, come vedi, ancor non m' abbandona.  

Love, which to no loved one permits excuse for loving, took me so strongly with delight in him, that, as thou seest, even now it leaves me not.

- *a nullo amato amar perdona* ‘to no loved one remits loving’ → “to no loved one permits excuse for loving”: *perdona* is expanded into “permits excuse for,” expressing exemption from the obligation to love; the Italian infinitive becomes an English gerund.
- *mi prese* ‘me took’ → “took me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *del costui piacer sì forte* ‘of this man’s charm so strongly’ → “so strongly with delight in him”: the adverbial phrase moves first, *di* becomes “with,” and the man’s attractiveness is expressed instead as delight felt in him; the demonstrative *costui* becomes “him.”
- *ancor* ‘still’ → “even now”: the single adverb becomes an emphatic temporal phrase.
- *non m' abbandona* ‘not me abandons’ → “it leaves me not”: “leaves” replaces ‘abandons,’ and both the object and the negative move after the verb.

> 106 Amor condusse noi ad una morte;  
> 107 Caina attende chi vita ci spense."  

Love led us to one death; Caïna waits for him who quenched our life."

- *attende* ‘awaits’ → “waits for”: a directly transitive verb becomes a verb followed by a preposition.
- *chi* ‘who’ → “him who”: the fused relative is expanded into an antecedent and a relative pronoun.
- *vita ci spense* ‘life for us quenched’ → “quenched our life”: the object moves after the verb, and the dative *ci* is expressed as the possessive “our.”

> 108 Queste parole da lor ci fur porte.  

These words from them were offered to us.

- *da lor ci fur porte* ‘by them to us were offered’ → “from them were offered to us”: the passive agent is expressed as a source with “from,” and the recipient moves after the verb.

> 109 Da che io intesi quelle anime offense,  
> 110 chinai il viso, e tanto il tenni basso,  
> 111 finchè il poeta mi disse: "Che pense?"  

After I had heard those wounded souls, I bowed my face, and held it low until the Poet said to me: "What art thou thinking of?"

- *Da che* ‘From when’ → “After”: the temporal connection is expressed as succession.
- *io intesi* ‘I heard’ → “I had heard”: the simple past becomes past perfect.
- *anime offense* ‘souls wounded’ → “wounded souls”: the participial adjective moves before the noun.
- *chinai il viso* ‘I lowered the face’ → “I bowed my face”: “bowed” replaces ‘lowered,’ and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *e tanto il tenni basso* ‘and so long it I held low’ → “and held it low”: *tanto*, expressing duration, is not separately rendered, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the recipient moves after the verb.
- *Che pense* ‘What thinkest thou?’ → “What art thou thinking of?”: the simple present becomes progressive, and “of” is supplied.

> 112 Quando risposi, cominciai : "O lasso,  
> 113 quanti dolci pensier, quanto disio  
> 114 menò costoro al doloroso passo!"  

When I answered, I began: "Ah me! what sweet thoughts, what longing led them to the woful pass!"

- *O lasso* ‘O wretched me’ → “Ah me!”: the adjective is omitted, leaving an interjection and pronoun to express the lament.
- *quanti* ‘how many’ → “what”: the exclamation no longer explicitly expresses number.
- *quanto* ‘how much’ → “what”: the exclamation no longer explicitly expresses amount or degree.
- *costoro* ‘these people’ → “them”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *doloroso passo* ‘painful step’ → “woful pass”: the phrase is recast idiomatically, with “pass” suggesting a predicament rather than a step.

> 115 Poi mi rivolsi a loro, e parlai io,  
> 116 e cominciai : "Francesca, i tuoi martiri  
> 117 a lagrimar mi fanno tristo e pio.  

Then I turned again to them; and I spoke, and began: "Francesca, thy torments make me weep with grief and pity.

- *mi rivolsi* ‘I turned myself’ → “I turned again”: the reflexive pronoun is not separately represented, and “again” makes repetition explicit.
- *parlai io* ‘spoke I’ → “I spoke”: the expressed subject moves before the verb.
- *a lagrimar mi fanno tristo e pio* ‘to weep me make sad and compassionate’ → “make me weep with grief and pity”: the infinitive moves after the causative verb and its object; the adjectives describing the speaker become abstract nouns introduced by “with.”

> 118 Ma dimmi : al tempo de' dolci sospiri,  
> 119 a che e come concedette amore,  
> 120 che conosceste i dubbiosi desiri?"  

But tell me: in the time of the sweet sighs, by what and how love granted you to know the dubious desires?"

- *al tempo* ‘at the time’ → “in the time”: the temporal preposition changes.
- *a che* ‘at what’ → “by what”: the preposition makes the question explicitly concern the means.
- *concedette amore, che conosceste* ‘granted love that you should know’ → “love granted you to know”: the subject moves before the verb, and the finite subordinate clause becomes an infinitive construction, with “you” placed directly after “granted.”

> 121 Ed ella a me : "Nessun maggior dolore,  
> 122 che ricordarsi del tempo felice  
> 123 nella miseria : e ciò sa il tuo dottore.  

And she to me: "*There is no greater pain than* *to recall a happy time in wretchedness*; and this thy teacher knows.

- *Nessun maggior dolore* ‘No greater pain’ → “There is no greater pain”: “There is” supplies the verb and introductory subject absent from the Italian.
- *ricordarsi del tempo felice* ‘to remember the time happy’ → “to recall a happy time”: the pronominal construction *ricordarsi di* becomes the directly transitive “recall”; the definite article becomes indefinite, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *ciò sa il tuo dottore* ‘this knows thy teacher’ → “this thy teacher knows”: the subject moves before the verb, while the object remains first.

## Lines 124–142 — The Kiss and the Swoon

> 124 Ma se a conoscer la prima radice  
> 125 del nostro amor tu hai cotanto affetto,  
> 126 farò come colui che piange e dice.  

But if thou hast such desire to learn the first root of our love, I will do like one who weeps and tells.

- *a conoscer la prima radice del nostro amor* ‘to know the first root of our love’ → “to learn the first root of our love”: knowing becomes learning; the infinitival phrase moves from before the subject and verb to after “desire”.
- *cotanto affetto* ‘so much desire’ → “such desire”: an expression of degree becomes a construction with “such”.
- *colui* ‘that one’ → “one”: the demonstrative becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *dice* ‘speaks’ → “tells”: speaking is rendered as telling.

> 127 Noi leggevamo un giorno per diletto  
> 128 di Lancillotto, come amor lo strinse;  
> 129 soli eravamo e senza alcun sospetto.  

One day, for pastime, we read of Lancelot, how love constrained him; we were alone, and without all suspicion.

- *Noi leggevamo* ‘we were reading’ → “we read”: the Italian imperfect becomes an English simple past, leaving the continuing action less explicit.
- *un giorno per diletto* ‘one day for pleasure’ → “One day, for pastime”: both adverbial phrases move before the subject and verb; pleasure is rendered as pastime.
- *come amor lo strinse* ‘how love him gripped’ → “how love constrained him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb; the image of gripping becomes constraint.
- *soli eravamo* ‘alone we were’ → “we were alone”: the predicate adjective moves after the verb.
- *senza alcun sospetto* ‘without any suspicion’ → “without all suspicion”: “all” replaces *alcun*, ‘any’, in this negative expression.

> 130 Per più fiate gli occhi ci sospinse  
> 131 quella lettura, e scolorocci il viso;  
> 132 ma solo un punto fu quel che ci vinse.  

Several times that reading urged our eyes to meet, and changed the colour of our faces; but one moment alone it was that overcame us.

- *Per più fiate* ‘for several times’ → “Several times”: the preposition is not separately represented; English uses a temporal noun phrase without one.
- *gli occhi ci sospinse quella lettura* ‘the eyes for us impelled that reading’ → “that reading urged our eyes to meet”: the subject moves before the verb and the object after it; the possessive dative *ci* becomes “our”. “to meet” is supplied, specifying the direction of the eyes’ movement.
- *scolorocci il viso* ‘made pale for us the face’ → “changed the colour of our faces”: the verb becomes an explanatory phrase, and the specific loss of colour becomes a general change of colour. The possessive dative becomes “our”, and the singular face becomes plural “faces”.
- *solo un punto* ‘only one point’ → “one moment alone”: *punto* is rendered explicitly as a moment of time; *solo* moves after the noun phrase and becomes “alone”.
- *fu quel che ci vinse* ‘was that which us overcame’ → “it was that overcame us”: the demonstrative-relative construction becomes a cleft construction with supplied “it”; the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 133 Quando leggemmo il disiato riso  
> 134 esser baciato da cotanto amante,  
> 135 questi, che mai da me non fia diviso,  
> 136 la bocca mi baciò tutto tremante:  

When we read how the fond smile was kissed by such a lover, he, who shall never be divided from me, kissed my mouth all trembling:

- *il disiato riso esser baciato* ‘the desired smile to be kissed’ → “how the fond smile was kissed”: the infinitival construction becomes a finite clause introduced by supplied “how”. “fond” describes affection rather than the smile’s being desired.
- *cotanto amante* ‘so great a lover’ → “such a lover”: the explicit expression of degree becomes a construction with “such”.
- *questi* ‘this one’ → “he”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *che mai da me non fia diviso* ‘who never from me not shall be divided’ → “who shall never be divided from me”: “never” moves after the auxiliary, and “from me” moves after the participle. Italian negative concord, *mai … non*, is represented by the single English negative “never”.
- *la bocca mi baciò* ‘the mouth to me he kissed’ → “kissed my mouth”: the object moves after the verb, and the possessive dative *mi* becomes “my”.

> 137 Galeotto fu il libro, e chi lo scrisse;  

the book, and he who wrote it, was a Galeotto;

- *Galeotto fu il libro, e chi lo scrisse* ‘Galeotto was the book, and whoever it wrote’ → “the book, and he who wrote it, was a Galeotto”: the compound subject moves before the verb and the predicate name after it. The supplied article “a” makes the name denote a type or role. The fused relative *chi* becomes “he who”, and the object pronoun follows “wrote”.

> 138 quel giorno più non vi leggemmo avante."  

that day we read in it no farther."

- *più non vi leggemmo avante* ‘no more there we read onward’ → “we read in it no farther”: the locative *vi* is made explicit as “in it” and moves after the verb. *Più non … avante* is compressed into “no farther”, also placed after the verb.

> 139 Mentre che l' uno spirto questo disse,  
> 140 l' altro piangeva sì, che di pietade  
> 141 io venni men così com' io morisse;  

Whilst the one spirit thus spake, the other wept so, that I fainted with pity, as if I had been dying;

- *Mentre che* ‘while that’ → “Whilst”: the two-word Italian conjunction is represented by a single English conjunction.
- *questo disse* ‘this said’ → “thus spake”: the demonstrative object becomes an adverb of manner, and the verb is consequently used intransitively.
- *l'altro piangeva* ‘the other was weeping’ → “the other wept”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, leaving the continuing action less explicit.
- *di pietade* ‘from pity’ → “with pity”: the preposition changes, and the phrase moves from before the subject and verb to after the verb.
- *io venni men* ‘I came less’ → “I fainted”: the Italian idiom is replaced by a single English verb.
- *così com' io morisse* ‘just as if I were dying’ → “as if I had been dying”: *così* is not separately represented; the imperfect subjunctive becomes an English past perfect progressive.

> 142 e caddi, come corpo morto cade.  

and fell, as a dead body falls.

- *come corpo morto* ‘as body dead’ → “as a dead body”: the indefinite article is supplied, and the adjective moves before the noun.

## Summary — Close Correspondence and English Recasting

The translation stays close to the Italian’s sequence of statements, repetitions, and imagery, but frequently rearranges words into English order. Subjects move before verbs, objects and object pronouns after them, and adjectives before nouns: *gli spiriti mali* becomes “the evil spirits”. Longer phrases are also repositioned to bring related words together. Yet the English preserves some marked Italian ordering, as in “Always before him stands a crowd of them”, and often retains the original’s elliptical constructions rather than supplying a complete sentence.

Changes of construction range from ordinary grammatical adjustments to substantial recasting. Reflexive and pronominal expressions become transitive verbs, passives, or simpler idioms: *tutta si confessa*, ‘wholly confesses itself’, becomes “it confesses all”, while *a farmisi sentire*, ‘to make themselves heard by me’, becomes “to reach me”. Conversely, *che mi fu tolta*, ‘which was taken from me’, becomes “of which I was bereft”, making the deprived person rather than the lost body the subject. Adjectives sometimes become noun phrases, as *tristo e pio* becomes “with grief and pity”. Italian past tenses are also redistributed among English simple past, past perfect, and progressive forms, without always preserving their distinctions of duration or completion.

Word choice sometimes sharpens an action or narrows its meaning: *volte*, ‘turned’, becomes “whirled”, and *talento*, ‘desire’, becomes “lust”. Elsewhere it weakens or changes an image: *muto*, ‘mute’, becomes “void”, and *scolorocci*, ‘made us pale’, is expanded into the less specific “changed the colour of our faces”. Some apparent departures belong to the English vocabulary’s historical register: “luxury” and “luxurious” carry their older associations with lust. Other choices shift the sense more noticeably, notably *ringhia*, ‘growls’, rendered as “grins”, and *disiato*, ‘desired’, as “fond”. The placement of “leaving” after “he saw me” also creates an attachment that differs from the Italian’s reference to Minos.

Words are supplied to clarify relationships or complete English expressions: “of winds” identifies the *briga*, “to meet” specifies the movement of the lovers’ eyes, and “There is” completes *Nessun maggior dolore*. Conversely, *novelle*, ‘news’, is left unrepresented, as are some separate indications of place, degree, or duration. More generally, the English routinely supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and omits Italian definite articles before possessives, as *il mio dottore* becomes “my teacher”. These regular adjustments coexist with considerable verbal closeness: “Love led us to one death” follows *Amor condusse noi ad una morte* almost word for word. The result is a translation whose structure remains visibly indebted to the Italian, even where English grammar, explanatory additions, or individual lexical choices reshape its expression.
