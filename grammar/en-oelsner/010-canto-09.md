# Inferno 9

## Lines 1–21 — Virgil’s Hesitation and Dante’s Fear

> 1 Quel color che viltà di fuor mi pinse,  
> 2 veggendo il duca mio tornare in volta,  
> 3 più tosto dentro il suo nuovo ristrinse.  

That colour which cowardice painted on my face, when I saw my Guide turn back, repressed in him more quickly his new colour.

- *di fuor mi pinse* ‘on the outside painted me’ → “painted on my face”: the verb comes first, and the general indication of outward appearance becomes a specific reference to the face.
- *veggendo* ‘seeing’ → “when I saw”: the gerund becomes a finite temporal clause.
- *il duca mio* ‘the leader my’ → “my Guide”: *duca* is rendered as “Guide,” and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *tornare in volta* ‘turn about’ → “turn back”: the Italian expression is rendered as a backward movement.
- *più tosto dentro il suo nuovo ristrinse* ‘more quickly within his new [colour] drew back’ → “repressed in him more quickly his new colour”: the final verb moves before its modifiers and object; “repressed” renders the drawing back inward; “in him” makes the reference of *dentro* explicit; and “colour,” understood after *nuovo*, is supplied.

> 4 Attento si fermò, com’ uom che ascolta;  
> 5 chè l’ occhio nol potea menare a lunga  
> 6 per l’ aer nero, e per la nebbia folta.  

He stopped attentive, like one who listens: for his eye could not lead him far, through the black air and the dense fog.

- *Attento si fermò* ‘attentive he stopped’ → “He stopped attentive”: the adjective moves after the verb, and the Italian pronominal verb becomes a simple English intransitive.
- *uom* ‘a man’ → “one”: the noun becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *l’occhio* ‘the eye’ → “his eye”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *nol potea menare* ‘not him could lead’ → “could not lead him”: the negative follows the modal verb, and the object pronoun follows the infinitive.
- *a lunga* ‘to a distance’ → “far”: the prepositional expression becomes an adverb.
- *per l’aer nero* ‘through the air black’ → “through the black air”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *e per la nebbia folta* ‘and through the fog thick’ → “and the dense fog”: the repeated preposition is omitted, and the adjective moves before the noun, with “dense” rendering *folta*.

> 7 “Pure a noi converrà vincer la punga,”  
> 8 cominciò ei; “se non . . . tal ne s’ offerse.  
> 9 Oh quanto tarda a me ch’ altri qui giunga! ”  

“Yet it behoves us to gain this battle,” he began; “if not . . . such help was offered to us. Oh! how long to me it seems till some one come!”

- *a noi converrà* ‘to us it will be necessary’ → “it behoves us”: the future becomes a present, and the initial prepositional phrase becomes the object of “behoves.”
- *vincer la punga* ‘to win the battle’ → “to gain this battle”: “gain” replaces ‘win,’ and the definite article becomes a demonstrative.
- *cominciò ei* ‘began he’ → “he began”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *tal ne s’offerse* ‘such a one offered himself to us’ → “such help was offered to us”: the personal expression *tal* is interpreted as “such help,” and the active reflexive construction becomes passive.
- *quanto tarda a me* ‘how slow it is for me’ → “how long to me it seems”: delay is expressed as an impression of elapsed time, with “seems” supplied and the verb moved after “to me.”
- *ch’altri qui giunga* ‘that someone should arrive here’ → “till some one come”: the clause becomes explicitly temporal through “till,” “come” renders ‘arrive,’ and *qui* is omitted.

> 10 Io vidi ben, sì com’ ei ricoperse  
> 11 lo cominciar con l’ altro, che poi venne,  
> 12 che fur parole alle prime diverse.  

I saw well how he covered the beginning with the other that came after, which were words differing from the first.

- *sì com’* ‘just how’ → “how”: the reinforcing *sì* is not separately represented.
- *lo cominciar* ‘the commencing’ → “the beginning”: the substantival infinitive becomes an ordinary noun.
- *che poi venne* ‘that afterwards came’ → “that came after”: the temporal adverb moves after the verb.
- *parole alle prime diverse* ‘words to the first different’ → “words differing from the first”: the adjective becomes a participle; its complement follows rather than precedes it; and Italian *a* is rendered by the “from” required with “differing.”

> 13 Ma nondimen paura il suo dir dienne,  
> 14 perch’ io traeva la parola tronca  
> 15 forse a peggior sentenzia, ch’ ei non tenne.  

But not the less his language gave me fear: for perhaps I drew his broken speech to a worse meaning than he held.

- *paura il suo dir dienne* ‘fear his speaking gave us’ → “his language gave me fear”: the initial object moves after the verb; the substantival infinitive *dir* becomes “language”; and the plural pronoun *ne* is rendered as singular “me.”
- *perch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *la parola tronca* ‘the word cut off’ → “his broken speech”: the definite article becomes a possessive, “speech” replaces ‘word,’ and the description follows English adjective order before the noun.
- *io traeva … forse* ‘I drew … perhaps’ → “perhaps I drew”: the adverb moves from after the object to before the subject and verb.
- *a peggior sentenzia* ‘to worse meaning’ → “to a worse meaning”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *ch’ei non tenne* ‘than he did not hold’ → “than he held”: the Italian comparative construction’s expletive *non* is omitted; it does not negate the verb here.

> 16 “In questo fondo della trista conca  
> 17 discende mai alcun del primo grado,  
> 18 che sol per pena ha la speranza cionca? ”  

“Into this bottom of the dreary shell, does any ever descend from the first degree, whose only punishment is hope cut off?”

- *In questo fondo* ‘in this bottom’ → “Into this bottom”: English explicitly marks motion towards the destination.
- *della trista conca* ‘of the sad basin’ → “of the dreary shell”: “dreary” replaces ‘sad,’ and *conca* is rendered as “shell” rather than ‘basin.’
- *discende mai alcun* ‘descends ever anyone’ → “does any ever descend”: English supplies the interrogative auxiliary “does,” and places the subject before the main verb and adverb.
- *del primo grado* ‘of the first level’ → “from the first degree”: the phrase identifying someone belonging to the first level is rendered as a phrase of origin; *grado* becomes “degree.”
- *che sol per pena ha la speranza cionca* ‘who only as punishment has the hope cut off’ → “whose only punishment is hope cut off”: the relative subject becomes possessive “whose,” and having something as punishment becomes a copular construction identifying the punishment. The adverb *sol* becomes the modifier “only” before “punishment,” and the article before “hope” is omitted.

> 19 Questa question fec’ io ; e quei : “Di rado  
> 20 incontra,” mi rispose, “che di nui  
> 21 faccia il cammino alcun per quale io vado.  

This question I made, and he replied to me: “Rarely it occurs that any of us makes this journey on which I go.

- *Questa question fec’ io* ‘this question made I’ → “This question I made”: the subject moves before the verb, while the object remains first.
- *e quei … mi rispose* ‘and that one … answered me’ → “and he replied to me”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes “he,” and the reporting verb moves from within the quotation to before it.
- *Di rado* ‘at rare intervals’ → “Rarely”: the prepositional expression becomes a single adverb.
- *che di nui faccia il cammino alcun* ‘that of us should make the journey anyone’ → “that any of us makes this journey”: the postponed subject moves before the verb, with *di nui* placed directly after it; the subjunctive becomes indicative; and the definite article becomes “this.”
- *per quale io vado* ‘along which I go’ → “on which I go”: the route expressed by *per* is rendered with “on.”

## Lines 22–54 — Virgil’s Reassurance and the Furies’ Threat

> 22 Vero è che altra fiata quaggiù fui  
> 23 congiurato da quella Eriton cruda,  
> 24 che richiamava l'ombre a' corpi sui.  

It is true, that once before I was down here, conjured by that fell Erichtho, who recalled the shadows to their bodies.

- *Vero è* ‘true is’ → “It is true”: English supplies an introductory “It” and places the adjective after the verb.
- *altra fiata* ‘another time’ → “once before”: the reference to another occasion becomes an explicit reference to an earlier occasion.
- *quaggiù fui congiurato* ‘down here I was conjured’ → “I was down here, conjured”: the place-adverb moves after “was”, and “conjured” is detached from it.
- *quella Eriton cruda* ‘that Erichtho cruel’ → “that fell Erichtho”: the adjective precedes the name; “fell” renders the cruelty expressed by *cruda*.
- *richiamava* ‘used to recall’ → “recalled”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, without an explicit indication of habitual action.
- *a' corpi sui* ‘to the bodies their own’ → “to their bodies”: the possessive moves before the noun, and its reflexive reference is left implicit.

> 25 Di poco era di me la carne nuda  
> 26 ch'ella mi fece entrar dentro a quel muro,  
> 27 per trarne un spirto del cerchio di Giuda.  

My flesh had been but short time divested of me, when she made me enter within that wall, to draw out a spirit from the Circle of Judas.

- *Di poco era di me la carne nuda* ‘for a short time was of me the flesh bare’ → “My flesh had been but short time divested of me”: the subject moves before the verb, the time-expression follows it, and “My” replaces the definite article. The imperfect becomes a pluperfect, while the adjective *nuda* becomes the participial “divested”; “but” emphasizes the shortness of the interval.
- *ch'* ‘that’ → “when”: English makes the conjunction’s temporal function explicit.
- *per trarne un spirto del cerchio di Giuda* ‘to draw thence a spirit of the circle of Judas’ → “to draw out a spirit from the Circle of Judas”: the reference back to a place in *-ne* becomes the less specific “out”; *del* is rendered as “from”, expressing the source of the removal rather than simply the spirit’s association with the circle.

> 28 Quell'è il più basso loco, e il più oscuro,  
> 29 e il più lontan dal ciel che tutto gira;  

That is the lowest place, and the most dark, and farthest from the Heaven, which encircles all;

- *e il più lontan* ‘and the farthest’ → “and farthest”: the repeated definite article is omitted.
- *che tutto gira* ‘which turns all’ → “which encircles all”: the translation takes *gira* spatially, as encompassing everything, rather than causatively, as making everything turn; the object moves after the verb.

> 30 ben so il cammin : però ti fa securo.  

well do I know the way : so reassure thyself.

- *ben so il cammin* ‘well I know the way’ → “well do I know the way”: English introduces emphatic “do” and places it before the subject.
- *ti fa securo* ‘make thyself secure’ → “reassure thyself”: the construction with a verb and predicative adjective becomes a single verb, and the reflexive pronoun follows it.

> 31 Questa palude, che il gran puzzo spira,  
> 32 cinge d'intorno la città dolente,  
> 33 u' non potemo entrare omai senz'ira.”  

This marsh, which breathes the mighty stench, all round begirds the doleful city, where we cannot now enter without anger.”

- *che il gran puzzo spira* ‘which the great stench breathes’ → “which breathes the mighty stench”: the verb moves before its object; “mighty” expresses the intensity of *gran*.
- *cinge d'intorno* ‘girds around’ → “all round begirds”: the adverbial expression precedes the verb, and “all” is supplied.
- *la città dolente* ‘the city doleful’ → “the doleful city”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *entrare omai* ‘enter now’ → “now enter”: the time-adverb moves before the infinitive.

> 34 Ed altro disse, ma non l'ho a mente:  

And more he said, but I have it not in memory

- *altro* ‘other things’ → “more”: the substantival adjective is rendered as an expression of additional quantity.
- *non l'ho a mente* ‘not it I have in mind’ → “I have it not in memory”: the object pronoun and negative follow the verb, and “in memory” replaces ‘in mind’.

> 35 perocchè l'occhio m'avea tutto tratto  
> 36 ver l'alta torre alla cima rovente,  

: for my eye had drawn me wholly to the high tower with glowing summit,

- *perocchè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *l'occhio* ‘the eye’ → “my eye”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *m'avea tutto tratto* ‘me had wholly drawn’ → “had drawn me wholly”: the object pronoun moves after the participle, and *tutto*, referring to the speaker in his entirety, is expressed by the adverb “wholly”.
- *ver* ‘towards’ → “to”: direction towards something is expressed as movement to a destination.
- *alla cima rovente* ‘at the summit glowing’ → “with glowing summit”: the prepositional construction becomes a description introduced by “with”; the article is omitted, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 37 ove in un punto furon dritte ratto  
> 38 tre furie infernal di sangue tinte,  
> 39 che membra femminili aveano, ed atto;  

where all at once [had risen up] three Hellish Furies, stained with blood ; who had the limbs and attitude of women,

- *ove in un punto furon dritte ratto* ‘where in an instant stood erect suddenly’ → “where all at once [had risen up]”: *in un punto* and *ratto* are compressed into “all at once”. Standing erect is expressed as rising up, and the simple past becomes a pluperfect.
- *tre furie infernal di sangue tinte* ‘three Furies infernal with blood stained’ → “three Hellish Furies, stained with blood”: “Hellish” precedes its noun, while “stained” precedes the phrase specifying what stains them.
- *che membra femminili aveano, ed atto* ‘who limbs feminine had, and bearing’ → “who had the limbs and attitude of women”: the verb precedes both objects, and an article is supplied. The adjective *femminili* becomes “of women”, placed after the coordinated nouns and applied to both.

> 40 e con idre verdissime eran cinte;  
> 41 serpentelli ceraste avean per crine,  
> 42 onde le fiere tempie eran avvinte.  

and were girt with greenest hydras ; for hair, they had little serpents and cerastes, wherewith their horrid temples were bound.

- *e con idre verdissime eran cinte* ‘and with hydras very green were girded’ → “and were girt with greenest hydras”: the passive verb moves before the prepositional phrase, and the adjective precedes its noun. The Italian absolute superlative, ‘very green’, is represented by the English superlative “greenest”.
- *serpentelli ceraste avean per crine* ‘little serpents, cerastes they had for hair’ → “for hair, they had little serpents and cerastes”: “for hair” moves to the beginning, and the objects follow the verb. The translation treats *serpentelli* and *ceraste* as two coordinated nouns and supplies “and” between them.
- *le fiere tempie* ‘the fierce temples’ → “their horrid temples”: a possessive replaces the article, and *fiere* is interpreted as expressing a frightening appearance.

> 43 E quei, che ben conobbe le meschine  
> 44 della regina dell'eterno pianto,  
> 45 “Guarda,” mi disse, “le feroci Erine.  

And he, knowing well the handmaids of the Queen of everlasting lamentation, said to me : “Mark the fierce Erynnis

- *E quei* ‘And that one’ → “And he”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *che ben conobbe* ‘who well recognized’ → “knowing well”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase, recognition becomes knowledge, and the adverb follows the participle.
- *dell'eterno pianto* ‘of the everlasting lamentation’ → “of everlasting lamentation”: the definite article is omitted.
- *Guarda, mi disse* ‘Look, to me he said’ → “said to me : “Mark”: the reporting clause moves before the quoted imperative, and the indirect object follows the verb. “Mark” expresses attentive observation rather than simply looking.

> 46 Questa è Megera dal sinistro canto;  

! This is Megæra on the left hand

- *dal sinistro canto* ‘from the left side’ → “on the left hand”: *da* is rendered according to its locative force here, and “hand” replaces ‘side’.

> 47 quella, che piange dal destro, è Aletto;  

; she, that weeps upon the right, is Alecto

- *quella* ‘that one’ → “she”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *dal destro* ‘from the right’ → “upon the right”: the preposition is rendered locatively rather than as indicating origin.

> 48 Tesifone è nel mezzo”; e tacque a tanto.  

; Tesiphone is in the middle” ; and therewith he was silent.

- *e tacque a tanto* ‘and he fell silent at that’ → “and therewith he was silent”: becoming silent is expressed as a state of silence, and the concluding adverbial expression moves before the subject.

> 49 Coll'unghie si fendea ciascuna il petto;  
> 50 batteansi a palme, e gridavan sì alto,  
> 51 ch'io mi strinsi al poeta per sospetto.  

With her claws each was rending her breast; they were smiting themselves with their palms, and crying so loudly, that I pressed close to the Poet for fear.

- *Coll'unghie* ‘with the nails’ → “With her claws”: a possessive replaces the article, and “claws” gives *unghie* a more specifically animal or ferocious sense.
- *si fendea ciascuna il petto* ‘for herself was cleaving each the breast’ → “each was rending her breast”: the subject moves before the verb; the reflexive construction with a definite article becomes a possessive construction with “her breast”.
- *batteansi a palme* ‘they were beating themselves with palms’ → “they were smiting themselves with their palms”: the possessive “their” is supplied.
- *gridavan sì alto* ‘they cried so high’ → “crying so loudly”: the finite verb becomes a participle sharing “were” with “smiting”; *alto* is rendered as loudness rather than height.
- *mi strinsi al poeta* ‘I drew myself tight to the poet’ → “I pressed close to the Poet”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive expression with “close”.
- *per sospetto* ‘through apprehension’ → “for fear”: the translation selects the sense of fear in *sospetto*, rather than suspicion or uncertainty.

> 52 “Venga Medusa, sì il farem di smalto,”  
> 53 dicevan tutte riguardando in giuso;  
> 54 “mal noi vengiammo in Teseo l'assalto.”  

“Let Medusa come, that we may change him into stone,” they all [said], looking downwards; “badly did we avenge the assault of Theseus.”

- *Venga Medusa* ‘May Medusa come’ → “Let Medusa come”: the independent subjunctive is expressed through “Let”, and the subject precedes “come” rather than following it.
- *sì il farem di smalto* ‘so we shall make him of enamel’ → “that we may change him into stone”: *sì* is interpreted as introducing purpose or intended result. The future becomes a clause with “may”; ‘make him of’ becomes “change him into”, and *smalto* is rendered by the stonelike substance implied here rather than by its ordinary sense of enamel or a hard coating.
- *dicevan tutte* ‘were saying all’ → “they all [said]”: the quantifier moves before the verb, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *mal noi vengiammo in Teseo l'assalto* ‘badly we avenged on Theseus the assault’ → “badly did we avenge the assault of Theseus”: English introduces “did” and inverts it with the subject after “badly”. The phrase identifying the person upon whom vengeance was taken becomes “of Theseus”, attached to “assault” and identifying its perpetrator instead.

## Lines 55–87 — Closed Eyes and Heaven’s Messenger

> 55 “Volgiti indietro, e tien lo viso chiuso:  
> 56 chè, se il Gorgon si mostra, e tu il vedessi,  
> 57 nulla sarebbe del tornar mai suso.”  

"Turn thee backwards, and keep thy eyes closed: for if the Gorgon shew herself, and thou shouldst see her, there would be no returning up again."

- *lo viso chiuso* ‘the face closed’ → “thy eyes closed”: the face is replaced by the eyes, with a corresponding change of number; the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *si mostra* ‘shows itself’ → “shew herself”: the Italian present indicative becomes an English subjunctive; the reflexive is rendered with a feminine personal pronoun.
- *tu il vedessi* ‘thou shouldst see it’ → “thou shouldst see her”: the imperfect subjunctive is expressed with “shouldst”; the masculine object pronoun becomes feminine and moves after the verb.
- *nulla sarebbe del tornar mai suso* ‘nothing would come of ever returning up’ → “there would be no returning up again”: the Italian idiom becomes an existential construction. The nominal infinitive becomes an English verbal noun, and “again” replaces the explicit ‘ever’ of *mai*.

> 58 Così disse il maestro; ed egli stessi  
> 59 mi volse, e non si tenne alle mie mani,  
> 60 che con le sue ancor non mi chiudessi.  

Thus said the Master, and he himself turned me, and trusted not to my hands, but closed me also with his own.

- *mi volse* ‘me he turned’ → “turned me”: the object pronoun follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *non si tenne alle mie mani* ‘did not content himself with my hands’ → “trusted not to my hands”: being content with, or relying solely upon, the hands is expressed as trusting to them; the reflexive construction disappears.
- *che con le sue ancor non mi chiudessi* ‘that with his he should not also close me’ → “but closed me also with his own”: the subordinate negative construction following the preceding negative becomes a positive clause introduced by “but”. The imperfect subjunctive becomes a simple past indicative; the object and the phrase specifying the hands move after the verb, and “own” is supplied.

> 61 O voi, che avete gl’ intelletti sani,  
> 62 mirate la dottrina, che s’ asconde  
> 63 sotto il velame degli versi strani!  

O ye, who have sane intellects, mark the doctrine, which conceals itself beneath the veil of the strange verses!

- *gl’ intelletti sani* ‘the intellects sound’ → “sane intellects”: the definite article is omitted, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *mirate* ‘look at’ → “mark”: the verb of looking is rendered as an injunction to attend or take notice.
- *versi strani* ‘verses strange’ → “strange verses”: the adjective precedes rather than follows the noun.

> 64 E già venia su per le torbid’ onde  
> 65 un fracasso d’ un suon pien di spavento,  
> 66 per cui tremavano ambedue le sponde;  

And now there came, upon the turbid waves, a crash of fearful sound, at which the shores both trembled;

- *E già venia* ‘and already was coming’ → “And now there came”: “now” replaces ‘already’; the imperfect becomes a simple past, and presentational “there” is supplied.
- *su per le torbid’ onde* ‘up along the turbid waves’ → “upon the turbid waves”: the directional phrase becomes a phrase of location, without an explicit equivalent for ‘up’.
- *d’ un suon pien di spavento* ‘of a sound full of fright’ → “of fearful sound”: the indefinite article is omitted, and the postposed phrase describing the sound becomes a preceding adjective.
- *per cui* ‘through which’ → “at which”: the causal connection is expressed with “at” rather than ‘through’.
- *tremavano ambedue le sponde* ‘trembled both the shores’ → “the shores both trembled”: the subject moves before the verb, and “both” follows rather than precedes the noun phrase.

> 67 non altrimenti fatto che d’ un vento  
> 68 impetuoso per gli avversi ardori,  
> 69 che fier la selva senza alcun rattento;  

a sound as of a wind, impetuous for the adverse heats, which smites the forest without any stay;

- *non altrimenti fatto che d’ un vento* ‘made no otherwise than of a wind’ → “a sound as of a wind”: the negative comparison becomes a positive comparison with “as”; *fatto* has no separate equivalent, and “a sound” is repeated from the preceding clause.

> 70 li rami schianta, abbatte, e porta fuori;  

shatters off the boughs, beats down, and sweeps away;

- *li rami schianta* ‘the boughs it shatters’ → “shatters off the boughs”: the object moves after the verb, and “off” explicitly expresses separation.
- *porta fuori* ‘carries out’ → “sweeps away”: carrying is rendered as sweeping, and outward movement as movement away.

> 71 dinanzi polveroso va superbo,  
> 72 e fa fuggir le fiere e li pastori.  

dusty in front, it goes superb, and makes the wild beasts and the shepherds flee.

- *dinanzi polveroso* ‘in front dusty’ → “dusty in front”: the adjective and its qualifying phrase exchange places.
- *fa fuggir le fiere e li pastori* ‘makes flee the wild beasts and the shepherds’ → “makes the wild beasts and the shepherds flee”: the subject of the infinitive moves before it, as required by this English causative construction.

> 73 Gli occhi mi sciolse, e disse: “Or drizza il nerbo  
> 74 del viso su per quella schiuma antica,  
> 75 per indi ove quel fummo è più acerbo.”  

He loosed my eyes, and said: "Now turn thy nerve of vision on that ancient foam, there where the smoke is harshest."

- *Gli occhi mi sciolse* ‘the eyes for me he loosed’ → “He loosed my eyes”: the dative pronoun becomes a possessive, and the object moves after the verb.
- *il nerbo del viso* ‘the strength of sight’ → “thy nerve of vision”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and *nerbo* is given the concrete rendering “nerve” rather than its figurative sense ‘strength’ or ‘power’.
- *su per quella schiuma antica* ‘up along that foam ancient’ → “on that ancient foam”: the directional phrase becomes “on”, with no explicit equivalent for ‘up’; the adjective moves before the noun.
- *per indi ove* ‘through there where’ → “there where”: the directional preposition has no separate equivalent.
- *quel fummo è più acerbo* ‘that smoke is more bitter’ → “the smoke is harshest”: the demonstrative becomes a definite article, and the comparative becomes a superlative. The sensory adjective is rendered as “harshest” rather than ‘most bitter’.

> 76 Come le rane innanzi alla nimica  
> 77 biscia per l’ acqua si dileguan tutte,  
> 78 fin ch’ alla terra ciascuna s’ abbica;  

As frogs, before their enemy the serpent, run all asunder through the water, till each squats upon the bottom:

- *Come le rane* ‘as the frogs’ → “As frogs”: the definite article is omitted.
- *innanzi alla nimica biscia* ‘before the hostile snake’ → “before their enemy the serpent”: the attributive adjective becomes the noun “enemy”, followed by an appositional noun phrase; “their” is supplied.
- *per l’ acqua si dileguan tutte* ‘through the water disperse themselves all’ → “run all asunder through the water”: the pronominal verb becomes “run … asunder”, making running explicit and leaving the reflexive unexpressed. The phrase describing the medium moves after the verb, while “all” is placed within the verbal phrase.
- *fin ch’ alla terra ciascuna s’ abbica* ‘until upon the ground each heaps itself’ → “till each squats upon the bottom”: the locative phrase moves to the end. ‘Ground’ is interpreted as the underwater “bottom”, and the reflexive image of heaping oneself becomes the intransitive posture “squats”.

> 79 vid’ io più di mille anime distrutte  
> 80 fuggir così dinanzi ad un, che al passo  
> 81 passava Stige colle piante asciutte.  

so I saw more than a thousand ruined spirits flee before one, who passed the Stygian ferry with soles unwet.

- *vid’ io* ‘saw I’ → “I saw”: subject–verb inversion is removed.
- *mille* ‘thousand’ → “a thousand”: English supplies an indefinite article with the numeral.
- *anime distrutte* ‘spirits ruined’ → “ruined spirits”: the participial adjective precedes rather than follows the noun.
- *così* ‘thus’ → “so”: the corresponding word moves from after the infinitive to the beginning of the main clause.
- *che al passo passava Stige* ‘who at a walking pace was crossing Styx’ → “who passed the Stygian ferry”: the manner expressed by *al passo* is not separately preserved. Instead, the river is described as a “ferry”, with its name converted into an adjective and a definite article supplied; the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *colle piante asciutte* ‘with the soles dry’ → “with soles unwet”: the definite article is omitted, and dryness is expressed negatively as “unwet”.

> 82 Dal volto rimovea quell’ aer grasso,  
> 83 menando la sinistra innanzi spesso;  
> 84 e sol di quell’ angoscia parea lasso.  

He waved that gross air from his countenance, often moving his left *hand* before *him*; and only of that trouble seemed he weary.

- *Dal volto* ‘from the face’ → “from his countenance”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves from the beginning of the clause to after the object.
- *rimovea* ‘was removing’ → “He waved”: removal is rendered through the particular motion used to accomplish it; the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *quell’ aer grasso* ‘that air thick’ → “that gross air”: the adjective precedes rather than follows the noun.
- *la sinistra* ‘the left’ → “his left *hand*”: the substantival adjective is expanded by supplying “*hand*”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *innanzi* ‘in front’ → “before *him*”: the person relative to whom the movement occurs is made explicit.
- *menando … spesso* ‘moving … often’ → “often moving”: the adverb moves from the end of the participial phrase to before the participle.
- *angoscia* ‘anguish’ → “trouble”: the translation uses a less intense term.

> 85 Ben m’ accorsi ch’ egli era del ciel messo,  
> 86 e volsimi al maestro; e quei fe’ segno,  
> 87 ch’ io stessi cheto, ed inchinassi ad esso.  

Well did I perceive that he was a Messenger of Heaven; and I turned to the Master; and he made a sign that I should stand quiet, and bow down to him.

- *Ben m’ accorsi* ‘well I became aware’ → “Well did I perceive”: the pronominal verb becomes non-reflexive “perceive”, with auxiliary “did” and subject–auxiliary inversion after “Well”.
- *del ciel messo* ‘of Heaven messenger’ → “a Messenger of Heaven”: the modifying phrase moves after the noun, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *volsimi al maestro* ‘turned myself to the master’ → “I turned to the Master”: the explicitly reflexive construction becomes an intransitive one.
- *e quei fe’ segno* ‘and that one made sign’ → “and he made a sign”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun, and an indefinite article is supplied in the verbal expression.
- *ch’ io stessi cheto* ‘that I remain quiet’ → “that I should stand quiet”: the imperfect subjunctive in the reported instruction is expressed with “should”; *stessi* is interpreted as standing rather than simply remaining.
- *ed inchinassi ad esso* ‘and bow to him’ → “and bow down to him”: “down” is supplied, and the subjunctive force is carried by “should” in the preceding coordinated clause.

## Lines 88–120 — The Gate Opens onto Burning Tombs

> 88 Ahi quanto mi parea pien di disdegno!  

Ah, how full he seemed to me of indignation!

- *mi parea pien* ‘to me he seemed full’ → “full he seemed to me”: the adjective moves before the verb, and the phrase expressing the observer moves after it.

> 89 Venne alla porta, e con una verghetta  
> 90 l' aperse, chè non ebbe alcun ritegno.  

He reached the gate, and with a wand opened it : for there was no resistance.

- *Venne alla porta* ‘He came to the gate’ → “He reached the gate”: coming to a destination becomes reaching it.
- *verghetta* ‘little rod’ → “wand”: the diminutive is not separately expressed, and the noun becomes more specific.
- *l’aperse* ‘it he opened’ → “opened it”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *non ebbe alcun ritegno* ‘he had no hindrance’ → “there was no resistance”: the construction becomes impersonal, and hindrance is expressed as resistance.

> 91 "O cacciati del ciel, gente dispetta,”  
> 92 cominciò egli in su l’ orribil soglia,  
> 93 "ond’ esta oltracotanza in voi s’ alletta?  

“O outcasts of Heaven! race despised!” began he, upon the horrid threshold, “why dwells this insolence in you?

- *cacciati del ciel* ‘ones driven from heaven’ → “outcasts of Heaven”: a participle becomes a noun, and expulsion from a place becomes a relationship expressed with “of”.
- *gente* ‘people’ → “race”: the collective noun is rendered with a term suggesting a distinct kind or stock.
- *ond’* ‘whence’ → “why”: a question about origin becomes a question about cause.
- *esta oltracotanza in voi s’alletta* ‘this insolence in you lodges itself’ → “dwells this insolence in you”: the reflexive verb becomes intransitive, and the verb precedes its subject in the English question.

> 94 Perchè ricalcitrate a quella voglia,  
> 95 a cui non puote il fin mai esser mozzo,  
> 96 e che più volte v’ ha cresciuta doglia?  

Why spurn ye at that Will, whose object never can be frustrated, and which often has increased your pain?

- *ricalcitrate a* ‘kick back against’ → “spurn ye at”: the physical expression is rendered with another verb of kicking or rejection, followed by “at”; the English question places the subject after the verb.
- *a cui non puote il fin mai esser mozzo* ‘whose end can never be cut off’ → “whose object never can be frustrated”: the cutting metaphor becomes frustration of a purpose; the subject precedes the auxiliary, and “never” precedes “can”.
- *più volte* ‘several times’ → “often”: a phrase counting repeated occasions becomes an adverb of frequency.
- *v’ha cresciuta doglia* ‘has increased pain for you’ → “has increased your pain”: the affected persons, expressed by an indirect-object pronoun, become possessors of the pain.

> 97 Che giova nelle Fata dar di cozzo?  

What profits it to butt against the Fates?

- *Che giova* ‘what profits’ → “What profits it”: English supplies an anticipatory “it” referring to the following infinitive.
- *nelle Fata dar di cozzo* ‘against the Fates to give a butt’ → “to butt against the Fates”: the noun-based expression becomes a verb, and the prepositional phrase follows it.

> 98 Cerbero vostro, se ben vi ricorda,  
> 99 ne porta ancor pelato il mento e il gozzo.”  

Your Cerberus, if ye remember, still bears his chin and his throat peeled for doing so.”

- *Cerbero vostro* ‘Cerberus your’ → “Your Cerberus”: the possessive precedes the name.
- *se ben vi ricorda* ‘if it comes well to your memory’ → “if ye remember”: an impersonal construction becomes an active verb with the rememberers as subject; *ben* is omitted.
- *ne* ‘from it’ → “for doing so”: the pronoun’s reference is expanded into an explicit action and placed at the end of the clause.
- *porta ancor pelato il mento e il gozzo* ‘bears still peeled the chin and the gullet’ → “still bears his chin and his throat peeled”: the adverb precedes the verb, the adjective follows the objects, definite articles become possessives, and “throat” is less anatomically specific than ‘gullet’.

> 100 Poi si rivolse per la strada lorda,  
> 101 e non fe’ motto a noi ; ma fe’ sembiante  
> 102 d’ uomo, cui altra cura stringa e morda,  
> 103 che quella di colui che gli è davante.  

Then he returned by the filthy way, and spake no word to us; but looked like one whom other care urges and incites than that of those who stand before him.

- *si rivolse* ‘turned himself back’ → “he returned”: the reflexive turning becomes a verb of return.
- *la strada lorda* ‘the way filthy’ → “the filthy way”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *non fe’ motto* ‘made no utterance’ → “spake no word”: making an utterance becomes speaking a word.
- *fe’ sembiante d’uomo* ‘made the appearance of a man’ → “looked like one”: the noun-based expression becomes a verb of appearance followed by a comparison, and ‘man’ becomes the pronoun “one”.
- *cui altra cura stringa e morda* ‘whom other care may grip and bite’ → “whom other care urges and incites”: the physical metaphors become verbs of prompting, and the Italian subjunctives become English indicatives.
- *colui che gli è davante* ‘him who is before him’ → “those who stand before him”: the singular becomes plural, and being before someone becomes standing before him.

> 104 E noi movemmo i piedi in ver la terra,  
> 105 sicuri appresso le parole sante.  

And we moved our feet towards the city, secure after the sacred words.

- *i piedi* ‘the feet’ → “our feet”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *le parole sante* ‘the words sacred’ → “the sacred words”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 106 Dentro v’ entrammo senza alcuna guerra ;  

We entered into it without any strife;

- *Dentro v’entrammo* ‘inside there we entered’ → “We entered into it”: the two locative expressions are consolidated into “into it”, with a pronoun referring to the place entered.
- *guerra* ‘war’ → “strife”: the translation uses a broader term for conflict.

> 107 ed io, ch’ avea di riguardar disio  
> 108 la condizion che tal fortezza serra,  
> 109 com’ io fui dentro, l’ occhio intorno invio ;  

and I, who was desirous to behold the condition which such a fortress encloses, as soon as I was in, sent my eyes around;

- *ch’avea di riguardar disio* ‘who had, to behold, desire’ → “who was desirous to behold”: possession of a noun becomes a construction with “was” and an adjective; the infinitive follows rather than precedes the expression of desire.
- *tal fortezza* ‘such fortress’ → “such a fortress”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *com’ io fui dentro* ‘as I was inside’ → “as soon as I was in”: the translation makes the immediacy of the temporal connection explicit.
- *l’occhio intorno invio* ‘the eye around I send’ → “sent my eyes around”: the present becomes past, the singular becomes plural, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the verb moves before its object.

> 110 e veggio ad ogni man grande campagna  
> 111 piena di duolo e di tormento rio.  

and saw, on either hand, a spacious plain full of sorrow and of evil torment.

- *veggio* ‘I see’ → “saw”: the present becomes past.
- *ad ogni man* ‘on every hand’ → “on either hand”: the distributive expression is rendered as a reference to two sides.
- *grande campagna* ‘great open country’ → “a spacious plain”: open country becomes a countable geographical feature with an indefinite article, and greatness is specified as spatial extent.
- *tormento rio* ‘torment evil’ → “evil torment”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 112 Sì come ad Arli, ove il Rodano stagna,  
> 113 sì com’ a Pola presso del Quarnaro,  
> 114 che Italia chiude e i suoi termini bagna,  

As at Arles, where the Rhone stagnates, as at Pola near the Quarnaro _gulf_, which shuts up Italy and bathes its confines,

- *Sì come … sì com’* ‘just as … just as’ → “As” … “as”: the reinforcing *sì* is not separately expressed in either comparison.
- *presso del Quarnaro* ‘near the Quarnaro’ → “near the Quarnaro _gulf_”: the identifying noun “_gulf_” is supplied.
- *che Italia chiude e i suoi termini bagna* ‘which Italy shuts in and its confines bathes’ → “which shuts up Italy and bathes its confines”: both objects move from before their verbs to after them.

> 115 fanno i sepolcri tutto il loco varo :  
> 116 così facevan quivi d’ ogni parte,  
> 117 salvo che il modo v’ era più amaro :  

the sepulchres make all the place uneven : so did they here on every side, only the manner here was bitterer

- *fanno i sepolcri* ‘make the sepulchres’ → “the sepulchres make”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *varo* ‘variegated’ → “uneven”: the translation interprets variation in the place’s appearance as physical irregularity.
- *quivi* ‘there’ → “here”: the spatial viewpoint changes.
- *salvo che* ‘except that’ → “only”: an explicit exception clause becomes a qualification introduced by an adverb.
- *v’* ‘there’ → “here”: the locative again changes from distant to immediate.
- *più amaro* ‘more bitter’ → “bitterer”: the separate comparative adverb becomes an inflectional ending.

> 118 chè tra gli avelli fiamme erano sparte,  
> 119 per le quali eran sì del tutto accesi,  
> 120 che ferro più non chieda verun’ arte.  

: for amongst the tombs were scattered flames, whereby they were made all over so glowing-hot, that iron more _hot_ no craft requires.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *tra gli avelli fiamme erano sparte* ‘amongst the tombs flames were scattered’ → “amongst the tombs were scattered flames”: the subject moves after the passive verb.
- *per le quali* ‘through which’ → “whereby”: a preposition and relative pronoun become a relative adverb.
- *eran sì del tutto accesi* ‘were so entirely kindled’ → “they were made all over so glowing-hot”: being kindled is rendered as being made intensely hot; completeness becomes the spatial expression “all over”, and “so” moves immediately before the adjective.
- *ferro più* ‘iron more’ → “iron more _hot_”: the adjective understood after the comparative is supplied explicitly.
- *non chieda verun’arte* ‘no art may require’ → “no craft requires”: the negative verb with an indefinite subject becomes a negative subject with an affirmative verb; the subject moves before the verb, the subjunctive becomes indicative, and “craft” selects the practical sense of *arte*.

## Lines 121–133 — The Heretics’ Tombs

> 121 Tutti gli lor coperchi eran sospesi,  
> 122 e fuor n’ uscivan sì duri lamenti,  
> 123 che ben parean di miseri e d’ offesi.  

Their covers were all raised up; and out of them proceeded moans so grievous, that they seemed indeed *the moans of spirits sad and wounded*.

- *Tutti gli lor coperchi eran sospesi* ‘All their covers were suspended’ → “Their covers were all raised up”: “all” moves from before the noun to after the verb; “raised up” replaces ‘suspended’.
- *fuor n’ uscivan* ‘out of them came forth’ → “out of them proceeded”: *uscivan* expresses coming out, whereas “proceeded” leaves the outward direction to “out of them”.
- *sì duri lamenti* ‘so hard lamentations’ → “moans so grievous”: “moans” replaces ‘lamentations’, ‘hard’ is rendered as “grievous”, and the adjective phrase follows rather than precedes the noun.
- *ben parean* ‘indeed they seemed’ → “they seemed indeed”: the strengthening adverb moves after the verb.
- *di miseri e d’ offesi* ‘of miserable ones and of injured ones’ → “*the moans of spirits sad and wounded*”: “the moans” is supplied as the complement of “seemed”; the substantival adjectives become adjectives qualifying the supplied noun “spirits”. ‘Miserable’ becomes “sad”, ‘injured’ becomes “wounded”, and the repeated preposition is not retained.

> 124 Ed io : “ Maestro, quai son quelle genti,  
> 125 che seppellite dentro da quell’ arche  
> 126 si fan sentir coi sospiri dolenti?”  

And I: "Master, what are these people who, buried within those chests, make themselves heard by their painful sighs?"

- *quelle* ‘those’ → “these”: the distal demonstrative becomes a proximal one.
- *arche* ‘tombs’ → “chests”: the translation chooses the word’s underlying sense of containers rather than its specifically funerary sense here.
- *si fan sentir* ‘themselves make to be heard’ → “make themselves heard”: the reflexive pronoun follows the causative verb, and the Italian infinitive becomes the English participle “heard”.
- *coi sospiri dolenti* ‘with the sorrowful sighs’ → “by their painful sighs”: “by” replaces ‘with’, the definite article becomes a possessive, “painful” replaces ‘sorrowful’, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 127 Ed egli a me: “ Qui son gli eresiarche  
> 128 co’ lor seguaci d’ ogni setta, e molto  
> 129 più che non credi, son le tombe carche.  

And he to me: "[Here] are the Arch-heretics with their followers of every sect; and much more, than thou thinkest, the tombs are laden.

- *eresiarche* ‘heresiarchs’ → “Arch-heretics”: the Italian term is rendered by an English compound rather than its direct cognate.
- *che non credi* ‘than thou dost not believe’ → “than thou thinkest”: *non*, which is expletive rather than negative in this comparison, is omitted; “thinkest” replaces ‘believest’.
- *son le tombe carche* ‘are the tombs laden’ → “the tombs are laden”: the subject moves before the verb.

> 130 Simile qui con simile è sepolto;  

Like with like is buried here;

- *Simile qui con simile è sepolto* ‘Like here with like is buried’ → “Like with like is buried here”: “here” moves from after the first “Like” to the end of the clause.

> 131 e i monimenti son più, e men caldi.”  

and the monuments are more and less hot."

- Literal.

> 132 E poi ch’ alla man destra si fu volto,  
> 133 passammo tra i martiri e gli alti spaldi.  

Then, after turning to the right hand, we passed between the tortures and the high battlements.

- *E* ‘And’ → “Then”: a coordinating conjunction becomes a temporal connective.
- *poi ch’* ‘after that’ → “after”: the conjunction introducing a finite clause becomes a preposition governing “turning”.
- *alla man destra* ‘to the hand right’ → “to the right hand”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *si fu volto* ‘he had turned himself’ → “turning”: the finite reflexive past-perfect construction becomes a nonfinite form, without an explicit reflexive or perfect tense. The Italian verb has a singular subject; the English construction grammatically shares the “we” of the main clause.

## Summary — Close Phrasing, Recast Syntax

Carlyle’s translation stays close to the Italian’s sequence of statements, comparisons, and descriptive details, but regularly rearranges their internal order. Verbs move before objects, postponed subjects move forward, and adjectives generally precede their nouns: *parole alle prime diverse* becomes “words differing from the first”. Yet the prose also preserves conspicuous inversions and unusual phrasing, as in “This question I made” and “iron more _hot_ no craft requires”. Its closeness is therefore often verbal as well as structural, rather than a consistent accommodation to ordinary English prose.

Changes of construction make Italian expressions workable in English. Gerunds and finite clauses sometimes exchange roles: *veggendo* becomes “when I saw”, while *che ben conobbe* becomes “knowing well”. Reflexive constructions frequently become simple English verbs, and possession of a quality becomes a predicate adjective, as *avea … disio* becomes “was desirous”. More substantial recasting occurs when *tal ne s’offerse*, ‘such a one offered himself to us’, becomes the passive “such help was offered to us”. Italian subjunctives are variously represented by modal auxiliaries or indicatives; imperfects often become simple pasts, and the narrative presents *invio* and *veggio* become “sent” and “saw”.

Word choice ranges from close equivalents to interpretive substitutions. *Conca* becomes “shell”, *smalto* becomes “stone”, and *angoscia* becomes the less intense “trouble”. Elsewhere a figurative expression is made more concrete: *il nerbo del viso*, ‘the strength of sight’, becomes “thy nerve of vision”. Conversely, the physical force of *stringa e morda*, ‘grip and bite’, is softened into “urges and incites”. Such choices coexist with closely preserved images and formulations, including “Like with like is buried here” and “the monuments are more and less hot”.

The translation also supplies what Italian leaves understood and omits elements English does not require. It restores the noun in “his new colour”, identifies the “Quarnaro _gulf_”, and expands *la sinistra* into “his left *hand*”. Repeated prepositions and reinforcing words sometimes disappear, as do *qui* in “till some one come” and the expletive *non* in comparative clauses. More generally, beyond the individual changes catalogued above, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and drops the articles that Italian places before possessives. Definite articles also frequently become possessives, especially with parts of the body. These routine adjustments sustain a translation that remains closely attached to the Italian wording while repeatedly clarifying, redistributing, or interpreting its grammatical relationships.
