# Inferno 29

## Lines 1–21 — Eyes Held by Kindred Grief

> 1 La molta gente e le diverse piaghe  
> 2 avean le luci mie sì inebriate,  
> 3 che dello stare a piangere eran vaghe;  

The many people and the diverse wounds had made my eyes so drunken that they longed to stay and weep;

- *La molta gente* ‘the much people’ → “The many people”: the Italian singular collective noun becomes an English plural, with “many” expressing number.
- *avean le luci mie sì inebriate* ‘had my lights so intoxicated’ → “had made my eyes so drunken”: the verb ‘intoxicate’ becomes “made” with an adjective; the figurative ‘lights’ becomes “eyes,” and the possessive moves before its noun.
- *dello stare a piangere eran vaghe* ‘of staying to weep were desirous’ → “they longed to stay and weep”: an adjective with a preceding prepositional complement becomes a verb with a following infinitive; ‘staying to weep’ becomes the coordinated “stay and weep.”

> 4 ma Virgilio mi disse: “Che pur guate?  

but Virgil said to me: “Why art thou gazing still?

- *Virgilio mi disse* ‘Virgil to me said’ → “Virgil said to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *Che pur guate* ‘why still gazest’ → “Why art thou gazing still”: the simple present becomes a progressive construction, and “still” moves to the end.

> 5 perchè la vista tua pur si soffolge  
> 6 laggiù tra l' ombre triste smozzicate?  

wherefore does thy sight still rest, down there, among the dismal mutilated shadows?

- *la vista tua* ‘the sight thy’ → “thy sight”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *si soffolge* ‘supports itself’ → “does” and “rest”: the reflexive expression becomes the intransitive “rest,” interpreting the image of supporting or fixing one's gaze; English interrogative “does” precedes the subject.
- *l' ombre triste smozzicate* ‘the shadows sad mutilated’ → “the dismal mutilated shadows”: both adjectives move before the noun, and ‘sad’ becomes “dismal.”

> 7 Tu non hai fatto sì all' altre bolge;  

Thou hast not done so at the other chasms;

- *non hai fatto* ‘not hast done’ → “hast not done”: the negative moves after the auxiliary.
- *bolge* ‘pouches’ → “chasms”: the translation substitutes a topographical description for the Italian noun's underlying image.

> 8 pensa, se tu annoverar le credi,  
> 9 che miglia ventidue la valle volge,  

consider, if thou thinkest to number them, that the valley goes round two-and-twenty miles;

- *se tu annoverar le credi* ‘if thou to number them believest’ → “if thou thinkest to number them”: the governing verb moves before its infinitive and object; “thinkest” renders the belief or expectation expressed by *credi*.
- *che miglia ventidue la valle volge* ‘that miles twenty-two the valley turns’ → “that the valley goes round two-and-twenty miles”: the subject and verb move before the measurement, the numeral moves before “miles,” and ‘turns’ becomes “goes round.”

> 10 e già la luna è sotto i nostri piedi;  

and the Moon already is beneath our feet;

- *già la luna* ‘already the moon’ → “the Moon already”: the adverb moves after the subject.

> 11 lo tempo è poco omai che n' è concesso,  

the time is now short, that is conceded to us;

- *è poco omai* ‘is little by now’ → “is now short”: the quantity of time becomes its duration, expressed by “short”; the temporal adverb moves before the adjective, and ‘by now’ is reduced to “now.”
- *n' è concesso* ‘to us is conceded’ → “is conceded to us”: the indirect object moves after the verb.

> 12 ed altro è da veder che tu non vedi.”  

and other *things* are to be seen than thou dost see.”

- *altro* ‘something else’ → “other *things*”: the substantival singular becomes an adjective with a supplied plural noun.
- *è da veder* ‘is to see’ → “are to be seen”: the infinitive becomes explicitly passive, and the finite verb becomes plural to agree with “things.”
- *che tu non vedi* ‘that thou dost not see’ → “than thou dost see”: the translation takes *che* comparatively after *altro*, rather than as a relative pronoun, and treats *non* as pleonastic rather than retaining a negative.

> 13 “Se tu avessi,” rispos' io appresso,  
> 14 “atteso alla cagion per ch' io guardava,  
> 15 forse m' avresti ancor lo star dimesso.”  

“Hadst thou,” I thereupon replied, “attended to the cause for which I looked, perhaps thou mightest have vouchsafed me yet to stay.”

- *Se tu avessi … atteso* ‘if thou hadst … attended’ → “Hadst thou,” and “attended”: the conditional conjunction is omitted, and inversion of auxiliary and subject marks the condition instead.
- *rispos' io appresso* ‘replied I afterward’ → “I thereupon replied”: the subject moves before the verb; the following temporal adverb moves before it too, with “thereupon” linking the reply more closely to the preceding speech.
- *io guardava* ‘I was looking’ → “I looked”: the Italian imperfect's ongoing action is rendered by an English simple past.
- *m' avresti ancor lo star dimesso* ‘to me thou wouldst have still the staying permitted’ → “thou mightest have vouchsafed me yet to stay”: “mightest” replaces the conditional's more direct ‘wouldst’; ‘permitted’ becomes the more gracious “vouchsafed”; the indirect object follows the verb; and the substantival infinitive ‘the staying’ becomes “to stay.”

> 16 Parte sen gia, ed io retro gli andava,  
> 17 lo duca, già facendo la risposta,  

Meantime the Guide was going on; and I went behind him, now making my reply,

- *sen gia … lo duca* ‘was going away … the guide’ → “the Guide was going on”: the postponed subject moves before the verb, and the departure expressed by *se ne* becomes onward movement.
- *ed io retro gli andava* ‘and I behind him was going’ → “and I went behind him”: the verb moves before the phrase expressing position, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *già* ‘already’ → “now”: the adverb marks the action then in progress rather than its having begun already.
- *facendo la risposta* ‘making the reply’ → “making my reply”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 18 e soggiungendo: “Dentro a quella cava,  
> 19 dov' io teneva or gli occhi sì a posta,  
> 20 credo che un spirto del mio sangue pianga  
> 21 la colpa che laggiù cotanto costa.”  

and adding: “Within that cavern where I kept my eyes so fixed, I believe that a spirit of my own blood laments the guilt which costs so much down there.”

- *quella cava* ‘that hollow’ → “that cavern”: the more general word for a hollow or excavation becomes “cavern.”
- *dov' io teneva or gli occhi sì a posta* ‘where I was keeping now the eyes so deliberately’ → “where I kept my eyes so fixed”: the imperfect becomes a simple past; *or* is omitted; the definite article becomes “my”; and the adverbial expression of deliberate attention becomes “fixed,” describing the eyes.
- *del mio sangue* ‘of my blood’ → “of my own blood”: “own” is supplied for emphasis.
- *pianga* ‘weep’ (subjunctive) → “laments”: weeping is expressed as lamentation, and the Italian subjunctive following a statement of belief becomes an English indicative.
- *che laggiù cotanto costa* ‘which down there so much costs’ → “which costs so much down there”: the verb moves before both adverbial expressions, and the expression of place moves to the end.

## Lines 22–54 — Geri’s Anger and Lamentations Below

> 22 Allor disse il maestro: “Non si franga  
> 23 lo tuo pensier da qui innanzi sopr’ ello;  
> 24 attendi ad altro, ed ei là si rimanga:  

Then the Master said: "Let not thy thought henceforth distract itself on him; attend to somewhat else, and let him stay there:

- *Allor disse il maestro* ‘then said the master’ → “Then the Master said”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *Non si franga lo tuo pensier da qui innanzi sopr’ ello* ‘let thy thought not be broken from here onward upon him’ → “Let not thy thought henceforth distract itself on him”: the image of being broken becomes reflexive distraction; the subject precedes “distract,” and the temporal phrase becomes the single adverb “henceforth,” placed before that verb.
- *ad altro* ‘to other [matter]’ → “to somewhat else”: the substantival adjective becomes an indefinite pronoun qualified by “else.”
- *ed ei là si rimanga* ‘and he there may remain’ → “and let him stay there”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “let,” its subject becomes “him,” and the place-adverb moves after the verb. The pronominal *si* is not separately represented.

> 25 ch’ io vidi lui a piè del ponticello  
> 26 mostrarti, e minacciar forte col dito,  
> 27 ed udi ’l nominar Geri del Bello.  

for I saw him, at the foot of the little bridge, point to thee, and vehemently threaten with his finger; and heard *them* call him Geri del Bello.

- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating causal conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *a piè del ponticello* ‘at foot of the little bridge’ → “at the foot of the little bridge”: English supplies the article before “foot”; the diminutive suffix of *ponticello* is expressed by “little.”
- *mostrarti* ‘show thee’ → “point to thee”: the transitive verb becomes “point” with a prepositional complement.
- *minacciar forte* ‘threaten strongly’ → “vehemently threaten”: the adverbial *forte* becomes “vehemently” and moves before the verb.
- *col dito* ‘with the finger’ → “with his finger”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *ed udi ’l nominar* ‘and heard him being named’ → “and heard *them* call him”: the unspecified agent of the naming is supplied as “them”; the construction becomes explicitly active, with “him” following “call.”

> 28 Tu eri allor sì del tutto impedito  
> 29 sopra colui che già tenne Altaforte,  
> 30 che non guardasti in là, sì fu partito.”  

Thou wast then so totally entangled upon him who once held Altaforte, that thou didst not look that way; so he departed."

- *del tutto impedito* ‘altogether hindered’ → “totally entangled”: the adverbial phrase becomes a single adverb, and being hindered or occupied becomes the image of entanglement.
- *colui* ‘that one’ → “him”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *già* ‘already’ → “once”: the adverb is interpreted as referring to a former time, rather than to something already accomplished.
- *in là* ‘over there’ → “that way”: the prepositional expression of place becomes an expression of direction.
- *sì fu partito* ‘until he was gone’ → “so he departed”: the translation takes *sì* consequentially rather than temporally; the compound expression *fu partito* becomes a simple past verb.

> 31 “O duca mio, la violenta morte  
> 32 che non gli è vendicata ancor,” diss’ io,  
> 33 “per alcun che dell’ onta sia consorte,  

"O my Guide! his violent death, which is not yet avenged for him," said I, "by any that is a partner of his shame,

- *O duca mio* ‘O leader mine’ → “O my Guide!”: “Guide” renders *duca*, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *la violenta morte* ‘the violent death’ → “his violent death”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *che non gli è vendicata ancor* ‘which not to him is avenged yet’ → “which is not yet avenged for him”: the negative follows the auxiliary, “yet” precedes the participle, and the dative pronoun becomes “for him,” placed after the participle.
- *che dell’ onta sia consorte* ‘who of the shame be a partner’ → “that is a partner of his shame”: the subjunctive becomes indicative, the complement follows “partner,” and the definite article becomes the possessive “his.”

> 34 fece lui disdegnoso: ond’ ei sen gío  
> 35 senza parlarmi, sì com’ io estimo;  
> 36 ed in ciò m’ ha e’ fatto a sè più pio.”  

made him indignant: therefore, as I suppose, he went away without speaking to me; and in that has made me pity him the more."

- *ond’* ‘whence’ → “therefore”: the connective is rendered explicitly as a logical consequence.
- *ei sen gío* ‘he went himself thence’ → “he went away”: the pronominal *se* is not separately represented, while *ne* is rendered by the less specifically locative “away.”
- *sì com’ io estimo* ‘just as I judge’ → “as I suppose”: *sì* is not separately expressed, “suppose” replaces ‘judge,’ and the parenthesis moves before the statement about his departure.
- *ed in ciò* ‘and in this’ → “and in that”: the demonstrative changes.
- *m’ ha e’ fatto a sè più pio* ‘me has he made toward himself more compassionate’ → “has made me pity him the more”: the object moves after the verb, and the explicit subject *e’* is omitted. The adjective *pio* becomes the verb “pity,” *a sè* becomes its direct object “him,” and the adjective’s comparative becomes the adverbial expression “the more.”

> 37 Così parlammo infino al loco primo  
> 38 che dello scoglio l’ altra valle mostra,  
> 39 se più lume vi fosse, tutto ad imo.  

Thus we spake, up to the first place of the cliff, which shows the other valley, if more light were there, quite to the bottom.

- *al loco primo* ‘to the place first’ → “to the first place”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *dello scoglio* ‘of the rock’ → “of the cliff”: “cliff” renders *scoglio*, and the phrase moves from after the relative pronoun to immediately after “place.”
- *che … l’ altra valle mostra* ‘which … the other valley shows’ → “which shows the other valley”: the object moves after the verb.
- *se più lume vi fosse* ‘if more light there were’ → “if more light were there”: the place-adverb moves after the verb.

> 40 Quando noi fummo in su l’ ultima chiostra  
> 41 di Malebolge, sì che i suoi conversi  
> 42 potean parere alla veduta nostra,  

When we were above the last cloister of Malebolge, so that its lay-brethren could appear to our view,

- *in su* ‘upon’ → “above”: the translation expresses a position above something rather than on it.
- *alla veduta nostra* ‘to the view ours’ → “to our view”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 43 lamenti saettaron me diversi,  
> 44 che di pietà ferrati avean gli strali:  
> 45 ond’ io gli orecchi con le man copersi.  

lamentations pierced me, manifold, which had their arrows barbed with pity: whereat I covered my ears with my hands.

- *saettaron* ‘shot arrows at’ → “pierced”: the act of shooting becomes its penetrating effect.
- *diversi* ‘various’ → “manifold”: the rendering emphasizes multiplicity rather than simply difference or variety.
- *che di pietà ferrati avean gli strali* ‘which with pity iron-tipped had the arrows’ → “which had their arrows barbed with pity”: the verb and object move before the participial phrase; the definite article becomes “their,” and being furnished with iron becomes being “barbed.”
- *ond’* ‘whence’ → “whereat”: the connective presents the preceding event as the occasion for the response.
- *io gli orecchi con le man copersi* ‘I the ears with the hands covered’ → “I covered my ears with my hands”: the verb moves before its object, and the definite articles before the body parts become possessives.

> 46 Qual dolor fora, se degli spedali  
> 47 di Valdichiana tra il luglio e il settembre,  
> 48 e di Maremma e di Sardigna i mali  
> 49 fossero in una fossa tutti insembre:  

Such [pain] as there would be, if the diseases in the hospitals of Valdichiana, between July and September, and of Maremma and Sardinia, were all together in one ditch:

- *Qual dolor fora* ‘what pain would be’ → “Such [pain] as there would be”: *qual* is expanded into the correlative expression “Such … as,” and existential “there” is supplied.
- *degli spedali … i mali* ‘of the hospitals … the illnesses’ → “the diseases in the hospitals”: the noun moves before its modifier, and the relation expressed by ‘of’ becomes location “in.”
- *tra il luglio e il settembre* ‘between the July and the September’ → “between July and September”: the articles before the months are omitted.
- *e di Maremma e di Sardigna* ‘and of Maremma and of Sardinia’ → “and of Maremma and Sardinia”: the second *di* is not repeated.
- *in una fossa tutti insembre* ‘in one ditch all together’ → “all together in one ditch”: the expression of gathering moves before the expression of place.

> 50 tal era quivi, e tal puzzo n’ usciva,  
> 51 qual suole uscir dalle marcite membre.  

such was there here; and such stench issued thence, as is wont to issue from putrid limbs.

- *tal era quivi* ‘such was there’ → “such was there here”: existential “there” is supplied, while the locative *quivi* is rendered as “here.”
- *n’ usciva* ‘thence was issuing’ → “issued thence”: the locative pronoun becomes an adverb following the verb, and the imperfect is rendered by the English simple past.
- *dalle marcite membre* ‘from the rotted limbs’ → “from putrid limbs”: the definite article is omitted, and the participial description becomes the adjective “putrid.”

> 52 Noi discendemmo in su l’ ultima riva  
> 53 del lungo scoglio, pur da man sinistra,  
> 54 ed allor fu la mia vista più viva  

We descended on the last bank of the long cliff, again to the left hand; and then my sight was more vivid,

- *scoglio* ‘rock’ → “cliff”: the broader term for a rocky formation receives a more specific English rendering.
- *pur da man sinistra* ‘still on the left hand’ → “again to the left hand”: *pur* is interpreted as repetition rather than continuation; the preposition becomes directional “to,” and the article before “left hand” is supplied.
- *ed allor fu la mia vista più viva* ‘and then was my sight more lively’ → “and then my sight was more vivid”: the subject moves before the verb, and *viva* is rendered as “vivid,” expressing visual distinctness rather than liveliness.

## Lines 55–87 — The Torment of the Falsifiers

> 55 giù ver lo fondo, dove la ministra  
> 56 dell' alto Sire, infallibil giustizia,  
> 57 punisce i falsator che qui registra.  

down towards the depth in which the ministress of the Great Sire, infallible Justice, punishes the falsifiers that she here registers.

- *lo fondo* ‘the bottom’ → “the depth”: the lowest part is rendered as a depth.
- *dove* ‘where’ → “in which”: a relative adverb becomes a preposition and relative pronoun.
- *alto* ‘high’ → “Great”: elevation is rendered as greatness.

> 58 Non credo che a veder maggior tristizia  
> 59 fosse in Egina il popol tutto infermo,  
> 60 quando fu l' aer sì pien di malizia,  

I do not think it was a greater sorrow to see the people in Ægina all infirm; when the air was so malignant,

- *Non credo che* ‘I do not believe that’ → “I do not think”: “think” replaces ‘believe’, and the conjunction is omitted.
- *a veder* ‘at seeing’ → “to see”: the prepositional construction becomes an English infinitive.
- *maggior tristizia fosse* ‘greater sadness were’ → “it was a greater sorrow”: an anticipatory “it” is introduced, the verb precedes its complement, an indefinite article is supplied, and the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *in Egina il popol tutto infermo* ‘in Ægina the people all sick’ → “the people in Ægina all infirm”: the place phrase moves after “the people”; ‘sick’ is rendered as “infirm”.
- *quando fu l’aer* ‘when was the air’ → “when the air was”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *sì pien di malizia* ‘so full of malignity’ → “so malignant”: the adjective and dependent noun phrase become a single adjective.

> 61 che gli animali infino al picciol vermo  
> 62 cascaron tutti, e poi le genti antiche,  
> 63 secondo che i poeti hanno per fermo,  
> 64 si ristorar di seme di formiche:  

that every animal, even to the little worm, dropt down; and afterwards, as Poets hold for sure, the ancient peoples were restored from seed of ants:

- *gli animali … cascaron tutti* ‘the animals … fell all’ → “every animal” … “dropt down”: the plural subject and postponed ‘all’ become a singular subject with “every”; ‘fell’ becomes “dropt down”, explicitly expressing downward movement.
- *infino al* ‘as far as the’ → “even to the”: the limit is expressed through an inclusive, emphatic turn of phrase.
- *le genti antiche* ‘the peoples ancient’ → “the ancient peoples”: the adjective moves before the noun, and the whole subject moves after the parenthetical clause.
- *secondo che* ‘according as’ → “as”: the connective is shortened.
- *i poeti hanno per fermo* ‘the poets have for certain’ → “Poets hold for sure”: the article is omitted, and ‘have for certain’ becomes the idiom “hold for sure”.
- *si ristorar* ‘restored themselves’ → “were restored”: the reflexive construction becomes passive.
- *di seme* ‘of seed’ → “from seed”: the preposition explicitly marks origin.

> 65 ch' era a veder per quella oscura valle  
> 66 languir gli spirti per diverse biche.  

than it was to see, through that dim valley, the spirits languishing in diverse heaps.

- *a veder … languir gli spirti* ‘to see … languish the spirits’ → “to see” … “the spirits languishing”: the subject of the dependent infinitive moves before its verb, and that infinitive becomes a present participle.
- *per quella oscura valle* ‘through that dark valley’ → “through that dim valley”: “dim” replaces ‘dark’.
- *per diverse biche* ‘through various heaps’ → “in diverse heaps”: the distributive use of *per* is rendered by the locative “in”.

> 67 Qual sopra il ventre, e qual sopra le spalle  
> 68 l' un dell' altro giacea, e qual carpone  
> 69 si trasmutava per lo tristo calle.  

This upon the belly, and that upon the shoulders of the other lay; and some were crawling on along the dismal path.

- *Qual … e qual* ‘one … and another’ → “This” … “and that”: the distributive pronouns become demonstratives.
- *l’un dell’altro giacea* ‘the one of the other lay’ → “of the other lay”: *l’un*, which resumes the preceding distributive subjects, has no separate rendering.
- *e qual carpone si trasmutava* ‘and another on all fours moved himself’ → “and some were crawling on”: singular ‘another’ becomes plural “some”; the adverb describing posture is absorbed into “crawling”; the reflexive verb becomes intransitive, and the imperfect is rendered by a progressive construction. “On” makes continued movement explicit.
- *per lo tristo calle* ‘along the sad path’ → “along the dismal path”: “dismal” replaces ‘sad’.

> 70 Passo passo andavam senza sermone,  
> 71 guardando ed ascoltando gli ammalati,  
> 72 che non potean levar le lor persone.  

Step by step we went, without speech, looking at and listening to the sick who could not raise their bodies.

- *Passo passo* ‘step step’ → “Step by step”: “by” supplies the English construction corresponding to the Italian repetition.
- *guardando ed ascoltando* ‘watching and listening’ → “looking at and listening to”: the Italian verbs take their object directly; the English expressions introduce it through “at” and “to”.
- *le lor persone* ‘their persons’ → “their bodies”: “bodies” gives the concrete bodily sense of *persone*.

> 73 Io vidi due sedere a sè poggiati,  
> 74 come a scaldar si poggia tegghia a tegghia,  
> 75 dal capo al piè di schianze maculati;  

I saw two sit leaning on each other, as pan is leant on pan to warm, from head to foot spotted with scabs;

- *a sè poggiati* ‘propped against themselves’ → “leaning on each other”: the past participle becomes a present participle, and *sè* is explicitly interpreted as reciprocal.
- *come a scaldar si poggia tegghia a tegghia* ‘as to warm one leans pan against pan’ → “as pan is leant on pan to warm”: the impersonal construction becomes passive, the purpose phrase moves to the end, and ‘against’ becomes “on”.
- *dal capo al piè* ‘from the head to the foot’ → “from head to foot”: both definite articles are omitted in the English idiom.
- *di schianze maculati* ‘with scabs spotted’ → “spotted with scabs”: the participle moves before its dependent phrase.

> 76 e non vidi giammai menare stregghia  
> 77 da ragazzo aspettato dal signorso,  
> 78 nè da colui che mal volentier vegghia,  

and never did I see currycomb plied by stable-boy for whom his master waits, nor by one who stays unwillingly awake,

- *e non vidi giammai* ‘and I did not ever see’ → “and never did I see”: the negative and adverb combine as “never”, whose initial position requires auxiliary–subject inversion.
- *menare stregghia* ‘ply currycomb’ → “currycomb plied”: the active infinitive becomes a passive participle, and the noun precedes it.
- *da ragazzo* ‘by boy’ → “by stable-boy”: the general noun is narrowed to a particular occupation.
- *aspettato dal signorso* ‘awaited by his lord’ → “for whom his master waits”: a passive participial phrase becomes an active relative clause; ‘lord’ becomes “master”.
- *nè da colui* ‘nor by that one’ → “nor by one”: the demonstrative expression becomes indefinite.
- *che mal volentier vegghia* ‘who unwillingly keeps vigil’ → “who stays unwillingly awake”: the verb is expanded into “stays” with the adjective “awake”, and the adverb falls within that expression.

> 79 come ciascun menava spesso il morso  
> 80 dell' unghie sopra sè per la gran rabbia  
> 81 del pizzicor, che non ha più soccorso.  

as each of these plied thick the clawing of his nails upon himself, for the great fury of their itch which has no other succour.

- *ciascun* ‘each’ → “each of these”: “of these” explicitly identifies the people referred to.
- *spesso* ‘often’ → “thick”: frequency is expressed through an idiom suggesting rapid succession.
- *il morso* ‘the bite’ → “the clawing”: the metaphor of biting is replaced by a name for the scratching action.
- *dell’unghie* ‘of the nails’ → “of his nails”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *del pizzicor* ‘of the itching’ → “of their itch”: a plural possessive replaces the definite article, referring collectively to the sufferers.
- *che non ha più soccorso* ‘which has no more succour’ → “which has no other succour”: *più* is interpreted as ‘other’ rather than ‘more’ or ‘further’.

> 82 E sì traevan giù l' unghie la scabbia,  
> 83 come coltel di scardova le scaglie,  
> 84 o d' altro pesce che più larghe l' abbia.  

And so the nails drew down the scurf, as does a knife the scales from bream or other fish that has them larger.

- *traevan giù l’unghie* ‘drew down the nails’ → “the nails drew down”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *come coltel* ‘as a knife’ → “as does a knife”: “does” supplies the verb left implicit in Italian.
- *di scardova le scaglie* ‘of bream the scales’ → “the scales from bream”: the dependent phrase moves after the noun, and “from” expresses removal rather than possession.
- *o d’altro pesce* ‘or of other fish’ → “or other fish”: the preposition is not repeated; the preceding “from” governs both alternatives.
- *che più larghe l’abbia* ‘that has them broader’ → “that has them larger”: ‘broader’ becomes “larger”; the adjective moves from before the pronoun and verb to after them, and the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.

> 85 "O tu che con le dita ti dismaglie,"  
> 86 cominciò il duca mio all' un di loro,  
> 87 "e che fai d' esse talvolta tanaglie,  

“ O thou! ” began my Guide to one of them, “ who with thy fingers dismailest thyself, and sometimes makest pincers of them;

- *che con le dita ti dismaglie* ‘who with the fingers thyself dismailest’ → “who with thy fingers dismailest thyself”: the relative clause moves after the reporting clause; “thy” replaces the definite article, and the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *cominciò il duca mio* ‘began the guide my’ → “began my Guide”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *all’un di loro* ‘to the one of them’ → “to one of them”: the definite article is omitted.
- *e che fai d’esse talvolta tanaglie* ‘and who makest of them sometimes pincers’ → “and sometimes makest pincers of them”: the repeated relative pronoun is omitted, the adverb moves before the verb, and “pincers” moves before “of them”.

## Lines 88–120 — The Alchemist’s Condemnation

> 88 dinne s' alcun Latino è tra costoro  
> 89 che son quinc' entro, se l' unghia ti basti  
> 90 eternalmente a cotesto lavoro.”  

tell [us] if there be any Latian among these who are here within; so may thy nails eternally suffice thee for that work.”

- *s' alcun Latino è* ‘if any Latian is’ → “if there be any Latian”: the subject follows the verb in an existential construction with supplied “there”; indicative ‘is’ becomes subjunctive “be”.
- *se* ‘if’ → “so may”: the Italian conditional form expresses a wish, which English makes explicit.
- *l' unghia* ‘the nail’ → “thy nails”: the singular becomes plural, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *ti basti eternalmente* ‘thee suffice eternally’ → “eternally suffice thee”: the adverb moves before the verb, and the object pronoun follows it.

> 91 “Latin sem noi, che tu vedi sì guasti  
> 92 qui ambo e due,” rispose l' un piangendo;  

“Latians are we, whom thou seest so disfigured here, both of us,” replied the one weeping;

- *guasti* ‘spoiled’ → “disfigured”: the translation specifies damage to appearance.
- *ambo e due* ‘both and two’ → “both of us”: the reinforcing combination is reduced to “both”, with “of us” supplied.

> 93 “ma tu chi se', che di noi domandasti?”  

“but who art thou that hast inquired of us?”

- *di noi domandasti* ‘about us didst inquire’ → “hast inquired of us”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, and the prepositional phrase moves after the verb.

> 94 E il duca disse: “Io son un che discendo  
> 95 con questo vivo giù di balzo in balzo,  
> 96 e di mostrar l' inferno a lui intendo.”  

And the Guide said : “I am one, who with this *living man* descend from steep to steep, and mean to show him Hell.”

- *duca* ‘leader’ → “Guide”: leadership is rendered more specifically as guidance.
- *discendo ... giù* ‘descend ... down’ → “descend”: the separate downward adverb is absorbed into the verb.
- *con questo vivo* ‘with this living one’ → “with this *living man*”: the substantivized adjective becomes an adjective modifying the supplied noun “man”; the phrase moves before “descend”.
- *di balzo in balzo* ‘from ledge to ledge’ → “from steep to steep”: “steep”, used as a noun, replaces the more specific term for a ledge or rocky height.
- *di mostrar l' inferno a lui intendo* ‘to show Hell to him I intend’ → “mean to show him Hell”: the governing verb moves before its infinitive, and the prepositional indirect object becomes an unprepositioned object before “Hell”.

> 97 Allor si ruppe lo comun rincalzo;  
> 98 e tremando ciascuno a me si volse  
> 99 con altri che l' udiron di rimbalzo.  

Then the mutual propping broke, and each turned trembling towards me, with others that by echo heard him.

- *si ruppe* ‘broke itself’ → “broke”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes a plain English intransitive verb, placed after its subject.
- *lo comun rincalzo* ‘the common support’ → “the mutual propping”: “mutual” makes the reciprocal relationship explicit, while “propping” presents the support as an activity.
- *e tremando ciascuno a me si volse* ‘and trembling each towards me turned himself’ → “and each turned trembling towards me”: the participle and directional phrase move after the verb; the reflexive pronoun is not separately expressed.
- *di rimbalzo* ‘by rebound’ → “by echo”: the metaphor of rebounding becomes specifically acoustic, and the phrase moves before “heard him”.

> 100 Lo buon maestro a me tutto s' accolse,  
> 101 dicendo: “Di' a lor ciò che tu vuoli.”  

The kind Master to me directed himself wholly, saying: “Tell them what thou wishest.”

- *buon* ‘good’ → “kind”: the general adjective becomes a more specific description of disposition.
- *a me tutto s' accolse* ‘to me wholly gathered himself’ → “to me directed himself wholly”: the verb of gathering is interpreted as directing oneself, and the adverb moves after the verb and reflexive pronoun.
- *Di' a lor* ‘Say to them’ → “Tell them”: the change of verb allows the indirect object without a preposition.
- *ciò che* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun become a single fused relative.

> 102 Ed io incominciai, poscia ch' ei volse:  

And I began, as he desired:

- *poscia ch' ei volse* ‘after he wished’ → “as he desired”: the temporal conjunction becomes an expression of accordance with his wish.

> 103 “Se la vostra memoria non s' imboli  
> 104 nel primo mondo dall' umane menti,  
> 105 ma s' ella viva sotto molti soli,  
> 106 ditemi chi voi siete e di che genti;  

“So may your memory not fade away from human minds in the first world, but may it live under many suns, tell me who ye are, and of what people;

- *Se* ‘If’ → “So may”: the conditional form is rendered explicitly as a wish.
- *non s' imboli* ‘not be stolen away’ → “not fade away”: the metaphor of theft becomes one of gradual disappearance.
- *nel primo mondo dall' umane menti* ‘in the first world from human minds’ → “from human minds in the first world”: the two prepositional phrases exchange places.
- *ma s' ella viva* ‘but if it live’ → “but may it live”: the second conditional form is likewise made explicitly optative.
- *di che genti* ‘of what peoples’ → “of what people”: the plural noun becomes singular or collective.

> 107 la vostra sconcia e fastidiosa pena  
> 108 di palesarvi a me non vi spaventi.”  

let not your ugly and disgusting punishment frighten you from revealing yourselves to me.”

- *non vi spaventi* ‘may it not frighten you’ → “let not” … “frighten you”: the independent subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”; the negative precedes the subject, and the object pronoun follows “frighten”.
- *di palesarvi a me* ‘of revealing yourselves to me’ → “from revealing yourselves to me”: “from” expresses deterrence in English, and the phrase moves after the verb that governs it.

> 109 “Io fui d' Arezzo, ed Albero da Siena,”  
> 110 rispose l' un, “mi fe' mettere al foco;  
> 111 ma quel per ch' io mori' qui non mi mena.  

“I was of Arezzo,” replied the one, “and Albero of Siena had me burned; but what I died for does not bring me here.

- *Io fui d' Arezzo, ed Albero da Siena ... rispose l' un* ‘I was of Arezzo, and Albero of Siena ... replied the one’ → “I was of Arezzo,” … “replied the one,” … “and Albero of Siena”: the reporting clause moves before the mention of Albero.
- *mi fe' mettere al foco* ‘made me be put to the fire’ → “had me burned”: the causative becomes “had”, and being put to the fire is compressed into “burned”.
- *quel per ch' io mori'* ‘that for which I died’ → “what I died for”: the demonstrative and relative become “what”, and the preposition moves to the end of the clause.
- *qui non mi mena* ‘here does not lead me’ → “does not bring me here”: ‘lead’ becomes “bring”; the locative moves to the end, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 112 Ver è ch' io dissi a lui, parlando a gioco,  
> 113 ‘Io mi saprei levar per l' aere a volo';  

’Tis true, I said to him, speaking in jest : ‘I could raise myself through the air in flight’;

- *Ver è ch'* ‘True it is that’ → “’Tis true,”: the subject precedes the predicate adjective, and the subordinating conjunction is omitted.
- *Io mi saprei levar* ‘I myself should know how to raise’ → “I could raise myself”: conditional ‘know how’ becomes the modal “could”, and the reflexive pronoun moves after the infinitive.

> 114 e quei, che avea vaghezza e senno poco,  
> 115 volle ch' io gli mostrassi l' arte; e solo  
> 116 perch' io nol feci Dedalo, mi fece  
> 117 ardere a tal, che l' avea per figliuolo.  

and he, who had a fond desire and little wit, willed that I should shew him the art; and only because I made him not a Dædalus, he made me be burned by one who had him for a son.

- *quei* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *vaghezza* ‘desire’ → “a fond desire”: an indefinite article and the qualifying adjective “fond” are supplied.
- *senno poco* ‘wit little’ → “little wit”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *ch' io gli mostrassi l' arte* ‘that I to him showed the art’ → “that I should shew him the art”: the imperfect subjunctive is rendered with “should”, and the dative pronoun becomes an unprepositioned object after the verb.
- *perch' io nol feci Dedalo* ‘because I not him made Daedalus’ → “because I made him not a Dædalus”: the negative and object pronoun move after the verb; the supplied indefinite article presents the name as a type rather than simply an identity.
- *mi fece ardere a tal* ‘made me burn by such a one’ → “he made me be burned by one”: the infinitive becomes explicitly passive, and ‘such a one’ is reduced to “one”.
- *che l' avea per figliuolo* ‘who had him for son’ → “who had him for a son”: an indefinite article is supplied before the predicate noun.

> 118 Ma nell' ultima bolgia delle diece  
> 119 me per alchimia, che nel mondo usai,  
> 120 dannò Minos, a cui fallir non lece.”  

But to the last budget of the ten, for the alchemy that I practised in the world, Minos, who may not err, condemned me.”

- *nell' ultima bolgia delle diece* ‘in the last pouch of the ten’ → “to the last budget of the ten”: the preposition is rendered as a destination, “to”, rather than a location.
- *per alchimia* ‘for alchemy’ → “for the alchemy”: the definite article is supplied.
- *che nel mondo usai* ‘which in the world I used’ → “that I practised in the world”: ‘used’ becomes the activity-specific “practised”, and the locative phrase moves after the verb.
- *me ... dannò Minos* ‘me ... condemned Minos’ → “Minos” … “condemned me”: the initially placed object moves after the verb, while the subject moves before it.
- *a cui fallir non lece* ‘to whom to err is not permitted’ → “who may not err”: the impersonal construction with a dative relative becomes a personal construction with a subject relative and modal verb.

## Lines 121–139 — Sienese Vanity and Capocchio’s Identity

> 121 Ed io dissi al poeta: “Or fu giammai  
> 122 gente sì vana come la sanese?  

And I said to the Poet: "Now was there ever people so vain as the Sienese?

- *fu giammai* ‘was ever’ → “was there ever”: English supplies existential “there”.

> 123 certo non la francesca sì d’ assai.”  

certainly the French not so by far."

- *non la francesca* ‘not the French’ → “the French not”: the negative moves after the subject.
- *d’ assai* ‘by much’ → “by far”: an English idiom replaces the Italian expression of degree.

> 124 Onde l’ altro lebbroso che m’ intese  
> 125 rispose al detto mio: “Trammene Stricca,  
> 126 che seppe far le temperate spese;  

Whereat the other leper, who heard me, responded to my words: "Except Stricca who contrived to spend so moderately;

- *Onde* ‘whence’ → “Whereat”: the connective is rendered as a response to the preceding remark.
- *m’ intese* ‘me heard’ → “heard me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *al detto mio* ‘to the saying mine’ → “to my words”: singular *detto* becomes plural “words”, and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *Trammene* ‘take out of it for me’ → “Except”: the imperative becomes an instruction to make an exception; the pronouns meaning ‘for me’ and ‘out of it’ are not separately rendered.
- *seppe far le temperate spese* ‘knew how to make the moderate expenditures’ → “contrived to spend so moderately”: knowing how becomes contriving; the verb-and-noun construction becomes “spend”, the adjective becomes an adverb, and “so” is supplied.

> 127 e Niccolò, che la costuma ricca  
> 128 del garofano prima discoperse  
> 129 nell’ orto dove tal seme s’ appicca;  

and Niccolò, who first discovered the costly usage of the clove, in the garden where such seed takes root;

- *la costuma ricca del garofano prima discoperse* ‘the rich usage of the clove first discovered’ → “first discovered the costly usage of the clove”: the object moves after the verb; *ricca* becomes “costly”, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *s’ appicca* ‘attaches itself’ → “takes root”: the reflexive verb becomes a non-reflexive English idiom.

> 130 e tranne la brigata, in che disperse  
> 131 Caccia d’ Ascian la vigna e la gran fronda,  
> 132 e l’ Abbagliato il suo senno proferse.  

and except the company in which Caccia of Asciano squandered his vineyard and his great forest, and the Abbagliato shewed his wit.

- *tranne* ‘take out of it’ → “except”: the imperative is rendered idiomatically, without separately expressing *ne*, ‘out of it’.
- *disperse Caccia d’ Ascian* ‘squandered Caccia of Asciano’ → “Caccia of Asciano squandered”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *la vigna* ‘the vineyard’ → “his vineyard”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *la gran fronda* ‘the great foliage’ → “his great forest”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “forest” replaces the collective expression ‘foliage’.
- *il suo senno proferse* ‘his judgement offered’ → “shewed his wit”: the object moves after the verb; ‘offered’ becomes “shewed”, and ‘judgement’ becomes “wit”.

> 133 Ma perchè sappi chi sì ti seconda  
> 134 contra i Sanesi, aguzza ver me l’ occhio  
> 135 sì che la faccia mia ben ti risponda;  

But that thou mayest know who thus seconds thee against the Sienese, sharpen thine eye towards me, that my face may give thee right response;

- *perchè sappi* ‘so that thou know’ → “that thou mayest know”: the purpose clause’s subjunctive is expressed with “mayest”.
- *ti seconda* ‘thee seconds’ → “seconds thee”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *aguzza ver me l’ occhio* ‘sharpen towards me the eye’ → “sharpen thine eye towards me”: the direct object moves before the directional phrase, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *sì che* ‘so that’ → “that”: *sì* is not separately rendered.
- *la faccia mia* ‘the face mine’ → “my face”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *ben ti risponda* ‘well to thee respond’ → “may give thee right response”: the subjunctive becomes a construction with “may”; the verb becomes “give” plus a noun, and the adverb *ben* becomes the adjective “right”.

> 136 sì vedrai ch’ io son l’ ombra di Capocchio,  
> 137 che falsai li metalli con alchimia;  

so shalt thou see I am the shadow of Capocchio, who falsified the metals by alchemy;

- *vedrai* ‘thou wilt see’ → “shalt thou see”: the future is expressed with “shalt”, with subject–auxiliary inversion after “so”.
- *ch’ io son* ‘that I am’ → “I am”: the conjunction introducing the reported statement is omitted.
- *con alchimia* ‘with alchemy’ → “by alchemy”: the preposition expresses method rather than using the literal ‘with’.

> 138 e ti dei ricordar, se ben t’ adocchio,  
> 139 com’ io fui di natura buona scimia.”  

and thou must recollect, if I rightly eye thee, how good an ape I was of Nature."

- *ti dei ricordar* ‘thou must remind thyself’ → “thou must recollect”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes a non-reflexive English verb.
- *se ben t’ adocchio* ‘if well thee I eye’ → “if I rightly eye thee”: *ben* becomes “rightly”, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *com’ io fui di natura buona scimia* ‘how I was of nature a good ape’ → “how good an ape I was of Nature”: “how” directly modifies “good”, the predicate noun phrase moves before the subject and verb, and “of Nature” moves to the end.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Reshaping

Carlyle’s translation stays close to the Italian’s sequence of expressions and often preserves its distinctive imagery and vocabulary: *inebriate* becomes “drunken,” *chiostra* “cloister,” *conversi* “lay-brethren,” and *ti dismaglie* “dismailest thyself.” Yet this closeness regularly yields to English word order. Subjects and verbs move before objects or complements postponed or anticipated in Italian; possessives and adjectives generally precede their nouns; and dependent phrases follow the words that govern them. Thus *che miglia ventidue la valle volge* becomes “that the valley goes round two-and-twenty miles.” The translation nevertheless retains occasional inversions and unusual arrangements, alongside its archaic pronouns and verb forms.

Changes of construction often make Italian expressions more explicit or more idiomatic in English. Adjectival descriptions become verbs, as *eran vaghe* becomes “they longed,” while *più pio* becomes “pity him the more.” Substantival infinitives become ordinary infinitives, and pronominal constructions may become passive or non-reflexive: *si ristorar* becomes “were restored,” and *si ruppe* simply “broke.” Wishes and independent subjunctives receive constructions with “may” or “let”; *Se tu avessi* becomes the inverted condition “Hadst thou.” Italian imperfects are frequently rendered by simple pasts, though progressive forms appear where the translator emphasizes continuing action.

Vocabulary ranges from close correspondence to interpretation. *Luci* becomes “eyes,” *bolge* “chasms,” and *ragazzo* “stable-boy”: metaphor may be removed, a topographical description substituted, or a general term narrowed by context. Elsewhere one image replaces another: *non s’ imboli*, ‘not be stolen away,’ becomes “not fade away,” and *il morso*, ‘the bite,’ becomes “the clawing.” Connectives and adverbs also receive interpretive renderings: *poscia ch’*, ‘after,’ becomes “as,” while *pur* becomes “again” rather than ‘still.’ Such choices sometimes alter emphasis or the relation between clauses rather than merely adjusting their wording.

Small additions and omissions recur throughout. English supplies existential “there,” explanatory nouns such as “things” and “man,” and an unspecified agent in “heard *them* call him.” Definite articles before body parts frequently become possessives; repeated prepositions, relative pronouns, and some pronominal elements disappear, as does *or* in the description of Dante’s fixed gaze. More generally, beyond the individual changes catalogued above, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and omits the Italian definite article before possessives. These routine adjustments help produce readable English without disturbing the translation’s generally close attachment to the Italian’s phrasing.
