# Inferno 15

## Lines 1–24 — Along the Banks, a Surprised Recognition

> 1 Ora cen porta l' un de' duri margini,  
> 2 e il fummo del ruscel di sopra aduggia  
> 3 sì che dal foco salva l' acqua e gli argini.  

Now one of the hard margins bears us on, and the smoke of the rivulet makes shade above, so that from the fire it shelters the water and the banks.

- *Ora cen porta l' un de' duri margini* ‘Now carries us onward the one of the hard margins’ → “Now one of the hard margins bears us on”: the subject moves before the verb; *l’un* becomes “one” without the definite article; and *cen porta* is expressed by “bears us on”.
- *aduggia* ‘shades’ → “makes shade”: a single verb becomes a verb-and-noun phrase.
- *salva* ‘saves’ → “shelters”: preservation is expressed as protection.

> 4 Quale i Fiamminghi tra Guizzante e Bruggia,  
> 5 temendo il fiotto che ver lor s' avventa,  
> 6 fanno lo schermo perchè il mar si fuggia;  

As the Flemings between Bruges and [Wissant], dreading the flood that rushes towards them, make their bulwark to repel the sea;

- *tra Guizzante e Bruggia* ‘between Wissant and Bruges’ → “between Bruges and [Wissant]”: the order of the two places is reversed.
- *temendo* ‘fearing’ → “dreading”: the translation chooses a stronger expression of fear.
- *il fiotto* ‘the surge’ → “the flood”: the word for a surge or rush of water is rendered more broadly.
- *che ver lor s' avventa* ‘that towards them hurls itself’ → “that rushes towards them”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and the directional phrase moves after it.
- *lo schermo* ‘the barrier’ → “their bulwark”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the general protective barrier becomes a “bulwark”.
- *perchè il mar si fuggia* ‘so that the sea may flee’ → “to repel the sea”: the finite purpose clause becomes an infinitive; the sea changes from the subject of retreating to the object of repulsion.

> 7 e quale i Padovan lungo la Brenta,  
> 8 per difender lor ville e lor castelli,  
> 9 anzi che Chiarentana il caldo senta :  

and as the Paduans, along the Brenta, to defend their villages and [castles] ere Chiarentana feels the heat:

- *lor castelli* ‘their castles’ → “[castles]”: the repeated possessive is omitted.
- *Chiarentana il caldo senta* ‘Chiarentana the heat may feel’ → “Chiarentana feels the heat”: the object moves after the verb, and the Italian subjunctive is represented by an English present indicative within the temporal clause.

> 10 a tale imagine eran fatti quelli,  
> 11 tutto che nè sì alti nè sì grossi,  
> 12 qual che si fosse, lo maestro felli.  

in like fashion those banks were formed, though not so high nor so large, the master, whoever it might be, made them.

- *a tale imagine* ‘in such an image’ → “in like fashion”: the expression of likeness becomes an idiom describing manner.
- *eran fatti quelli* ‘were made those’ → “those banks were formed”: the demonstrative subject moves before the verb and is expanded with “banks”; “formed” replaces ‘made’.
- *nè sì alti* ‘neither so high’ → “not so high”: the first member of the paired negative construction is rendered with “not” rather than ‘neither’.
- *sì grossi* ‘so thick’ → “so large”: a particular dimension becomes a more general description of size.
- *qual che si fosse* ‘whoever he might be’ → “whoever it might be”: “it” replaces the masculine personal reference, and the qualification moves from before *lo maestro* to after “the master”.

> 13 Già eravam dalla selva rimossi  
> 14 tanto, ch' io non avrei visto dov' era,  
> 15 perch' io indietro rivolto mi fossi,  

Already we were so far removed from the wood, that I should not have seen where it was, had I turned back,

- *Già eravam dalla selva rimossi tanto* ‘Already we were from the wood removed so much’ → “Already we were so far removed from the wood”: the phrase identifying the starting point moves after “removed”; *tanto* moves before it and is expressed spatially as “so far”.
- *non avrei visto* ‘I would not have seen’ → “I should not have seen”: the conditional uses the traditional first-person English auxiliary “should” rather than ‘would’.
- *perch' io indietro rivolto mi fossi* ‘even if I had turned myself backwards’ → “had I turned back”: the concessive force of *perch’* is not explicitly retained; the conjunction is replaced by conditional inversion, and the reflexive construction becomes the intransitive “turned back”.

> 16 quando incontrammo d' anime una schiera,  
> 17 che venia lungo l' argine; e ciascuna  
> 18 ci riguardava, come suol da sera  
> 19 guardar l' un l' altro sotto nuova luna;  

when we met a troop of spirits, who were coming alongside the bank; and each looked at us, as in the evening men are wont to look at one another under a new moon;

- *d' anime una schiera* ‘of souls a troop’ → “a troop of spirits”: the modifying phrase moves after its noun, and “spirits” replaces ‘souls’.
- *che venia* ‘which was coming’ → “who were coming”: the singular agreement with *schiera* becomes plural agreement with its members, and the relative pronoun likewise refers to the individuals.
- *lungo l' argine* ‘along the bank’ → “alongside the bank”: the translation specifies a position beside the bank.
- *ci riguardava* ‘us looked at’ → “looked at us”: the pronominal object moves after the verb and its English preposition.
- *come suol da sera guardar l' un l' altro* ‘as one is wont at evening to look at the other’ → “as in the evening men are wont to look at one another”: “men” is supplied as a generic plural subject, the singular verb becomes plural, and *l’un l’altro* becomes the reciprocal “one another”; the temporal phrase moves before the subject and verb.
- *sotto nuova luna* ‘under new moon’ → “under a new moon”: English supplies an indefinite article.

> 20 e sì ver noi aguzzavan le ciglia,  
> 21 come vecchio sartor fa nella cruna.  

and towards us sharpened their vision, as an aged tailor does at the eye of his needle.

- *e sì ver noi aguzzavan le ciglia* ‘and so towards us they sharpened the brows’ → “and towards us sharpened their vision”: *sì* is omitted; the bodily image of sharpening the brows is rendered as sharpening “vision”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *vecchio sartor* ‘old tailor’ → “an aged tailor”: an indefinite article is supplied, and “aged” replaces ‘old’.
- *nella cruna* ‘in the needle’s eye’ → “at the eye of his needle”: “at” replaces ‘in’, and the compact noun *cruna* is expanded into “the eye of his needle”, with “his” supplied.

> 22 Così adocchiato da cotal famiglia,  
> 23 fui conosciuto da un, che mi prese  
> 24 per lo lembo e gridò: "Qual maraviglia!"  

Thus eyed by that family, I was recognised by one who took me by the skirt, and said: “What a wonder!”

- *cotal famiglia* ‘such a family’ → “that family”: a description of kind becomes a demonstrative reference.
- *fui conosciuto* ‘I was known’ → “I was recognised”: the translation selects the sense of recognition rather than the state of being known.
- *che mi prese* ‘who me took’ → “who took me”: the pronominal object moves after the verb.
- *lo lembo* ‘the hem’ → “the skirt”: the translation names the hanging portion of the garment rather than its edge.
- *gridò* ‘cried out’ → “said”: the force of the vocal action is reduced to neutral speech.
- *Qual maraviglia* ‘What wonder’ → “What a wonder!”: English supplies an indefinite article.

## Lines 25–57 — Dante Speaks With Brunetto

> 25 Ed io, quando il suo braccio a me distese,  
> 26 ficcai gli occhi per lo cotto aspetto  
> 27 sì che il viso abbruciato non difese  
> 28 la conoscenza sua al mio intelletto;  

And I, when he stretched out his arm to me, fixed my eyes on his baked aspect, so that the scorching of his visage hindered not my mind from knowing him;

- *il suo braccio a me distese* ‘his arm to me he stretched out’ → “he stretched out his arm to me”: the verb precedes its object and the phrase indicating the recipient, rather than following them.
- *ficcai gli occhi* ‘I thrust the eyes’ → “fixed my eyes”: the forceful verb becomes “fixed,” and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *per lo cotto aspetto* ‘over the baked aspect’ → “on his baked aspect”: “on” replaces *per*, and the definite article becomes “his.”
- *il viso abbruciato* ‘the face burnt’ → “the scorching of his visage”: the participle becomes the noun “scorching,” while the face becomes its dependent phrase; “his” replaces the definite article.
- *non difese la conoscenza sua al mio intelletto* ‘did not withhold recognition of him from my intellect’ → “hindered not my mind from knowing him”: the recipient of recognition becomes the direct object of “hindered”; the noun *conoscenza* becomes the verbal phrase “knowing him,” with *sua* understood objectively. “Mind” replaces ‘intellect,’ and the negative follows the verb.

> 29 e chinando la mia alla sua faccia,  
> 30 risposi: “Siete voi qui, ser Brunetto?”  

and bending my face to his, I answered: “Are you here, Ser Brunetto?”

- *chinando la mia alla sua faccia* ‘bending mine to his face’ → “bending my face to his”: the noun understood after *mia* is supplied, while the noun explicitly attached to *sua* is left understood.

> 31 E quegli: “O figliuol mio, non ti dispiaccia  
> 32 se Brunetto Latini un poco teco  
> 33 ritorna indietro, e lascia andar la traccia.”  

And he: “O my son! let it not displease thee, if Brunetto Latini turn back with thee a little, and let go his train.”

- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *figliuol mio* ‘son my’ → “my son”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *non ti dispiaccia* ‘may it not displease thee’ → “let it not displease thee”: the independent subjunctive is expressed by a construction with “let”; the object pronoun follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *un poco teco ritorna indietro* ‘a little with thee returns back’ → “turn back with thee a little”: the verb moves before both accompanying phrases, whose relative order is reversed. ‘Returns back’ becomes “turn back,” and the Italian present indicative becomes an English subjunctive.
- *lascia andar la traccia* ‘lets go the train’ → “let go his train”: the indicative becomes an English subjunctive, and the definite article becomes “his.”

> 34 Io dissi lui: “Quanto posso ven preco;  

I said: “With all my power I do beseech it of you;

- *Io dissi lui* ‘I said to him’ → “I said”: the recipient *lui* is omitted.
- *Quanto posso* ‘as much as I can’ → “With all my power”: a clause expressing the extent of ability becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *ven preco* ‘you for it I beseech’ → “I do beseech it of you”: the preverbal pronouns follow the verb; the person besought becomes “of you,” while the thing requested becomes the direct object “it.” Emphatic “do” is supplied.

> 35 e se volete che con voi m' asseggia,  
> 36 faròl, se piace a costui, chè vo seco.”  

and if you wish me to sit down with you, I will do so, if it pleases him there, for I go with him.”

- *volete che con voi m’ asseggia* ‘you wish that with you I sit down’ → “you wish me to sit down with you”: the finite subordinate clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction, and “with you” moves after the verb.
- *faròl* ‘I will do it’ → “I will do so”: the object pronoun incorporated in the Italian verb is represented by “so.”
- *se piace a costui* ‘if it pleases this man’ → “if it pleases him there”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun supplemented by “there.”

> 37 “O figliuol,” disse, “qual di questa greggia  
> 38 s’ arresta punto, giace poi cent’ anni  
> 39 senza arrostarsi quando il foco il feggia.  

“O my son,” he said, “whoever of this flock stops one instant, lies a hundred years thereafter without fanning himself when the fire strikes him.

- *O figliuol* ‘O son’ → “O my son”: “my” is supplied.
- *s’ arresta punto* ‘stops himself at all’ → “stops one instant”: the pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive verb, and *punto* is given an explicitly temporal rendering.
- *giace poi cent’ anni* ‘lies then a hundred years’ → “lies a hundred years thereafter”: *poi* moves after the duration and becomes the more explicitly relational “thereafter.”
- *il feggia* ‘him strike’ → “strikes him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the Italian present subjunctive becomes an English present indicative.

> 40 Però va oltre; io ti verrò a’ panni,  

Therefore go on; I will follow at thy skirts;

- *io ti verrò a’ panni* ‘I shall come to thee at the clothes’ → “I will follow at thy skirts”: ‘come’ becomes “follow,” the dative *ti* is represented by the possessive “thy,” and the general *panni* becomes the more specific “skirts.”

> 41 e poi rigiugnerò la mia masnada,  
> 42 che va piangendo i suoi eterni danni.”  

and then will I rejoin my band, that go lamenting their eternal losses.”

- *rigiugnerò* ‘I shall rejoin’ → “will I rejoin”: the English subject follows the auxiliary rather than occupying its ordinary position before it.
- *che va piangendo i suoi eterni danni* ‘that goes weeping its eternal losses’ → “that go lamenting their eternal losses”: singular agreement with *masnada* becomes plural agreement with the members of the band, in both “go” and “their.” *Piangendo* is rendered as lamentation rather than simply weeping.

> 43 Io non osava scender della strada  
> 44 per andar par di lui; ma il capo chino  
> 45 tenea, come uom che reverente vada.  

I durst not descend from the road to go level with him; but kept my head bent down, like one who walks in reverence.

- *Io non osava* ‘I did not dare’ → “I durst not”: the negative follows the lexical verb rather than preceding it; the Italian imperfect is represented by the English simple past.
- *par di lui* ‘equal to him’ → “level with him”: equality is expressed specifically as physical level, with a corresponding change of preposition.
- *il capo chino tenea* ‘the head bent I kept’ → “kept my head bent down”: the verb moves before its object and the adjective; the definite article becomes “my,” and “down” is supplied.
- *come uom che reverente vada* ‘like a man who goes reverent’ → “like one who walks in reverence”: *uom* becomes the indefinite “one”; the adjective becomes the prepositional phrase “in reverence” and moves after the verb. *Vada* is rendered by the more specific “walks,” with indicative replacing subjunctive.

> 46 Ei cominciò: “Qual fortuna o destino  
> 47 anzi l’ ultimo dì quaggiù ti mena?  
> 48 e chi è questi che mostra il cammino?”  

He began: “What chance, or destiny, brings thee, ere thy last day, down here? and who is this that shews the way?”

- *fortuna* ‘fortune’ → “chance”: the translation selects the sense of chance rather than retaining the broader ‘fortune.’
- *anzi l’ ultimo dì quaggiù ti mena* ‘before the last day down here thee leads’ → “brings thee, ere thy last day, down here”: the verb and its object move before both adverbial phrases, and the object follows rather than precedes the verb. ‘Leads’ becomes “brings,” and the definite article before *ultimo dì* becomes “thy.”

> 49 “Lassù di sopra in la vita serena,”  
> 50 rispos’ io lui, “mi smarri’ in una valle,  
> 51 avanti che l’ età mia fosse piena.  

“There above, up in the clear life, I lost myself,” replied I, “in a valley, before my age was full.

- *Lassù di sopra* ‘up there above’ → “There above, up”: the spatial expressions are redistributed, with “up” placed immediately before the following phrase.
- *la vita serena* ‘the life serene’ → “the clear life”: “clear” replaces ‘serene,’ and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *rispos’ io lui* ‘replied I to him’ → “replied I”: the recipient is omitted, and the reporting clause moves from before the statement of becoming lost to after “I lost myself.”
- *mi smarri’* ‘myself I lost’ → “I lost myself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *avanti che* ‘before that’ → “before”: the two-word conjunction becomes a single conjunction.
- *l’ età mia* ‘the age mine’ → “my age”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *fosse* ‘were’ → “was”: the Italian imperfect subjunctive becomes an English past indicative.

> 52 Pur ier mattina le volsi le spalle;  

Only yester morn I turned my back to it;

- *le volsi le spalle* ‘to it I turned the shoulders’ → “I turned my back to it”: the indirect-object pronoun moves to the end; plural ‘shoulders’ becomes singular “back,” following the English idiom, and the definite article becomes “my.”

> 53 questi m’ apparve, tornand’ io in quella,  
> 54 e riducemi a ca per questo calle.”  

he appeared to me, as I was returning into it, and guides me home again by this path.”

- *questi m’ apparve* ‘this one to me appeared’ → “he appeared to me”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *tornand’ io in quella* ‘returning I into that’ → “as I was returning into it”: the gerund construction becomes a finite temporal clause with “as” and a past-progressive verb. The subject moves before the verb, and the demonstrative becomes “it.”
- *riducemi a ca* ‘leads me back to home’ → “guides me home again”: the verb’s sense of return is expressed separately by “again,” placed after the destination; ‘leads’ becomes “guides,” and the destination is expressed by adverbial “home” without a preposition.
- *per questo calle* ‘through this path’ → “by this path”: the route is expressed with “by” rather than ‘through.’

> 55 Ed egli a me: “Se tu segui tua stella,  
> 56 non puoi fallire al glorioso porto,  
> 57 se ben m’ accorsi nella vita bella;  

And he to me: “If thou follow thy star, thou canst not fail of glorious haven, if I discerned rightly in the fair life;

- *segui* ‘followest’ → “follow”: the Italian present indicative becomes an English subjunctive in the conditional clause.
- *non puoi* ‘not canst’ → “thou canst not”: the negative moves after the modal verb.
- *fallire al glorioso porto* ‘fail at the glorious haven’ → “fail of glorious haven”: the Italian construction with *a* becomes the English idiom “fail of,” and the definite article is omitted.
- *se ben m’ accorsi* ‘if well I became aware’ → “if I discerned rightly”: the pronominal expression becomes the simple verb “discerned”; *ben* becomes “rightly” and moves after the verb.
- *nella vita bella* ‘in the life beautiful’ → “in the fair life”: the adjective moves before the noun.

## Lines 58–90 — Enmity Foretold and Gratitude Remembered

> 58 e s' io non fossi sì per tempo morto,  
> 59 veggendo il cielo a te così benigno,  
> 60 dato t' avrei all' opera conforto.  

and if I had not died so early, seeing heaven so kind to thee, I would have cheered thee in the work.

- *s' io non fossi sì per tempo morto* ‘if I had not so early died’ → “if I had not died so early”: the Italian pluperfect subjunctive becomes an English counterfactual past perfect, and the expression of time moves after the verb.
- *il cielo* ‘the heaven’ → “heaven”: the definite article is omitted.
- *a te così benigno* ‘to thee so kind’ → “so kind to thee”: the complement follows the adjective instead of preceding it.
- *dato t' avrei all' opera conforto* ‘given thee I would have, for the work, encouragement’ → “I would have cheered thee in the work”: giving encouragement becomes the verb “cheered,” with the recipient as its direct object; the inverted verbal construction is reordered, and *all' opera* becomes “in the work.”

> 61 Ma quell' ingrato popolo maligno,  
> 62 che discese di Fiesole ab antico  
> 63 e tiene ancor del monte e del macigno,  
> 64 ti si farà, per tuo ben far, nimico:  

But that ungrateful, malignant people, who of old came down from Fiesole, and still savours of the mountain and the rock, will make itself an enemy to thee for thy good deeds;

- *quell' ingrato popolo maligno* ‘that ungrateful people malignant’ → “that ungrateful, malignant people”: the second adjective moves before the noun.
- *discese di Fiesole ab antico* ‘came down from Fiesole of old’ → “of old came down from Fiesole”: the expression of time moves before the verb.
- *tiene ancor del monte e del macigno* ‘retains still something of the mountain and of the rock’ → “still savours of the mountain and the rock”: the idiom of retaining characteristics becomes “savours of”; “still” precedes the verb, and the second ‘of’ is omitted.
- *ti si farà, per tuo ben far, nimico* ‘to thee it will make itself, for thy doing well, enemy’ → “will make itself an enemy to thee for thy good deeds”: the recipient moves after “enemy,” an indefinite article is supplied, and the intervening causal phrase moves to the end. The substantivized infinitive *ben far* becomes the plural noun phrase “good deeds.”

> 65 ed è ragion: chè tra li lazzi sorbi  
> 66 si disconvien fruttare al dolce fico.  

and there is cause : for amongst the tart sorbtrees, it befits not the sweet fig to fructify.

- *ed è ragion* ‘and it is right’ → “and there is cause”: the statement of rightness or fittingness becomes an existential statement that there is a reason.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *si disconvien fruttare al dolce fico* ‘it is unfitting to bear fruit for the sweet fig’ → “it befits not the sweet fig to fructify”: the negative sense of *disconvien* becomes an explicit “not.” The impersonal construction is recast with “the sweet fig” as the object of “befits,” and the infinitive follows that object.

> 67 Vecchia fama nel mondo li chiama orbi,  
> 68 gente avara, invidiosa e superba:  
> 69 da' lor costumi fa che tu ti forbi.  

Old report on earth proclaims them blind, a people avaricious, envious, and proud : look that thou cleanse thyself of their customs.

- *nel mondo* ‘in the world’ → “on earth”: both the preposition and the noun change, and the article is omitted.
- *li chiama* ‘calls them’ → “proclaims them”: the verb becomes more emphatic, and the object pronoun follows it.
- *gente* ‘people’ → “a people”: an indefinite article makes the collective noun explicitly countable.
- *da' lor costumi* ‘from their customs’ → “of their customs”: “of” replaces ‘from,’ and the phrase moves from the beginning of the injunction to its end.
- *fa che tu ti forbi* ‘make that thou thyself cleanse’ → “look that thou cleanse thyself”: the directive construction becomes the English idiom “look that,” and the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.

> 70 La tua fortuna tanto onor ti serba,  
> 71 che l' una parte e l' altra avranno fame  
> 72 di te; ma lungi fia dal becco l' erba.  

Thy fortune reserves such honour for thee, that both parties will have a hunger of thee; but far from the goat shall be the grass.

- *tanto onor ti serba* ‘so much honour for thee reserves’ → “reserves such honour for thee”: “such” replaces the quantitative ‘so much’; the verb precedes its object, and the recipient pronoun becomes a following prepositional phrase.
- *l' una parte e l' altra* ‘the one party and the other’ → “both parties”: the two coordinated singular expressions become one plural noun phrase.
- *avranno fame di te* ‘will have hunger of thee’ → “will have a hunger of thee”: an indefinite article is supplied before “hunger.”
- *lungi fia dal becco l' erba* ‘far will be from the beak the grass’ → “far from the goat shall be the grass”: the translation takes *becco* in its alternative sense ‘he-goat,’ rather than ‘beak.’ The phrase “from the goat” also moves before the verb.

> 73 Faccian le bestie Fiesolane strame  
> 74 di lor medesme, e non tocchin la pianta,  
> 75 s' alcuna surge ancora in lor letame,  

Let the beasts of Fiesole make litter of themselves, and not touch the plant, if any yet springs up amid their rankness,

- *Faccian le bestie Fiesolane strame di lor medesme* ‘may the Fiesolan beasts make litter of themselves’ → “Let the beasts of Fiesole make litter of themselves”: the independent exhortative subjunctive becomes a “Let” construction, with the subject before “make” rather than after the Italian verb. The geographical adjective becomes “of Fiesole.”
- *e non tocchin la pianta* ‘and may they not touch the plant’ → “and not touch the plant”: the second subjunctive becomes a bare infinitive governed by the preceding “Let.”
- *s' alcuna surge ancora* ‘if any rises still’ → “if any yet springs up”: “springs up” replaces ‘rises,’ and the temporal adverb moves before the verb.
- *in lor letame* ‘in their dung’ → “amid their rankness”: “amid” replaces ‘in,’ and the concrete noun becomes the abstract “rankness.”

> 76 in cui riviva la sementa santa  
> 77 di quei Roman, che vi rimaser, quando  
> 78 fu fatto il nido di malizia tanta.”  

in which the holy seed revives of those Romans who remained there, when the nest of so much malice [was made]."

- *in cui riviva la sementa santa* ‘in which may revive the seed holy’ → “in which the holy seed revives”: the subjunctive becomes indicative; the subject moves before the verb, and the adjective before the noun. This placement of “revives” separates “seed” from its following “of those Romans.”
- *vi rimaser* ‘there remained’ → “remained there”: the locative follows the verb instead of preceding it.
- *quando fu fatto il nido di malizia tanta* ‘when was made the nest of malice so much’ → “when the nest of so much malice [was made]”: the subject and its complement precede the passive verb instead of following it; the expression of quantity moves before “malice.”

> 79 “Se fosse tutto pieno il mio dimando,”  
> 80 risposi lui “voi non sareste ancora  
> 81 dell' umana natura posto in bando:  

“Were my desire all fulfilled,” I answered him, “you had not yet been banished from human nature

- *Se fosse tutto pieno il mio dimando* ‘if were entirely full my request’ → “Were my desire all fulfilled”: conditional inversion replaces *Se*, and the subject moves before the predicate complement. *Dimando* becomes “desire” rather than ‘request,’ while *pieno* becomes “fulfilled” rather than ‘full.’
- *risposi lui* ‘I answered to him’ → “I answered him”: the Italian dative pronoun becomes the direct object of the English verb.
- *voi non sareste ancora … posto in bando* ‘you would not yet be … put in banishment’ → “you had not yet been banished”: the present conditional becomes an English counterfactual pluperfect, and the phrase ‘put in banishment’ becomes the single participle “banished.”
- *dell' umana natura* ‘from the human nature’ → “from human nature”: the definite article is omitted.

> 82 chè in la mente m' è fitta, ed or mi accora,  
> 83 la cara e buona imagine paterna  
> 84 di voi, quando nel mondo ad ora ad ora  
> 85 m' insegnavate come l' uom s' eterna;  

: for in my memory is fixed, and now goes to my heart, the dear [and] kind, paternal image of you, when in the world, hour by hour, you taught me how man makes himself eternal;

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *in la mente m' è fitta* ‘in the mind is fixed for me’ → “in my memory is fixed”: the personal dative becomes the possessive “my,” and ‘mind’ becomes “memory.”
- *ed or mi accora* ‘and now pains me at heart’ → “and now goes to my heart”: the verb expressing heartfelt pain becomes an idiomatic phrase expressing emotional effect, without explicitly naming pain.
- *la cara e buona imagine paterna* ‘the dear and good image paternal’ → “the dear [and] kind, paternal image”: “kind” replaces ‘good,’ and “paternal” moves before the noun.
- *ad ora ad ora* ‘at hour, at hour’ → “hour by hour”: the repeated prepositional expression becomes an English “by” construction. The idiom, ordinarily meaning ‘from time to time,’ is rendered as a succession of hours.
- *m' insegnavate* ‘me you were teaching’ → “you taught me”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *come l' uom s' eterna* ‘how the man eternalizes himself’ → “how man makes himself eternal”: the generic definite article is omitted, and the reflexive verb becomes a construction with “makes,” a reflexive object, and the adjective “eternal.”

> 86 e quant' io l' abbia in grado, mentre io vivo  
> 87 convien che nella mia lingua si scerna.  

and whilst I live, beseems my tongue should shew what gratitude I have for it.

- *quant' io l' abbia in grado* ‘how much I hold it in favour’ → “what gratitude I have for it”: the idiom of valuing or appreciating something becomes an explicit expression of gratitude. The direct object pronoun becomes “for it,” the subjunctive becomes indicative, and the clause moves from the beginning to the end.
- *mentre io vivo* ‘while I live’ → “whilst I live”: the temporal clause moves ahead of the clause expressing gratitude.
- *convien che nella mia lingua si scerna* ‘it is fitting that in my tongue it be discerned’ → “beseems my tongue should shew”: the impersonal construction is rendered without an expressed anticipatory subject or conjunction. The passive *si scerna* becomes active “should shew,” with “my tongue,” formerly part of a prepositional phrase, as its subject.

> 88 Ciò che narrate di mio corso scrivo,  
> 89 e serbolo a chiosar con altro testo  
> 90 a donna che saprà, se a lei arrivo.  

That which you relate about my course, I write; and keep it, with another text, for a Lady to comment, who will be able if I get to her.

- *di mio corso* ‘of my course’ → “about my course”: “about” replaces ‘of’ to mark the subject of the narration.
- *a chiosar con altro testo a donna* ‘to gloss with another text for a lady’ → “with another text, for a Lady to comment”: the accompanying phrase moves before the purpose construction, and the lady is explicitly made the subject of its infinitive. “Comment” replaces ‘gloss.’
- *che saprà* ‘who will know’ → “who will be able”: knowledge or knowing how is rendered as ability.
- *se a lei arrivo* ‘if to her I arrive’ → “if I get to her”: “get” replaces ‘arrive,’ and the destination follows the verb instead of preceding it.

## Lines 91–124 — Defying Fortune and Parting From Brunetto

> 91 Tanto vogl'io che vi sia manifesto,  
> 92 pur che mia coscienza non mi garra,  
> 93 che alla fortuna, come vuol, son presto.  

Thus much I would have you know; so conscience chide me not, I am prepared for Fortune as she wills.

- *Tanto* ‘So much’ → “Thus much”: the expression of extent is recast.
- *vogl'io che vi sia manifesto* ‘wish I that it be manifest to you’ → “I would have you know”: the subject moves before the verb, and a clause about something being manifest becomes a construction expressing the wish that the hearer know it.
- *pur che* ‘provided that’ → “so”: the condition is expressed by the older conditional use of “so”.
- *mia coscienza non mi garra* ‘my conscience not chide me’ → “conscience chide me not”: the possessive is omitted, and the negative follows the verb and its object.
- *che alla fortuna, come vuol, son presto* ‘that for fortune, as she wills, I am ready’ → “I am prepared for Fortune as she wills”: the resumptive *che* is omitted; the main predicate precedes the prepositional and comparative phrases instead of following them; ‘ready’ becomes “prepared”. The article attached to *fortuna* is not represented.

> 94 Non è nuova agli orecchi miei tale arra:  

Not new to my ears is such earnest:

- *Non è nuova agli orecchi miei tale arra* ‘Not is new to the ears my such earnest’ → “Not new to my ears is such earnest”: the copula moves after the adjective and its accompanying phrase, and the possessive moves before ‘ears’.

> 95 però giri fortuna la sua rota,  
> 96 come le piace, e il villan la sua marra."  

therefore, let Fortune turn her wheel as pleases her, and the boor his mattock."

- *giri fortuna la sua rota* ‘may fortune turn her wheel’ → “let Fortune turn her wheel”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”, and the subject precedes the main verb rather than following it. The article before *fortuna* is omitted.
- *il villan* ‘the peasant’ → “the boor”: the rural designation is rendered by an English term that can also imply uncouthness.

> 97 Lo mio maestro allora in sulla gota  
> 98 destra si volse indietro, e riguardommi;  
> 99 poi disse: "Bene ascolta chi la nota."  

Thereupon my Master turned backward on his right, and looked at me, then said: "He listens well who notes it."

- *allora* ‘then’ → “Thereupon”: the temporal adverb becomes a more closely connective expression and moves before the subject.
- *in sulla gota destra si volse indietro* ‘over his right cheek turned himself backward’ → “turned backward on his right”: ‘cheek’ is omitted, “right” stands independently, and the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb. The directional phrase moves after the verb.
- *Bene ascolta chi la nota* ‘Well listens whoever it notes’ → “He listens well who notes it”: the free relative becomes a construction with an explicit antecedent, “He”; “well” follows the verb, and the object of ‘notes’ follows that verb.

> 100 Nè per tanto di men parlando vommi  
> 101 con ser Brunetto, e dimando chi sono  
> 102 li suoi compagni più noti e più sommi.  

Not the less I go on speaking with Ser Brunetto, and ask who are the most noted and highest of his companions.

- *Nè per tanto di men* ‘Nor for that any less’ → “Not the less”: the opening conjunction and the explicit reference ‘for that’ are compressed into a concessive expression.
- *parlando vommi con ser Brunetto* ‘speaking I go my way with Ser Brunetto’ → “I go on speaking with Ser Brunetto”: the construction expressing movement while speaking becomes the continuative “go on speaking”; the reflexive element is not separately expressed.
- *chi sono li suoi compagni più noti e più sommi* ‘who are his companions most known and most high’ → “who are the most noted and highest of his companions”: the companions become a partitive phrase after “of”, while the adjectives identify the people being asked about.

> 103 Ed egli a me: "Saper d'alcuno è buono;  
> 104 degli altri fia laudabile tacerci,  
> 105 chè il tempo saria corto a tanto suono.  

And he to me: "It is good to know of some; of the rest it will be laudable that we keep silence, as the time would be too short for so much talk.

- *Saper d'alcuno è buono* ‘To know of someone is good’ → “It is good to know of some”: the infinitive subject moves after the predicate, with introductory “It” supplied; the indefinite singular *alcuno* becomes “some”.
- *degli altri* ‘of the others’ → “of the rest”: the other people are expressed collectively as the remainder.
- *fia laudabile tacerci* ‘it will be laudable for us to be silent’ → “it will be laudable that we keep silence”: the infinitive becomes a finite clause with “that we”, taking *ci* as inclusive of the speakers; ‘be silent’ becomes the noun construction “keep silence”.
- *saria corto* ‘would be short’ → “would be too short”: “too” makes the implied insufficiency explicit.
- *tanto suono* ‘so much sound’ → “so much talk”: the literal auditory term is replaced by the activity it denotes here.

> 106 In somma sappi, che tutti fur cherci  
> 107 e letterati grandi e di gran fama,  
> 108 d'un peccato medesmo al mondo lerci.  

In brief, know that all were clerks, and great scholars, and of great renown; by one same crime on earth defiled.

- *In somma* ‘In sum’ → “In brief”: summation is expressed as brevity.
- *letterati grandi* ‘men of letters great’ → “great scholars”: the designation becomes “scholars”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *d'un peccato medesmo al mondo lerci* ‘with one sin same in the world filthy’ → “by one same crime on earth defiled”: ‘sin’ becomes “crime”, with “same” moved before it; ‘in the world’ becomes “on earth”. The adjective ‘filthy’ becomes the participle “defiled”, accompanied by “by” rather than ‘with’.

> 109 Priscian sen va con quella turba grama,  

Priscian goes with that wretched crowd,

- *sen va* ‘goes away’ → “goes”: the pronominal construction is simplified, and the directional force of *ne* is not expressed.
- *quella turba grama* ‘that crowd wretched’ → “that wretched crowd”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 110 e Francesco d'Accorso; anco vedervi,  
> 111 s'avessi avuto di tal tigna brama,  
> 112 colui potei che dal servo de' servi  
> 113 fu trasmutato d'Arno in Bacchiglione,  

and Francesco d'Accorso; also, if thou hadst had any longing for such scurf, thou mightest have seen him there, who by the Servant of servants was translated from the Arno to the Bacchiglione,

- *anco vedervi … colui potei* ‘also to see there that one thou couldst’ → “also,” and “thou mightest have seen him there”: the infinitive and its object are brought together after the intervening condition. The past expression of ability becomes a modal perfect expressing an unreal possibility; the demonstrative ‘that one’ becomes “him”, and the locative attached to *veder* becomes the separate “there”.
- *s'avessi avuto di tal tigna brama* ‘if thou hadst had for such scurf longing’ → “if thou hadst had any longing for such scurf”: “any” is supplied, and the noun precedes its prepositional complement instead of following it.
- *dal servo de' servi* ‘by the servant of the servants’ → “by the Servant of servants”: the article before the second ‘servants’ is omitted.
- *d'Arno in Bacchiglione* ‘from Arno into Bacchiglione’ → “from the Arno to the Bacchiglione”: articles are supplied before both names, and the directional *in* is rendered as “to”.

> 114 dove lasciò li mal protesi nervi.  

where he left his ill-strained nerves.

- *li mal protesi nervi* ‘the ill-stretched sinews’ → “his ill-strained nerves”: the definite article becomes a possessive; ‘stretched out’ becomes “strained”. “Nerves” uses the older anatomical sense corresponding to ‘sinews’.

> 115 Di più direi; ma il venir e il sermone  
> 116 più lungo esser non può: però ch'io veggio  
> 117 là surger nuovo fummo dal sabbione.  

I would say more, but my going and my speech must not be longer: for there I see new smoke arising from the great sand.

- *Di più direi* ‘More I would say’ → “I would say more”: the expression of additional speech moves after the verb.
- *ma il venir e il sermone più lungo esser non può* ‘but the coming and the speech longer be cannot’ → “but my going and my speech must not be longer”: the definite articles become possessives, ‘coming’ becomes “going”, and inability becomes a formulation of necessity or prohibition. The modal precedes the infinitive, and “longer” follows it.
- *però ch'* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating causal conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *io veggio là surger nuovo fummo* ‘I see there rise new smoke’ → “there I see new smoke arising”: “there” moves before the subject, and the infinitive becomes a participle placed after ‘smoke’.
- *dal sabbione* ‘from the great expanse of sand’ → “from the great sand”: the augmentative noun is represented by “sand” with the separate adjective “great”.

> 118 Gente vien con la quale esser non deggio;  

People are coming with whom I may not be;

- *Gente vien* ‘People come’ → “People are coming”: the simple present becomes progressive; the Italian singular collective noun and singular verb are represented by an English plural construction.
- *con la quale esser non deggio* ‘with whom be I ought not’ → “with whom I may not be”: the infinitive moves after the modal, and obligation is expressed as lack of permission.

> 119 sieti raccomandato il mio 'Tesoro,'  
> 120 nel quale io vivo ancora; e più non cheggio."  

let my 'Treasure,' in which I still live, be commended to thee; and more I ask not."

- *sieti raccomandato il mio 'Tesoro,'* ‘be to thee commended my Treasure’ → “let my 'Treasure,'” and “be commended to thee”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”; the subject moves before the passive verb, while the recipient moves after it.
- *nel quale io vivo ancora* ‘in which I live still’ → “in which I still live”: the adverb moves before the verb.
- *e più non cheggio* ‘and more I do not ask’ → “and more I ask not”: the negative follows the verb rather than preceding it.

> 121 Poi si rivolse, e parve di coloro  
> 122 che corrono a Verona il drappo verde  
> 123 per la campagna; e parve di costoro  
> 124 quegli che vince e non colui che perde.  

Then he turned back, and seemed like one of those who run for the green cloth at Verona through the open field; and of them seemed he who gains, not he who loses.

- *Poi si rivolse* ‘Then he turned himself around’ → “Then he turned back”: the reflexive becomes an intransitive construction, with the direction expressed by “back”.
- *e parve di coloro* ‘and seemed of those’ → “and seemed like one of those”: the partitive expression is expanded with “like one”, making the comparison explicit.
- *che corrono a Verona il drappo verde* ‘who run at Verona the cloth green’ → “who run for the green cloth at Verona”: the direct object becomes a phrase with “for”; the place phrase moves after it, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *per la campagna* ‘through the countryside’ → “through the open field”: the general terrain noun becomes “field”, qualified by the supplied adjective “open”.
- *e parve di costoro* ‘and seemed of these’ → “and of them seemed”: the partitive phrase moves before the verb, and the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *quegli che vince* ‘that one who wins’ → “he who gains”: the demonstrative becomes “he”, and ‘wins’ is expressed by the older use of “gains”.
- *e non colui che perde* ‘and not that one who loses’ → “not he who loses”: the conjunction is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes “he”.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Recasting

The translation remains close to the Italian in its sequence of expressions, imagery, and often its phrasing, but regularly rearranges words into English order. Verbs precede objects that Dante places before them, adjectives move before nouns, and pronouns follow the verbs on which they depend. Thus *eran fatti quelli* becomes “those banks were formed,” with “banks” also supplied to identify the demonstrative. Some Italian inversions nevertheless survive, as in “far from the goat shall be the grass” and “Not new to my ears is such earnest.” The prose consequently combines English syntactic adjustments with conspicuously close echoes of Dante’s arrangements.

Changes of construction often go beyond rearrangement. *Perchè il mar si fuggia*, ‘so that the sea may flee,’ becomes “to repel the sea”: a finite purpose clause becomes an infinitive, and the sea changes from subject to object. *Volette*’s construction, expressed in the text as *volete che con voi m’ asseggia*, becomes “you wish me to sit down with you,” replacing a finite subordinate clause with an object and infinitive. Nouns and verbs also exchange functions: *dato t’ avrei all’ opera conforto* becomes “I would have cheered thee in the work,” while *s’ eterna* expands into “makes himself eternal.” Italian subjunctives are variously represented by English indicatives, subjunctives, or constructions with “let”; singular agreement with collective nouns sometimes becomes plural agreement with their members.

Vocabulary ranges from close correspondence to interpretation and shifts of emphasis. “Baked aspect,” “sweet fig,” and “holy seed” preserve distinctive images, whereas *aguzzavan le ciglia* becomes “sharpened their vision,” replacing the bodily expression with the faculty it represents. *Letame*, ‘dung,’ becomes the less concrete “rankness”; *gridò*, ‘cried out,’ is softened to “said,” while *temendo*, ‘fearing,’ becomes the stronger “dreading.” Elsewhere the departure follows English idiom: *le volsi le spalle*, ‘I turned my shoulders to it,’ becomes “I turned my back to it.” Archaic choices such as “durst,” “beseems,” and “earnest” help retain a formal texture even where the wording is recast.

Small additions and omissions recur throughout. Possessives replace definite articles where English identifies bodily parts or belongings more naturally that way, as in “my eyes” and “his train”; explanatory words appear in “the eye of his needle” and “him there.” Conversely, repeated possessives, some recipient pronouns, and occasional adverbial elements are left unexpressed. Beyond the changes recorded individually in the comparison, English regularly supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and omits Italian definite articles before possessives. These routine adjustments accompany a translation that generally preserves Dante’s concrete language while reshaping its grammar and occasionally clarifying, broadening, or tempering its force.
