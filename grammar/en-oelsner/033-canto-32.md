# Inferno 32

## Lines 1–21 — Describing the Deepest Pit

> 1 S' io avessi le rime aspre e chiocce,  
> 2 come si converrebbe al tristo buco,  
> 3 sopra il qual pontan tutte l' altre rocce,  

If I had rhymes rough and hoarse, as would befit the dismal hole, on which all the other rocky steeps converge and weigh,

- *le rime* ‘the rhymes’ → “rhymes”: the definite article is omitted.
- *come si converrebbe al* ‘as would be fitting for the’ → “as would befit the”: the Italian pronominal construction with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive English verb.
- *sopra il qual* ‘above which’ → “on which”: the preposition is rendered as expressing support rather than simply position above.
- *pontan* ‘rest’ or ‘abut’ → “converge and weigh”: one verb is expanded into two, expressing both convergence and pressure; it also moves from before the subject to after it.
- *rocce* ‘rocks’ → “rocky steeps”: the noun becomes an adjective modifying a supplied noun.

> 4 io premerei di mio concetto il suco  
> 5 più pienamente; ma perch' io non l' abbo,  
> 6 non senza tema a dicer mi conduco:  

I should press out the juice of my conception more fully; but since I have them not, not without fear I bring myself to tell *thereof*:

- *premerei* ‘I would press’ → “I should press out”: the conditional is expressed with “should”, and “out” makes the extraction explicit.
- *di mio concetto il suco* ‘of my conception the juice’ → “the juice of my conception”: the dependent phrase moves after its noun.
- *ma perch' io non l' abbo* ‘but because I not them have’ → “but since I have them not”: “since” expresses the causal connection, and both the object pronoun and the negative move after the verb.
- *a dicer mi conduco* ‘to speak myself I bring’ → “I bring myself to tell *thereof*”: the finite verb precedes the reflexive pronoun and infinitive; “thereof” supplies an explicit reference to what is to be described.

> 7 chè non è impresa da pigliare a gabbo  
> 8 descriver fondo a tutto l' universo,  
> 9 nè da lingua che chiami mamma e babbo.  

for to describe the bottom of all the Universe is not an enterprise for being taken up in sport, nor for a tongue that cries *mamma* and *papa*.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating causal conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *non è impresa* ‘not is an enterprise’ → “is not an enterprise”: the negative follows the verb, and English supplies an indefinite article before the predicate noun.
- *da pigliare a gabbo* ‘to take in jest’ → “for being taken up in sport”: the infinitive becomes a passive gerund after “for”; “taken up” presents the enterprise as something undertaken.
- *descriver fondo a tutto l' universo* ‘to describe bottom to all the universe’ → “to describe the bottom of all the Universe”: the infinitival subject moves before the predicate; “the” is supplied before “bottom”, and the relation expressed by *a* becomes an “of” construction.
- *nè da lingua* ‘nor for tongue’ → “nor for a tongue”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *che chiami* ‘that might call’ → “that cries”: the subjunctive characterizing a possible kind of tongue becomes an indicative verb, and calling is rendered as crying.

> 10 Ma quelle Donne aiutino il mio verso,  
> 11 ch' aiutaro Anfion a chiuder Tebe,  
> 12 sì che dal fatto il dir non sia diverso.  

But may those Ladies help my verse, who helped Amphion with *walls* to close in Thebes; so that my words may not be diverse from the fact.

- *quelle Donne aiutino* ‘those Ladies may help’ → “may those Ladies help”: the subjunctive wish is expressed by “may” placed before the subject.
- *a chiuder Tebe* ‘to enclose Thebes’ → “with *walls* to close in Thebes”: “with walls” is supplied, and the single verb is rendered by “close in”.
- *dal fatto il dir non sia diverso* ‘from the fact the saying may not be different’ → “my words may not be diverse from the fact”: the substantivized infinitive becomes the plural noun “words”, “my” is supplied, and the opening complement moves to the end. The subjunctive is expressed with “may”.

> 13 O sopra tutte mal creata plebe,  
> 14 che stai nel loco, onde 'l parlare è duro,  
> 15 me' foste state qui pecore o zebe!  

O ye beyond all *others*, miscreated rabble, who are in the place, to speak of which is hard, better had ye here *on earth* been sheep or goats!

- *O sopra tutte mal creata plebe* ‘O above all ill-created rabble’ → “O ye beyond all *others*, miscreated rabble”: “ye” is supplied before the qualifying phrase; “beyond” renders *sopra*, and “others” makes explicit the comparison implicit in *tutte*. The two-word expression *mal creata* becomes the compound “miscreated”.
- *che stai* ‘who stayest’ → “who are”: the singular verb addressing the collective noun becomes plural, agreeing with the supplied “ye”; remaining or standing in a place is rendered simply as being there.
- *onde 'l parlare è duro* ‘whence the speaking is hard’ → “to speak of which is hard”: *onde* is interpreted as ‘of which’, rather than ‘from which’. The substantivized infinitive becomes an infinitival subject, placed before “of which”.
- *foste state* ‘you had been’ → “had ye” and “been”: the Italian pluperfect subjunctive is rendered by an inverted English past-perfect construction expressing the unreal alternative.
- *qui* ‘here’ → “here *on earth*”: “on earth” is supplied to specify the intended reference of “here”.
- *zebe* ‘she-goats’ → “goats”: the explicit female designation is lost.

> 16 Come noi fummo giù nel pozzo scuro  
> 17 sotto i piè del gigante, assai più bassi,  
> 18 ed io mirava ancora all' alto muro,  

When we were down in the dark pit, under the Giant's feet, much lower, and I still was gazing at the high wall,

- *Come* ‘As’ → “When”: the temporal connection is expressed with “When”.
- *pozzo scuro* ‘pit dark’ → “dark pit”: the adjective moves before its noun.
- *sotto i piè del gigante* ‘under the feet of the giant’ → “under the Giant's feet”: the postposed possessive phrase becomes a possessive placed before “feet”.
- *mirava ancora* ‘was gazing still’ → “still was gazing”: the imperfect is expressed by the past progressive, and the adverb moves before the verbal phrase.

> 19 dicere udimmi: “Guarda come passi:  
> 20 fa sì che tu non calchi con le piante  
> 21 le teste de' fratei miseri lassi.”  

I heard a *voice* say to me: “Look how thou passest: take care that with thy soles thou tread not on the heads of the weary wretched brothers.”

- *dicere udimmi* ‘say I heard to myself’ → “I heard a *voice* say to me”: the verb of hearing moves before the infinitive, “a voice” supplies its understood speaker, and the enclitic pronoun becomes “to me” after “say”.
- *fa sì che* ‘make so that’ → “take care that”: the Italian causative expression becomes an English phrase of caution.
- *tu non calchi con le piante* ‘thou not tread with the soles’ → “with thy soles thou tread not”: the instrumental phrase moves before the subject and verb, the negative moves after the verb, and the definite article becomes the possessive “thy”.
- *le teste* ‘the heads’ → “on the heads”: the direct object of the Italian verb becomes a prepositional complement of the English verb.
- *fratei miseri lassi* ‘brothers wretched weary’ → “weary wretched brothers”: both adjectives move before the noun, and their order is reversed.

## Lines 22–54 — The Frozen Lake and Its Shades

> 22 Per ch’ io mi volsi e vidimi davante  
> 23 e sotto i piedi un lago, che per gelo  
> 24 avea di vetro e non d’ acqua sembiante.  

Whereat I turned myself, and saw before me and beneath my feet a lake, which through frost had the semblance of glass and not of water.

- *Per ch’* ‘Because of which’ → “Whereat”: the causal connection becomes a connection with the preceding event.
- *io mi volsi* ‘I myself turned’ → “I turned myself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *vidimi davante* ‘saw myself before’ → “saw before me”: the enclitic *mi* is construed with *davante*, not as the object of seeing, and becomes the pronoun following “before”.
- *sotto i piedi* ‘beneath the feet’ → “beneath my feet”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *avea di vetro e non d’ acqua sembiante* ‘had of glass and not of water semblance’ → “had the semblance of glass and not of water”: the noun precedes its dependent phrases, and an article is supplied.

> 25 Non fece al corso suo sì grosso velo  
> 26 di verno la Danoia in Osteric,  
> 27 nè Tanai là sotto il freddo cielo,  

Never did the Danube of Austria make so thick a veil for his course in winter, nor *the Don* afar beneath the frigid sky,

- *Non fece al corso suo sì grosso velo di verno la Danoia in Osteric* ‘Did not make for its course so thick a veil in winter the Danube in Austria’ → “Never did the Danube of Austria make so thick a veil for his course in winter,”: simple negation becomes temporal “Never”; the subject moves before the main verb, and the phrase concerning its course moves after the object. The river receives the personal possessive “his”, and location “in” Austria becomes “of Austria”.
- *nè Tanai là* ‘nor Don there’ → “nor *the Don* afar”: an article is supplied before the river’s name, and “afar” expresses distance rather than merely location.

> 28 com’ era quivi: chè, se Tambernic  
> 29 vi fosse su caduto o Pietrapana,  
> 30 non avria pur dall’ orlo fatto cric.  

as there was here: for if Tambernic had fallen on it, or Pietrapana, it would not even at the edge have given a creak.

- *com’ era quivi* ‘as was here’ → “as there was here”: existential “there” is supplied.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *se Tambernic vi fosse su caduto o Pietrapana* ‘if Tambernic there had upon fallen or Pietrapana’ → “if Tambernic had fallen on it, or Pietrapana,”: *vi … su* becomes “on it”, placed after the participle; the Italian pluperfect subjunctive becomes the English past-perfect form used in a hypothetical condition.
- *non avria pur dall’ orlo fatto cric* ‘it would not even from the edge have made cric’ → “it would not even at the edge have given a creak.”: “at” replaces ‘from’, and the imitated sound becomes a noun with an article; ‘made’ becomes “given”.

> 31 E come a gracidar si sta la rana  
> 32 col muso fuor dell’ acqua, quando sogna  
> 33 di spigolar sovente la villana:  

And as the frog to croak, sits with his muzzle out of the water, when the [peasant-woman] oft dreams that she is gleaning:

- *E come a gracidar si sta la rana* ‘And as to croak stays the frog’ → “And as the frog to croak, sits”: the subject moves before both the purpose phrase and the verb. *Si sta* is rendered by the more specific posture “sits”, without a separate equivalent for its pronominal element.
- *col muso* ‘with the muzzle’ → “with his muzzle”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *quando sogna di spigolar sovente la villana* ‘when dreams of gleaning often the peasant woman’ → “when the [peasant-woman] oft dreams that she is gleaning:”: the subject and adverb move before the verb; the preposition and infinitive become a finite clause with an explicit subject and a progressive verb.

> 34 livide, insin là dove appar vergogna,  
> 35 eran l’ ombre dolenti nella ghiaccia,  
> 36 mettendo i denti in nota di cicogna.  

so, livid, up to where *the hue of shame* appears, *the doleful shades* were in the ice, sounding with their teeth like storks.

- *livide* ‘livid’ → “so, livid,”: “so” is supplied to complete the comparison introduced by *come*.
- *insin là* ‘up to there’ → “up to”: the anticipatory location word is omitted before the following relative clause.
- *dove appar vergogna* ‘where appears shame’ → “where *the hue of shame* appears”: shame is interpreted as its visible colouring, with “the hue of” supplied; the subject moves before the verb.
- *eran l’ ombre dolenti nella ghiaccia* ‘were the shades doleful in the ice’ → “*the doleful shades* were in the ice”: the subject moves before the verb, and its adjective precedes the noun.
- *mettendo i denti in nota di cicogna* ‘putting the teeth into the note of a stork’ → “sounding with their teeth like storks.”: the construction becomes one of producing sound, with the teeth expressed as its instrument. A possessive replaces the article, and the singular phrase ‘of a stork’ becomes an explicit comparison with plural “storks”.

> 37 Ognuna in giù tenea volta la faccia;  

Each held his face turned downwards;

- *in giù tenea volta la faccia* ‘downwards held turned the face’ → “held his face turned downwards;”: the directional phrase moves to the end, the object precedes its participle, and a possessive replaces the definite article.

> 38 da bocca il freddo e dagli occhi il cor tristo  
> 39 tra lor testimonianza si procaccia.  

by the mouth their cold, and by the eyes *the sorrow* of their hearts is testified amongst them.

- *da bocca il freddo* ‘from the mouth the cold’ → “by the mouth their cold,”: the source of the evidence becomes its means, and a possessive replaces the article before ‘cold’.
- *dagli occhi il cor tristo* ‘from the eyes the sad heart’ → “by the eyes *the sorrow* of their hearts”: “by” again expresses means; the adjective ‘sad’ becomes the noun “sorrow”, with the heart dependent on it. Singular ‘heart’ becomes plural “hearts”, and a possessive is supplied.
- *tra lor testimonianza si procaccia* ‘among them procures testimony for itself’ → “is testified amongst them.”: the reflexive active construction becomes passive, the noun ‘testimony’ becomes the verb “testified”, and the phrase ‘among them’ moves to the end.

> 40 Quand’ io ebbi d’ intorno alquanto visto,  
> 41 volsimi a’ piedi, e vidi due sì stretti  
> 42 che il pel del capo avieno insieme misto.  

When I had looked round awhile, I turned towards my feet; and saw two so pressed *against each other*, that they had the hair of their heads intermixed.

- *Quand’ io ebbi d’ intorno alquanto visto* ‘When I had around somewhat seen’ → “When I had looked round awhile,”: ‘seen’ becomes “looked”; *alquanto* is interpreted temporally as “awhile”; and the adverbial expressions move after the main verb.
- *volsimi a’ piedi* ‘I turned myself to the feet’ → “I turned towards my feet;”: the reflexive pronoun is omitted, a possessive replaces the article, and “towards” expresses direction.
- *due sì stretti* ‘two so pressed’ → “two so pressed *against each other*,”: the reciprocal relation is made explicit.
- *che il pel del capo avieno insieme misto* ‘that the hair of the head they had together mixed’ → “that they had the hair of their heads intermixed.”: the object moves after the subject and verb; singular ‘head’ becomes plural “heads”, with a possessive; and ‘together mixed’ is compressed into “intermixed”.

> 43 “Ditemi voi, che sì stringete i petti,”  
> 44 diss’ io, “chi siete.” E quei piegaro i colli;  

“Tell me, ye who thus *together* press your bosoms,” said I, “who you are.” And they bended their necks;

- *che sì stringete i petti* ‘who thus press the breasts’ → “who thus *together* press your bosoms,”: “together” is supplied, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *E quei piegaro i colli* ‘And those bent the necks’ → “And they bended their necks;”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes the personal pronoun “they”, and a possessive replaces the definite article.

> 45 e poi ch’ ebber li visi a me eretti,  
> 46 gli occhi lor, ch’ eran pria pur dentro molli,  
> 47 gocciar su per le labbra, e il gelo strinse  
> 48 le lagrime tra essi, e riserrolli.  

and when they had raised their faces towards me, their eyes, which only inwardly were moist before, gushed at the lids, and the frost bound fast the tears between them, and closed them up again.

- *e poi ch’ ebber li visi a me eretti* ‘and after they had the faces to me raised’ → “and when they had raised their faces towards me,”: ‘after’ becomes “when”; the participle moves immediately after its auxiliary; a possessive replaces the article; and “towards” makes the direction explicit.
- *gli occhi lor* ‘the eyes of them’ → “their eyes,”: the postposed possessive becomes a possessive before the noun.
- *ch’ eran pria pur dentro molli* ‘which were before only within moist’ → “which only inwardly were moist before,”: ‘only within’ moves before the verb, while ‘before’ moves to the end.
- *gocciar su per le labbra* ‘dripped over the lips’ → “gushed at the lids,”: “gushed” intensifies ‘dripped’; the phrase describing movement over a surface becomes the locative “at”; and *labbra* is interpreted as the rims or lids of the eyes rather than the lips of the mouth.
- *strinse* ‘constricted’ → “bound fast”: the single verb becomes a phrase expressing tight binding.
- *riserrolli* ‘closed them again’ → “closed them up again.”: the particle “up” is supplied.

> 49 Con legno legno mai spranga non cinse  
> 50 forte così: ond’ ei, come due becchi,  
> 51 cozzaro insieme, tant’ ira li vinse.  

Wood with wood no cramp did ever gird so strongly: wherefore *they*, like two he-goats, butted one another; such rage came over them.

- *Con legno legno mai spranga non cinse forte così* ‘With wood wood ever a cramp did not gird strongly so’ → “Wood with wood no cramp did ever gird so strongly:”: the object precedes rather than follows ‘with wood’; verbal negation becomes the determiner “no” before “cramp”; “ever” follows the auxiliary; and ‘strongly so’ becomes “so strongly”.
- *cozzaro insieme* ‘butted together’ → “butted one another;”: the adverb becomes an explicit reciprocal object.
- *tant’ ira li vinse* ‘so much anger conquered them’ → “such rage came over them.”: quantity becomes “such”, and the verb of conquest becomes the idiom “came over”.

> 52 Ed un, ch’ avea perduti ambo gli orecchi  
> 53 per la freddura, pur col viso in giue  
> 54 disse: “Perchè cotanto in noi ti specchi?  

And one, who had lost both ears by the cold, with his face still downwards said: “Why art thou looking so much at us?

- *pur col viso in giue* ‘still with the face downwards’ → “with his face still downwards”: “still” moves within the phrase, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *Perchè cotanto in noi ti specchi* ‘Why so much in us do you mirror yourself’ → “Why art thou looking so much at us?”: the reflexive expression is interpreted as gazing at someone rather than mirroring oneself in them. The simple present becomes progressive; ‘in us’ becomes “at us”; and both adverbial expressions move after the verb.

## Lines 55–87 — Traitors in the Frozen Lake

> 55 Se vuoi saper chi son cotesti due,  
> 56 la valle onde Bisenzio si dichina  
> 57 del padre loro Alberto e di lor fue.  

If thou desirest to know who are these two, the valley whence the Bisenzio descends was theirs and their father Albert’s.

- *vuoi* ‘thou wilt / wishest’ → “thou desirest”: the verb of wishing is rendered by a verb of desire.
- *cotesti* ‘those near thee’ → “these”: the demonstrative loses its particular reference to proximity to the person addressed.
- *si dichina* ‘inclines itself downward’ → “descends”: the pronominal construction becomes a simple intransitive verb.
- *del padre loro Alberto e di lor fue* ‘their father Albert’s and theirs was’ → “was theirs and their father Albert’s”: the verb moves before the possessive predicates, and their order is reversed; the postposed possessive *loro* becomes the preceding “their”.

> 58 D’ un corpo usciro; e tutta la Caina  
> 59 potrai cercare, e non troverai ombra  
> 60 degna più d’ esser fitta in gelatina:  

They issued from one body; and thou mayest search the whole Caïna, and shalt not find a shade more worthy to be fixed in gelatine:

- *D’ un corpo usciro* ‘From one body they issued’ → “They issued from one body”: the verb precedes rather than follows the phrase of origin.
- *tutta la Caina potrai cercare* ‘all Caïna thou wilt be able to search’ → “thou mayest search the whole Caïna”: the object moves after the verb; the future expression of ability becomes the present modal “mayest”.
- *degna più* ‘worthy more’ → “more worthy”: the comparative adverb moves before the adjective.
- *d’ esser* ‘of being’ → “to be”: the Italian prepositional construction becomes an English to-infinitive.
- *fitta* ‘stuck’ → “fixed”: the translation expresses secure placement rather than the more concrete action of sticking something into a substance.

> 61 non quelli, a cui fu rotto il petto e l’ ombra  
> 62 con esso un colpo per la man d’ Artù;  

not him, whose breast and shadow at one blow were pierced by Arthur’s hand;

- *quelli* ‘he’ → “him”: the Italian masculine singular demonstrative becomes an English personal pronoun in the objective case, continuing the construction governed by ‘find’.
- *a cui* ‘to whom’ → “whose”: the Italian dative expressing the person affected becomes a possessive relative.
- *fu rotto* ‘was broken’ → “were pierced”: breaking becomes piercing, and the singular Italian verb becomes plural to agree with the coordinated English subject.
- *con esso un colpo* ‘with one and the same blow’ → “at one blow”: the preposition changes, and the emphatic force of *esso* is not separately expressed.
- *per la man d’ Artù* ‘by the hand of Arthur’ → “by Arthur’s hand”: the following possessive phrase becomes a preceding possessive.

> 63 non Focaccia; non questi, che m’ ingombra  
> 64 col capo sì ch’ io non veggio oltre più,  
> 65 e fu nomato Sassol Mascheroni:  
> 66 se Tosco se’, ben sa’ omai chi fu.  

not Focaccia; not this one, who so obstructs me with his head that I see no farther, and who was named Sassol Mascheroni: if thou beest a Tuscan, well knowest thou now who he was.

- *questi* ‘this’ → “this one”: English adds “one” to support the independently used demonstrative.
- *che m’ ingombra col capo sì* ‘who me obstructs with the head so’ → “who so obstructs me with his head”: the object pronoun follows the verb, “so” moves before it, and “his” replaces the definite article.
- *non veggio oltre più* ‘I see beyond no more’ → “I see no farther”: the two adverbs *oltre più* are compressed into the comparative “farther”.
- *e fu nomato* ‘and was named’ → “and who was named”: the relative pronoun is repeated in English.
- *se Tosco se’* ‘if Tuscan thou art’ → “if thou beest a Tuscan”: the predicate moves after the verb, and the nationality adjective becomes a noun with an indefinite article.

> 67 E perchè non mi metti in più sermoni,  
> 68 sappi ch’ io fui il Camicion de’ Pazzi,  
> 69 ed aspetto Carlin che mi scagioni.”  

And that thou mayest not put me to further speech, know that I was Camicion de’ Pazzi, and am waiting for Carlino to excuse me.”

- *perchè* ‘so that’ → “that”: the purpose conjunction is rendered without an explicit ‘so’.
- *non mi metti in più sermoni* ‘thou put me not into more speeches’ → “thou mayest not put me to further speech”: the purpose construction is expressed with “mayest”; the object pronoun moves after the verb, ‘into’ becomes “to”, and plural *sermoni* becomes the uncountable singular “speech”.
- *il Camicion de’ Pazzi* ‘the Camicion de’ Pazzi’ → “Camicion de’ Pazzi”: the article before the personal name is omitted.
- *aspetto* ‘I await’ → “am waiting for”: the simple present becomes progressive, and a directly transitive verb becomes a verb followed by “for”.
- *Carlin che mi scagioni* ‘Carlino who may exculpate me’ → “Carlino to excuse me”: the relative clause with a subjunctive becomes an infinitive construction; “excuse” renders the more specifically exculpatory *scagioni*.

> 70 Poscia vid’ io mille visi, cagnazzi  
> 71 fatti per freddo: onde mi vien riprezzo,  
> 72 e verrà sempre, de’ gelati guazzi.  

Afterwards I saw a thousand visages, made doggish by the cold: whence shuddering comes over me, and always will come, *when I think of* the frozen fords.

- *vid’ io* ‘saw I’ → “I saw”: the explicit subject moves before the verb.
- *cagnazzi fatti per freddo* ‘doggish made by cold’ → “made doggish by the cold”: the adjective follows rather than precedes the participle, and English supplies an article before “cold”.
- *mi vien riprezzo* ‘to me comes shuddering’ → “shuddering comes over me”: the subject moves before the verb, and the dative becomes the idiomatic phrase “over me”.
- *e verrà sempre* ‘and will come always’ → “and always will come”: the adverb moves before the verbal phrase.
- *de’ gelati guazzi* ‘from the frozen pools’ → “*when I think of* the frozen fords”: the relation of source or cause becomes an explicit temporal clause about recollection, supplying both thinking and its subject; *guazzi* is interpreted as “fords”.

> 73 E mentre che andavamo in ver lo mezzo,  
> 74 al quale ogni gravezza si raduna,  
> 75 ed io tremava nell’ eterno rezzo,  

And as we were going towards the middle at which all weight unites, and I was shivering in the eternal shade,

- *mentre che* ‘while’ → “as”: the temporal conjunction is replaced by another English temporal conjunction.
- *andavamo* ‘we went’ → “we were going”: the Italian imperfect is expressed by the English past progressive.
- *si raduna* ‘gathers itself together’ → “unites”: the pronominal construction becomes a simple intransitive verb.
- *tremava* ‘trembled’ → “was shivering”: the verb is rendered by the more specifically cold-associated “shivering”, and the imperfect becomes a past progressive.

> 76 se voler fu o destino, o fortuna,  
> 77 non so; ma passeggiando tra le teste,  
> 78 forte percossi il piè nel viso ad una.  

whether it was will, or destiny or chance, I know not; but, walking amid the heads, I hit my foot violently against the face of one.

- *voler fu* ‘will it was’ → “it was will”: the predicate noun moves after the verb.
- *forte percossi il piè* ‘hard I struck the foot’ → “I hit my foot violently”: the adverb moves after the object, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *nel viso ad una* ‘in the face to one’ → “against the face of one”: ‘in’ becomes “against”, while the dative identifying the affected head becomes an English possessive phrase.

> 79 Piangendo mi sgridò: “Perchè mi peste?  
> 80 se tu non vieni a crescer la vendetta  
> 81 di Mont’ Aperti, perchè mi moleste?”  

Weeping it cried out to me: “Why tramplest thou on me? If thou comest not to increase the vengeance for Montaperti, why dost thou molest me?”

- *mi sgridò* ‘it scolded me’ → “it cried out to me”: a transitive verb of rebuke becomes a verb of crying out followed by a prepositional phrase; the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *mi peste* ‘thou tramplest me’ → “tramplest thou on me”: the direct object becomes a prepositional complement, and English uses interrogative subject–verb order.
- *di Mont’ Aperti* ‘of Montaperti’ → “for Montaperti”: the preposition makes explicit the relation of vengeance to its occasion.
- *mi moleste* ‘me thou molestest’ → “dost thou molest me”: English supplies the interrogative auxiliary “dost” and places the object after the main verb.

> 82 Ed io: “Maestro mio, or qui m’ aspetta,  
> 83 sì ch’ io esca d’ un dubbio per costui;  
> 84 poi mi farai, quantunque vorrai, fretta.”  

And I: “My Master! now wait me here, that I may rid me of a doubt respecting him; then shalt thou, however much thou pleasest, make me haste.”

- *Maestro mio* ‘Master mine’ → “My Master”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *qui m’ aspetta* ‘here me await’ → “wait me here”: the object pronoun follows the imperative, and the adverb moves to the end.
- *sì ch’* ‘so that’ → “that”: ‘so’ is not separately expressed.
- *io esca d’ un dubbio* ‘I may come out of a doubt’ → “I may rid me of a doubt”: an intransitive expression of emergence becomes a transitive, reflexive expression of removal.
- *per costui* ‘concerning this one’ → “respecting him”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *mi farai … fretta* ‘thou wilt make haste for me’ → “shalt thou” and “make me haste”: the Italian idiom for hurrying someone becomes an English causative construction, with “me” as the understood subject of “haste”; English also inverts subject and auxiliary after “then”.
- *quantunque vorrai* ‘as much as thou wilt wish’ → “however much thou pleasest”: the future becomes present, and wishing is expressed through the idiom of what someone pleases.

> 85 Lo duca stette; ed io dissi a colui  
> 86 che bestemmiava duramente ancora:  
> 87 “Qual se’ tu, che così rampogni altrui?”  

The master stood; and to that *shade*, which still kept bitterly reviling, I said: “What art thou, who thus reproachest others?”

- *Lo duca* ‘The guide’ → “The master”: the designation changes from guidance to authority.
- *ed io dissi a colui* ‘and I said to that one’ → “and to that *shade*” and “I said”: the recipient and its following relative clause move before the subject and verb; “shade” is supplied for the independently used demonstrative.
- *che bestemmiava duramente ancora* ‘who was blaspheming harshly still’ → “which still kept bitterly reviling”: the relative becomes “which”, agreeing with the supplied “shade”; “kept” explicitly expresses continuation, *bestemmiava* is interpreted as reviling, “bitterly” replaces ‘harshly’, and “still” moves before the verbal phrase.
- *Qual* ‘Who’ → “What”: the question of identity is expressed with “What” rather than the personal interrogative.

## Lines 88–120 — Bocca Refuses His Name

> 88 “Or tu chi se’, che vai per l’ Antenora  
> 89 percotendo,” rispose, “altrui le gote  
> 90 sì che, se fossi vivo, troppo fora?”  

“Nay, who art thou,” he answered, “that through the Antenora goest, smiting the cheeks of others; so that, if thou wert alive, it were too much?”

- *Or* ‘Now’ → “Nay”: the introductory particle becomes an expression of contradiction.
- *tu chi se’* ‘thou who art’ → “who art thou”: the subject follows the verb in English interrogative order.
- *che vai per l’ Antenora percotendo, rispose* ‘who goest through the Antenora smiting, he answered’ → “he answered, “that through the Antenora goest, smiting”: the attribution is moved before the relative clause rather than after *percotendo*; the relative pronoun changes, and the prepositional phrase precedes rather than follows the verb.
- *altrui le gote* ‘of others the cheeks’ → “the cheeks of others”: the possessive expression follows its noun.
- *troppo fora* ‘too much it would be’ → “it were too much”: the predicate follows the verb, and the Italian conditional is rendered by the English subjunctive.

> 91 “Vivo son io, e caro esser ti puote,”  
> 92 fu mia risposta, “se domandi fama,  
> 93 ch’ io metta il nome tuo tra l’ altre note.”  

“I am alive,” was my reply; “and if thou seekest fame, it may be precious to thee, that I put thy name among the other notes.”

- *Vivo son io, e caro esser ti puote, fu mia risposta, se domandi fama* ‘Alive am I, and dear be to thee it can, was my reply, if thou askest for fame’ → “I am alive,” was my reply; “and if thou seekest fame, it may be precious to thee”: the opening predicate-first order becomes subject–verb–predicate; the attribution is moved immediately after the declaration of being alive; the conditional clause is brought forward; the modal precedes its infinitive; *caro* becomes “precious”; and asking for fame becomes seeking it.
- *il nome tuo* ‘the name thy’ → “thy name”: the possessive precedes the noun.

> 94 Ed egli a me: “Del contrario ho io brama;  
> 95 levati quinci, e non mi dar più lagna:  
> 96 chè mal sai lusingar per questa lama.”  

And he to me: “The contrary is *what* I long for; take thyself away! and pester me no more: for thou ill knowest *how* to flatter on this icy slope.”

- *Del contrario ho io brama* ‘For the contrary have I desire’ → “The contrary is *what* I long for”: the noun expressing desire becomes the verb phrase “long for”, and the object of desire becomes the subject of a copular construction.
- *levati quinci* ‘remove thyself from here’ → “take thyself away”: “away” replaces the more explicitly local ‘from here’.
- *e non mi dar più lagna* ‘and do not give me more trouble’ → “and pester me no more”: the construction with ‘give’ and a noun becomes the verb “pester”; the negative expression follows it.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *mal sai lusingar* ‘ill thou knowest to flatter’ → “thou ill knowest *how* to flatter”: the adverb follows the subject, and “*how*” makes explicit the sense of knowing how to do something.
- *per questa lama* ‘through this hollow’ → “on this icy slope”: the preposition becomes locative, the low-lying ground is rendered as a “slope”, and “icy” is supplied.

> 97 Allor lo presi per la cuticagna,  
> 98 e dissi: “E’ converrà che tu ti nomi,  
> 99 o che capel qui su non ti rimagna.”  

Then I seized him by the afterscalp, and said: “It will be necessary that thou name thyself, or that not a hair remain upon thee here!”

- *lo presi per la cuticagna* ‘him I took by the nape’ → “I seized him by the afterscalp”: the object follows the verb; “seized” strengthens ‘took’, and “afterscalp” specifies the scalp at the back of the head.
- *E’ converrà* ‘It will be required’ → “It will be necessary”: the impersonal verb is rendered by a copular phrase with an adjective.
- *o che capel qui su non ti rimagna* ‘or that hair up here not remain to thee’ → “or that not a hair remain upon thee here”: the negative is attached to “a hair” rather than to the verb; the unarticled singular gains “a”, and the locative and personal expressions are recast as “upon thee here”.

> 100 Ond’ egli a me: “Perchè tu mi dischiomi,  
> 101 nè ti dirò ch’ io sia, nè mostrerolti,  
> 102 se mille fiate in sul capo mi tomi.”  

Whence he to me: “Even if thou unhair me, I will not tell thee who I am; nor shew it thee, though thou fall foul upon my head a thousand times.”

- *Perchè tu mi dischiomi* ‘Though thou unhair me’ → “Even if thou unhair me”: the concessive conjunction becomes an explicitly hypothetical concession, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *nè ti dirò ch’ io sia* ‘nor to thee will I say who I am’ → “I will not tell thee who I am”: the first *nè* becomes an ordinary negative rather than “nor”; the indirect object follows the verb, and ‘say’ becomes “tell”.
- *nè mostrerolti* ‘nor will I show it thee’ → “nor shew it thee”: the future auxiliary is not repeated but is shared with the preceding clause.
- *se mille fiate in sul capo mi tomi* ‘if a thousand times upon the head thou fallest on me’ → “though thou fall foul upon my head a thousand times”: the conditional is rendered concessively; ‘fall’ becomes the more aggressive “fall foul”; the personal pronoun becomes the possessive “my”; and the expression of frequency moves to the end.

> 103 Io avea già i capelli in mano avvolti,  
> 104 e tratti glien’ avea più d’ una ciocca,  
> 105 latrando lui con gli occhi in giù raccolti;  

I already had his hair coiled on my hand, and had plucked off more than one tuft of it, he barking and keeping down his eyes,

- *Io avea già* ‘I had already’ → “I already had”: the adverb precedes the auxiliary.
- *i capelli* ‘the hairs’ → “his hair”: the plural becomes an English collective singular, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *in mano avvolti* ‘in hand wound’ → “coiled on my hand”: the participle precedes the prepositional phrase, “on” replaces ‘in’, and “my” is supplied.
- *e tratti glien’ avea più d’ una ciocca* ‘and pulled from him of it I had more than one tuft’ → “and had plucked off more than one tuft of it”: the participle follows the auxiliary; ‘pulled’ becomes “plucked off”; the pronoun meaning ‘from him’ is not separately rendered; and the partitive expression follows “tuft”.
- *latrando lui* ‘barking he’ → “he barking”: the subject precedes the participle.
- *con gli occhi in giù raccolti* ‘with the eyes downward gathered’ → “and keeping down his eyes”: a prepositional description becomes a coordinated participial clause; the lowered position becomes an action of “keeping down”, and the article becomes a possessive.

> 106 quando un altro gridò: “Che hai tu, Bocca?  
> 107 non ti basta sonar con le mascelle,  
> 108 se tu non latri? qual diavol ti tocca?”  

when another cried: “What ails thee, Bocca? is it not enough for thee to chatter with thy jaws, but thou must bark too? what Devil is upon thee?”

- *Che hai tu* ‘What hast thou’ → “What ails thee”: the idiom with ‘have’ becomes a question about affliction, with the person changed from subject to object.
- *non ti basta* ‘does it not suffice thee’ → “is it not enough for thee”: the verb becomes a copular construction, and the indirect object is expressed with “for”.
- *sonar con le mascelle* ‘to sound with the jaws’ → “to chatter with thy jaws”: the general verb of sounding becomes “chatter”, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *se tu non latri* ‘if thou dost not bark’ → “but thou must bark too”: the negative conditional is recast as an affirmative adversative clause, with “must” and “too” supplied.
- *qual diavol ti tocca* ‘what devil touches thee’ → “what Devil is upon thee”: the verb of touching becomes a copular phrase with a preposition.

> 109 “Omai,” diss’ io, “non vo’ che tu favelle,  
> 110 malvagio traditor, chè alla tua onta  
> 111 io porterò di te vere novelle.”  

“Now,” said I, “accursed traitor! I do not want thee to speak; for to thy shame I will bear true tidings of thee.”

- *non vo’ che tu favelle* ‘I do not wish that thou speak’ → “I do not want thee to speak”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction.
- *malvagio traditor* ‘wicked traitor’ → “accursed traitor”: the adjective changes from a description of wickedness to a term of execration, and the vocative is moved before the preceding clause.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *di te vere novelle* ‘of thee true tidings’ → “true tidings of thee”: the prepositional phrase follows rather than precedes the noun phrase.

> 112 “Va via,” rispose, “e ciò che tu vuoi, conta;  
> 113 ma non tacer, se tu di qua entr’ eschi,  
> 114 di quei ch’ ebbe or così la lingua pronta.  

“Go away!” he answered; “and tell what pleases thee; but be not silent, if thou gettest out from hence, respecting him, who now had his tongue so ready.

- *e ciò che tu vuoi, conta* ‘and what thou wishest, tell’ → “and tell what pleases thee”: the object follows the imperative, and wishing is recast as being pleased, making the person an object rather than the subject of that clause.
- *non tacer* ‘do not keep silent’ → “be not silent”: the negative imperative formed with an Italian infinitive is rendered by “be” and an adjective.
- *se tu di qua entr’ eschi* ‘if thou from in here goest out’ → “if thou gettest out from hence”: the expression of origin follows the verb, and ‘from in here’ is compressed into “from hence”, without a separate rendering of the inward location.
- *di quei* ‘of him’ → “respecting him”: the preposition is expanded into an explicit expression of reference.
- *ebbe or così la lingua pronta* ‘had now so the tongue ready’ → “now had his tongue so ready”: the time adverb moves before the verb, the degree adverb moves next to the adjective, and the article becomes a possessive.

> 115 Ei piange qui l’ argento de’ Franceschi.  

Here he laments the Frenchman’s silver.

- *Ei piange qui* ‘He weeps here’ → “Here he laments”: the place adverb is moved to the beginning, and ‘weeps’ is rendered as “laments”.
- *l’ argento de’ Franceschi* ‘the silver of the French’ → “the Frenchman’s silver”: the plural designation becomes a generic singular, and the possessive precedes the noun.

> 116 ‘Io vidi,’ potrai dir, ‘quel da Duera  
> 117 là dove i peccatori stanno freschi.’  

‘Him of Duera,’ thou canst say, ‘I saw there, where the sinners stand pinched in ice.’

- *Io vidi, potrai dir, quel da Duera* ‘I saw, thou wilt be able to say, that one from Duera’ → “Him of Duera,’ thou canst say, ‘I saw”: the subject and verb are moved after the reporting clause, while their object is placed before it; future ability becomes present ability; the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun; and ‘from’ becomes “of”.
- *freschi* ‘cool’ → “pinched in ice”: the adjective is replaced by an explanatory participial phrase, supplying both the physical effect and the ice.

> 118 Se fossi domandato altri chi v’ era  
> 119 tu hai da lato quel di Beccheria,  
> 120 di cui segò Fiorenza la gorgiera.  

Shouldst thou be asked who else was there, thou hast beside thee the Beccheria whose gorge was slit by Florence.

- *Se fossi domandato* ‘If thou wert asked’ → “Shouldst thou be asked”: the conditional conjunction is replaced by inversion, and the Italian imperfect subjunctive becomes a construction with “Shouldst”.
- *altri chi v’ era* ‘others who there was’ → “who else was there”: the separately placed expression for other people becomes the adverb “else”, and “there” follows the verb.
- *da lato* ‘at the side’ → “beside thee”: the noun phrase becomes a preposition, with “thee” supplied.
- *quel di Beccheria* ‘that one of Beccheria’ → “the Beccheria”: the demonstrative expression becomes a definite article, and ‘of’ is omitted.
- *di cui segò Fiorenza la gorgiera* ‘of whom sawed Florence the throat’ → “whose gorge was slit by Florence”: the possessive relative becomes “whose”; the active clause becomes passive, making the throat the subject and Florence the agent; and ‘sawed’ becomes “slit”.

## Lines 121–139 — Two Heads in One Frozen Hole

> 121 Gianni de' Soldanier credo che sia  
> 122 più là con Ganellone e Tribaldello,  
> 123 ch' aprì Faenza quando si dormia."  

Gianni de’ Soldanier, I think, is farther on, with Ganellone, and Tribaldello who unbarred Faenza when it slept.”

- *credo che sia più là* ‘I believe that he is farther there’ → “I think, is farther on”: the governing verb becomes a parenthetical remark, and its subordinate clause becomes the main assertion; subjunctive *sia* becomes indicative “is,” and *là* is rendered as “on”.
- *aprì* ‘opened’ → “unbarred”: the translation specifies a means of opening.
- *quando si dormia* ‘when people were sleeping’ → “when it slept”: the impersonal construction becomes a clause with Faenza as its subject; the imperfect is rendered by the simple past.

> 124 Noi eravam partiti già da ello,  
> 125 ch' io vidi due ghiacciati in una buca  
> 126 sì che l' un capo all' altro era cappello;  

We had already left him, when I saw two frozen in one hole so *closely*, that the one head was a cap to the other;

- *Noi eravam partiti già da ello* ‘We had departed already from him’ → “We had already left him”: “left” takes a direct object instead of the Italian verb’s *da* phrase, and “already” moves before the participle.
- *ch’* ‘that’ → “when”: the connective is rendered explicitly as temporal.
- *in una buca* ‘in a hole’ → “in one hole”: the indefinite article is given numerical force.
- *sì che* ‘so that’ → “so *closely*, that”: “closely” is supplied to specify what “so” qualifies.
- *l’ un capo all’ altro era cappello* ‘the one head to the other was a cap’ → “the one head was a cap to the other”: the phrase “to the other” moves after the predicate noun.

> 127 e come il pan per fame si manduca,  
> 128 così il sovran li denti all' altro pose  
> 129 là 've il cervel si giunge con la nuca.  

and as bread is chewed for hunger, so the uppermost put his teeth into the other there where the brain joins with the nape.

- *come il pan per fame si manduca* ‘as the bread for hunger is chewed’ → “as bread is chewed for hunger”: the generic definite article is omitted, and the phrase expressing the cause follows the verb.
- *così il sovran li denti all’ altro pose* ‘so the upper one the teeth to the other put’ → “so the uppermost put his teeth into the other”: “uppermost” replaces ‘upper one’; the verb moves before its object, the definite article becomes the possessive “his,” and “into” gives *all’ altro* a more explicitly penetrative sense.
- *si giunge* ‘joins itself’ → “joins”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes an English intransitive verb.

> 130 Non altrimenti Tideo si rose  
> 131 le tempie a Menalippo per disdegno,  
> 132 che quei faceva il teschio e l' altre cose.  

Not otherwise did Tydeus gnaw the temples of Menalippus for rage, than he the skull and the other parts.

- *Non altrimenti Tideo si rose* ‘Not otherwise Tydeus gnawed for himself’ → “Not otherwise did Tydeus gnaw”: the initial negative expression requires auxiliary “did” and subject–auxiliary inversion; the reflexive *si*, marking the subject’s involvement, has no separate equivalent.
- *le tempie a Menalippo* ‘the temples to Menalippus’ → “the temples of Menalippus”: the Italian dative indicating the possessor becomes an English possessive “of” phrase.
- *per disdegno* ‘for indignation’ → “for rage”: the translation substitutes a stronger term for anger.
- *che quei faceva* ‘than that one did’ → “than he”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the verb standing for the preceding action is omitted.
- *l’ altre cose* ‘the other things’ → “the other parts”: the general noun is narrowed to bodily parts.

> 133 " O tu, che mostri per sì bestial segno  
> 134 odio sopra colui che tu ti mangi,  
> 135 dimmi il perchè," diss' io; "per tal convegno,  

“O thou! who by such brutal token shewest thy hate on him whom thou devourest, tell me why,” I said; “on this condition,

- *mostri per sì bestial segno odio* ‘showest by so bestial a sign hatred’ → “by such brutal token shewest thy hate”: the phrase expressing means moves before the verb; ‘so bestial a sign’ becomes “such brutal token,” and “thy” is supplied before “hate”.
- *sopra colui* ‘upon that one’ → “on him”: the demonstrative is replaced by a personal pronoun.
- *che tu ti mangi* ‘whom thou eatest for thyself’ → “whom thou devourest”: “devourest” intensifies the verb, while the emphatic reflexive *ti* has no separate equivalent.
- *dimmi il perchè* ‘tell me the why’ → “tell me why”: the substantivized interrogative becomes an interrogative adverb introducing an understood question.
- *diss’ io* ‘said I’ → “I said”: subject and verb exchange positions.
- *per tal convegno* ‘by such an agreement’ → “on this condition”: the agreement is expressed as a condition, with a corresponding change of preposition; “this” replaces ‘such’.

> 136 che se tu a ragion di lui ti piangi,  
> 137 sappiendo chi voi siete, e la sua pecca,  
> 138 nel mondo suso ancor io te ne cangi,  
> 139 se quella, con ch' io parlo, non si secca."  

that if thou with reason complainest of him, I, knowing who ye are and his offence, may yet repay thee in the world above, if that, wherewith I speak, be not dried up.”

- *di lui ti piangi* ‘of him thou complainest’ → “complainest of him”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb, and the Italian pronominal verb is rendered by plain “complainest”.
- *sappiendo chi voi siete, e la sua pecca, nel mondo suso ancor io te ne cangi* ‘knowing who ye are, and his offence, in the world above yet I recompense thee for it’ → “I, knowing who ye are and his offence, may yet repay thee in the world above”: the main subject moves before the participial phrase, and the place phrase moves after the main verb and object. The subjunctive *cangi* is expressed with “may,” “yet” moves between the auxiliary and the verb, and *ne*, ‘for it,’ is left unexpressed.
- *non si secca* ‘does not dry up’ → “be not dried up”: the Italian indicative becomes an English subjunctive in the condition, while the pronominal verb becomes a passive-form expression.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Constructions

Carlyle’s translation stays close to the Italian’s vocabulary and sequence of expression, but regularly rearranges its syntax. Subjects and verbs move before objects or complements that Dante places first; adjectives and possessives generally precede their nouns. Thus *di mio concetto il suco* becomes “the juice of my conception”, and *fratei miseri lassi* becomes “weary wretched brothers”, reversing the adjectives as well as moving them. Yet the English retains considerable inversion and compression, together with direct verbal correspondences such as *rime aspre e chiocce*, “rhymes rough and hoarse”, and *fitta in gelatina*, “fixed in gelatine”.

Changes of construction often make Italian expressions more idiomatic or explicit in English. Pronominal verbs can become plain intransitives, as *si dichina* becomes “descends”; elsewhere an active construction becomes passive, as *di cui segò Fiorenza la gorgiera* becomes “whose gorge was slit by Florence”. Subjunctives are frequently expressed with auxiliaries such as “may”, while imperfects become progressive forms. Nouns and verbs also exchange functions: *testimonianza si procaccia* becomes “is testified”, and *non mi dar più lagna*, ‘do not give me more trouble’, becomes “pester me no more”.

The choice of words sometimes expands, sharpens, or interprets the Italian rather than merely reproducing it. *Pontan* becomes “converge and weigh”, *rocce* becomes “rocky steeps”, and *freschi*, ‘cool’, becomes “pinched in ice”. Elsewhere specificity is reduced: *zebe*, ‘she-goats’, becomes “goats”. Supplied words clarify relations or references: “against each other” makes the mutual pressure of *stretti* explicit, “with walls” expands *a chiuder Tebe*, and “when I think of” turns the brief phrase *de’ gelati guazzi* into an express reference to recollection. Such additions coexist with omissions: emphatic or pronominal elements sometimes receive no separate equivalent, including *esso* in *con esso un colpo* and *ne*, ‘for it’, in *te ne cangi*.

Throughout, English also requires small grammatical adjustments that the detailed comparison generally leaves unrecorded. Subject pronouns are supplied where Italian verb endings alone identify the person, and articles accompanying Italian possessives disappear in English possessive phrases. Definite articles with parts of the body are also repeatedly replaced by possessives, as *col muso* becomes “with his muzzle”. The resulting prose preserves much of the Italian’s concrete diction and unusual phrasing while accommodating English grammar through reordered clauses, altered constructions, and selective explanation.
