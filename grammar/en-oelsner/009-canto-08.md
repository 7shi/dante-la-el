# Inferno 8

## Lines 1–24 — Signals and the Approaching Boat

> 1 Io dico seguitando, ch' assai prima  
> 2 che noi fussimo al piè dell' alta torre,  
> 3 gli occhi nostri n' andar suso alla cima,  

I say continuing, that, long before we reached the foot of the high tower, our eyes went upwards to its summit,

- *assai prima che noi fussimo al piè* ‘much before we were at the foot’ → “long before we reached the foot”: the degree expression becomes an expression of elapsed time; being at the foot becomes reaching it, and the Italian imperfect subjunctive becomes an English simple past.
- *gli occhi nostri* ‘the eyes our’ → “our eyes”: the possessive precedes the noun and replaces the definite article.
- *n’ andar* ‘went off’ → “went”: the pronominal particle accompanying the verb of movement has no separate English equivalent.
- *alla cima* ‘to the summit’ → “to its summit”: a possessive replaces the definite article.

> 4 per due fiammette che i vedemmo porre,  
> 5 e un' altra da lungi render cenno  
> 6 tanto, che a pena il potea l' occhio torre.  

because of two flamelets, that we saw put there, and another from far give signal back, so far that the eye could scarcely catch it.

- *che i vedemmo porre* ‘that there we saw place’ → “that we saw put there”: the locative *i* moves to the end; the Italian active infinitive with an unspecified agent becomes an English passive participial construction.
- *render cenno* ‘return signal’ → “give signal back”: the meaning of returning is distributed between the verb and the following adverb.
- *tanto* ‘so much’ → “so far”: the translation explicitly identifies distance as the measure.
- *a pena il potea l’ occhio torre* ‘scarcely it could the eye take’ → “the eye could scarcely catch it”: the subject moves before the modal verb, the object follows the infinitive, and the adverb follows the modal; taking becomes catching.

> 7 Ed io mi volsi al mar di tutto il senno;  
> 8 dissi: "Questo che dice? e che risponde  
> 9 quell' altro foco? e chi son quei che il fenno?"  

And I turn[ed] to the Sea of all intelligence; [I] said: “What says this? and what replies yon other fire? And who are they that made it?”

- *mi volsi* ‘turned myself’ → “turn[ed]”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *tutto il senno* ‘all the understanding’ → “all intelligence”: the definite article disappears, and *senno* is rendered as intellectual capacity rather than understanding or judgment.
- *Questo che dice* ‘this what says’ → “What says this”: the interrogative comes first and the demonstrative subject follows the verb.
- *quell’ altro foco* ‘that other fire’ → “yon other fire”: the demonstrative is rendered by a more explicitly distant one.
- *quei che il fenno* ‘those that it made’ → “they that made it”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative, and the object follows the verb.

> 10 Ed egli a me: "Su per le sucide onde  
> 11 già puoi scorgere quello che s' aspetta,  
> 12 se il fummo del pantan nol ti nasconde."  

And he to me: “Over the squalid waves, already thou mayest discern what is expected, if the vapour of the fen conceal it not from thee.”

- *Su per* ‘up over’ → “Over”: the upward or onward directional element is not separately expressed.
- *puoi* ‘canst’ → “mayest”: ability is expressed through a modal that here conveys possibility.
- *quello che s’ aspetta* ‘that which is awaited’ → “what is expected”: the demonstrative and relative become a fused relative; the Italian *si* construction becomes an English passive, and awaiting becomes expecting.
- *fummo* ‘smoke’ → “vapour”: the translation interprets the substance rather than retaining the literal noun.
- *nol ti nasconde* ‘does not hide it from thee’ → “conceal it not from thee”: the preverbal object pronouns become a following object and prepositional phrase, and the negative follows the object; the Italian present indicative becomes the English subjunctive form.

> 13 Corda non pinse mai da se saetta,  
> 14 che sì corresse via per l' aer snella,  
> 15 com' io vidi una nave piccioletta  
> 16 venir per l' acqua verso noi in quella,  

Never did cord impel from itself an arrow, that ran through the air so quickly, as a little bark which I saw come towards us then [through the water],

- *Corda non pinse mai* ‘cord never impelled’ → “Never did cord impel”: the negative adverb is fronted, requiring auxiliary inversion and the base form of the verb.
- *saetta* ‘arrow’ → “an arrow”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *che sì corresse via per l’ aer snella* ‘that so should run away through the air swift’ → “that ran through the air so quickly”: the imperfect subjunctive becomes a simple past indicative; *via* is not separately represented; the adjective describing the arrow becomes an adverb modifying its movement, with the degree word moved beside it.
- *com’ io vidi una nave piccioletta venir* ‘as I saw a ship little come’ → “as a little bark which I saw come”: the ship becomes the immediate term of comparison, while the seeing becomes a supplied relative clause; the diminutive adjective precedes its noun, and the general noun for a ship becomes the more specific “bark”.
- *per l’ acqua verso noi in quella* ‘through the water towards us at that moment’ → “towards us then [through the water]”: the water phrase moves after the direction and time expressions; the elliptical Italian temporal phrase becomes a single adverb.

> 17 sotto il governo d'un sol galeoto,  
> 18 che gridava: "Or se' giunta, anima fella?"  

under the guidance of a single steersman, who cried : “Now art thou arrived, fell spirit?”

- *governo* ‘steering’ → “guidance”: the specific nautical activity is expressed by a broader noun.
- *galeoto* ‘oarsman’ → “steersman”: the translation assigns a different nautical role.
- *gridava* ‘was crying’ → “cried”: the ongoing action expressed by the imperfect is rendered in the simple past.
- *anima fella* ‘soul fell’ → “fell spirit”: the adjective precedes the noun, and “spirit” replaces ‘soul’.

> 19 "Flegias, Flegias, tu gridi a voto,"  
> 20 disse lo mio signore, "a questa volta;  
> 21 più non ci avrai, che sol passando il loto."  

“Phlegyas, Phlegyas,” said my Lord, “this time thou criest in vain; thou shalt not have us longer than while we pass the wash.”

- *tu gridi a voto, disse lo mio signore, a questa volta* ‘thou criest in vain, said my lord, on this occasion’ → “said my Lord, “this time thou criest in vain;”: the reporting clause moves before the statement about crying in vain, rather than interrupting it; the temporal phrase moves to the beginning of that statement and loses its preposition.
- *più non ci avrai, che sol passando il loto* ‘no more wilt thou have us than only while crossing the mud’ → “thou shalt not have us longer than while we pass the wash.”: *più* is explicitly interpreted as duration; the negative and object are reordered; *sol* is omitted; the gerund becomes a finite clause with “we” as its subject; and mud becomes “wash”.

> 22 Quale colui, che grande inganno ascolta,  
> 23 che gli sia fatto, e poi se ne rammarca,  
> 24 fecesi Flegias nell' ira accolta.  

As one who listens to some great deceit which has been done to him, and then sore resents it: such grew Phlegyas in his gathered rage.

- *Quale colui* ‘as that one’ → “As one”: the demonstrative force is dropped.
- *che grande inganno ascolta* ‘who great deceit hears’ → “who listens to some great deceit”: hearing becomes listening to; “some” is supplied, and the object moves after the verb.
- *che gli sia fatto* ‘which be done to him’ → “which has been done to him”: the present subjunctive passive becomes a present perfect indicative passive, and the preverbal indirect-object pronoun becomes a following prepositional phrase.
- *e poi se ne rammarca* ‘and then grieves over it’ → “and then sore resents it”: grieving or complaining becomes resenting; “sore” adds intensity; the pronominal construction becomes a transitive verb with a direct object.
- *fecesi Flegias* ‘made himself Phlegyas’ → “such grew Phlegyas”: reflexive making becomes intransitive becoming, and “such” supplies an explicit correlative to the opening comparison.
- *nell’ ira accolta* ‘in the anger gathered’ → “in his gathered rage”: a possessive replaces the definite article, and the participial adjective moves before the noun.

## Lines 25–57 — Indignation on the Muddy Lake

> 25 Lo duca mio discese nella barca,  
> 26 e poi mi fece entrare appresso lui,  
> 27 e sol quand’ io fui dentro parve carca.  

My Guide descended into the skiff, and then made me enter after him; and not till I was in, did it seem laden.

- *Lo duca mio* ‘the leader my’ → “My Guide”: ‘leader’ becomes “Guide,” and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *barca* ‘boat’ → “skiff”: a more specific kind of boat is named.
- *mi fece entrare* ‘me made enter’ → “made me enter”: the object pronoun moves after the causative verb.
- *sol quand’ io fui dentro parve carca* ‘only when I was inside it seemed laden’ → “not till I was in, did it seem laden”: the affirmative restriction becomes a negative temporal construction, requiring inversion and the auxiliary “did.”

> 28 Tosto che il duca ed io nel legno fui,  
> 29 secando se ne va l’ antica prora  
> 30 dell’ acqua più che non suol con altrui.  

Soon as my Guide and I were in the boat, its ancient prow went on, cutting more of the water than it is wont with others.

- *il duca* ‘the leader’ → “my Guide”: “Guide” replaces ‘leader,’ and “my” replaces the definite article.
- *nel legno fui* ‘in the wood I was’ → “were in the boat”: the metaphorical ‘wood’ becomes “boat”; the locative phrase follows the verb; and the Italian singular verb becomes plural to agree with the compound subject.
- *secando ... dell’ acqua più* ‘cutting ... of the water more’ → “cutting more of the water”: the participial phrase moves after the main clause, and “more” precedes its complement.
- *se ne va l’ antica prora* ‘goes onward the ancient prow’ → “its ancient prow went on”: the subject moves before the verb, the present becomes past, and “its” replaces the definite article.
- *che non suol* ‘than it is not wont’ → “than it is wont”: the Italian comparative construction’s non-negative *non* is omitted.

> 31 Mentre noi correvam la morta gora,  
> 32 dinanzi mi si fece un pien di fango,  
> 33 e disse: “Chi se’ tu, che vieni anzi ora?”  

Whilst we were running through the dead channel, there rose before me one full of mud, and said: "Who art thou, that comest before thy time?"

- *noi correvam la morta gora* ‘we ran the dead channel’ → “we were running through the dead channel”: the imperfect becomes an English past progressive, and “through” replaces the Italian verb’s direct construction with the water traversed.
- *dinanzi mi si fece* ‘before me made himself’ → “there rose before me”: the reflexive expression for presenting oneself becomes “rose,” with introductory “there.”
- *anzi ora* ‘before time’ → “before thy time”: the possessive “thy” is supplied.

> 34 Ed io a lui: “S’ io vegno, non rimango;  
> 35 ma tu chi sei, che sei sì fatto brutto?”  

And I to him: "If I come, I remain not; but thou, who art thou, that hast become so foul?"

- *non rimango* ‘not remain’ → “I remain not”: the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *tu chi sei* ‘you who are’ → “thou, who art thou”: the initial pronoun is retained as a detached topic and repeated within the question.
- *che sei sì fatto brutto* ‘who are thus made ugly’ → “that hast become so foul”: the description of a present condition becomes a present-perfect construction of becoming; ‘ugly’ is rendered as “foul.”

> 36 Rispose: “Vedi che son un che piango.”  

He answered: "Thou seest that I am one who weep."

- Literal.

> 37 Ed io a lui: “Con piangere e con lutto,  
> 38 spirito maledetto, ti rimani!  
> 39 ch’ io ti conosco, ancor sia lordo tutto.”  

And I to him: "With weeping, and with sorrow, accursed spirit, remain thou! for I know thee, all filthy as thou art."

- *spirito maledetto* ‘spirit accursed’ → “accursed spirit”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *ti rimani* ‘remain yourself’ → “remain thou”: the pronominal *ti* is not separately represented; English instead expresses the imperative’s subject.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *io ti conosco* ‘I thee know’ → “I know thee”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *ancor sia lordo tutto* ‘although you be filthy all’ → “all filthy as thou art”: the concessive clause is recast with “as,” the adjective phrase is fronted, “all” precedes “filthy,” and the subjunctive becomes indicative.

> 40 Allora stese al legno ambo le mani:  
> 41 per che il maestro accorto lo sospinse,  
> 42 dicendo: “Via costà con gli altri cani!”  

Then he stretched both hands to the boat, whereat the wary Master thrust him off, saying: "Away there with the other dogs!"

- *stese al legno ambo le mani* ‘stretched to the wood both the hands’ → “stretched both hands to the boat”: the direct object precedes the prepositional phrase, ‘wood’ becomes “boat,” and the article before ‘hands’ is omitted.
- *per che* ‘for which’ → “whereat”: the causal connective becomes a relative adverb expressing the occasion for the following action.
- *il maestro accorto* ‘the master wary’ → “the wary Master”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *lo sospinse* ‘him pushed’ → “thrust him off”: the object follows the verb, “thrust” strengthens ‘pushed,’ and “off” makes the direction explicit.

> 43 Lo collo poi con le braccia mi cinse,  
> 44 baciommi il volto, e disse: “Alma sdegnosa,  
> 45 benedetta colei che in te s’ incinse.  

And he put his arms about my neck, kissed my face, and said: "Indignant soul! blessed be she that bore thee.

- *Lo collo poi con le braccia mi cinse* ‘the neck then with the arms he encircled for me’ → “And he put his arms about my neck”: the arms become the direct object rather than the instrument, and the neck becomes the complement of “about.” The possessive “my” represents *mi*, “his” is supplied, and *poi* is replaced by the looser connection “And.”
- *baciommi il volto* ‘kissed me the face’ → “kissed my face”: the personal clitic becomes a possessive modifying the body part.
- *Alma sdegnosa* ‘soul indignant’ → “Indignant soul”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *benedetta colei* ‘blessed she’ → “blessed be she”: “be” is supplied to express the blessing.
- *che in te s’ incinse* ‘who with you became pregnant’ → “that bore thee”: becoming pregnant is replaced by bearing a child; the reflexive construction and its prepositional complement become an active transitive verb.

> 46 Quei fu al mondo persona orgogliosa;  
> 47 bontà non è che sua memoria fregi:  
> 48 così s’ è l’ ombra sua qui furiosa.  

In your world, that was an arrogant personage; good there is none to ornament the memory of him: so is his shadow here in fury.

- *Quei fu al mondo* ‘that one was in the world’ → “In your world, that was”: the locative phrase is fronted, “your” replaces the article, and ‘that one’ is shortened to “that.”
- *persona orgogliosa* ‘a person proud’ → “an arrogant personage”: “personage” replaces ‘person,’ ‘proud’ is rendered as “arrogant,” and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *bontà non è* ‘goodness is not’ → “good there is none”: the abstract noun becomes substantival “good,” and the negative statement becomes an existential construction with “there” and “none.”
- *che sua memoria fregi* ‘that his memory may adorn’ → “to ornament the memory of him”: the subjunctive relative clause becomes an infinitive; the object follows the verb; and the possessive becomes the postposed phrase “of him.”
- *s’ è l’ ombra sua* ‘is for itself the shadow his’ → “is his shadow”: the pronominal *si* is not separately represented, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *furiosa* ‘furious’ → “in fury”: the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase containing a noun.

> 49 Quanti si tengon or lassù gran regi,  
> 50 che qui staranno come porci in brago,  
> 51 di sè lasciando orribili dispregi!”  

How many up there now think themselves great kings, that shall lie here like swine in mire, leaving behind them horrible reproaches!"

- *si tengon* ‘themselves hold’ → “think themselves”: the idiom of holding oneself to be something becomes “think,” and the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.
- *or lassù* ‘now up there’ → “up there now”: the two adverbials exchange places and move before the verb.
- *che qui staranno* ‘who here will stay’ → “that shall lie here”: ‘stay’ becomes the more specific “lie,” and “here” follows the verb.
- *di sè lasciando* ‘of themselves leaving’ → “leaving behind them”: the participle moves first, and the phrase specifying whose reputation remains becomes the idiom of leaving something behind.
- *dispregi* ‘expressions of contempt’ → “reproaches”: contempt is rendered as expressed blame.

> 52 Ed io: “Maestro, molto sarei vago  
> 53 di vederlo attuffare in questa broda,  
> 54 prima che noi uscissimo del lago.”  

And I: "Master, I should be glad to see him dipped in this swill, ere we quit the lake."

- *molto sarei vago* ‘much I should be desirous’ → “I should be glad”: *molto* is omitted, and eagerness or desire becomes anticipated pleasure.
- *vederlo attuffare* ‘see him plunge’ → “see him dipped”: the infinitive is understood passively and rendered by a passive participle.
- *broda* ‘thin broth’ → “swill”: the translation uses a different disparaging term for liquid.
- *prima che noi uscissimo del lago* ‘before we should go out of the lake’ → “ere we quit the lake”: the Italian imperfect subjunctive becomes an English present, and ‘go out of’ becomes the transitive “quit.”

> 55 Ed egli a me: “Avanti che la proda  
> 56 ti si lasci veder, tu sarai sazio;  
> 57 di tal disio converrà che tu goda.”  

And he to me: "Before the shore comes to thy view, thou shalt be satisfied; it is fitting that thou shouldst be gratified in such a wish."

- *Avanti che la proda ti si lasci veder* ‘before the shore lets itself be seen by you’ → “Before the shore comes to thy view”: the reflexive causative construction becomes an idiom with “comes”; the infinitive ‘see’ becomes the noun “view,” and the personal pronoun becomes its possessive.
- *sazio* ‘sated’ → “satisfied”: a word suggesting satiety is rendered by a more general term.
- *di tal disio* ‘of such a desire’ → “in such a wish”: “in” replaces ‘of,’ and the phrase moves from the beginning of the clause to its end.
- *converrà* ‘it will be fitting’ → “it is fitting”: the future becomes present.
- *che tu goda* ‘that you enjoy’ → “that thou shouldst be gratified”: the active verb becomes a passive construction, and the subjunctive is expressed with “shouldst.”

## Lines 58–90 — Filippo Argenti and the Gates of Dis

> 58 Dopo ciò poco, vidi quello strazio  
> 59 far di costui alle fangose genti,  
> 60 che Dio ancor ne lodo e ne ringrazio.  

A little after this, I saw the muddy people make such rending of him, that even now I praise and thank God for it.

- *Dopo ciò poco* ‘after this a little’ → “A little after this”: the expression of elapsed time moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *vidi quello strazio far di costui alle fangose genti* ‘I saw that rending done to this man by the muddy people’ → “I saw the muddy people make such rending of him”: the infinitive construction, in which *alle fangose genti* identifies the agents, becomes an active construction with “the muddy people” before “make.” Demonstrative *quello* becomes degree-marking “such,” and *costui* becomes the personal pronoun “him.”
- *che Dio ancor ne lodo e ne ringrazio* ‘that God still for it I praise and for it I thank’ → “that even now I praise and thank God for it”: “God” moves after the coordinated verbs; the repeated *ne* becomes one concluding “for it”; and *ancor* is expanded to the emphatic “even now.”

> 61 Tutti gridavano: “A Filippo Argenti! ”  

All cried: "At Filippo Argenti!"

- *gridavano* ‘were crying’ → “cried”: the imperfect becomes a simple English past, without explicitly marking continuing or repeated action.

> 62 Lo Fiorentino spirito bizzarro  
> 63 in se medesmo si volgea co’ denti.  

The passionate Florentine spirit turned with his teeth upon himself.

- *Lo Fiorentino spirito bizzarro* ‘the Florentine spirit irascible’ → “The passionate Florentine spirit”: *bizzarro* is rendered more broadly as “passionate,” and its adjective moves before both the noun and “Florentine.”
- *in se medesmo si volgea co’ denti* ‘upon himself he was turning with the teeth’ → “turned with his teeth upon himself”: the phrase referring to himself moves to the end; the imperfect becomes a simple past; and the definite article before “teeth” becomes the possessive “his.”

> 64 Quivi il lasciammo, che più non ne narro;  

Here we left him, so that of him I tell no more;

- *Quivi* ‘there’ → “Here”: the spatial demonstrative changes.
- *il lasciammo* ‘him we left’ → “we left him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *che più non ne narro* ‘for I no longer tell of him’ → “so that of him I tell no more”: the translation takes *che* consequentially rather than causally; *ne* becomes a separate prepositional phrase before the subject, and the negative expression moves after the verb.

> 65 ma negli orecchi mi percosse un duolo,  
> 66 per ch’io avanti intento l’ occhio sbarro.  

but in my ears a wailing smote me, whereat I bent my eyes intently forward.

- *negli orecchi* ‘in the ears’ → “in my ears”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *mi percosse un duolo* ‘me struck a grief’ → “a wailing smote me”: *duolo* is rendered as the audible expression of grief; the subject moves before the verb and the object pronoun after it.
- *per ch’* ‘because of which’ → “whereat”: the explicit causal connection becomes a compact relative adverb.
- *io avanti intento l’ occhio sbarro* ‘I forward, intent, the eye open wide’ → “I bent my eyes intently forward”: opening the eye wide becomes directing the eyes; the present becomes a past tense, singular ‘eye’ becomes plural “eyes,” and the definite article becomes “my.” The adjective *intento* becomes the adverb “intently,” while the directional expression moves to the end.

> 67 Lo buon maestro disse: “Omai, figliuolo,  
> 68 s’ appressa la città che ha nome Dite,  
> 69 co’ gravi cittadin, col grande stuolo.”  

The kind Master said: "Now, Son, the city that is named of Dis draws nigh, with its grave citizens, with its great company."

- *buon* ‘good’ → “kind”: the translation specifies kindness rather than using the broader adjective.
- *s’ appressa la città che ha nome Dite* ‘draws near the city that has the name Dis’ → “the city that is named of Dis draws nigh”: the subject and its relative clause move before the main verb. Having a name becomes a passive naming construction, with “of” supplied before “Dis.”
- *co’ gravi cittadin, col grande stuolo* ‘with the grave citizens, with the great host’ → “with its grave citizens, with its great company”: possessives replace the definite articles, and *stuolo* is rendered by the less specifically collective or military “company.”

> 70 Ed io: “Maestro, già le sue meschite  
> 71 là entro certo nella valle cerno  
> 72 vermiglie, come se di fuoco uscite  
> 73 fossero.” Ed ei mi disse: “Il foco eterno,  

And I: "Master, already I discern its mosques, distinctly there within the valley, red as if they had come out of fire." And to me he said: "The eternal fire,

- *le sue meschite là entro certo nella valle cerno* ‘its mosques there within, certainly, in the valley I discern’ → “I discern its mosques, distinctly there within the valley”: the verb moves before its object and the intervening expressions. *Certo* becomes “distinctly,” emphasizing clarity of perception rather than certainty, and moves before “there within.”
- *vermiglie* ‘vermilion’ → “red”: the translation uses a less specific colour term.
- *come se di fuoco uscite fossero* ‘as if out of fire they were come’ → “as if they had come out of fire”: the verb precedes the expression of origin, and the Italian perfect auxiliary ‘be’ becomes English “had.” Both constructions express an unreal anterior event.
- *Ed ei mi disse* ‘and he to me said’ → “And to me he said”: the indirect object becomes an explicit prepositional phrase and moves before the subject.
- *Il foco eterno* ‘the fire eternal’ → “The eternal fire”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 74 ch’ entro l’ affoca, le dimostra rosse,  
> 75 come tu vedi in questo basso inferno.”  

which causes them to glow within, shows them red, as thou seest, in this low Hell."

- *ch’ entro l’ affoca* ‘which within sets it ablaze’ → “which causes them to glow within”: the single verb becomes a causative phrase, and “within” moves to its end. The singular object referring to the city becomes plural “them,” referring to the mosques.
- *le dimostra rosse* ‘them shows red’ → “shows them red”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 76 Noi pur giugnemmo dentro all’ alte fosse,  
> 77 che vallan quella terra sconsolata;  
> 78 le mura mi parea, che ferro fosse.  

We now arrived in the deep fosses, which moat that joyless city; the walls seemed to me as if they were of iron.

- *pur* ‘at last’ → “now”: the particle marking eventual arrival becomes a straightforward temporal adverb.
- *dentro all’* ‘inside the’ → “in the”: the fuller expression of an interior position is compressed.
- *vallan* ‘encircle’ → “moat”: the translation specifies encirclement by a moat.
- *quella terra sconsolata* ‘that city comfortless’ → “that joyless city”: “joyless” replaces ‘comfortless,’ and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *le mura mi parea, che ferro fosse* ‘the walls—it seemed to me that it was iron’ → “the walls seemed to me as if they were of iron”: the Italian singular, impersonal *parea* becomes a verb with “the walls” as its subject. The following clause becomes an “as if” comparison, singular *fosse* becomes plural “they were,” and the predicate noun *ferro* becomes the material phrase “of iron.”

> 79 Non senza prima far grande aggirata,  
> 80 venimmo in parte, dove il nocchier, forte,  
> 81 “Uscite,” ci gridò, “qui è l’ entrata.”  

Not before making a long circuit, did we come to a place where the boatman loudly cried to us: "Go out: here is the entrance."

- *Non senza prima far grande aggirata* ‘not without first making a great circuit’ → “Not before making a long circuit”: ‘not without first’ becomes “Not before,” shifting from a prerequisite to a temporal formulation. *Grande* becomes “long,” and an indefinite article is supplied before the singular count noun.
- *venimmo in parte* ‘we came to a place’ → “did we come to a place”: the initial negative expression produces subject–auxiliary inversion and requires “did.”
- *ci gridò* ‘to us he cried’ → “cried to us”: the indirect object follows the verb as a prepositional phrase, and the reporting clause is placed before the command rather than interrupting it.

> 82 Io vidi più di mille in sulle porte  
> 83 da’ ciel piovuti, che stizzosamente  
> 84 dicean: “Chi è costui, che senza morte  
> 85 va per lo regno della morta gente?”  

Above the gates I saw more than a thousand spirits, rained from [the] Heaven[s], who angrily exclaimed: "Who is that, who, without death, goes through the kingdom of the dead?"

- *Io vidi più di mille in sulle porte* ‘I saw more than a thousand on the gates’ → “Above the gates I saw more than a thousand spirits”: the location moves to the beginning, “Above” replaces ‘on,’ and “spirits” supplies a noun for the substantivally used number.
- *da’ ciel piovuti* ‘from the heavens rained’ → “rained from [the] Heaven[s]”: the participle moves before the expression of origin.
- *che stizzosamente dicean* ‘who angrily were saying’ → “who angrily exclaimed”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the neutral verb of saying becomes the more forceful “exclaimed.”
- *Chi è costui* ‘who is this one’ → “Who is that”: the demonstrative identifying the person changes from proximal to distal.
- *della morta gente* ‘of the dead people’ → “of the dead”: the noun *gente* is omitted, and “dead” functions substantivally.

> 86 E il savio mio maestro fece segno  
> 87 di voler lor parlar segretamente.  

And my sage Master made a sign of wishing to speak with them in secret.

- *savio mio maestro* ‘wise my master’ → “my sage Master”: the descriptive adjective moves after the possessive.
- *fece segno* ‘made sign’ → “made a sign”: English supplies an indefinite article before the singular count noun.
- *di voler* ‘of to wish’ → “of wishing”: the Italian infinitive becomes an English gerund after the preposition.
- *lor parlar* ‘to them to speak’ → “to speak with them”: the pronominal indirect object becomes a following prepositional phrase, and “with” presents them as participants in the conversation.
- *segretamente* ‘secretly’ → “in secret”: an adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.

> 88 Allor chiusero un poco il gran disdegno,  
> 89 e disser: “Vien tu solo, e quei sen vada,  
> 90 che sì ardito entrò per questo regno.  

Then they somewhat shut up their great disdain, and said: "Come thou alone; and let that one go, who has entered so daringly into this kingdom.

- *chiusero un poco il gran disdegno* ‘they shut a little the great disdain’ → “they somewhat shut up their great disdain”: *chiusero* becomes the phrasal verb “shut up”; *un poco* becomes “somewhat” and moves before the verb; and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *e quei sen vada* ‘and may that one go away’ → “and let that one go”: the independent subjunctive expressing a command becomes a “let” construction, and the departure expressed by *andarsene* is reduced to “go,” without ‘away.’
- *sì ardito* ‘so bold’ → “so daringly”: an adjective describing the subject becomes an adverb modifying the action and moves after the verb.
- *entrò* ‘entered’ → “has entered”: the simple past becomes a present perfect.
- *per* ‘through’ → “into”: movement through the kingdom becomes movement into it.

## Lines 91–123 — The Guide Is Turned Away

> 91 Sol si ritorni per la folle strada;  

Let him return alone his foolish way;

- *Sol si ritorni* ‘alone let him return’ → “Let him return alone”: “alone” moves after the verb; the pronominal *si* has no separate English equivalent.
- *per la folle strada* ‘along the foolish road’ → “his foolish way”: the preposition is omitted, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 92 provi se sa: chè tu qui rimarrai,  
> 93 che gli hai scorta sì buia contrada.”  

try, if he can: for thou shalt stay here, that hast escorted him through so dark a country.”

- *provi se sa* ‘let him try whether he knows how’ → “try, if he can”: the third-person subjunctive command becomes the bare “try”; knowing how is expressed as ability.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *tu qui rimarrai* ‘thou here wilt remain’ → “thou shalt stay here”: the adverb moves after the verb, and the future is expressed with “shalt”.
- *che gli hai scorta sì buia contrada* ‘who for him hast guided so dark a country’ → “that hast escorted him through so dark a country”: the Italian makes the country traversed the direct object and the person guided an indirect object; English makes “him” the direct object and supplies “through” before the country. An indefinite article is also supplied.

> 94 Pensa lettor, se io mi sconfortai  
> 95 nel suon delle parole maledette:  
> 96 ch’ io non credetti ritornarci mai.  

Judge, Reader, if I was discouraged at the sound of the accursed words: for I believed not that I ever should return [hither].

- *Pensa* ‘think’ → “Judge”: an invitation to consider becomes an invitation to judge.
- *io mi sconfortai* ‘I lost heart’ → “I was discouraged”: the Italian reflexive verb becomes a construction with “was” and a participle.
- *nel suon* ‘in the sound’ → “at the sound”: the preposition expresses the occasion or cause of discouragement rather than its setting.
- *delle parole maledette* ‘of the words accursed’ → “of the accursed words”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: subordination becomes coordination.
- *io non credetti ritornarci mai* ‘I did not believe ever to return here’ → “I believed not that I ever should return [hither]”: the infinitive becomes a finite clause introduced by “that”; “should” expresses futurity from a past standpoint. The negative follows “believed”, while *mai* moves before the verb of returning.

> 97 “O caro duca mio, che più di sette  
> 98 volte m’ hai sicurtà renduta, e tratto  
> 99 d’ alto periglio che incontra mi stette,  
> 100 non mi lasciar,” diss’ io, “così disfatto;  

“O my loved Guide, who more than seven times hast restored me to safety, and rescued from deep peril that stood before me, leave me not so undone,” I said;

- *caro duca mio* ‘dear guide mine’ → “my loved Guide”: “loved” replaces ‘dear’, and the possessive moves before the adjective and noun.
- *m’ hai sicurtà renduta* ‘to me hast safety restored’ → “hast restored me to safety”: Italian makes *sicurtà* the thing restored and *m’* its recipient; English makes “me” the direct object and “to safety” the resulting condition.
- *tratto* ‘drawn’ → “rescued”: the translation makes the saving effect explicit rather than retaining the image of drawing someone out.
- *d’ alto periglio* ‘from high peril’ → “from deep peril”: “deep” replaces the Italian adjective ‘high’.
- *che incontra mi stette* ‘that against me stood’ → “that stood before me”: opposition is expressed spatially as being “before” someone, and the verb precedes that phrase.
- *non mi lasciar* ‘not me leave’ → “leave me not”: the verb moves before its object and the negative.
- *diss’ io* ‘said I’ → “I said”: subject and verb exchange places.

> 101 e se l’ andar più oltre c’ è negato,  
> 102 ritroviam l’ orme nostre insieme ratto.”  

“and if to go farther be denied us, let us retrace our steps together rapidly.”

- *l’ andar* ‘the going’ → “to go”: the substantivized infinitive becomes an English infinitive without an article.
- *più oltre* ‘more beyond’ → “farther”: the two-word comparative expression becomes one comparative adverb.
- *c’ è negato* ‘to us is denied’ → “be denied us”: the indicative ‘is’ becomes the English subjunctive “be” after “if”, and the recipient moves after the passive verb.
- *ritroviam l’ orme nostre* ‘let us find again the tracks of ours’ → “let us retrace our steps”: finding the tracks again becomes retracing them; “steps” replaces ‘tracks’, and the possessive moves before the noun.

> 103 E quel signor, che lì m’ avea menato,  
> 104 mi disse: “Non temer, chè il nostro passo  
> 105 non ci può torre alcun: da tal n’ è dato.  

And that Lord, who had led me thither, said to me: “Fear not, for our passage none can take from us: by Such has it been given to us.

- *lì m’ avea menato* ‘there me had led’ → “had led me thither”: the object and place-adverb move after the verb, and the locative ‘there’ becomes the directional “thither”.
- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the recipient follows the verb.
- *Non temer* ‘not fear’ → “Fear not”: the negative follows the verb.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: subordination becomes coordination.
- *il nostro passo non ci può torre alcun* ‘our passage not from us can take anyone’ → “our passage none can take from us”: *non … alcun* becomes the single negative subject “none”, which moves before the verb; *ci* becomes “from us” after the infinitive.
- *da tal n’ è dato* ‘by such a one to us is given’ → “by Such has it been given to us”: the present passive becomes a present perfect passive; the recipient moves to the end, and “it” explicitly resumes “our passage”.

> 106 Ma qui m’ attendi; e lo spirito lasso  
> 107 conforta e ciba di speranza buona,  
> 108 ch’ io non ti lascerò nel mondo basso.”  

But thou, wait here for me; and comfort and feed thy wearied spirit with good hope: for I will not forsake thee in the low world.”

- *qui m’ attendi* ‘here me await’ → “wait here for me”: the transitive verb becomes “wait” with a “for” phrase, and “here” moves after the verb.
- *lo spirito lasso conforta e ciba* ‘the spirit weary comfort and feed’ → “comfort and feed thy wearied spirit”: the object moves after the coordinated verbs; the definite article becomes “thy”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *di speranza buona* ‘of hope good’ → “with good hope”: “with” replaces *di*, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: subordination becomes coordination.
- *io non ti lascerò* ‘I not thee shall leave’ → “I will not forsake thee”: “forsake” gives ‘leave’ the stronger sense of abandonment, and the object follows the verb.
- *nel mondo basso* ‘in the world low’ → “in the low world”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 109 Così sen va, e quivi m’ abbandona  
> 110 lo dolce padre, ed io rimango in forse:  
> 111 chè il sì, e il no nel capo mi tenzona.  

Thus the gentle Father goes, and leaves me here, and I remain in doubt: for yes and no contend within my head.

- *sen va* ‘goes away’ → “goes”: the departure expressed by *ne* is left unexpressed, and the pronominal construction becomes a simple intransitive verb.
- *quivi m’ abbandona* ‘there me abandons’ → “leaves me here”: “here” replaces ‘there’, “leaves” softens ‘abandons’, and both object and place-adverb follow the verb.
- *lo dolce padre* ‘the sweet father’ → “the gentle Father”: “gentle” replaces ‘sweet’; the subject moves from after the two coordinated verbs to before them.
- *in forse* ‘in perhaps’ → “in doubt”: the substantivized adverb *forse* becomes an abstract noun.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: subordination becomes coordination.
- *il sì, e il no* ‘the yes and the no’ → “yes and no”: the articles marking the two answers as nouns are omitted.
- *nel capo mi tenzona* ‘in the head for me contends’ → “contend within my head”: the singular verb becomes plural; the personal dative becomes the possessive “my”, and the phrase locating the contention moves after the verb.

> 112 Udir non potei quello ch’ a lor si porse:  
> 113 ma ei non stette là con essi guari,  
> 114 che ciascun dentro a pruova si ricorse.  

I could not hear that which [was] offered to them; but he had not long stood with them, when they all, vying with one another, rushed in again.

- *Udir non potei* ‘hear I could not’ → “I could not hear”: the infinitive moves from the beginning to after the modal and negative.
- *a lor si porse* ‘to them was offered’ → “[was] offered to them”: the Italian passive construction with *si* becomes an English passive with “was”, and the recipient moves after the verb.
- *ei non stette là con essi guari* ‘he did not stay there with them long’ → “he had not long stood with them”: the simple past becomes a past perfect; “stood” replaces ‘stayed’, *là* is omitted, and “long” moves before the participle.
- *che* ‘that’ → “when”: English makes the temporal connection explicit after “not long”.
- *ciascun* ‘each’ → “they all”: the distributive singular becomes a collective plural.
- *dentro* ‘inside’ → “in”: the adverb moves from before the verb to after it.
- *a pruova* ‘in rivalry’ → “vying with one another”: an adverbial phrase becomes a participial phrase, with the reciprocal relationship explicitly stated.
- *si ricorse* ‘ran back’ → “rushed” and “again”: “rushed” emphasizes haste, while the returning force of *ri-* is rendered as repetition by “again”; the pronominal form has no separate equivalent.

> 115 Chiuser le porte quei nostri avversari  
> 116 nel petto al mio signor, che fuor rimase,  
> 117 e rivolsesi a me con passi rari.  

These our adversaries closed the gates on the breast of my [Lord] who remained without; and turned to me with slow steps.

- *Chiuser le porte quei nostri avversari* ‘closed the gates those our adversaries’ → “These our adversaries closed the gates”: the subject moves before the verb and object, and “These” replaces ‘those’.
- *nel petto al mio signor* ‘in the breast to my lord’ → “on the breast of my [Lord]”: “on” replaces ‘in’, and the Italian dative identifying the person affected becomes an “of” phrase expressing possession.
- *che fuor rimase* ‘who outside remained’ → “who remained without”: the place-adverb moves after the verb.
- *rivolsesi a me* ‘turned himself to me’ → “turned to me”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive English verb.
- *con passi rari* ‘with steps sparse’ → “with slow steps”: the spacing of the steps is interpreted as slowness, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 118 Gli occhi alla terra, e le ciglia avea rase  
> 119 d’ ogni baldanza, e dicea ne’ sospiri:  
> 120 “Chi m’ ha negate le dolenti case?”  

He had his eyes upon the ground, and his eyebrows shorn of all boldness, and said with sighs: “Who hath denied me the doleful houses?”

- *Gli occhi alla terra, e le ciglia avea rase* ‘the eyes toward the earth, and the eyebrows he had shorn’ → “He had his eyes upon the ground, and his eyebrows shorn”: the subject and verb move before both objects; the definite articles become possessives, and “upon the ground” renders the direction of the eyes as their resting position.
- *d’ ogni baldanza* ‘of every boldness’ → “of all boldness”: distributive ‘every’ becomes the collective “all”.
- *dicea ne’ sospiri* ‘was saying in the sighs’ → “said with sighs”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, “with” replaces ‘in’, and the article is omitted.
- *m’ ha negate* ‘to me has denied’ → “hath denied me”: the recipient moves after the verb and is expressed without a preposition.

> 121 Ed a me disse: “Tu, perch’ io m’ adiri,  
> 122 non sbigottir: ch’ io vincerò la pruova,  
> 123 qual che alla difension dentro s’ aggiri.  

And to me he said: “Thou, be not dismayed, though I get angry: for I will master the trial, whatever be contrived within for hindrance.

- *perch’ io m’ adiri* ‘because I grow angry’ → “though I get angry”: the translation adopts a concessive rather than a causal interpretation of *perch’*; the clause moves from before the prohibition to after it.
- *non sbigottir* ‘do not lose heart’ → “be not dismayed”: the intransitive verb becomes “be” with a participle, and the negative follows “be”.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: subordination becomes coordination.
- *io vincerò la pruova* ‘I shall win the trial’ → “I will master the trial”: “master” replaces the expression of winning or overcoming.
- *qual che alla difension dentro s’ aggiri* ‘whoever may bustle about within for the defence’ → “whatever be contrived within for hindrance”: the Italian can refer to whoever is actively occupied with the defence inside; the translation instead makes the subject an unspecified thing contrived. The reflexive verb becomes passive, “hindrance” replaces ‘defence’, and the purpose-phrase moves to the end.

## Lines 124–130 — The City Will Be Opened

> 124 Questa lor tracotanza non è nuova,  

This insolence of theirs is nothing new:

- *Questa lor tracotanza* ‘this their insolence’ → “This insolence of theirs”: the possessive before the noun becomes a possessive phrase after it.
- *non è nuova* ‘is not new’ → “is nothing new”: negation of the adjective becomes a construction with the pronoun “nothing”.

> 125 chè già l’ usaro a men segreta porta,  
> 126 la qual senza serrame ancor si trova.  

for they shewed it once at a less secret gate, which still is found unbarred.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *già l’usaro* ‘formerly they used it’ → “they shewed it once”: exercising or practising the insolence becomes showing it; *già* is rendered as “once” and moved after the verb and object.
- *a men segreta porta* ‘at less secret gate’ → “at a less secret gate”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *senza serrame* ‘without a fastening’ → “unbarred”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adjective and moves from before *ancor si trova* to after “still is found”.

> 127 Sovr’ essa vedestù la scritta morta;  

Over it thou sawest the dead inscription;

- *vedestù* ‘sawest thou’ → “thou sawest”: the subject pronoun, attached to the Italian verb, moves before it.
- *la scritta morta* ‘the inscription dead’ → “the dead inscription”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 128 e già di qua da lei discende l’ erta,  
> 129 passando per li cerchi senza scorta  
> 130 tal, che per lui ne fia la terra aperta.”  

and already, on this side of it, comes down the steep, passing the circles without escort, one by whom the city shall be opened to us.”

- *discende* ‘descends’ → “comes down”: the single verb becomes a verb with a directional adverb.
- *passando per li cerchi* ‘passing through the circles’ → “passing the circles”: the preposition is omitted, making “the circles” the direct object of “passing”.
- *tal* ‘such a one’ → “one”: the demonstrative force of *tal* is omitted.
- *che per lui* ‘that through him’ → “by whom”: the connective and the phrase containing a personal pronoun become a single relative phrase.
- *ne fia la terra aperta* ‘to us will be the land opened’ → “the city shall be opened to us”: *terra* is rendered in its urban sense as “city”; the subject moves before the auxiliary, the participle follows the auxiliary directly, and the pronominal *ne* becomes the final phrase “to us”.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Constructions

Carlyle’s translation remains close to the Italian in its sequence of statements, its images, and much of its vocabulary, while repeatedly rearranging phrases into English order. Objects and subjects placed before or after the verb for Italian emphasis generally take their more usual English positions; descriptive adjectives and possessives move before their nouns. Thus *il maestro accorto* becomes “the wary Master”. More generally, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending, and omits the articles that Italian places before possessives. Yet the translation also preserves conspicuously literal expressions, including “the dead inscription” for *la scritta morta* and “his eyebrows shorn of all boldness” for *le ciglia … rase d’ ogni baldanza*.

Changes of construction often go beyond rearrangement. Reflexive and pronominal verbs become simple English verbs, as *mi volsi* becomes “turn[ed]”; infinitives and gerunds become finite clauses, while some finite clauses become infinitives. In *m’ hai sicurtà renduta*, ‘hast restored safety to me’ becomes “hast restored me to safety”, changing the grammatical roles of the person and the thing restored. Italian imperfects frequently become simple pasts, and subjunctives receive several different English equivalents. Negative expressions can also reshape whole clauses: *sol quand’ io fui dentro parve carca* becomes “not till I was in, did it seem laden”, replacing an affirmative restriction with a negative construction and auxiliary inversion.

Word choice ranges from close equivalence to interpretive substitution. *Fummo*, ‘smoke’, becomes “vapour”; *galeoto*, ‘oarsman’, becomes “steersman”; and *vermiglie*, ‘vermilion’, becomes the less specific “red”. Elsewhere the English makes an implication explicit: *duolo*, ‘grief’, becomes “a wailing”, and *tratto*, ‘drawn’, becomes “rescued”. Grammatical categories also shift, as *furiosa* becomes “in fury” and *sì ardito* becomes “so daringly”. Occasionally the interpretation changes more substantially: *qual che … s’ aggiri*, referring to whoever may be active within, is rendered as “whatever be contrived within”.

Omissions and additions are usually small but recurrent. Pronominal particles, directional elements, and intensifiers sometimes receive no separate equivalent, as with *via* in the arrow comparison and *molto* before *sarei vago*. Conversely, English supplies possessives, explanatory nouns, or emphasis: *anzi ora* becomes “before thy time”, *più di mille* becomes “more than a thousand spirits”, and *ancor* becomes “even now”. The resulting prose is therefore closely tethered to Dante’s wording without being mechanically word-for-word: it preserves many compressed or unusual expressions while clarifying grammatical relationships and occasionally narrowing, broadening, or redirecting their sense.
