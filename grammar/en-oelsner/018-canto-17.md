# Inferno 17

## Lines 1–21 — The Beast of Fraud

> 1 “Ecco la fiera con la coda aguzza,  
> 2 che passa i monti, e rompe muri ed armi;  
> 3 ecco colei che tutto il mondo appuzza.”  

“Behold the savage beast with the pointed tail, that passes mountains, and breaks through walls and weapons; behold him that pollutes the whole world.”

- *fiera* ‘wild beast’ → “savage beast”: wildness is expressed as savagery.
- *coda aguzza* ‘tail pointed’ → “pointed tail”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *passa i monti* ‘passes the mountains’ → “passes mountains”: the definite article is omitted.
- *rompe muri ed armi* ‘breaks walls and weapons’ → “breaks through walls and weapons”: breaking becomes breaking through obstacles.
- *colei* ‘her’ → “him”: the feminine pronoun becomes masculine.
- *che tutto il mondo appuzza* ‘that the whole world makes stink’ → “that pollutes the whole world”: the object follows the verb, and the specific reference to stench becomes the more general “pollutes”.

> 4 Sì cominciò lo mio duca a parlarmi,  
> 5 ed accennolle che venisse a proda,  
> 6 vicino al fin de’ passeggiati marmi;  

Thus began my Guide to speak to me; and beckoned him to come ashore, near the end of our rocky path;

- *accennolle che venisse a proda* ‘signalled to her that she should come to the bank’ → “beckoned him to come ashore”: the feminine recipient becomes masculine; the subordinate clause with a subjunctive becomes an object followed by an infinitive; and coming to the bank becomes coming ashore.
- *de’ passeggiati marmi* ‘of the traversed marbles’ → “of our rocky path”: the plural material noun becomes a singular path described by its material. The participle indicating that the stones have been walked over disappears, while the possessive “our” is supplied.

> 7 e quella sozza imagine di froda  
> 8 sen venne, ed arrivò la testa e il busto;  
> 9 ma in su la riva non trasse la coda.  

and that uncleanly image of Fraud came onward, and landed his head and bust, but drew not his tail upon the bank.

- *sen venne* ‘came along’ → “came onward”: the pronominal expression is rendered with an explicit indication of forward movement; its particles have no separate English equivalents.
- *la testa e il busto* ‘the head and the bust’ → “his head and bust”: the definite articles become a single possessive.
- *in su la riva non trasse la coda* ‘upon the bank did not draw the tail’ → “drew not his tail upon the bank”: the phrase indicating place moves after the verb and object, and the definite article before the tail becomes a possessive.

> 10 La faccia sua era faccia d’uom giusto,  
> 11 tanto benigna avea di fuor la pelle;  
> 12 e d’un serpente tutto l’altro fusto.  

His face was the face of a just man, so mild an aspect had it outwardly; and the rest was all a reptile's body.

- *La faccia sua* ‘the face his’ → “His face”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *faccia d’uom giusto* ‘face of man just’ → “the face of a just man”: definite and indefinite articles are supplied, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *tanto benigna avea di fuor la pelle* ‘so benign it had outwardly the skin’ → “so mild an aspect had it outwardly”: skin is interpreted as outward appearance. The adjective describing the condition of the skin becomes an attributive adjective within an indefinite noun phrase; that phrase precedes the verb, while the adverb moves to the end.
- *e d’un serpente tutto l’altro fusto* ‘and of a serpent all the remaining trunk’ → “and the rest was all a reptile's body”: the compressed construction becomes an explicit clause with “was”. The remaining trunk is recast as “the rest”, with “body” in the predicate; the preceding phrase of possession becomes a possessive before “body”. “Reptile” is broader than ‘serpent’.

> 13 Due branche avea pilose infin l’ascelle;  

He had two paws, hairy to the armpits;

- *Due branche avea* ‘two paws had’ → “He had two paws”: the object moves after the verb, and the subject implicit in the verb is expressed.
- *infin l’ascelle* ‘as far as the armpits’ → “to the armpits”: the expression marking the full extent is reduced to a simple preposition.

> 14 lo dosso e il petto ed ambedue le coste  
> 15 dipinte avea di nodi e di rotelle:  

the neck and the breast, and both the flanks, were painted with knots and circlets:

- *lo dosso* ‘the back’ → “the neck”: the translation identifies a different part of the body from the ordinary meaning of *dosso*.
- *dipinte avea* ‘painted he had’ → “were painted”: an active construction describing the condition in which he had these body parts becomes passive, with the body parts as subjects.
- *di nodi e di rotelle* ‘with knots and with little wheels’ → “with knots and circlets”: the repeated preposition is omitted, and the diminutive wheels are interpreted as circular markings.

> 16 con più color, sommesse e soprapposte  
> 17 non fer mai drappo Tartari nè Turchi,  
> 18 nè fur tai tele per Aragne imposte.  

never did Tartars or Turks [make cloth] with more colours, [groundwork and] broidery; nor by Arachne were such webs laid on her loom.

- *con più color, sommesse e soprapposte non fer mai drappo Tartari nè Turchi* ‘with more colours, underlayings and overlayings not made ever cloth Tartars nor Turks’ → “never did Tartars or Turks [make cloth] with more colours, [groundwork and] broidery”: the phrase beginning with *con* moves from the beginning of the Italian clause to a position after the subject, verb, and object. The substantival expressions *sommesse e soprapposte* are interpreted as the fabric’s groundwork and its ornamental embroidery; plural forms become English mass nouns. The negative expressions combine as initial “never”, requiring the auxiliary “did”. The subject moves before the main verb and object, and ‘nor’ becomes “or” within the negative construction.
- *nè fur tai tele per Aragne imposte* ‘nor were such webs by Arachne set up’ → “nor by Arachne were such webs laid on her loom”: the agent phrase moves before the verb and subject. The setting up of the webs is interpreted specifically as laying them on a loom, with “on her loom” supplied.

> 19 Come tal volta stanno a riva i burchi,  
> 20 che parte sono in acqua e parte in terra;  

As at times the wherries lie on shore, that are part in water and part on land;

- *stanno a riva i burchi* ‘lie at the bank the barges’ → “the wherries lie on shore”: the subject moves before the verb; the boats are called wherries rather than barges, and being at the bank becomes lying on shore.
- *parte sono* ‘partly are’ → “are part”: the adverbial expression moves after the verb.

> 21 e come là tra li Tedeschi lurchi  

and as there amongst the guzzling Germans

- *li Tedeschi lurchi* ‘the Germans gluttonous’ → “the guzzling Germans”: the adjective moves before the noun, and gluttony is expressed through the participial description “guzzling”.

## Lines 22–54 — The Beast and the Woful Folk

> 22 lo bevero s’assetta a far sua guerra:  

the beaver adjusts himself to wage his war:

- *s’assetta* ‘settles himself’ → “adjusts himself”: the verb describes an adjustment rather than a settling into position.
- *a far sua guerra* ‘to make his war’ → “to wage his war”: “wage” replaces the literal ‘make’.

> 23 così la fiera pessima si stava  
> 24 su l’orlo che, di pietra, il sabbion serra.  

so lay that worst of savage beasts upon the brim which closes the great sand with stone.

- *la fiera pessima* ‘the worst beast’ → “that worst of savage beasts”: the article becomes a demonstrative, and the superlative becomes a partitive construction with a plural noun; “savage” makes explicit the wildness implicit in *fiera*.
- *si stava* ‘remained’ → “lay”: the translation specifies a posture, and places the verb before its subject.
- *che, di pietra, il sabbion serra* ‘which, with stone, the great sand encloses’ → “which closes the great sand with stone”: the verb precedes its object, and the phrase expressing the enclosing material moves to the end.

> 25 Nel vano tutta sua coda guizzava,  
> 26 torcendo in su la venenosa forca  
> 27 che, a guisa di scorpion, la punta armava.  

In the void glanced all his tail, twisting upwards the venomed fork, which, as in scorpions, armed the point.

- *tutta sua coda guizzava* ‘all his tail was darting’ → “glanced all his tail”: the verb precedes its subject; “glanced” renders the darting motion with a simple past rather than an explicitly continuing construction.
- *venenosa* ‘poisonous’ → “venomed”: an adjective describing poisonous quality becomes one suggesting something furnished with venom.
- *a guisa di scorpion* ‘in the manner of a scorpion’ → “as in scorpions”: comparison of manner becomes comparison with what occurs in an animal, and the singular becomes plural.
- *la punta armava* ‘the point armed’ → “armed the point”: the verb moves before its object.

> 28 Lo duca disse: “Or convien che si torca  
> 29 la nostra via un poco infino a quella  
> 30 bestia malvagia che colà si corca.”  

My Guide said: "Now must we bend our way a little, to that wicked brute which couches there."

- *Lo duca* ‘the leader’ → “My Guide”: the article becomes a possessive, and ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”.
- *Or convien che si torca la nostra via* ‘now it is necessary that our way turn’ → “Now must we bend our way”: the impersonal construction and subordinate subjunctive become a modal construction; the travellers become the agents, and their way becomes the object.
- *infino a* ‘as far as’ → “to”: the explicit expression of extent is reduced to a preposition of destination.
- *quella bestia malvagia* ‘that beast wicked’ → “that wicked brute”: the adjective moves before the noun, and “brute” replaces ‘beast’.
- *che colà si corca* ‘which there lays itself down’ → “which couches there”: the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb, and the adverb moves after it.

> 31 Però scendemmo alla destra mammella  
> 32 e dieci passi femmo in su lo stremo,  
> 33 per ben cessar la rena e la fiammella:  

Then we descended on the right, and made ten paces towards the edge, that we might quite avoid the sand and flames;

- *Però* ‘therefore’ → “Then”: a consequential connection becomes a temporal one.
- *alla destra mammella* ‘at the right breast’ → “on the right”: the bodily expression of direction is replaced by a plain directional phrase.
- *dieci passi femmo* ‘ten steps we made’ → “made ten paces”: the object moves after the verb, and ‘steps’ becomes “paces”.
- *in su lo stremo* ‘upon the edge’ → “towards the edge”: position or movement along the edge becomes movement towards it.
- *per ben cessar* ‘to avoid thoroughly’ → “that we might quite avoid”: the infinitive of purpose becomes a finite clause with “might”.
- *la fiammella* ‘the little flame’ → “flames”: the singular becomes plural, and the diminutive and article are omitted.

> 34 e quando noi a lei venuti semo,  
> 35 poco più oltre veggio in su la rena  
> 36 gente seder propinqua al loco scemo.  

and when we came to him, I saw upon the sand, a little farther onwards, people sitting near the empty space.

- *e quando noi a lei venuti semo* ‘and when we to her are come’ → “and when we came to him”: the destination moves after the verb, the compound present perfect becomes a simple past, and the feminine pronoun becomes masculine.
- *poco più oltre veggio in su la rena* ‘a little farther onwards I see upon the sand’ → “I saw upon the sand, a little farther onwards”: the phrase ‘a little farther onwards’ moves from before the verb of seeing to after the phrase locating the people upon the sand; the present becomes past.
- *gente seder* ‘people sit’ → “people sitting”: the infinitive after a verb of perception becomes a present participle.
- *al loco scemo* ‘to the empty place’ → “the empty space”: the preposition required by Italian *propinqua* is unnecessary after English “near”, and ‘place’ becomes “space”.

> 37 Quivi il maestro: “Acciocchè tutta piena  
> 38 esperienza d’ esto giron porti,”  
> 39 mi disse, “va, e vedi la lor mena.  

Here my Master said to me: "That thou mayest carry full experience of this round, go and see the state of these.

- *Quivi il maestro … mi disse* ‘here the master … to me said’ → “Here my Master said to me”: the article becomes a possessive, the indirect object follows the verb, and the reporting clause is brought together before the speech.
- *Acciocchè … porti* ‘so that … thou carry’ → “That thou mayest carry”: the purpose conjunction and subjunctive are rendered with “mayest”, and the verb moves before its object.
- *tutta piena* ‘entirely full’ → “full”: the intensifying *tutta* is omitted.
- *la lor mena* ‘their behaviour’ → “the state of these”: conduct is rendered as condition, and the possessive becomes an *of*-phrase with a demonstrative pronoun.

> 40 Li tuoi ragionamenti sian là corti;  
> 41 mentre che torni parlerò con questa,  
> 42 che ne conceda i suoi omeri forti.”  

Let thy talk with them be brief, till thou returnest, I will speak with this *beast*, that he may lend us his strong shoulders."

- *Li tuoi ragionamenti sian là corti* ‘thy discourses be there short’ → “Let thy talk with them be brief”: the plural noun becomes singular, the exhortatory subjunctive becomes a “Let” construction, and the locative ‘there’ is replaced by “with them”; ‘short’ becomes “brief”.
- *mentre che torni* ‘while thou returnest’ → “till thou returnest”: the conjunction is interpreted as marking the endpoint of the intervening activity.
- *con questa* ‘with this one’ → “with this *beast*”: the feminine demonstrative pronoun becomes a determiner with an explicitly supplied noun.
- *che ne conceda* ‘that she grant us’ → “that he may lend us”: the subjunctive becomes a modal construction, the grammatical gender changes, and ‘grant’ becomes “lend”.
- *i suoi omeri forti* ‘his shoulders strong’ → “his strong shoulders”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 43 Così ancor su per la strema testa  
> 44 di quel settimo cerchio, tutto solo  
> 45 andai, ove sedea la gente mesta.  

Thus also, on the utmost limit of that seventh circle, all alone I went to where the woful folk were seated.

- *su per* ‘up along’ → “on”: the directional expression becomes a preposition of position.
- *la strema testa* ‘the extreme head’ → “the utmost limit”: the figurative ‘head’ is rendered as a boundary.
- *ove sedea la gente mesta* ‘where sat the sad people’ → “to where the woful folk were seated”: “to” explicitly marks destination; the subject moves before the verb; the singular collective construction becomes plural; and ‘sat’ becomes “were seated”. “Woful folk” replaces ‘sad people’, with the adjective moved before the noun.

> 46 Per gli occhi fuori scoppiava lor duolo;  

Through the eyes their grief was bursting forth;

- *fuori scoppiava lor duolo* ‘outward was bursting their grief’ → “their grief was bursting forth”: the subject moves before the verb, and the adverb moves after it.

> 47 di qua, di là soccorrien con le mani,  
> 48 quando a’ vapori, e quando al caldo suolo.  

on this side, on that, they with their hands kept warding off, sometimes the flames, sometimes the burning soil.

- *soccorrien con le mani* ‘they came to their aid with the hands’ → “they with their hands kept warding off”: the verb is interpreted as a defensive action and made transitive; “kept” explicitly expresses its repetition, the article becomes a possessive, and the phrase about the hands moves before the verb.
- *quando a’ vapori, e quando al caldo suolo* ‘sometimes to the vapours, and sometimes to the hot soil’ → “sometimes the flames, sometimes the burning soil”: the prepositional complements become direct objects of “warding off”; ‘and’ is omitted; *vapori* is interpreted as flames rather than vapours; and ‘hot’ becomes “burning”.

> 49 Non altrimenti fan di state i cani,  
> 50 or col ceffo, or coi piè, quando son morsi  
> 51 o da pulci o da mosche o da tafani.  

Not otherwise the dogs in summer do, now with snout, now with paw, when they are bitten by fleas, or flies, or breeses.

- *fan di state i cani* ‘do in summer the dogs’ → “the dogs in summer do”: the subject and verb exchange positions around the seasonal phrase.
- *or col ceffo, or coi piè* ‘now with the muzzle, now with the feet’ → “now with snout, now with paw”: the articles are omitted, ‘muzzle’ becomes “snout”, and plural ‘feet’ becomes singular “paw”.
- *o da pulci o da mosche o da tafani* ‘either by fleas or by flies or by breeses’ → “by fleas, or flies, or breeses”: the initial alternative marker is omitted, and the preposition is expressed only once.

> 52 Poi che nel viso a certi gli occhi porsi,  
> 53 ne’ quali il doloroso foco casca,  
> 54 non ne conobbi alcun; ma io m’accorsi  

After I had set my eyes upon the visages of several on whom the dolorous fire falls, I knew not any of them; but I observed

- *Poi che … gli occhi porsi* ‘after … the eyes I set’ → “After I had set my eyes”: the simple past becomes past perfect, the object moves after the verb, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *nel viso a certi* ‘in the face of certain ones’ → “upon the visages of several”: ‘in’ becomes “upon”, the singular face becomes plural, and the indefinite ‘certain ones’ becomes the quantitative “several”; the phrase moves after the verb and its object.
- *ne’ quali* ‘in whom’ → “on whom”: the preposition changes.
- *non ne conobbi alcun* ‘not of them I recognized any’ → “I knew not any of them”: the negative follows the verb, the partitive phrase moves after “any”, and ‘recognized’ becomes “knew”.
- *ma io m’accorsi* ‘but I became aware’ → “but I observed”: the pronominal expression is replaced by a non-pronominal verb of observation.

## Lines 55–87 — The Pouches and the Descent

> 55 che dal collo a ciascun pendea una tasca,  
> 56 che avea certo colore e certo segno,  
> 57 e quindi par che il loro occhio si pasca.  

that from the neck of each there hung a pouch, which had a certain colour and a certain impress, and thereon it seems their eye is feasting.

- *a ciascun* ‘to each’ → “of each”: the Italian possessive dative becomes an English possessive phrase.
- *pendea una tasca* ‘hung a pouch’ → “there hung a pouch”: introductory “there” is supplied.
- *certo colore e certo segno* ‘certain colour and certain sign’ → “a certain colour and a certain impress”: indefinite articles are supplied; “impress” makes *segno* specifically an impressed mark.
- *quindi* ‘thence’ → “thereon”: a word indicating source becomes one indicating what the eyes rest upon.
- *par che* ‘it seems that’ → “it seems”: the conjunction introducing the subordinate clause is omitted.
- *si pasca* ‘feed itself’ → “is feasting”: the reflexive subjunctive becomes an intransitive indicative progressive, with ‘feeding’ rendered as “feasting”.

> 58 E com’ io riguardando tra lor vegno,  
> 59 in una borsa gialla vidi azzurro,  
> 60 che d’ un leone avea faccia e contegno.  

And as I came amongst them looking, on a yellow purse I saw azure, that had the semblance and gesture of a lion.

- *io riguardando tra lor vegno* ‘I, looking, among them come’ → “I came amongst them looking”: the present becomes a past tense; the verb precedes the prepositional phrase, and the participle moves to the end.
- *in una borsa gialla* ‘in a purse yellow’ → “on a yellow purse”: “on” replaces ‘in’, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *che d’ un leone avea faccia e contegno* ‘that of a lion had face and bearing’ → “that had the semblance and gesture of a lion”: the possessive phrase moves after the nouns; ‘face’ and ‘bearing’ become “semblance” and “gesture”, and a definite article is supplied.

> 61 Poi procedendo di mio sguardo il curro  
> 62 vidine un’ altra come sangue rossa  
> 63 mostrare un’ oca bianca più che burro.  

Then, my look continuing its course, I saw another of them, [red as] blood, display a goose more white than butter.

- *procedendo di mio sguardo il curro* ‘proceeding of my gaze the chariot’ → “my look continuing its course”: the gaze, rather than its metaphorical chariot, becomes the subject; ‘proceeding’ becomes “continuing”, and the chariot becomes an object, “its course”, with a possessive supplied.
- *vidine un’ altra* ‘I saw of them another’ → “I saw another of them”: the partitive represented by *ne* follows “another”.
- *come sangue rossa* ‘like blood red’ → “[red as] blood”: the adjective moves before the comparison, and ‘like’ becomes “as”.
- *mostrare* ‘to show’ → “display”: ‘show’ becomes “display”, with the bare infinitive required after a verb of perception.
- *un’ oca bianca più che burro* ‘a goose white more than butter’ → “a goose more white than butter”: the comparative modifier moves before the adjective.

> 64 Ed un, che d’ una scrofa azzurra e grossa  
> 65 segnato avea lo suo sacchetto bianco,  
> 66 mi disse : “ Che fai tu in questa fossa?  

And one who, with a sow azure and pregnant, had his argent sacklet stamped, said to me: “What art thou doing in this pit?

- *d’ una scrofa azzurra e grossa* ‘of a sow azure and big’ → “with a sow azure and pregnant”: *di* introduces the marking and is rendered “with”; *grossa* is interpreted as ‘pregnant’ rather than simply ‘big’ or ‘fat’.
- *segnato avea lo suo sacchetto bianco* ‘marked he had his sacklet white’ → “had his argent sacklet stamped”: the participle moves after the auxiliary and object, producing an English ‘have something stamped’ construction; ‘marked’ becomes “stamped”, and ‘white’ becomes the heraldic “argent”, placed before the noun.
- *mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *Che fai tu* ‘what doest thou’ → “What art thou doing”: the simple present becomes a progressive construction.

> 67 Or te ne va; e perchè se’ vivo anco,  
> 68 sappi che il mio vicin Vitaliano  
> 69 sederà qui dal mio sinistro fianco.  

Get thee gone; and, as thou art still alive, know that my neighbour Vitaliano shall sit here at my left side.

- *Or te ne va* ‘now go thyself away’ → “Get thee gone”: ‘now’ is omitted, and the reflexive imperative with a particle of departure becomes the English idiom “Get thee gone”.
- *perchè* ‘because’ → “as”: the causal conjunction is rendered by another causal conjunction.
- *se’ vivo anco* ‘thou art alive still’ → “thou art still alive”: the adverb moves before the adjective.
- *dal mio sinistro fianco* ‘from my left flank’ → “at my left side”: *da* has a locative rendering, “at”, and ‘flank’ becomes “side”.

> 70 Con questi Fiorentin son Padovano;  
> 71 spesse fiate m’intronan gli orecchi,  
> 72 gridando: ‘Vegna il cavalier sovrano,  
> 73 che recherà la tasca con tre becchi.’”  

With these Florentines am I, a Paduan; many a time they din my ears, shouting: ‘Let the sovereign cavalier come, who will bring the pouch with three goats!’”

- *son Padovano* ‘I am Paduan’ → “am I, a Paduan”: the adjective of origin becomes a noun with an indefinite article; the supplied subject follows the verb.
- *spesse fiate* ‘frequent times’ → “many a time”: the plural phrase becomes a distributive singular construction.
- *m’intronan gli orecchi* ‘to me they din the ears’ → “they din my ears”: the possessive dative becomes a possessive adjective.
- *Vegna il cavalier sovrano* ‘may come the knight sovereign’ → “Let the sovereign cavalier come”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a “Let” construction; the subject precedes “come”, the adjective precedes its noun, and ‘knight’ is rendered “cavalier”.
- *becchi* ‘he-goats’ → “goats”: the specification of sex is omitted.

> 74 Qui distorse la bocca, e di fuor trasse  
> 75 la lingua, come ’l bue che il naso lecchi.  

Then he writhed his mouth, and thrust his tongue out, like an ox that licks his nose.

- *Qui* ‘here’ → “Then”: the spatial expression receives a temporal rendering.
- *distorse la bocca* ‘he twisted the mouth’ → “he writhed his mouth”: ‘twisted’ becomes “writhed”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *e di fuor trasse la lingua* ‘and out he drew the tongue’ → “and thrust his tongue out”: ‘drew’ becomes “thrust”; “out” moves after the object, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *come ’l bue* ‘like the ox’ → “like an ox”: the definite article becomes indefinite.
- *che il naso lecchi* ‘that the nose lick’ → “that licks his nose”: the subjunctive becomes indicative, the object follows the verb, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 76 Ed io, temendo no ’l più star crucciasse  
> 77 lui che di poco star m’avea monito,  
> 78 torna’ mi indietro dall’anime lasse.  

And I, dreading lest longer stay might anger him who had admonished me to stay short time, turned back from those fore-wearied souls.

- *temendo* ‘fearing’ → “dreading”: a stronger term for fear is used.
- *no* ‘not’ → “lest”: the negative expression after a verb of fearing introduces the feared event rather than becoming an English negation.
- *’l più star* ‘the longer staying’ → “longer stay”: the article is omitted, and the substantivized infinitive becomes the noun “stay”.
- *crucciasse* ‘should vex’ → “might anger”: the imperfect subjunctive is expressed with “might”, and ‘vex’ becomes “anger”.
- *di poco star m’avea monito* ‘of staying little me had admonished’ → “had admonished me to stay short time”: both complements move after the verb; *di* with an infinitive becomes an English infinitive complement, and the duration implicit in *poco* is made explicit by “short time”.
- *torna’ mi indietro* ‘I turned myself back’ → “turned back”: the reflexive pronoun is omitted.
- *dall’anime lasse* ‘from the souls weary’ → “from those fore-wearied souls”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative, and the adjective moves before the noun, with ‘weary’ rendered by the compound “fore-wearied”.

> 79 Trovai lo duca mio ch’ era salito  
> 80 già in su la groppa del fiero animale,  
> 81 e disse a me: “ Or sie forte ed ardito.  

I found my Guide, who had already mounted on the haunch of the dreadful animal; and he said to me: “Now be stout and bold!

- *lo duca mio* ‘the leader my’ → “my Guide”: ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *era salito già* ‘was ascended already’ → “had already mounted”: the Italian pluperfect formed with *essere* becomes an English pluperfect with “had”; “already” moves before the participle, and ‘ascended’ becomes “mounted”.
- *in su la groppa* ‘up on the rump’ → “on the haunch”: the combined prepositions are reduced to “on”, and ‘rump’ becomes “haunch”.
- *fiero* ‘fierce’ → “dreadful”: the adjective describes the fear inspired rather than fierceness itself.
- *forte* ‘strong’ → “stout”: strength is expressed through a word suggesting courage or resolution.

> 82 Omai si scende per sì fatte scale;  
> 83 monta dinanzi: ch’ io voglio esser mezzo,  
> 84 sì che la coda non possa far male.”  

Now by such stairs must we descend; mount thou in front: for I wish to be in the middle, that the tail may not do hurt to *thee*.”

- *si scende* ‘one descends’ → “must we descend”: the impersonal construction becomes first-person plural, with “must” adding necessity.
- *per sì fatte scale* ‘by thus-made stairs’ → “by such stairs”: *sì fatte* is compressed into “such”, and the phrase moves before the verb.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *esser mezzo* ‘to be middle’ → “to be in the middle”: the predicate adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *sì che* ‘so that’ → “that”: the explicit equivalent of ‘so’ is omitted.
- *non possa far male* ‘not may do harm’ → “may not do hurt to *thee*”: the negative follows the English modal; ‘harm’ becomes “hurt”, and “to *thee*” explicitly supplies the person who might be harmed.

> 85 Qual è colui, ch’ ha sì presso il riprezzo  
> 86 della quartana, ch’ ha già l’unghie smorte,  
> 87 e trema tutto, pur guardando il rezzo,  

As one who has the shivering of the quartan so near, that he has his nails already pale, and trembles all, still keeping the shade,

- *Qual è colui* ‘such as is he’ → “As one”: the comparative opening is compressed, the verb is omitted, and the demonstrative pronoun becomes indefinite.
- *ch’ ha sì presso il riprezzo della quartana* ‘who has so near the shivering of the quartan’ → “who has the shivering of the quartan so near”: “so near” moves after the object.
- *ch’ ha già l’unghie smorte* ‘that he has already the nails pale’ → “that he has his nails already pale”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “already” moves after the object.
- *pur guardando il rezzo* ‘merely looking at the shade’ → “still keeping the shade”: *pur* is rendered as continuative “still”, and *guardando* is interpreted as keeping to the shade rather than looking at it.

## Lines 88–120 — The Descent on Geryon

> 88 tal divenn' io alle parole porte;  

Such I became when these words were uttered;

- *tal divenn’ io* ‘such became I’ → “Such I became”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *alle parole porte* ‘at the words offered’ → “when these words were uttered”: a prepositional phrase becomes a temporal clause; the definite article becomes “these”, and ‘offered’ becomes “uttered”.

> 89 ma vergogna mi fer le sue minacce,  
> 90 che innanzi a buon signor fa servo forte.  

but his threats excited in me shame, which makes a servant brave in presence of a worthy master.

- *vergogna mi fer le sue minacce* ‘shame to me made his threats’ → “his threats excited in me shame”: the subject moves from last to first, and ‘made shame in me’ becomes “excited in me shame”.
- *innanzi a buon signor* ‘before a good lord’ → “in presence of a worthy master”: the phrase moves after the object and its complement; ‘before’ is expanded, ‘good’ becomes “worthy”, and ‘lord’ becomes “master”.
- *fa servo forte* ‘makes servant strong’ → “makes a servant brave”: an indefinite article is supplied, and ‘strong’ is interpreted as “brave”.

> 91 Io m' assettai in su quelle spallacce;  

I placed myself on those huge shoulders;

- *m’ assettai* ‘seated myself’ → “placed myself”: the translation does not explicitly express sitting.
- *quelle spallacce* ‘those hulking shoulders’ → “those huge shoulders”: the augmentative suffix is represented by “huge”, without its pejorative colouring.

> 92 “Sì” (volli dir, ma la voce non venne  
> 93 com' io credetti) “fa che tu m'abbracce.”  

[I] wished to say, only the voice came not as I thought: “See that thou embrace me.”

- *Sì … fa che tu m’abbracce* ‘So … bring it about that you embrace me’ → “See that thou embrace me”: the introductory *Sì* is not separately represented, and the causative instruction becomes the idiomatic “See that”. The spoken words are placed together after the account of the attempted speech, rather than interrupted by it.
- *ma* ‘but’ → “only”: the adversative conjunction becomes a qualification.
- *la voce non venne* ‘the voice did not come’ → “the voice came not”: the negative follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *com’ io credetti* ‘as I believed’ → “as I thought”: belief is expressed as thought.

> 94 Ma esso, che altra volta mi sovvenne  
> 95 ad altro forse, tosto ch' io montai  
> 96 con le braccia m'avvinse e mi sostenne;  

But he, who at other times assisted me in other difficulties, soon as I mounted, clasped me with his arms, and held me up;

- *altra volta* ‘another time’ → “at other times”: the singular becomes plural.
- *mi sovvenne / ad altro forse* ‘came to my aid at another uncertainty’ → “assisted me in other difficulties”: the substantival *forse*, literally ‘perhaps’, is interpreted as a difficulty or predicament; its singular becomes plural, and the prepositional construction changes.
- *con le braccia m’avvinse* ‘with the arms bound me’ → “clasped me with his arms”: ‘bound’ becomes “clasped”, the instrumental phrase moves after the verb and object, and “his” replaces the definite article.
- *mi sostenne* ‘supported me’ → “held me up”: a single verb becomes a phrasal expression.

> 97 e disse: “Gerion, moviti omai!  
> 98 le rote larghe e lo scender sia poco:  
> 99 pensa la nuova soma che tu hai.”  

then he said: “Geryon, now move thee! be thy circles large, and gradual thy descent: think of the unusual burden that thou hast.”

- *e disse* ‘and said’ → “then he said”: coordination becomes an explicit indication of sequence.
- *moviti omai* ‘move yourself now’ → “now move thee”: the adverb moves before the imperative.
- *le rote larghe e lo scender sia poco* ‘the circles wide and the descending be little’ → “be thy circles large, and gradual thy descent”: the verb is brought forward to govern both clauses, and possessives replace the definite articles. The substantival infinitive becomes “descent”; ‘little’ is interpreted as “gradual”, and ‘wide’ becomes “large”.
- *pensa* ‘consider’ → “think of”: a directly transitive verb becomes a verb with a preposition.
- *la nuova soma* ‘the new load’ → “the unusual burden”: ‘new’ is interpreted as ‘unaccustomed’ rather than simply recent, and ‘load’ becomes “burden”.

> 100 Come la navicella esce del loco  
> 101 in dietro in dietro, sì quindi si tolse;  

As the bark goes from its station backwards, backwards, so the _monster_ took himself from thence;

- *la navicella* ‘the little boat’ → “the bark”: the diminutive is not explicitly represented.
- *esce del loco* ‘goes out of the place’ → “goes from its station”: ‘goes out’ becomes “goes”, “its” replaces the definite article, and the general ‘place’ becomes “station”.
- *sì quindi si tolse* ‘so from there he removed himself’ → “so the _monster_ took himself from thence”: the descriptive subject “the _monster_” is supplied, ‘removed’ becomes “took”, and the phrase indicating departure moves after the verb.

> 102 e poi ch' al tutto si sentì a giuoco,  
> 103 là ov' era il petto, la coda rivolse,  
> 104 e quella tesa, come anguilla, mosse,  
> 105 e con le branche l' aria sè raccolse.  

and when he felt himself quite loose, there where his breast had been he turned his tail, and stretching moved it, like an eel, and with his paws gathered the air to him.

- *e poi ch’* ‘and after’ → “and when”: explicit succession becomes a more general temporal connection.
- *al tutto si sentì a giuoco* ‘wholly he felt himself at liberty’ → “he felt himself quite loose”: the idiom *a giuoco* becomes “loose”, and the expression of completeness moves next to that adjective.
- *là ov’ era il petto* ‘there where was the breast’ → “there where his breast had been”: the subject moves before the verb, “his” replaces the definite article, and the imperfect becomes a past perfect.
- *la coda rivolse* ‘the tail he turned’ → “he turned his tail”: the object moves after the verb, and “his” replaces the definite article.
- *e quella tesa, come anguilla, mosse* ‘and that, stretched out, like an eel, he moved’ → “and stretching moved it, like an eel”: the participle describing the tail becomes an active participle describing the mover; the demonstrative becomes “it” and follows the verb, while the comparison moves to the end.
- *e con le branche l’ aria sè raccolse* ‘and with the paws the air to himself he gathered’ → “and with his paws gathered the air to him”: “his” replaces the definite article; the verb moves before its object, and the reflexive recipient becomes “him” after the object.

> 106 Maggior paura non credo che fosse,  
> 107 quando Feton abbandonò li freni,  
> 108 per che il ciel, come pare ancor, si cosse;  

Greater fear there was not, I believe, when Phaeton let loose the reins, whereby the sky, as yet appears, was burnt;—

- *Maggior paura non credo che fosse* ‘greater fear I do not believe there was’ → “Greater fear there was not, I believe”: the negative moves from ‘believe’ to the assertion about fear; the subordinate clause becomes the main assertion, and “I believe” becomes parenthetical.
- *abbandonò* ‘abandoned’ → “let loose”: relinquishing is expressed through a phrasal verb.
- *come pare ancor* ‘as appears still’ → “as yet appears”: the adverb moves before the verb, and ‘still’ becomes “yet”.
- *si cosse* ‘became scorched’ → “was burnt”: the pronominal verb expressing a change of state becomes an English passive.

> 109 nè quando Icaro misero le reni  
> 110 sentì spennar per la scaldata cera,  
> 111 gridando il padre a lui: “Mala via tieni!” —  

nor when poor Icarus felt his loins unfeathering by the heating of the wax, his father crying to him, “An ill way thou goest!”—

- *Icaro misero* ‘Icarus wretched’ → “poor Icarus”: the adjective moves before the name, and ‘wretched’ becomes the pitying “poor”.
- *le reni / sentì spennar* ‘the loins he felt lose their feathers’ → “felt his loins unfeathering”: the object moves after “felt”, “his” replaces the definite article, and the infinitive becomes an English participle.
- *per la scaldata cera* ‘because of the heated wax’ → “by the heating of the wax”: the participial adjective becomes an action noun, changing the focus from the wax’s condition to the process of heating it.
- *gridando il padre a lui* ‘crying the father to him’ → “his father crying to him”: the subject moves before the participle, and “his” replaces the definite article.
- *Mala via tieni* ‘an ill way you keep’ → “An ill way thou goest”: ‘keep’ or ‘follow’, with ‘way’ as its object, becomes “goest”, with ‘way’ expressing the course taken.

> 112 che fu la mia, quando vidi ch'i' era  
> 113 nell' aer d'ogni parte, e vidi spenta  
> 114 ogni veduta, fuor che della fiera.  

than was mine, when I saw myself in the air on all sides, and saw extinguished every sight, save of the beast.

- *vidi ch’i’ era* ‘I saw that I was’ → “I saw myself”: a finite subordinate clause becomes a reflexive object followed by a complement.
- *d’ogni parte* ‘on every side’ → “on all sides”: distributive singular becomes plural.

> 115 Ella sen va nuotando lenta lenta;  
> 116 ruota e discende, ma non me n' accorgo  
> 117 se non ch'al viso di sotto mi venta.  

He goes on swimming slowly, slowly; wheels and descends; but I perceive it not, otherwise than by a wind upon my face and from below.

- *Ella* ‘she’ → “He”: the grammatical feminine referring to *fiera* becomes masculine.
- *sen va* ‘goes away from there’ → “goes on”: the pronominal expression of movement away becomes an expression of continuation; *ne* has no separate equivalent.
- *lenta lenta* ‘slow, slow’ → “slowly, slowly”: adjectives agreeing with the subject become adverbs modifying the movement.
- *non me n’ accorgo* ‘I do not become aware of it’ → “I perceive it not”: the pronominal verb with an *of*-complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object; the negative moves after the verb and object.
- *se non ch’al viso di sotto mi venta* ‘except that at the face from below it blows on me’ → “otherwise than by a wind upon my face and from below”: the impersonal clause becomes a prepositional phrase naming the means of perception. The verb becomes the noun “wind”, the affected-person pronoun becomes the possessive “my”, and “and” is supplied between the indications of contact and direction.

> 118 Io sentia già dalla man destra il gorgo  
> 119 far sotto noi un orribile stroscio;  
> 120 per che con gli occhi in giù la testa sporgo.  

Already, on the right hand, I heard the whirlpool make a hideous roaring under us; whereat, with eyes downwards, my head I stretched.

- *Io sentia già dalla man destra* ‘I was hearing already from the right hand’ → “Already, on the right hand, I heard”: the imperfect becomes a simple past; both adverbial expressions move before the subject, and ‘from’ becomes “on”.
- *far sotto noi un orribile stroscio* ‘make under us a horrible crashing’ → “make a hideous roaring under us”: the locative phrase moves after the object, ‘horrible’ becomes “hideous”, and the crashing sound becomes “roaring”.
- *per che* ‘for which reason’ → “whereat”: an explicit causal connection becomes an expression of reaction.
- *con gli occhi in giù* ‘with the eyes downwards’ → “with eyes downwards”: the definite article is omitted.
- *la testa sporgo* ‘the head I stretch out’ → “my head I stretched”: “my” replaces the definite article, the present becomes a past tense, and the outward direction expressed by *sporgo* is not separately represented.

## Lines 121–136 — Geryon’s Descent and Departure

> 121 Allor fu’ io più timido allo scoscio :  
> 122 però ch’ io vidi fochi e sentii pianti,  
> 123 ond’ io tremando tutto mi raccoscio.  

Then was I more timorous [as regards dismounting] : for I saw fires and heard lamentings, so that I cower all trembling.

- *allo scoscio* ‘at the plunge’ → “[as regards dismounting]”: the revision interprets *scoscio* as dismounting rather than the plunging descent, and expands the preposition into a phrase specifying what the fear concerns.
- *però ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *ond’* ‘whence’ → “so that”: the connective expressing consequence becomes an explicit result clause.
- *io tremando tutto mi raccoscio* ‘I, trembling all over, draw up my thighs’ → “I cower all trembling”: the specific action of drawing up the thighs becomes the more general “cower”; the phrase describing trembling moves after the main verb, and *tutto* precedes rather than follows it.

> 124 E vidi poi, chè nol vedea davanti,  
> 125 lo scendere e il girar per li gran mali  
> 126 che s'appressavan da diversi canti.  

And then I saw—[for] I had not seen [it] before—the sinking and the wheeling, through the great evils which drew near on diverse sides.

- *E vidi poi* ‘And I saw then’ → “And then I saw”: the temporal adverb moves before the subject and verb.
- *chè nol vedea davanti* ‘because I did not see it before’ → “[for] I had not seen [it] before”: the subordinating conjunction becomes coordinating “for”, and the imperfect becomes a past perfect, explicitly placing the failure to see before the subsequent perception.
- *lo scendere e il girar* ‘the descending and the turning’ → “the sinking and the wheeling”: the movements are rendered more specifically as sinking and circling.
- *da diversi canti* ‘from diverse sides’ → “on diverse sides”: the preposition indicating the directions from which things approach becomes one indicating their positions.

> 127 Come il falcon ch’ è stato assai sull’ali,  
> 128 che senza veder logoro o uccello,  
> 129 fa dire al falconiere : “ Oimè tu cali!”—  

As the falcon, that has been long upon his wings—that, without seeing bird or lure, makes the falconer cry, “Ah, ah! thou stoopest”—

- *assai* ‘much’ → “long”: the adverb of quantity is expressed specifically as duration.
- *sull’ali* ‘upon the wings’ → “upon his wings”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *logoro o uccello* ‘lure or bird’ → “bird or lure”: the two objects exchange places.
- *fa dire al falconiere* ‘makes say to the falconer’ → “makes the falconer cry”: the Italian causative’s infinitive precedes the person made to speak, introduced by *a*; English places that person directly after “makes”. The neutral verb ‘say’ becomes the more expressive “cry”.
- *Oimè* ‘Alas’ → “Ah, ah!”: the single exclamation of distress becomes a repeated interjection.
- *tu cali* ‘thou descendest’ → “thou stoopest”: the general verb of descent becomes a term associated with a bird’s downward flight.

> 130 discende lasso, onde si mosse snello,  
> 131 per cento rote, e da lungi si pone  
> 132 dal suo maestro, disdegnoso e fello :  

descends weary; then swiftly moves himself with many a circle, and far from his master sets himself disdainful and sullen

- *onde si mosse snello* ‘whence he moved briskly’ → “then swiftly moves himself”: the relative adverb of place becomes a temporal adverb, and the past *mosse* becomes present “moves”. Instead of identifying the place from which the falcon previously set off briskly, the translation presents a subsequent movement; the adjectival *snello*, describing the falcon, becomes an adverb describing that movement.
- *per cento rote* ‘through a hundred turns’ → “with many a circle”: “with” replaces the preposition marking the course of movement, the definite number becomes an indefinite multitude, and the plural noun becomes singular in the distributive construction “many a”.
- *e da lungi si pone dal suo maestro* ‘and far away sets himself from his master’ → “and far from his master sets himself”: the phrase identifying the person from whom the falcon keeps its distance moves before the verb, directly after “far”.
- *fello* ‘fierce’ → “sullen”: the adjective is interpreted as resentful ill humour rather than fierceness.

> 133 così ne pose al fondo Gerione  
> 134 a piè a piè della stagliata rocca,  
> 135 e, discarcate le nostre persone,  
> 136 si dileguò come da corda cocca.  

: so at the bottom Geryon set us, close to the foot of the ragged rock; and, from our weight relieved, he bounded off like an arrow from the string.

- *così ne pose al fondo Gerione* ‘so us set at the bottom Geryon’ → “so at the bottom Geryon set us”: the place phrase moves before the verb, the named subject moves from after the verb to before it, and the object pronoun moves after it.
- *a piè a piè* ‘at foot at foot’ → “close to the foot”: the repeated phrase is rendered by “close”, making its intensifying force explicit rather than reproducing the repetition.
- *stagliata* ‘sheer’ → “ragged”: an adjective describing the rock’s steep, sharply cut face becomes one describing an uneven surface.
- *discarcate le nostre persone* ‘our persons unloaded’ → “from our weight relieved”: the absolute participial construction, whose grammatical subject is ‘our persons’, becomes a participial phrase describing Geryon. The persons unloaded are recast as the weight from which he is relieved.
- *si dileguò* ‘he vanished’ → “he bounded off”: disappearance is replaced by a particular manner of rapid departure.
- *come da corda cocca* ‘like from string nock’ → “like an arrow from the string”: the noun moves before the prepositional phrase, articles are supplied, and the arrow’s nock—the part named for the whole—is rendered directly as “an arrow”.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Reshaping

The translation remains close to the Italian in the sequence of its clauses, its concrete vocabulary, and many of its emphatic repetitions. “backwards, backwards” echoes *in dietro in dietro*, while “slowly, slowly” preserves the repetition of *lenta lenta*, changing adjectives into adverbs. Yet Italian word order is frequently rearranged: subjects placed after verbs move before them, objects follow rather than precede their verbs, and descriptive adjectives generally move before their nouns. Some inversions remain, however, as in “In the void glanced all his tail”, giving the English a marked rather than consistently ordinary prose order.

Changes of construction often make compressed Italian expressions more explicit. *Or convien che si torca la nostra via* becomes “Now must we bend our way”: an impersonal expression of necessity with a subordinate clause becomes a modal construction with the travellers as agents. *per ben cessar* becomes “that we might quite avoid”, expanding an infinitive of purpose into a finite clause. Elsewhere, reflexive or pronominal verbs become simple English verbs, substantivized infinitives become nouns, and subjunctives are rendered with auxiliaries or “Let”. Tenses also shift, especially where Italian narrative presents become English pasts or an English past perfect makes the sequence of events explicit.

The vocabulary ranges from close equivalents to interpretation and occasional substantial departure. *appuzza*, ‘makes stink’, becomes the less specifically sensory “pollutes”; *pelle*, ‘skin’, becomes “aspect”; and *serpente* becomes the broader “reptile”. More conspicuous differences include *dosso*, ‘back’, rendered as “neck”, and *stagliata*, ‘sheer’, as “ragged”. In the falcon comparison, *onde si mosse snello* becomes “then swiftly moves himself”, replacing a reference to the place of an earlier departure with a subsequent action. Such choices go beyond adjustments required by English grammar.

Words are both supplied and left unexpressed. “on her loom” specifies what the Italian leaves implicit, while “to *thee*” identifies the person potentially harmed. Conversely, the intensifier in *tutta piena*, the diminutive force of *fiammella*, and repeated prepositions sometimes disappear. Italian definite articles before body parts frequently become English possessives, and feminine references to the beast become masculine. More generally, beyond the individual changes recorded in the comparison, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings already identify the subject, and omits articles before possessives: *lo duca mio* becomes “my Guide”. The result combines sustained verbal closeness with grammatical expansion, selective compression, and interpretive choices that sometimes alter the precision or emphasis of the Italian.
