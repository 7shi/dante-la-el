# Inferno 28

## Lines 1–21 — Bloodshed Beyond All Telling

> 1 Chi poria mai pur con parole sciolte  
> 2 dicer del sangue e delle piaghe appieno,  
> 3 ch’i’ ora vidi, per narrar più volte?  

Who, even with words set free, could ever fully tell, by oft relating, the blood and the wounds that I now saw?

- *poria mai pur con parole sciolte* ‘could ever even with loose words’ → “even with words set free, could ever”: the verb and adverb move after the phrase beginning “even”. The adjective becomes a participial phrase; the translation preserves the image of freedom rather than explicitly rendering the expression as ‘in prose’.
- *dicer ... appieno* ‘tell ... fully’ → “fully tell”: the adverb moves before the verb.
- *del sangue e delle piaghe* ‘of the blood and of the wounds’ → “the blood and the wounds”: the prepositions disappear, making the nouns direct objects of “tell”.
- *per narrar più volte* ‘for narrating more times’ → “by oft relating”: the infinitive becomes a gerund, and the expression of repetition becomes an adverb. The Italian has concessive force, ‘even with repeated telling’, whereas “by” presents repetition as a means. The phrase also moves before the objects of “tell”.

> 4 Ogni lingua per certo verria meno  
> 5 per lo nostro sermone e per la mente,  
> 6 c’ hanno a tanto comprender poco seno.  

Every tongue assuredly would fail, because of our speech and our memory that have small capacity to comprehend so much.

- *per certo* ‘for certain’ → “assuredly”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *verria meno* ‘would come less’ → “would fail”: the Italian idiom becomes a single English verb.
- *per lo nostro sermone e per la mente* ‘because of our speech and because of the mind’ → “because of our speech and our memory”: the repeated preposition is omitted, and a possessive replaces the article before *mente*. “Memory” selects a narrower sense than ‘mind’.
- *c’ hanno ... poco seno* ‘which have ... little room’ → “that have small capacity”: the spatial metaphor of *seno*, literally ‘bosom’, becomes an abstract statement of capacity.
- *a tanto comprender* ‘for so much comprehending’ → “to comprehend so much”: the object follows the infinitive, and the whole phrase moves after “small capacity”.

> 7 S’ ei s’ adunasse ancor tutta la gente,  
> 8 che già in su la fortunata terra  
> 9 di Puglia fu del suo sangue dolente  

If all the people too were gathered, who of old upon Apulia’s fateful land wailed for their blood,

- *S’ ei s’ adunasse ancor tutta la gente* ‘If there should assemble again all the people’ → “If all the people too were gathered”: the pronominal verb becomes an English passive, and its subject moves before it. The impersonal *ei* has no separate equivalent; *ancor* receives the additive interpretation “too”, rather than ‘again’.
- *che già* ‘who already’ → “who of old”: *già* is interpreted as referring to the distant past.
- *in su la fortunata terra di Puglia* ‘on upon the fortunate land of Apulia’ → “upon Apulia’s fateful land”: the compound preposition becomes “upon”, and the geographical complement becomes a preceding possessive. “Fateful” interprets *fortunata* as subject to fortune, rather than simply fortunate.
- *fu del suo sangue dolente* ‘was sorrowful for its blood’ → “wailed for their blood”: a verb of audible lament replaces the copula and adjective. The complement moves after that verb, and the singular possessive referring to the collective *gente* becomes plural “their”.

> 10 per li Troiani, e per la lunga guerra  
> 11 che dell’ anella fe’ sì alte spoglie,  
> 12 come Livio scrive che non erra,  

by [reason of the Trojans], and [of] that long war which made so vast a spoil of rings, as Livy writes, who errs not;

- *per li Troiani, e per* ‘through the Trojans, and through’ → “by [reason of the Trojans], and [of]”: the causal relation is expanded into “by [reason of”, with that construction shared by the second coordinated phrase.
- *la lunga guerra* ‘the long war’ → “that long war”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative.
- *che dell’ anella fe’ sì alte spoglie* ‘which of the rings made so high spoils’ → “which made so vast a spoil of rings”: the complement referring to the rings moves after “spoil” and loses its article. Plural *spoglie* becomes singular “a spoil”, and the metaphorical ‘high’ becomes “vast”.
- *che non erra* ‘who does not err’ → “who errs not”: the negative follows the finite verb instead of preceding it.

> 13 con quella che sentì di colpi doglie,  
> 14 per contrastare a Roberto Guiscardo,  

with those who, by withstanding Robert Guiscard, felt the pains of blows;

- *con quella* ‘with that’ → “with those”: the singular pronoun referring to the collective *gente* becomes a plural pronoun referring to its members.
- *sentì di colpi doglie* ‘felt of blows pains’ → “felt the pains of blows”: the complement follows its noun, and “the” is supplied.
- *per contrastare a Roberto Guiscardo* ‘for opposing to Robert Guiscard’ → “by withstanding Robert Guiscard”: the causal infinitival phrase becomes a gerundial phrase with “by”. The English verb takes a direct object rather than the Italian prepositional complement, and the phrase moves before “felt”.

> 15 e l’ altra, il cui ossame ancor s’ accoglie  
> 16 a Ceperan, là dove fu bugiardo  
> 17 ciascun Pugliese, e là da Tagliacozzo,  
> 18 ove senz’ arme vinse il vecchio Alardo;  

and the rest whose bones are gathered still at Ceperano, where each Apulian proved false; and there at Tagliacozzo, where old Alardo conquered without weapons;

- *e l’ altra* ‘and the other’ → “and the rest”: the singular expression for another collective group becomes an expression for the remaining people.
- *il cui ossame ancor s’ accoglie* ‘whose mass of bones still is gathered’ → “whose bones are gathered still”: the collective singular *ossame* becomes plural “bones”, with corresponding plural agreement; “still” moves after the verb.
- *là dove fu bugiardo ciascun Pugliese* ‘there where was deceitful each Apulian’ → “where each Apulian proved false”: *là* has no separate equivalent, and the subject moves before the verb. “Proved” replaces ‘was’, while “false” renders the adjective as unfaithfulness rather than specifically lying.
- *e là da Tagliacozzo* ‘and there by Tagliacozzo’ → “and there at Tagliacozzo”: the locative *da* is rendered as “at”.
- *ove senz’ arme vinse il vecchio Alardo* ‘where without weapons conquered the old Alardo’ → “where old Alardo conquered without weapons”: the subject moves before the verb, the phrase “without weapons” moves after it, and the article before *vecchio Alardo* is omitted.

> 19 e qual forato suo membro, e qual mozzo  
> 20 mostrasse : da equar sarebbe nulla  
> 21 al modo della nona bolgia sozzo.  

and one should shew his limbs transpierced, and another his cut off: it were nought to equal the hideous mode of the ninth chasm.

- *e qual forato suo membro, e qual mozzo mostrasse* ‘and one pierced his member, and another cut off should show’ → “and one should shew his limbs transpierced, and another his cut off”: the verb moves from the end of the coordinated construction to follow the first subject. Singular *membro* becomes plural “limbs”, *forato* moves after its noun, and a second “his” is supplied. The subjunctive is expressed with “should”.
- *da equar sarebbe nulla* ‘for equalling it would be nothing’ → “it were nought to equal”: the conditional becomes the irrealis “were”, and the infinitival phrase moves after “nought”.
- *al modo della nona bolgia sozzo* ‘to the mode of the ninth pouch filthy’ → “the hideous mode of the ninth chasm”: the preposition disappears because English “equal” takes a direct object. The final adjective moves before “mode”, with “hideous” emphasizing horror rather than filth; the figurative ‘pouch’ becomes the topographical “chasm”.

## Lines 22–54 — The Sowers of Schism

> 22 Già veggia, per mezzul perdere o lulla,  
> 23 com' io vidi un, così non si pertugia,  
> 24 rotto dal mento infin dove si trulla:  

Even a cask, through loss of middle-piece or cant, yawns not so wide as one I saw, ripped from the chin down to the part that utters vilest sound

- *Già veggia* ‘indeed a cask’ → “Even a cask”: the emphatic opening becomes an explicitly concessive comparison.
- *per mezzul perdere o lulla* ‘through losing a middle stave or side stave’ → “through loss of middle-piece or cant”: the infinitive becomes the noun “loss,” with its objects introduced by “of”; the technical stave names become “middle-piece” and “cant.”
- *com' io vidi un, così* ‘as I saw one, so’ → “so wide as one I saw”: the comparative clauses are reordered, “wide” is supplied, and “one” moves before “I saw.”
- *non si pertugia* ‘does not become holed’ → “yawns not”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb describing an opening.
- *rotto* ‘broken’ → “ripped”: the translation specifies tearing rather than breakage generally.
- *infin dove si trulla* ‘as far as where one farts’ → “down to the part that utters vilest sound”: “down” makes the direction explicit; the impersonal clause becomes a relative clause modifying the supplied “part,” and the bodily verb becomes an elaborate euphemism.

> 25 tra le gambe pendevan le minugia;  
> 26 la corata pareva, e il tristo sacco  
> 27 che merda fa di quel che si trangugia.  

: between his legs the entrails hung; the pluck appeared, and the wretched sack that makes excrement of what it swallowed.

- *tra le gambe* ‘between the legs’ → “between his legs”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *pendevan le minugia* ‘hung the entrails’ → “the entrails hung”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *merda fa* ‘shit makes’ → “makes excrement”: the object moves after the verb, and the blunt noun becomes a more formal term.
- *di quel che si trangugia* ‘of that which one gulps down’ → “of what it swallowed”: the impersonal construction becomes an active clause whose subject is the sack; the present becomes past, and “swallowed” does not reproduce the force of gulping down.

> 28 Mentre che tutto in lui veder m' attacco,  
> 29 guardommi e con le man s' aperse il petto,  
> 30 dicendo: "Or vedi come io mi dilacco;  

Whilst I stood all occupied in seeing him, he looked at me, and with his hands opened his breast, saying : " Now see how I dilacerate myself!

- *tutto in lui veder m' attacco* ‘wholly to seeing him I attach myself’ → “I stood all occupied in seeing him”: the reflexive present becomes a past construction describing absorption; “stood” is supplied, and the object of seeing moves after its verb.
- *con le man s' aperse il petto* ‘with the hands opened himself the breast’ → “with his hands opened his breast”: the reflexive pronoun and definite articles are replaced by possessive constructions.

> 31 vedi come storpiato è Maometto.  

see how Mahomet is mangled!

- *storpiato è Maometto* ‘mangled is Mahomet’ → “Mahomet is mangled”: the subject moves before the verb and the participle after it.

> 32 Dinanzi a me sen va piangendo Alì  
> 33 fesso nel volto dal mento al ciuffetto;  

Before me Ali weeping goes, cleft in the face from chin to forelock;

- *sen va piangendo Alì* ‘goes away weeping Ali’ → “Ali weeping goes”: the subject moves to the beginning and the participle before the finite verb; the sense of departure conveyed by *sen va* is not separately expressed.
- *dal mento al ciuffetto* ‘from the chin to the little tuft’ → “from chin to forelock”: both definite articles disappear; “forelock” specifies the tuft’s position without reproducing the diminutive.

> 34 e tutti gli altri, che tu vedi qui,  
> 35 seminator di scandalo e di scisma  
> 36 fur vivi, e però son fessi così.  

and all the others, whom thou seest here, were in their lifetime sowers of scandal and of schism; and therefore are they thus cleft.

- *seminator di scandalo e di scisma / fur vivi* ‘sowers of scandal and of schism were alive’ → “were in their lifetime sowers of scandal and of schism”: the verb moves before its complement, and the adjective *vivi*, expressing when they were sowers, becomes the temporal phrase “in their lifetime.”
- *son fessi così* ‘are cleft thus’ → “are they thus cleft”: “thus” moves before the participle.

> 37 Un diavolo è qua dietro che n' accisma  
> 38 sì crudelmente, al taglio della spada  
> 39 rimettendo ciascun di questa risma,  
> 40 quando avem volta la dolente strada;  

A Devil is here behind, who splits us thus cruelly, reapplying each of this class to his sword's edge, when we have wandered round the doleful road;

- *n' accisma* ‘dresses us up’ → “splits us”: the figurative verb for dressing or arranging is replaced by a direct description of cutting.
- *sì* ‘so’ → “thus”: an expression of degree becomes an expression of manner.
- *al taglio della spada* ‘to the edge of the sword’ → “to his sword's edge”: the phrase moves after the participle and its object; the definite article becomes “his,” and the construction with “of” becomes a possessive.
- *rimettendo* ‘putting back’ → “reapplying”: the general verb of putting again becomes the more specific verb “reapplying.”
- *di questa risma* ‘of this ream’ → “of this class”: the figurative collective noun is rendered by its intended sense.
- *avem volta* ‘have rounded’ → “have wandered round”: the translation adds the manner of movement expressed by “wandered.”

> 41 però che le ferite son richiuse  
> 42 prima ch' altri dinanzi gli rivada.  

for the wounds heal up ere any goes again before him.

- *però che* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *le ferite son richiuse* ‘the wounds are closed again’ → “the wounds heal up”: a passive or resultant-state construction becomes an active intransitive one; healing is made explicit, while “again” is not separately represented.
- *altri* ‘another’ → “any”: the indefinite pronoun is rendered with broader distributive force.
- *dinanzi gli rivada* ‘before him goes again’ → “goes again before him”: the phrase expressing position moves after the verb, and the Italian subjunctive is rendered by an English indicative.

> 43 Ma tu chi se' che in su lo scoglio muse,  
> 44 forse per indugiar d' ire alla pena,  
> 45 ch' è giudicata in su le tue accuse?"  

But who art thou, that musest on the cliff, perhaps in order to delay thy going to the punishment, adjudged upon thy accusations?

- *in su lo scoglio muse* ‘upon the rock musest’ → “musest on the cliff”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase, and “cliff” gives a more specific form to the rock.
- *per indugiar d' ire* ‘to delay going’ → “in order to delay thy going”: the purpose construction is expanded, and the infinitive becomes a verbal noun with the supplied possessive “thy.”
- *ch' è giudicata* ‘which is adjudged’ → “adjudged”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial modifier.

> 46 "Nè morte il giunse ancor, nè colpa il mena,"  
> 47 rispose il mio maestro, "a tormentarlo;  

" " Not yet has death come to him; nor does guilt lead him," replied my Master, " to torment him;

- *Nè morte il giunse ancor* ‘neither death reached him yet’ → “Not yet has death come to him”: the first member of the negative pairing becomes “Not”; the negative and temporal expressions move to the front, producing inversion; the simple past becomes present perfect, and transitive reaching becomes coming “to him.”
- *nè colpa il mena* ‘nor guilt him leads’ → “nor does guilt lead him”: English supplies the auxiliary “does,” inverts it with the subject, and places the object after the main verb.

> 48 ma per dar lui esperienza piena,  
> 49 a me, che morto son, convien menarlo  
> 50 per lo inferno quaggiù di giro in giro;  

but to give him full experience, it behooves me, who am dead, to lead him through the Hell down here, from round to round;

- *per dar lui esperienza piena* ‘to give him experience full’ → “to give him full experience”: the adjective moves before its noun.
- *a me, che morto son, convien menarlo* ‘to me, who dead am, it is necessary to lead him’ → “it behooves me, who am dead, to lead him”: the main verb moves before “me,” the Italian dative becomes the object of “behooves,” and “dead” moves after its verb.

> 51 e questo è ver così com' io ti parlo."  

and this is true as that I speak to thee."

- *così com' io ti parlo* ‘just as I speak to thee’ → “as that I speak to thee”: “that” is supplied, making the following clause an explicit proposition used for comparison; *così* has no separate equivalent.

> 52 Più fur di cento che, quando l' udiro,  
> 53 s' arrestaron nel fosso a riguardarmi,  
> 54 per maraviglia obbliando il martiro.  

More than a hundred, when they heard him, stopped in the foss to look at me, through wonder forgetting their torment.

- *Più fur di cento che* ‘more were than a hundred who’ → “More than a hundred”: the verb expressing their number and the relative pronoun disappear; the numbered group becomes the subject of the main verb “stopped.”
- *s' arrestaron* ‘stopped themselves’ → “stopped”: the reflexive construction becomes an English intransitive verb.
- *obbliando il martiro* ‘forgetting the torment’ → “forgetting their torment”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

## Lines 55–87 — Warnings of Treachery and Death

> 55 "Or di' a Fra Dolcin dunque che s' armi,  
> 56 tu che forse vedrai lo sole in breve,  
> 57 s' egli non vuol qui tosto seguitarmi,  

“Well, then, thou who perhaps shalt see the Sun ere long, tell Fra Dolcino, if he wish not speedily to follow me *down here*,

- *Or ... dunque* ‘Now ... then’ → “Well, then,”: the opening adverb becomes a conversational interjection, and the two words are brought together.
- *di' a Fra Dolcin ... che s'armi* ‘tell Fra Dolcino ... that he arm himself’ → “tell Fra Dolcino,”: the command is placed after the address to the listener; its content, the injunction to arm himself, is deferred to the following range and recast as an infinitive.
- *in breve* ‘in a short time’ → “ere long”: a prepositional phrase becomes an idiomatic expression meaning before long.
- *s'egli non vuol* ‘if he does not wish’ → “if he wish not”: the Italian indicative becomes an English subjunctive.
- *tosto* ‘soon’ → “speedily”: nearness in time is expressed as speed.
- *qui* ‘here’ → “*down here*”: the translation supplies a direction.

> 58 sì di vivanda, che stretta di neve  
> 59 non rechi la vittoria al Noarese,  
> 60 ch' altrimenti acquistar non saria lieve."  

so to arm himself with victuals, that stress of snow may not bring victory to the Novarese, which otherwise would not be easy to attain.”

- *sì di vivanda* ‘so with provisions’ → “so to arm himself with victuals,”: the infinitive deferred from line 55 is inserted between the adverb and the provisioning phrase; the Italian singular mass noun is rendered by a plural.
- *che stretta di neve non rechi la vittoria al Noarese* ‘that confinement by snow not bring the victory to the Novarese’ → “that stress of snow may not bring victory to the Novarese,”: confinement or constraint becomes “stress”; the subjunctive is expressed with “may,” and the definite article before victory is omitted.
- *ch'altrimenti acquistar non saria lieve* ‘which otherwise to acquire would not be easy’ → “which otherwise would not be easy to attain”: the infinitive moves from before the finite verb to after the adjective.

> 61 Poi che l' un piè per girsene sospese,  
> 62 Maometto mi disse esta parola,  
> 63 indi a partirsi in terra lo distese.  

After lifting up one foot to go away, Mahomet said *this to me*; then *on the ground he* *stretched it to depart*.

- *Poi che l'un piè per girsene sospese* ‘After he raised one foot to go away’ → “After lifting up one foot to go away,”: a finite temporal clause becomes a participial phrase, with the object placed after the verb.
- *mi disse esta parola* ‘to me said this word’ → “said *this to me*”: the indirect object moves after the direct object; “this” stands alone, with the noun *parola* omitted.
- *a partirsi in terra lo distese* ‘to depart on the ground it he stretched’ → “*on the ground he* *stretched it to depart*”: the purpose infinitive moves to the end, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 64 Un altro, che forata avea la gola  
> 65 e tronco il naso infin sotto le ciglia,  
> 66 e non avea ma che un' orecchia sola,  

Another, who had his throat pierced through, and nose cut off up to the eyebrows, and had but one single ear,

- *che forata avea la gola* ‘who pierced had the throat’ → “who had his throat pierced through,”: the participle moves after the object, the definite article becomes a possessive, and “through” makes the extent of the piercing explicit.
- *e tronco il naso infin sotto le ciglia* ‘and cut off the nose as far as below the eyebrows’ → “and nose cut off up to the eyebrows,”: the participle moves after the noun, the article before nose is omitted, and “up to” leaves out the distinction expressed by *sotto*, below.
- *e non avea ma che un'orecchia sola* ‘and did not have other than one ear alone’ → “and had but one single ear,”: the negative restrictive construction becomes an affirmative verb with “but”; the adjective moves before the noun.

> 67 restato a riguardar per maraviglia  
> 68 con gli altri, innanzi agli altri aprì la canna  
> 69 ch' era di fuor d' ogni parte vermiglia;  

standing to gaze in wonder with the rest, before the rest opened his weasand, *which outwardly* *was red on every part,*

- *restato a riguardar* ‘having stayed to look’ → “standing to gaze”: a past participle expressing that he had remained becomes a present participle expressing his posture; looking is rendered more intently as gazing.
- *con gli altri ... innanzi agli altri* ‘with the others ... before the others’ → “with the rest, before the rest”: the plural designation of other people becomes the collective “the rest” in both places.
- *la canna* ‘the windpipe’ → “his weasand”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *ch'era di fuor d'ogni parte vermiglia* ‘which was outside on every part vermilion’ → “*which outwardly* *was red on every part,*”: the phrase expressing exterior position becomes an adverb placed before the verb; the colour adjective moves before the phrase describing its extent, and the more specific vermilion becomes “red.”

> 70 e disse: "Tu, cui colpa non condanna,  
> 71 e cui io vidi su in terra Latina,  
> 72 se troppa simiglianza non m' inganna,  

and said: “Thou ! whom guilt condemns not, and whom I have seen above on Latian ground, *unless too much resemblance deceive me*;

- *io vidi* ‘I saw’ → “I have seen”: the simple past becomes a present perfect.
- *se troppa simiglianza non m'inganna* ‘if too much resemblance does not deceive me’ → “*unless too much resemblance deceive me*”: the negative condition becomes an affirmative clause introduced by “unless”; the Italian indicative becomes an English subjunctive, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 73 rimembriti di Pier da Medicina,  
> 74 se mai torni a veder lo dolce piano,  
> 75 che da Vercelli a Marcabò dichina.  

remember Pier da Medicina, if ever thou return *to see the gentle plain that from Vercelli* *slopes to Marcabò.*

- *rimembriti di Pier da Medicina* ‘bethink thyself of Pier da Medicina’ → “remember Pier da Medicina”: the reflexive construction with a preposition becomes a transitive verb with a direct object.
- *torni* ‘thou returnest’ → “thou return”: the Italian indicative becomes an English subjunctive in the conditional clause.
- *lo dolce piano* ‘the sweet plain’ → “*the gentle plain*”: “gentle” replaces the literal sweetness of the adjective.
- *che da Vercelli a Marcabò dichina* ‘that from Vercelli to Marcabò slopes’ → “that from Vercelli* *slopes to Marcabò.*”: the destination phrase moves after the verb.

> 76 E fa saper ai due miglior di Fano,  
> 77 a messer Guido ed anco ad Angiolello,  
> 78 che, se l' antiveder qui non è vano,  

And make known to the worthiest two of Fano, to Messer Guido and to Angiolello likewise, *that, unless our foresight here be vain,*

- *fa saper* ‘cause to know’ → “make known”: the causative construction with an active infinitive becomes one with a passive participle.
- *ai due miglior di Fano* ‘to the two best of Fano’ → “to the worthiest two of Fano,”: “worthiest” specifies the kind of superiority, and the superlative moves before the numeral.
- *ed anco ad Angiolello* ‘and also to Angiolello’ → “and to Angiolello likewise,”: the additive adverb becomes “likewise” and moves after the name.
- *che, se l'antiveder qui non è vano* ‘that, if the foreseeing here is not vain’ → “*that, unless our foresight here be vain,*”: the substantivized infinitive becomes the noun “foresight,” its definite article becomes the supplied possessive “our,” and the negative condition becomes an affirmative clause with “unless”; the indicative becomes an English subjunctive.

> 79 gittati saran fuor di lor vasello,  
> 80 e mazzerati presso alla Cattolica,  
> 81 per tradimento d' un tiranno fello.  

*they shall be cast out of their ship*, and drowned near the Cattolica, *by a fell tyrant's* *treachery.*

- *gittati saran* ‘cast they shall be’ → “*they shall be cast”: the participle moves from its emphatic initial position to after the auxiliary.
- *vasello* ‘vessel’ → “ship”: the translation uses a more specific designation.
- *mazzerati* ‘drowned with a weight attached’ → “drowned”: the translation omits the means of drowning contained in the Italian verb.
- *per tradimento d'un tiranno fello* ‘by treachery of a tyrant fell’ → “*by a fell tyrant's* *treachery.*”: the phrase with *di* becomes a possessive placed before its governing noun, and the adjective moves before *tyrant*.

> 82 Tra l' isola di Cipro e di Maiolica  
> 83 non vide mai sì gran fallo Nettuno,  
> 84 non da pirati, non da gente Argolica.  

Between the isles of Cyprus and Majorca, Neptune never saw so great a crime—not even with Pirates, not even with Argives.

- *Tra l'isola di Cipro e di Maiolica* ‘Between the island of Cyprus and of Majorca’ → “Between the isles of Cyprus and Majorca,”: the singular noun shared by the two names becomes plural, and the second preposition is omitted.
- *non vide mai sì gran fallo Nettuno* ‘never saw so great a wrongdoing Neptune’ → “Neptune never saw so great a crime”: the subject moves from the end to the beginning, and the broader term for wrongdoing becomes “crime.”
- *non da pirati, non da gente Argolica* ‘not from pirates, not from Argive people’ → “not even with Pirates, not even with Argives”: “even” is supplied twice, “with” replaces the preposition indicating the source or agents of the wrongdoing, and the collective noun with an adjective becomes the plural noun “Argives.”

> 85 Quel traditor, che vede pur con l' uno,  
> 86 e tien la terra, che tal è qui meco,  
> 87 vorrebbe di veder esser digiuno,  

That traitor who sees with but one eye, and holds the land which one *who is here with* *me would wish that he had never seen,*

- *che vede pur con l'uno* ‘who sees only with the one’ → “who sees with but one eye,”: the restrictive adverb moves after the preposition and becomes “but”; the definite article is omitted, and the understood noun “eye” is supplied.
- *che tal è qui meco vorrebbe di veder esser digiuno* ‘which someone here with me would wish to be fasting from seeing’ → “which one *who is here with* *me would wish that he had never seen,*”: the compressed description of the person becomes an explicit relative clause; the metaphor of fasting from sight becomes a statement of never having seen. The infinitival complement becomes a finite clause with “that,” and the English past perfect presents the seeing as a prior experience.

## Lines 88–120 — Silenced Speakers and a Headless Trunk

> 88 farà venirli a parlamento seco;  
> 89 poi farà sì che al vento di Focara  
> 90 non farà lor mestier voto nè preco.”  

will make them come to parley with him; then act so, that they shall need no vow nor prayer for Focara's wind."

- *farà venirli* ‘will make come them’ → “will make them come”: the object pronoun moves before the infinitive.
- *seco* ‘with himself’ → “with him”: the reflexive becomes an ordinary personal pronoun.
- *farà sì* ‘will do so’ → “act so”: “act” replaces ‘do’; the future auxiliary is shared with the preceding clause rather than repeated.
- *al vento di Focara* ‘for the wind of Focara’ → “for Focara's wind”: the phrase moves after the statement about needing vows and prayers, and the name becomes a possessive before “wind”.
- *non farà lor mestier voto nè preco* ‘neither vow nor prayer will be necessary to them’ → “they shall need no vow nor prayer”: those needing something become the subject; ‘vow’ and ‘prayer’, the Italian subjects, become objects of “need”.

> 91 Ed io a lui: “Dimostrami e dichiara,  
> 92 se vuoi ch’io porti su di te novella,  
> 93 chi è colui dalla veduta amara.”  

And I to him : "Shew me and explain, so thou wouldst have me carry tidings up of thee, who he is that rues that sight."

- *se* ‘if’ → “so”: the translation uses “so” in its conditional sense.
- *vuoi ch’io porti* ‘you want that I carry’ → “thou wouldst have me carry”: a present-tense expression of desire with a subordinate subjunctive becomes “wouldst have” followed by an object and an infinitive.
- *su di te novella* ‘up of you tidings’ → “tidings up of thee”: the object moves before the adverb and the phrase specifying whom the tidings concern.
- *chi è colui dalla veduta amara* ‘who is that one with the bitter sight’ → “who he is that rues that sight”: the embedded question receives English subject–verb order. The description with *dalla* becomes a relative clause with the supplied verb “rues”; the translation interprets the bitterness as distress caused by seeing, rather than simply describing a bitter look. “That” also makes the sight more explicitly particular.

> 94 Allor pose la mano alla mascella  
> 95 d’un suo compagno, e la bocca gli aperse  
> 96 gridando: “Questi è desso, e non favella;  

Then he laid his hand upon the jaw of one of his companions; and opened the mouth of him, saying : "This is he, and he speaks not;

- *pose la mano alla mascella* ‘laid the hand to the jaw’ → “he laid his hand upon the jaw”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘to’ becomes the positional “upon”.
- *d’un suo compagno* ‘of a companion of his’ → “of one of his companions”: the singular noun becomes a plural within a partitive construction.
- *e la bocca gli aperse* ‘and the mouth to him opened’ → “and opened the mouth of him”: the verb moves before the object; the dative pronoun identifying the affected person becomes a possessive phrase after “mouth”.
- *gridando* ‘shouting’ → “saying”: the translation omits the indication of loudness.
- *Questi è desso* ‘This is that very one’ → “This is he”: the emphatic identification in *desso* is reduced to an ordinary pronoun.
- *non favella* ‘not speaks’ → “speaks not”: the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.

> 97 questi scacciato il dubitar sommerse  
> 98 in Cesare, affermando che il fornito  
> 99 sempre con danno l’attender sofferse.”  

this outcast quenched the doubt in Cæsar, affirming that to men prepared delay is always hurtful."

- *questi scacciato* ‘this one, driven out’ → “this outcast”: the participial description becomes a noun.
- *il dubitar sommerse in Cesare* ‘the doubting drowned in Cæsar’ → “quenched the doubt in Cæsar”: the verb moves before its object; the substantivized infinitive becomes “doubt”, and the metaphor changes from drowning to quenching.
- *il fornito sempre con danno l’attender sofferse* ‘the prepared one always with harm endured waiting’ → “to men prepared delay is always hurtful”: the singular prepared person becomes plural “men”; ‘waiting’, originally the object of ‘endured’, becomes the subject “delay”. The past-tense statement becomes a present-tense generalization, while ‘with harm’ becomes the predicate adjective “hurtful”.

> 100 O quanto mi pareva sbigottito  
> 101 con la lingua tagliata nella strozza,  
> 102 Curio, ch’a dire fu così ardito!  

Oh, how dejected, with tongue slit in his gorge, seemed Curio to me, who was so daring in his speech!

- *O quanto mi pareva sbigottito* and *Curio* ‘Oh, how to me seemed dismayed Curio’ → “Oh, how dejected” and “seemed Curio to me”: the adjective moves before the verb, while the experiencer “to me” moves after the named subject. “Dejected” emphasizes downcast spirits rather than the dismay or bewilderment of *sbigottito*.
- *con la lingua tagliata nella strozza* ‘with the tongue cut in the throat’ → “with tongue slit in his gorge”: the article before ‘tongue’ disappears, the article before ‘throat’ becomes “his”, and “slit” specifies a particular kind of cutting.
- *ch’a dire fu così ardito* ‘who to speak was so bold’ → “who was so daring in his speech”: the infinitive becomes a noun with the supplied possessive “his”; its phrase moves after the adjective, and the preposition becomes “in”.

> 103 Ed un, ch’avea l’una e l’altra man mozza,  
> 104 levando i moncherin per l’aura fosca,  
> 105 sì che il sangue facea la faccia sozza,  

And one who had both hands cut off, raising the stumps through the dim air so that their blood defiled his face,

- *l’una e l’altra man mozza* ‘the one and the other hand cut off’ → “both hands cut off”: the distributive singular construction becomes a plural with “both”.
- *l’aura fosca* ‘the air dark’ → “the dim air”: the adjective moves before the noun, with “dim” rendering *fosca*.
- *il sangue* ‘the blood’ → “their blood”: the definite article becomes a possessive explicitly referring to the stumps.
- *facea la faccia sozza* ‘made the face filthy’ → “defiled his face”: the construction with ‘make’ and a predicate adjective becomes a single transitive verb; the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 106 gridò: “Ricordera’ ti anche del Mosca,  
> 107 che dissi, lasso! ‘Capo ha cosa fatta,’  
> 108 che fu il mal seme della gente tosca.”  

said : "Thou wilt recollect the Mosca, too, ah me! who said, 'A thing done has an end!' which was the seed of evil to the Tuscan people."

- *gridò* ‘shouted’ → “said”: the indication of loudness is omitted.
- *Ricordera’ ti anche del Mosca* ‘You will remind yourself also of the Mosca’ → “Thou wilt recollect the Mosca, too”: the reflexive construction with *di* becomes a transitive verb with a direct object; “too” follows that object rather than preceding it.
- *lasso!* ‘wretched!’ → “ah me!”: the adjective used as a lament becomes an interjection and moves before “who said”.
- *Capo ha cosa fatta* ‘Head has thing done’ → “A thing done has an end!”: the subject moves before the verb and the object after it. Indefinite articles are supplied, and *capo* is interpreted figuratively as an ‘end’ or conclusion.
- *il mal seme della gente tosca* ‘the evil seed of the people Tuscan’ → “the seed of evil to the Tuscan people”: the adjective *mal* becomes the noun “evil” in an “of” phrase; the original possessive relation becomes “to”. “Tuscan” moves before its noun.

> 109 Ed io gli aggiunsi: “E morte di tua schiatta”;  

"And death to thy kindred!" I added [thereto];

- *Ed io gli aggiunsi* ‘And I to him added’ → “And” and “I added [thereto]”: the conjunction moves from the narrative introduction into the quoted speech, while the introductory attribution moves after the quotation. The personal dative ‘to him’ is rendered as “[thereto]”, referring instead to what has just been said.
- *morte di tua schiatta* ‘death of your lineage’ → “death to thy kindred!”: ‘of’ becomes “to”, and “kindred” presents the family as relatives rather than as a line of descent.

> 110 per ch’egli accumulando duol con duolo,  
> 111 sen gìo come persona trista e matta.  

wherefore he, accumulating pain on pain, went away as one distressed and mad.

- *per ch’egli* ‘for which he’ → “wherefore he”: the connective prepositional phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *accumulando duol con duolo* ‘accumulating grief with grief’ → “accumulating pain on pain”: emotional grief is rendered by the broader “pain”, while “on” replaces ‘with’ to express accumulation.
- *sen gìo* ‘himself thence went’ → “went away”: the pronominal expression becomes a verb with an adverb; the reflexive element has no separate equivalent.
- *come persona trista e matta* ‘like a person sad and mad’ → “as one distressed and mad”: “one” replaces the noun *persona*, and “distressed” renders the sadness expressed by *trista*.

> 112 Ma io rimasi a riguardar lo stuolo,  
> 113 e vidi cosa ch’io avrei paura,  
> 114 senza più prova, di contarla solo;  

But I remained to view the troop, and saw a thing which I should be afraid even to relate, without more proof;

- *cosa* ‘thing’ → “a thing”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *ch’io avrei paura* ‘which I would have fear’ → “which I should be afraid”: the construction with ‘have’ and a noun becomes “be” with an adjective. “Should” expresses the first-person conditional.
- *senza più prova, di contarla solo* ‘without more proof, of telling it merely’ → “even to relate, without more proof”: the phrase about proof moves from before the infinitive clause to after it. The infinitive is adapted to the English adjective “afraid”; the resumptive object pronoun *la* is omitted because “which” already supplies the object. *Solo*, ‘merely’, becomes the intensifying “even” and moves before the infinitive.

> 115 se non che coscienza mi assicura,  
> 116 la buona compagnia che l’uom francheggia  
> 117 sotto l’osbergo del sentirsi pura.  

but that conscience reassures me, that good companion which fortifies a man beneath the hauberk of his self-felt purity.

- *se non che* ‘except that’ → “but that”: the exceptive expression is compressed into the English connective.
- *mi assicura* ‘me reassures’ → “reassures me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *la buona compagnia* ‘the good company’ → “that good companion”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative, and the abstract or collective noun becomes a personal one.
- *che l’uom francheggia* ‘which the man emboldens’ → “which fortifies a man”: the verb moves before its object; the generic definite article becomes indefinite, and “fortifies” gives the verb a metaphor of strengthening.
- *del sentirsi pura* ‘of feeling itself pure’ → “of his self-felt purity”: the substantivized reflexive infinitive becomes the compound adjective “self-felt”, and *pura* becomes the noun “purity”. The feminine *pura* points back to conscience; “his” instead assigns the purity explicitly to the man.

> 118 Io vidi certo, ed ancor par ch’io ’l veggia,  
> 119 un busto senza capo andar, sì come  
> 120 andavan gli altri della trista greggia.  

Certainly I saw, and still seem to see [it,] a trunk going without a head, as the others of that dismal herd were going.

- *Io vidi certo* ‘I saw certainly’ → “Certainly I saw”: the adverb moves to the beginning of the clause.
- *ed ancor par ch’io ’l veggia* ‘and still it seems that I see it’ → “and still seem to see [it,]”: the impersonal verb and subordinate subjunctive become a personal construction sharing the preceding “I”, with an infinitive after “seem”.
- *un busto senza capo andar* ‘a trunk without head go’ → “a trunk going without a head”: the infinitive dependent on the verb of seeing becomes a participle and moves before the phrase describing the missing head. An indefinite article is supplied before “head”.
- *sì come* ‘just as’ → “as”: the reinforcing *sì* has no separate equivalent.
- *andavan gli altri* ‘were going the others’ → “the others” and “were going”: the subject moves before the verb, which follows the subject’s qualifying phrase.
- *della trista greggia* ‘of the sad flock’ → “of that dismal herd”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative; “dismal” renders *trista*, and “herd” replaces ‘flock’.

## Lines 121–142 — The Severed Head Speaks

> 121 E il capo tronco tenea per le chiome,  
> 122 pesol con mano a guisa di lanterna,  

And it was holding by the hair the severed head, swinging in his hand like a lantern;

- *il capo tronco* ‘the head severed’ → “the severed head”: the adjective precedes the noun, and the object moves from before the verb to after the verb and its prepositional phrase.
- *tenea* ‘held’ → “it was holding”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English past progressive.
- *le chiome* ‘the hairs’ → “the hair”: the Italian plural becomes an English collective singular.
- *pesol* ‘hanging’ → “swinging”: the translation introduces motion rather than merely suspension.
- *con mano* ‘with hand’ → “in his hand”: the preposition changes, and a possessive is supplied.
- *a guisa di lanterna* ‘in the manner of a lantern’ → “like a lantern”: the phrase expressing manner becomes a direct comparison.

> 123 e quel mirava noi, e dicea: “O me!”  

and that looked at us and said: "O me!"

- *mirava noi* ‘regarded us’ → “looked at us”: a direct object becomes the object of a preposition.

> 124 Di sè faceva a sè stesso lucerna,  
> 125 ed eran due in uno; ed uno in due;  
> 126 com’ esser può, quei sa che sì governa.  

Of itself it made for itself a lamp, and they were two in one, and one in two; how this can be, He knows who so ordains.

- *lucerna* ‘lamp’ → “a lamp”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *com’ esser può* ‘how be can’ → “how this can be”: the modal precedes the infinitive, and the demonstrative subject “this” is supplied.
- *governa* ‘governs’ → “ordains”: governing is rendered as determining or ordering.

> 127 Quando diritto al piè del ponte fue,  
> 128 levò il braccio alto con tutta la testa  
> 129 per appressarne le parole sue,  

When it was just at the foot of our bridge, it raised its arm high up, with all the head, to bring near to us its words,

- *diritto* ‘directly’ → “just”: the adverb is rendered as an expression of exact position.
- *del ponte* ‘of the bridge’ → “of our bridge”: the definite article is replaced by a supplied possessive.
- *il braccio* ‘the arm’ → “its arm”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *alto* ‘high’ → “high up”: “up” is supplied.
- *le parole sue* ‘the words its’ → “its words”: the postposed possessive moves before the noun.

> 130 che furo: “Or vedi la pena molesta  
> 131 tu che, spirando, vai veggendo i morti;  
> 132 vedi se alcuna è grande come questa.  

which were: "Now see the grievous penalty, thou, who breathing goest to view the dead; see if any be as great as this!

- *la pena molesta* ‘the penalty grievous’ → “the grievous penalty”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *vai veggendo* ‘goest seeing’ → “goest to view”: the gerund becomes an infinitive of purpose; the Italian construction can express continuing activity rather than movement undertaken for a purpose.
- *è grande come questa* ‘is great as this’ → “be as great as this”: the indicative becomes an English subjunctive, and the first “as” is supplied to complete the English comparison.

> 133 E perchè tu di me novella porti,  
> 134 sappi ch’ io son Bertram dal Bornio, quelli  
> 135 che diedi al re giovane mai comforti.  

And that thou mayest carry tidings of me, know, that I am Bertran de Born, he who to the Young King gave evil counsels.

- *perchè tu di me novella porti* ‘so that thou of me news may carry’ → “that thou mayest carry tidings of me”: the purpose conjunction becomes “that”; the subjunctive is expressed through “mayest”; the phrase “of me” moves after the object; and singular *novella* becomes plural “tidings”.
- *quelli che diedi al re giovane mai comforti* ‘that one who gave to the king young evil encouragements’ → “he who to the Young King gave evil counsels”: the recipient moves before the verb, the adjective moves before “King”, and encouragements become counsels. The Italian relative verb *diedi* agrees in the first person with the speaker; the English recasts the relative clause after “he”.

> 136 Io feci il padre e il figlio in sè ribelli;  

I made the father and the son rebels to each other;

- *in sè ribelli* ‘against themselves rebellious’ → “rebels to each other”: the reflexive expression is interpreted reciprocally, the adjective becomes a noun, and the prepositional phrase follows it.

> 137 Achitofel non fe’ più d’ Ansalone  
> 138 e di David co’ malvagi pungelli.  

Ahithophel did not do more with Absalom and David by his malicious instigations.

- *non fe’* ‘not did’ → “did not do”: English forms the negative with an auxiliary and an infinitive.
- *d’ Ansalone e di David* ‘of Absalom and of David’ → “with Absalom and David”: “with” renders the relationship expressed by *di*, and the preposition is not repeated.
- *co’ malvagi pungelli* ‘with the malicious goads’ → “by his malicious instigations”: the instrumental preposition changes, the definite article becomes a supplied possessive, and the metaphorical goads become abstract instigations.

> 139 Perch’ io partii così giunte persone,  
> 140 partito porto il mio cerebro, lasso!  
> 141 dal suo principio ch’ è in questo troncone.  

Because I parted persons thus united, I carry my brain, ah me! parted from its source which is in this trunk.

- *partii così giunte persone* ‘parted thus united persons’ → “parted persons thus united”: the participial phrase moves after the noun.
- *partito porto il mio cerebro, lasso!* ‘parted I carry my brain, wretched!’ → “I carry my brain, ah me! parted”: the fronted participle moves after the object and the exclamation; the adjective describing the speaker becomes an interjection.
- *principio* ‘beginning’ → “source”: the translation selects the sense of origin rather than temporal beginning.
- *troncone* ‘severed trunk’ → “trunk”: the Italian noun’s suggestion of a cut-off portion is not separately expressed.

> 142 Così s’ osserva in me lo contrapasso.”  

Thus the law of retribution is observed in me.”

- *s’ osserva in me lo contrapasso* ‘is observed in me the counter-suffering’ → “the law of retribution is observed in me”: the subject moves from the end to the beginning, the Italian *si*-passive becomes an English auxiliary passive, and the compact noun *contrapasso* is expanded into the explanatory phrase “the law of retribution”.

## Summary — Close Detail, Recast Syntax

The translation stays close to the Italian’s sequence of particulars, often preserving its images and repetitions, but regularly rearranges its syntax. Subjects placed after verbs move before them, objects follow their verbs, and adjectives generally precede their nouns: *storpiato è Maometto* becomes “Mahomet is mangled”, and *la pena molesta* becomes “the grievous penalty”. More conspicuous displacements are also resolved: the fronted *partito* follows the object and exclamation in “I carry my brain, ah me! parted”. Yet the English retains some marked ordering and older forms, including “Before me Ali weeping goes” and “who errs not”, rather than consistently adopting ordinary English prose order.

Changes of construction frequently accompany this rearrangement. Reflexive or pronominal expressions become simple English verbs, as *s’arrestaron* becomes “stopped”; prepositional complements become direct objects, as *rimembriti di Pier da Medicina* becomes “remember Pier da Medicina”. Infinitives become nouns or gerunds, while descriptive expressions become finite clauses. Thus *dalla veduta amara*, ‘with the bitter sight’, becomes “that rues that sight”. More extensive recasting occurs when *non farà lor mestier voto nè preco*, ‘neither vow nor prayer will be necessary to them’, becomes “they shall need no vow nor prayer”: the people affected become the grammatical subject. Tense and mood also shift, sometimes producing an English generalization or conditional idiom rather than matching the Italian forms.

Word choice ranges from close, sometimes unusual equivalents to explanatory paraphrase. “words set free” retains the image in *parole sciolte* rather than explicitly saying ‘in prose’, while “hauberk” preserves *osbergo*. Elsewhere, imagery becomes abstract meaning: *poco seno* becomes “small capacity”, *risma* becomes “class”, and *pungelli* becomes “instigations”. Some choices narrow or alter the emphasis: *mente* becomes “memory”, *gridando* becomes the less forceful “saying”, and *mazzerati* becomes simply “drowned”, leaving the attached weight unexpressed. The blunt *merda* becomes the more formal “excrement”, while *dove si trulla* is expanded into the euphemistic “the part that utters vilest sound”. At the close, *contrapasso* receives the explanatory expansion “the law of retribution”.

Small omissions and additions recur throughout. Repeated prepositions, reinforcing words, diminutive force, and nouns recoverable from context sometimes disappear; possessives, articles, and clarifying terms are supplied, as in “one eye” for *l’uno* and “our bridge” for *del ponte*. Collective singulars often become English plurals, notably *ossame* as “bones”. Beyond the changes individually recorded in the comparison, English routinely supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits Italian articles before possessives. These grammatical adjustments are pervasive, but they do not erase the translation’s substantial verbal closeness: alongside its paraphrases, it preserves much of the Italian’s concrete vocabulary, repeated phrasing, and sharply particular imagery.
