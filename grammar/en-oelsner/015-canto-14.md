# Inferno 14

## Lines 1–18 — The Barren Plain and Divine Vengeance

> 1 Poichè la carità del natio loco  
> 2 mi strinse, raunai le fronde sparte,  
> 3 e rende' le a colui ch' era già fioco.  

The love of my native place constraining me, I gathered up the scattered leaves; and gave them back to him, who was already hoarse.

- *Poichè … mi strinse* ‘Since … constrained me’ → “constraining me”: the causal conjunction disappears, and the finite past-tense clause becomes a participial construction; the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *del natio loco* ‘of the native place’ → “of my native place”: the definite article becomes a possessive, making the personal reference explicit.
- *raunai* ‘gathered’ → “I gathered up”: “up” is added to the verb.
- *le fronde sparte* ‘the leaves scattered’ → “the scattered leaves”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *rende' le* ‘returned them’ → “gave them back”: the single verb becomes a verb with an adverb.
- *a colui* ‘to that one’ → “to him”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.

> 4 Indi venimmo al fine, ove si parte  
> 5 lo secondo giron dal terzo, e dove  
> 6 si vede di giustizia orribil arte.  

Then we came to the limit, where the second round is separated from the third, and where is seen [a] fearful [device] of justice.

- *Indi* ‘from there’ → “Then”: the adverb is taken temporally rather than spatially.
- *al fine* ‘to the end’ → “to the limit”: the endpoint is expressed as a boundary.
- *ove si parte lo secondo giron dal terzo* ‘where parts the second round from the third’ → “where the second round is separated from the third”: the subject moves before the verb, and the pronominal *si parte* becomes an English passive.
- *di giustizia orribil arte* ‘of justice fearful art’ → “[a] fearful [device] of justice”: the modifying phrase moves after the noun, an indefinite article is supplied, and *arte* is rendered as a contrivance or “device” rather than ‘art’.

> 7 A ben manifestar le cose nuove,  
> 8 dico che arrivammo ad una landa,  
> 9 che dal suo letto ogni pianta rimuove.  

To make the new things clear, I say we reached a plain which from its bed repels all plants.

- *A ben manifestar le cose nuove* ‘To make fully manifest the things new’ → “To make the new things clear”: the verb becomes “make” with a predicative adjective following the object; *ben* has no separate rendering, and “new” moves before its noun.
- *dico che* ‘I say that’ → “I say”: the conjunction introducing the reported statement is omitted.
- *arrivammo ad* ‘we arrived at’ → “we reached”: an intransitive verb with a preposition becomes a transitive verb.
- *una landa* ‘a tract of waste land’ → “a plain”: the English uses a more general topographical term, without explicitly expressing the uncultivated character of *landa*.
- *ogni pianta rimuove* ‘every plant removes’ → “repels all plants”: the verb moves before its object; “repels” replaces ‘removes’, and the distributive singular becomes a plural with “all”.

> 10 La dolorosa selva l'è ghirlanda  
> 11 intorno, come il fosso tristo ad essa;  

The dolorous wood is a garland to it round about, as to the wood the dismal fosse;

- *l'è ghirlanda intorno* ‘to it is garland round about’ → “is a garland to it round about”: the pronoun’s equivalent moves after the predicate noun, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *come il fosso tristo ad essa* ‘as the fosse dismal to it’ → “as to the wood the dismal fosse”: the pronoun is replaced by its explicit referent, “the wood”; that prepositional phrase moves before the noun phrase, and the adjective moves before “fosse”.

> 12 quivi fermammo i passi a randa a randa.  

here we stayed our [steps] close to its very edge.

- *quivi* ‘there’ → “here”: the spatial reference changes from ‘there’ to the narrator’s immediate position.
- *fermammo i passi* ‘we stopped the steps’ → “we stayed our [steps]”: the definite article becomes a possessive, explicitly identifying whose steps are stopped.
- *a randa a randa* ‘at the edge, at the edge’ → “close to its very edge”: the repeated expression is recast with “close” and “very”, and “its” supplies an explicit possessive reference.

> 13 Lo spazzo era un' arena arida e spessa,  
> 14 non d' altra foggia fatta che colei,  
> 15 che fu da' piè di Caton già soppressa.  

The ground was a sand, dry and thick, not different in its fashion from that which once was trodden by the feet of Cato.

- *Lo spazzo* ‘the space’ → “The ground”: the English names the surface rather than the open space or expanse.
- *non d' altra foggia fatta che colei* ‘not made of another fashion than that’ → “not different in its fashion from that”: the construction with ‘made’ is replaced by an adjectival comparison; ‘another’ modifying ‘fashion’ becomes “different” describing the sand, “its” is supplied, and comparative ‘than’ becomes “from”.
- *che fu da' piè di Caton già soppressa* ‘which was by the feet of Cato already trodden down’ → “which once was trodden by the feet of Cato”: the agent phrase moves after the participle; *già* is interpreted as “once” rather than ‘already’ and moves before the auxiliary; ‘trodden down’ is reduced to “trodden”.

> 16 O vendetta di Dio, quanto tu dei  
> 17 esser temuta da ciascun che legge  
> 18 ciò che fu manifesto agli occhi miei!  

O vengeance of God! how shouldst thou be feared by every one who reads what was revealed to my eyes!

- *quanto tu dei esser temuta* ‘how much thou must be feared’ → “how shouldst thou be feared”: “how” alone expresses the degree conveyed by *quanto*; the obligation is phrased with “shouldst” rather than ‘must’, and the modal verb moves before the subject.
- *ciò che* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun become a single fused relative.
- *fu manifesto* ‘was manifest’ → “was revealed”: a construction with a predicate adjective becomes a verbal passive.
- *agli occhi miei* ‘to the eyes mine’ → “to my eyes”: the possessive moves before the noun.

## Lines 19–51 — Fire and Defiance on Burning Sand

> 19 D' anime nude vidi molte gregge,  
> 20 che piangean tutte assai miseramente,  
> 21 e parea posta lor diversa legge.  

I saw many herds of naked souls, who were all lamenting very miserably; and there seemed imposed upon them a diverse law.

- *D' anime nude vidi molte gregge* ‘of souls naked I saw many herds’ → “I saw many herds of naked souls”: the opening phrase modifying *gregge* moves after “herds,” and “naked” precedes its noun.
- *piangean tutte* ‘wept all’ → “were all lamenting”: weeping becomes lamentation, the imperfect becomes an English past progressive, and “all” precedes the principal verb.
- *parea posta lor diversa legge* ‘seemed imposed on them diverse law’ → “there seemed imposed upon them a diverse law”: English supplies introductory “there” and the indefinite article before “diverse law.”

> 22 Supin giaceva in terra alcuna gente,  
> 23 alcuna si sedea tutta raccolta,  
> 24 ed altra andava continuamente.  

Some were lying supine upon the ground; some sitting all crouched up; and others roaming incessantly.

- *Supin giaceva* ‘supine lay’ → “were lying supine”: the description follows the verb rather than preceding it; the imperfect becomes progressive, and the singular verb agreeing with collective *gente* becomes plural.
- *alcuna gente* ‘some people’ → “Some”: the noun is omitted, leaving “Some” as a pronoun, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *si sedea* ‘sat’ → “sitting”: the finite imperfect becomes a participle sharing “were” from the preceding clause.
- *tutta raccolta* ‘all drawn together’ → “all crouched up”: the description of being gathered together is rendered as an English expression for bodily posture.
- *altra* ‘other’ → “others”: the singular adjective referring to collective *gente* becomes a plural pronoun.
- *andava* ‘went’ → “roaming”: the general verb of movement becomes the more specific “roaming,” and the finite imperfect becomes another participle sharing “were.”

> 25 Quella che giva intorno era più molta,  
> 26 e quella men che giaceva al tormento,  
> 27 ma più al duolo avea la lingua sciolta.  

Those that moved about were much more numerous; and those that were lying in the torment were fewer, but uttered louder cries of pain.

- *Quella che giva intorno era* ‘that which went around was’ → “Those that moved about were”: the singular reference to collective *gente* becomes plural throughout.
- *più molta* ‘more numerous’ → “much more numerous”: “much” explicitly strengthens the comparison.
- *quella men che giaceva al tormento* ‘that less which lay in the torment’ → “those that were lying in the torment were fewer”: the singular collective becomes plural, the imperfect becomes progressive, and the elliptical *men* becomes “were fewer,” placed after the relative clause.
- *ma più al duolo avea la lingua sciolta* ‘but more at pain had the tongue loosened’ → “but uttered louder cries of pain”: the image of having a loosened tongue becomes an explicit description of crying; “more” becomes “louder,” and pain is expressed as the source of the cries.

> 28 Sopra tutto il sabbion d'un cader lento  
> 29 piovean di foco dilatate falde,  
> 30 come di neve in alpe senza vento.  

Over all the great sand, falling slowly, rained dilated flakes of fire, like *those* of snow in Alps without a wind.

- *tutto il sabbion* ‘all the sand’ → “all the great sand”: “great” is supplied, drawing out the expansive force of *sabbion*.
- *d'un cader lento* ‘with a slow falling’ → “falling slowly”: the prepositional phrase containing a substantival infinitive becomes a participial phrase, and the adjective becomes an adverb.
- *piovean di foco dilatate falde* ‘rained of fire dilated flakes’ → “rained dilated flakes of fire”: “of fire” moves after the noun it modifies.
- *come di neve* ‘like of snow’ → “like *those* of snow”: “those” supplies the comparison’s omitted reference to flakes.
- *in alpe* ‘in mountain country’ → “in Alps”: the singular *alpe* is interpreted as naming the Alps rather than mountain terrain generally.
- *senza vento* ‘without wind’ → “without a wind”: the indefinite article is supplied.

> 31 Quali Alessandro in quelle parti calde  
> 32 d' India vide sopra lo suo stuolo  
> 33 fiamme cadere infino a terra salde;  

As the flames which Alexander, in those hot regions of India, saw fall upon his host, entire to the ground;

- *Quali ... fiamme* ‘such as ... flames’ → “As the flames which”: the comparative construction becomes one with an explicit relative pronoun; “flames” moves to the beginning, and “the” is supplied.
- *in quelle parti calde d' India* ‘in those regions hot of India’ → “in those hot regions of India”: the adjective moves before “regions.”
- *vide sopra lo suo stuolo ... cadere* ‘saw above his host ... fall’ → “saw fall upon his host”: the infinitive moves immediately after “saw,” and the phrase locating the flames above the host becomes a phrase expressing their fall upon it.
- *infino a terra salde* ‘as far as the ground whole’ → “entire to the ground”: the adjective moves before the phrase expressing the endpoint; “to” does not separately express the emphasis of *infino*.

> 34 per ch'ei provvide a scalpitar lo suolo  
> 35 con le sue schiere, per ciò che il vapore  
> 36 me' si stingueva mentre ch'era solo:  

whereat he with his legions took care to tramp the soil, for the fire was more easily extinguished while alone:

- *per ch'* ‘for which reason’ → “whereat”: the explicitly causal phrase becomes a relative adverb indicating the occasion for the action.
- *provvide a* ‘made provision to’ → “took care to”: provision is expressed through an English idiom of attending to an action.
- *con le sue schiere* ‘with his troops’ → “with his legions”: “legions” is more specific than *schiere*, and the phrase moves from after the action to before “took care.”
- *per ciò che* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *il vapore* ‘the vapour’ → “the fire”: the translation interprets the word as the fiery substance rather than retaining its literal designation.
- *me' si stingueva* ‘better was extinguished’ → “was more easily extinguished”: ‘better’ is interpreted as greater ease, and the adverbial expression follows the auxiliary rather than preceding the verb.
- *mentre ch'era solo* ‘while it was alone’ → “while alone”: the finite clause is reduced by omitting its subject and copula.

> 37 tale scendeva l’ eternale ardore;  
> 38 onde l'arena s'accendea, com'esca  
> 39 sotto focile, a doppiar lo dolore.  

so fell the eternal heat, by which the sand was kindled, like *tinder* under *flint* and steel, redoubling the pain.

- *tale* ‘such’ → “so”: the comparative adjective becomes an adverb.
- *scendeva* ‘descended’ → “fell”: the general downward movement is expressed specifically as falling.
- *onde* ‘whence’ → “by which”: the connective expressing consequence becomes a relative phrase explicitly identifying the preceding heat as the cause.
- *s'accendea* ‘caught fire’ → “was kindled”: the pronominal verb expressing ignition becomes a passive construction.
- *sotto focile* ‘under a fire-steel’ → “under *flint* and steel”: the single implement becomes a pair, with “flint” supplied.
- *a doppiar lo dolore* ‘to double the pain’ → “redoubling the pain”: the infinitival construction becomes a participial phrase, and ‘double’ becomes “redoubling.”

> 40 Senza riposo mai era la tresca  
> 41 delle misere mani, or quindi or quinci  
> 42 iscotendo da sè l'arsura fresca.  

Ever restless was the dance of miserable hands, now here, now there, shaking off the fresh burning.

- *Senza riposo mai* ‘without rest ever’ → “Ever restless”: the prepositional expression becomes an adjective, and the temporal adverb moves to the front.
- *delle misere mani* ‘of the miserable hands’ → “of miserable hands”: the definite article is omitted.
- *or quindi or quinci* ‘now from there now from here’ → “now here, now there”: the order of the two locations is reversed, and their directional force is omitted.
- *iscotendo da sè* ‘shaking from themselves’ → “shaking off”: the phrase referring back to the sufferers becomes the particle “off.”
- *l'arsura fresca* ‘the burning fresh’ → “the fresh burning”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 43 Io cominciai: “Maestro tu che vinci  
> 44 tutte le cose, fuor che i demon duri,  
> 45 che all' entrar della porta incontro uscinci,  

I began: “Master, thou who conquerest all things, save the hard Demons, that came forth against us at the entrance of the gate,

- *tutte le cose* ‘all the things’ → “all things”: the definite article is omitted.
- *i demon duri* ‘the demons hard’ → “the hard Demons”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *all' entrar della porta* ‘at the entering of the gate’ → “at the entrance of the gate”: the substantival infinitive denoting entry becomes a noun that can denote the place of entry, and the phrase moves after the verb and “against us.”
- *incontro uscinci* ‘against us came out’ → “came forth against us”: the verb precedes the expression of opposition, and the attached pronoun *ci* becomes the separate object of “against.”

> 46 chi è quel grande che non par che curi  
> 47 l'incendio, e giace dispettoso e torto  
> 48 sì che la pioggia non par che il maturi?”  

who is that great spirit, who seems to care not for the fire, and lies disdainful and contorted, so that the rain seems not to ripen him?”

- *quel grande* ‘that great one’ → “that great spirit”: “spirit” supplies a noun for the substantival adjective.
- *che non par che curi* ‘who does not seem that he care’ → “who seems to care not”: the finite clause introduced by the second *che*, with subjunctive *curi*, becomes an infinitive; the negation moves from “seems” to “care.”
- *la pioggia non par che il maturi* ‘the rain does not seem that it ripen him’ → “the rain seems not to ripen him”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an infinitive, the negation moves after “seems,” and the object pronoun follows “ripen” rather than preceding the verb.

> 49 E quel medesmo, che si fue accorto  
> 50 ch'io domandava il mio duca di lui,  
> 51 gridò: “Qual io fui vivo, tal son morto.  

And he himself, remarking that I asked my Guide concerning him, exclaimed: “What I was living, that am I dead.

- *quel medesmo* ‘that same one’ → “he himself”: the demonstrative expression becomes a personal pronoun with an intensifying reflexive.
- *che si fue accorto* ‘who had noticed’ → “remarking”: the finite relative clause in the pluperfect becomes a present participle, leaving the prior timing of the recognition unexpressed.
- *domandava* ‘was asking’ → “asked”: the imperfect’s presentation of an ongoing action becomes an English simple past.
- *il mio duca* ‘my leader’ → “my Guide”: leadership is rendered specifically as guidance.
- *gridò* ‘shouted’ → “exclaimed”: the verb expresses emphatic utterance rather than explicitly specifying a raised voice.
- *Qual io fui vivo, tal son morto* ‘such as I was alive, such I am dead’ → “What I was living, that am I dead”: the comparison becomes a “What”–“that” construction; English supplies the second subject and places it after “am.”

## Lines 52–84 — Capaneus’s Defiance and the Red Rivulet

> 52 Se Giove stanchi il suo fabbro da cui  
> 53 crucciato prese la folgore acuta,  
> 54 onde l'ultimo dì percosso fui,  

Though Jove weary out his smith, from whom in anger he took the sharp bolt with which on my last day I was transfixed;

- *Se* ‘If’ → “Though”: the condition is rendered as a concession.
- *crucciato* ‘angered’ → “in anger”: an adjective describing the subject becomes an adverbial phrase.
- *la folgore acuta* ‘the bolt sharp’ → “the sharp bolt”: the adjective precedes its noun.
- *l'ultimo dì* ‘the last day’ → “on my last day”: the temporal noun phrase receives a preposition, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *percosso fui* ‘struck I was’ → “I was transfixed”: the auxiliary precedes the participle, and striking becomes the more specific piercing through.

> 55 o s' egli stanchi gli altri a muta a muta  
> 56 in Mongibello alla fucina negra,  
> 57 chiamando : ‘ Buon Vulcano, aiuta aiuta,’  
> 58 sì com'ei fece alla pugna di Flegra,  

and though he weary out the others, one by one, at the black forge in Mongibello, crying: ‘Help, help, good Vulcan!’ as he did at the strife of Phlegra;

- *o s' egli* ‘or if he’ → “and though he”: alternative conditions become coordinated concessions.
- *a muta a muta* ‘shift by shift’ → “one by one”: successive groups or shifts become successive individuals.
- *in Mongibello alla fucina negra* ‘in Mongibello at the forge black’ → “at the black forge in Mongibello”: the two place phrases exchange positions, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *chiamando* ‘calling’ → “crying”: the translation makes the manner of calling more emphatic.
- *Buon Vulcano, aiuta aiuta* ‘Good Vulcan, help help’ → “Help, help, good Vulcan!”: the address follows rather than precedes the imperatives.
- *sì com'ei fece* ‘just as he did’ → “as he did”: the reinforcing *sì* is not separately expressed.
- *alla pugna* ‘at the battle’ → “at the strife”: the specific term for combat becomes the broader “strife”.

> 59 e me saetti di tutta sua forza,  
> 60 non ne potrebbe aver vendetta allegra.”  

and hurl at me with all his might, yet should he not thereby have joyful vengeance.”

- *e me saetti* ‘and shoot arrows at me’ → “and hurl at me”: the verb’s reference to shooting is replaced by hurling, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *non ne potrebbe aver vendetta allegra* ‘not from it could he have vengeance joyful’ → “yet should he not thereby have joyful vengeance”: “yet” is supplied; the explicit possibility expressed by *potrebbe* becomes conditional “should”; *ne* becomes the adverb “thereby”; and the negative, pronoun, and adjective are reordered.

> 61 Allora il duca mio parlò di forza  
> 62 tanto ch'io non l' avea sì forte udito :  

Then my Guide spake with a force such as I had not heard before:

- *il duca mio* ‘my leader’ → “my Guide”: leadership is rendered as guidance, and the postposed possessive moves before the noun.
- *di forza tanto ch'io non l' avea sì forte udito* ‘with force so much that I had not heard him so loudly’ → “with a force such as I had not heard before”: a degree-and-result construction becomes a comparison describing “a force”. The object *l'* (‘him’) and the repeated description *sì forte* are not separately retained; “before” makes the temporal comparison explicit.

> 63 “ O Capaneo, in ciò che non s' ammorza  
> 64 la tua superbia, se' tu più punito :  

“O Capaneus! in that thy pride remains unquenched, thou art punished more:

- *non s' ammorza la tua superbia* ‘is not quenched thy pride’ → “thy pride remains unquenched”: the subject moves before the verb, and a negative verbal construction becomes “remains” with a negative adjective, explicitly emphasizing continuation.
- *se' tu più punito* ‘art thou more punished’ → “thou art punished more”: the subject precedes the verb, and the degree adverb follows the participle.

> 65 nullo martirio, fuor che la tua rabbia,  
> 66 sarebbe al tuo furor dolor compito.”  

no torture, except thy own raving, would be pain proportioned to thy fury.”

- *la tua rabbia* ‘thy rage’ → “thy own raving”: “own” is supplied, and rage is rendered as its expression in raving.
- *sarebbe al tuo furor dolor compito* ‘would be to thy fury complete pain’ → “would be pain proportioned to thy fury”: *compito* is interpreted as fully adequate or commensurate, rather than simply complete. The phrase referring to the fury moves after “pain” and becomes the complement of “proportioned”.

> 67 Poi si rivolse a me con miglior labbia,  

Then to me he turned with gentler lip,

- *si rivolse a me* ‘he turned to me’ → “to me he turned”: the phrase identifying the person addressed moves before the subject and verb.
- *con miglior labbia* ‘with better countenance’ → “with gentler lip”: ‘better’ is specified as gentler, and the countenance or expression is represented by the lip.

> 68 dicendo : “Quel fu l' un de' sette regi  
> 69 ch' assiser Tebe ; ed ebbe, e par ch' egli abbia,  
> 70 Dio in disdegno, e poco par che il pregi ;  

saying: “That was the one of the seven kings who laid siege to Thebes; and he held, and seems to hold, God in defiance and prize him lightly;

- *ch' assiser Tebe* ‘who besieged Thebes’ → “who laid siege to Thebes”: a single transitive verb becomes a verbal phrase with a prepositional complement.
- *ed ebbe, e par ch' egli abbia, Dio in disdegno* ‘and he had, and it seems that he has, God in disdain’ → “and he held, and seems to hold, God in defiance”: having in disdain becomes holding in defiance. The impersonal *par* with a finite subjunctive clause becomes personal “seems” with an infinitive.
- *e poco par che il pregi* ‘and little it seems that he prizes him’ → “and prize him lightly”: the repeated *par che* is not separately translated; “prize” instead shares the preceding infinitival construction. *Poco* becomes “lightly” and moves after the verb and object, while the object pronoun also moves after the verb.

> 71 ma, come io dissi lui, li suoi dispetti  
> 72 sono al suo petto assai debiti fregi.  

but, as I told him, his revilings are ornaments that well befit his breast.

- *come io dissi lui* ‘as I said to him’ → “as I told him”: saying to someone becomes telling someone, without a preposition.
- *li suoi dispetti* ‘his expressions of contempt’ → “his revilings”: the contempt is rendered specifically as abusive speech.
- *sono al suo petto assai debiti fregi* ‘are to his breast very due ornaments’ → “are ornaments that well befit his breast”: the noun moves before its description, and the adjective *debiti* becomes the verb of a relative clause. *Assai* becomes “well” rather than ‘very’, and the breast becomes the object of “befit”.

> 73 Or mi vien dietro, e guarda che non metti  
> 74 ancor li piedi nell' arena arsiccia,  
> 75 ma sempre al bosco li ritieni stretti.”  

Now follow me, and see thou place not yet thy feet upon the burning sand; but always keep them back close to the wood.”

- *mi vien dietro* ‘come behind me’ → “follow me”: the verb and directional adverb become a single transitive verb, with the pronoun following it.
- *e guarda che non metti ancor* ‘and see that thou dost not yet put’ → “and see thou place not yet”: the conjunction *che* is omitted, and the Italian present indicative becomes an uninflected English subjunctive. The verb precedes rather than follows the negative and temporal adverb.
- *li piedi* ‘the feet’ → “thy feet”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *nell' arena arsiccia* ‘in the scorched sand’ → “upon the burning sand”: the preposition specifies the surface, “burning” replaces the description of something scorched, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *al bosco li ritieni stretti* ‘to the wood keep them close’ → “keep them back close to the wood”: the place phrase moves to the end, while “keep them back” brings out the restraining sense of *ritieni*.

> 76 Tacendo divenimmo là ove spiccia  
> 77 fuor della selva un picciol fiumicello,  
> 78 lo cui rossore ancor mi raccapriccia.  

In silence we came to where there gushes forth from the wood a little rivulet, the redness of which still makes me shudder.

- *Tacendo* ‘being silent’ → “In silence”: the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *divenimmo là ove* ‘we came there where’ → “we came to where”: the demonstrative place adverb is replaced by a directional preposition.
- *spiccia fuor della selva* ‘gushes out of the wood’ → “there gushes forth from the wood”: introductory “there” is supplied, and the outward movement is distributed between “forth” and “from”.
- *lo cui rossore* ‘whose redness’ → “the redness of which”: the possessive relative construction becomes a noun followed by an *of*-phrase.

> 79 Quale del Bulicame esce un ruscello,  
> 80 che parton poi tra lor le peccatrici,  
> 81 tal per l' arena giù sen giva quello.  

As from the Bulicame issues [a] streamlet, which the sinful women share amongst themselves: so this ran down across the sand.

- *che parton poi tra lor le peccatrici* ‘which then divide among themselves the female sinners’ → “which the sinful women share amongst themselves”: the subject moves before the verb; the feminine noun becomes an adjective and noun; “share” replaces ‘divide’; and *poi* (‘then’) is omitted.
- *per l' arena* ‘through the sand’ → “across the sand”: the preposition explicitly describes traversal, and the phrase moves after the verb and “down”.
- *giù sen giva quello* ‘down went on its way that one’ → “this ran down”: the subject moves before the verb and changes from ‘that one’ to “this”. Going becomes running, the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the pronominal *se ne* is not separately expressed.

> 82 Lo fondo suo ed ambo le pendici  
> 83 fatt' eran pietra, e i margini da lato :  
> 84 per ch'io m' accorsi che il passo era lici.  

Its bottom and both its shelving banks were petrified, and also the margins near it: whereby I discerned that our passage lay there.

- *Lo fondo suo* ‘the bottom its’ → “Its bottom”: the postposed possessive moves before the noun.
- *ed ambo le pendici* ‘and both the slopes’ → “and both its shelving banks”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the slopes are explicitly identified as banks, with “shelving” retaining their sloping character.
- *fatt' eran pietra* ‘made were stone’ → “were petrified”: the auxiliary moves before the participle, and the construction with the predicate noun ‘stone’ becomes a single passive verb.
- *e i margini da lato* ‘and the margins at the side’ → “and also the margins near it”: “also” is supplied, and position at the side becomes proximity to an explicitly supplied “it”.
- *che il passo era lici* ‘that the passage was there’ → “that our passage lay there”: the definite article becomes “our”, and the verb of being becomes the more specifically locative “lay”.

## Lines 85–117 — The Source of the Infernal Rivers

> 85 “Tra tutto l’ altro ch’io t’ho dimostrato,  
> 86 poscia che noi entrammo per la porta,  
> 87 lo cui sogliare a nessuno è negato,  

“Amidst all the rest that I have shown thee, since we entered by the gate whose threshold is denied to none,

- *tutto l’ altro* ‘all the other things’ → “all the rest”: the substantival *altro* is rendered by “the rest”.
- *ch’io t’ho dimostrato* ‘that I to thee have shown’ → “that I have shown thee”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *per la porta* ‘through the gate’ → “by the gate”: “by” replaces the preposition expressing passage through.
- *lo cui sogliare a nessuno è negato* ‘whose threshold to none is denied’ → “whose threshold is denied to none”: the indirect object moves after the passive verb.

> 88 cosa non fu dagli tuoi occhi scorta  
> 89 notabil come lo presente rio,  
> 90 che sopra sè tutte fiammelle ammorta.”  

thy eyes have discerned nothing so notable as the present stream, which quenches all the flames above it.”

- *cosa non fu dagli tuoi occhi scorta* ‘a thing was not by thy eyes discerned’ → “thy eyes have discerned nothing”: the passive becomes active, the simple past becomes present perfect, and the noun and verbal negation become “nothing”.
- *notabil come* ‘notable as’ → “so notable as”: “so” is supplied to complete the English comparison.
- *sopra sè* ‘above itself’ → “above it”: the reflexive becomes a personal pronoun, and the phrase moves to the end of the clause.
- *tutte fiammelle ammorta* ‘all little flames quenches’ → “quenches all the flames”: the verb moves before its object, an article is supplied, and the diminutive force of *fiammelle* is not retained.

> 91 Queste parole fur del duca mio :  

These were words of my Guide:

- *Queste parole fur* ‘These words were’ → “These were words”: “These” becomes an independent pronoun, while “words” moves into the predicate.
- *del duca mio* ‘of the leader my’ → “of my Guide”: *duca* is rendered as “Guide”, and the possessive moves before the noun.

> 92 per che il pregai che mi largisse il pasto  
> 93 di cui largito m’aveva il disio.  

wherefore I prayed him to bestow on me the food, for which he had bestowed the appetite.

- *per che* ‘for which’ → “wherefore”: the connective phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *il pregai* ‘him I prayed’ → “I prayed him”: the object moves after the verb.
- *che mi largisse il pasto* ‘that he should bestow on me the food’ → “to bestow on me the food”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an infinitive construction.
- *di cui largito m’aveva il disio* ‘of which bestowed on me he had the desire’ → “for which he had bestowed the appetite”: “for” replaces ‘of’, “appetite” replaces ‘desire’, and the auxiliary precedes the participle; *m’* has no expressed equivalent.

> 94 “In mezzo mar siede un paese guasto,”  
> 95 diss’ egli allora, “che s’ appella Creta,  
> 96 sotto il cui rege fu già il mondo casto.  

“In the middle of the sea lies a waste country,” he then said, “which is named Crete, under whose King the world once was chaste.

- *In mezzo mar* ‘In mid-sea’ → “In the middle of the sea”: the compact expression is expanded into a noun phrase with articles and “of”.
- *siede* ‘sits’ → “lies”: the verb of position is changed.
- *un paese guasto* ‘a country waste’ → “a waste country”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *diss’ egli allora* ‘said he then’ → “he then said”: both the subject and the adverb move before the verb.
- *che s’ appella* ‘which calls itself’ → “which is named”: the pronominal construction becomes an English passive.
- *fu già il mondo casto* ‘was once the world chaste’ → “the world once was chaste”: the subject moves before the verb, and the adverb now precedes it.

> 97 Una montagna v’è, che già fu lieta  
> 98 d’ acque e di fronde, che si chiamò Ida;  
> 99 ora è diserta come cosa vieta.  

A mountain is there, called Ida, which once was glad with waters and with foliage; now it is deserted like an antiquated thing.

- *Una montagna v’è* ‘A mountain there is’ → “A mountain is there”: the locative follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *d’ acque e di fronde* ‘of waters and of leaves’ → “with waters and with foliage”: “with” replaces both instances of *di*, and plural *fronde* becomes the collective singular “foliage”.
- *che si chiamò Ida* ‘which was called Ida’ → “called Ida”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase and moves before the preceding relative clause.
- *come cosa vieta* ‘like a thing worn-out’ → “like an antiquated thing”: an indefinite article is supplied, the adjective moves before the noun, and “antiquated” emphasizes age or obsolescence rather than deterioration.

> 100 Rea la scelse già per cuna fida  
> 101 del suo figliuolo; e, per celarlo meglio,  
> 102 quando piangea, vi facea far le grida.  

Rhea of old chose it for the faithful cradle of her son; and the better to conceal him, when he wept, caused cries to be made on it.

- *Rea la scelse già* ‘Rhea it chose once’ → “Rhea of old chose it”: the object moves after the verb, while *già* becomes “of old” and moves before it.
- *per cuna fida* ‘for a cradle faithful’ → “for the faithful cradle”: the English supplies a definite article, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *per celarlo meglio* ‘to conceal him better’ → “the better to conceal him”: the comparative adverb moves before the infinitive and gains the idiomatic “the”.
- *vi facea far le grida* ‘there she caused someone to make the cries’ → “caused cries to be made on it”: the inner active infinitive becomes passive, the article before *grida* disappears, and the locative *vi* becomes the more explicit “on it” at the end.

> 103 Dentro dal monte sta dritto un gran veglio,  
> 104 che tien volte le spalle inver Damiata,  
> 105 e Roma guata sì come suo speglio.  

Within the mountain stands erect a great Old Man, who keeps his shoulders turned towards Damietta, and looks at Rome as if it were his mirror.

- *tien volte le spalle* ‘keeps turned the shoulders’ → “keeps his shoulders turned”: the participle moves after the object, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *e Roma guata* ‘and Rome regards’ → “and looks at Rome”: the object moves after the verb, and the transitive verb becomes “looks” with the preposition “at”.
- *sì come suo speglio* ‘just as his mirror’ → “as if it were his mirror”: the comparison becomes an explicit hypothetical clause; *sì* has no separate equivalent.

> 106 La sua testa è di fin’ oro formata,  

His head is shapen of fine gold,

- *di fin’ oro formata* ‘of fine gold formed’ → “shapen of fine gold”: the participle moves before the phrase expressing material.

> 107 e puro argento son le braccia e il petto,  

his arms and his breast are pure silver;

- *e puro argento son le braccia e il petto* ‘and pure silver are the arms and the breast’ → “his arms and his breast are pure silver”: the opening conjunction is omitted, the subject and predicate exchange positions, and possessives replace the articles before the body parts.

> 108 poi è di rame infino alla forcata;  

then he is of brass to the cleft;

- *rame* ‘copper’ → “brass”: a different metal is named.
- *infino alla forcata* ‘as far as the fork’ → “to the cleft”: the expression of extent is shortened, and “cleft” replaces ‘fork’.

> 109 da indi in giuso è tutto ferro eletto,  
> 110 salvo che il destro piede è terra cotta,  
> 111 e sta in su quel, più che in su l’ altro, eretto.  

from thence downwards he is all of chosen iron, save that the right foot is of baked clay; and he rests more on this than on the other.

- *tutto ferro eletto* ‘all chosen iron’ → “all of chosen iron”: “of” is supplied, making the material a prepositional complement.
- *è terra cotta* ‘is baked earth’ → “is of baked clay”: “of” is supplied, and *terra* is rendered more specifically as “clay”.
- *e sta in su quel, più che in su l’ altro, eretto* ‘and stands on that, more than on the other, upright’ → “and he rests more on this than on the other”: “rests” replaces the expression ‘stands upright’, leaving uprightness unexpressed; “this” replaces ‘that’, and “more” moves before the first prepositional phrase.

> 112 Ciascuna parte, fuor che l’ oro, è rotta  
> 113 d’ una fessura che lagrime goccia,  
> 114 le quali accolte foran quella grotta.  

Every part, except the gold, is broken with a fissure that drops tears, which collected perforate that grotto.

- *è rotta d’ una fessura* ‘is broken by a fissure’ → “is broken with a fissure”: “with” replaces the preposition indicating what causes the break.
- *che lagrime goccia* ‘that tears drops’ → “that drops tears”: the verb moves before its object.

> 115 Lor corso in questa valle si diroccia;  

Their course descends from rock to rock into this valley;

- *in questa valle si diroccia* ‘into this valley tumbles down’ → “descends from rock to rock into this valley”: the pronominal verb is expanded into a verb and an explicit description of movement between rocks; the destination moves after the verb.

> 116 fanno Acheronte, Stige e Flegetonta;  

they form Acheron, Styx, and Phlegethon,

- Literal.

> 117 poi sen van giù per questa stretta doccia  

then, by this narrow conduit, go down

- *poi sen van giù per questa stretta doccia* ‘then they go away down through this narrow channel’ → “then, by this narrow conduit, go down”: the route phrase moves before the verb, “by” replaces ‘through’, and *doccia* is rendered as “conduit”; the departure expressed by *ne* in *sen van* has no separate equivalent.

## Lines 118–142 — The Rivers and the Unburnt Path

> 118 infin là dove più non si dismonta;  
> 119 fanno Cocito; e qual sia quello stagno,  
> 120 tu il vederai: però qui non si conta.”  

to where there is no more descent; they form Cocytus, and thou shalt see what kind of lake that is: here therefore I describe it not.”

- *infin là dove più non si dismonta* ‘as far as there where one no longer descends’ → “to where there is no more descent”: the expression of extent is compressed, and the impersonal verb becomes a construction with “there is” and the noun “descent”.
- *e qual sia quello stagno, tu il vederai* ‘and of what sort that pond may be, thou shalt see it’ → “and thou shalt see what kind of lake that is”: the indirect question moves after the main verb, and the resumptive *il* disappears; the subjunctive becomes indicative, and *stagno* is rendered as “lake”.
- *però qui non si conta* ‘therefore here it is not recounted’ → “here therefore I describe it not”: the adverbs change order; the impersonal construction becomes first-person active, and recounting becomes describing.

> 121 Ed io a lui: “Se il presente rigagno  
> 122 si deriva così dal nostro mondo,  
> 123 perchè ci appar pure a questo vivagno?”  

And I to him: “If the present rill thus flows down from our world, why does it appear to us only on this bank?”

- *si deriva così dal nostro mondo* ‘is derived thus from our world’ → “thus flows down from our world”: derivation becomes downward flowing, the passive construction becomes intransitive, and “thus” moves before the verb.
- *ci appar* ‘to us it appears’ → “does it appear to us”: English supplies the interrogative auxiliary and places the indirect object after the verb.
- *a questo vivagno* ‘at this edge’ → “on this bank”: the preposition changes, and the general word for an edge is rendered specifically as “bank”.

> 124 Ed egli a me: “Tu sai che il loco è tondo,  

And he to me: “Thou knowest that the place is round;

- Literal.

> 125 e, tutto che tu sii venuto molto  
> 126 pur a sinistra giù calando al fondo,  
> 127 non se’ ancor per tutto il cerchio volto:  

and though thou hast come far, always to the left, descending towards the bottom, thou hast not yet turned through the entire circle:

- *tutto che tu sii venuto molto* ‘although thou be come much’ → “though thou hast come far”: the perfect subjunctive becomes indicative, the perfect auxiliary changes from ‘be’ to “hast”, and *molto* is expressed as distance.
- *giù calando al fondo* ‘down descending to the bottom’ → “descending towards the bottom”: *giù* is not separately rendered, and “towards” replaces the preposition indicating a destination.
- *non se’ ancor per tutto il cerchio volto* ‘thou art not yet through all the circle turned’ → “thou hast not yet turned through the entire circle”: the perfect auxiliary changes from ‘be’ to “hast”, the participle moves before the prepositional phrase, and “the entire circle” replaces ‘all the circle’.

> 128 per che, se cosa n’ apparisce nuova,  
> 129 non dee addur maraviglia al tuo volto.”  

wherefore if aught new appears to us, it ought not to bring wonder on thy countenance.”

- *per che* ‘for which’ → “wherefore”: the prepositional relative expression becomes a single connective adverb.
- *se cosa n’ apparisce nuova* ‘if a thing to us appears new’ → “if aught new appears to us”: *cosa* becomes the indefinite pronoun “aught”; “new” is attached directly to that pronoun rather than following the verb, and “to us” moves after the verb.
- *al tuo volto* ‘to thy face’ → “on thy countenance”: the preposition changes, and “countenance” replaces ‘face’.

> 130 Ed io ancor: “Maestro, ove si trova  
> 131 Flegetonte e Letè: chè dell’ un taci,  
> 132 e l’ altro di’ che si fa d’ esta piova?”  

And I again: “Master, where is Phlegethon and Lethe found: for thou speakest not of the one, and sayest the other is formed by this rain?”

- *ove si trova Flegetonte e Letè* ‘where is found Phlegethon and Lethe’ → “where is Phlegethon and Lethe found”: the Italian *si*-construction becomes an explicit English passive, with the subject placed between auxiliary and participle.
- *dell’ un taci* ‘of the one thou art silent’ → “thou speakest not of the one”: silence becomes negated speaking, and the prepositional phrase moves after the verb.
- *e l’ altro di’ che si fa d’ esta piova* ‘and the other thou sayest that it is made from this rain’ → “and sayest the other is formed by this rain”: the fronted topic becomes the subject of the following clause, *che* is omitted, and ‘made from’ becomes “formed by”.

> 133 “In tutte tue question certo mi piaci,”  
> 134 rispose; “ma il bollor dell’ acqua rossa  
> 135 dovea ben solver l’ una che tu faci.  

“In all thy questions truly thou pleasest me,” he answered; “but the boiling of the red water might well resolve one of those thou askest.

- *certo* ‘certainly’ → “truly”: an expression of certainty becomes one of truth or sincerity.
- *mi piaci* ‘me thou pleasest’ → “thou pleasest me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *dell’ acqua rossa* ‘of the water red’ → “of the red water”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *dovea ben solver* ‘ought indeed to have resolved’ → “might well resolve”: past obligation or expectation becomes a more tentative modal expression, without an explicit perfect infinitive.
- *l’ una che tu faci* ‘the one that thou makest’ → “one of those thou askest”: the definite expression becomes an explicit partitive, the relative pronoun is omitted, and making a question becomes asking one.

> 136 Letè vedrai, ma fuor di questa fossa,  
> 137 là ove vanno l’ anime a lavarsi,  
> 138 quando la colpa pentuta è rimossa.”  

Lethe thou shalt see, but out of this abyss, there where the spirits go to wash themselves, when their guilt is taken off by penitence.”

- *fossa* ‘pit’ → “abyss”: the translation uses a stronger term for depth.
- *vanno l’ anime* ‘go the souls’ → “the spirits go”: the subject moves before the verb, and “spirits” replaces ‘souls’.
- *la colpa pentuta è rimossa* ‘the repented guilt is removed’ → “their guilt is taken off by penitence”: the definite article becomes a possessive; the participle modifying guilt becomes a phrase expressing the means of removal, and ‘removed’ becomes “taken off”.

> 139 Poi disse: “Omai è tempo da scostarsi  
> 140 dal bosco; fa che di retro a me vegne;  

Then he said: “Now it is time to quit the wood; see that thou follow me;

- *da scostarsi dal bosco* ‘to move oneself away from the wood’ → “to quit the wood”: the pronominal verb and its prepositional complement become a transitive verb with a direct object.
- *fa che di retro a me vegne* ‘make that behind me thou come’ → “see that thou follow me”: the causative imperative becomes an injunction to ensure compliance, and coming behind someone becomes following them.

> 141 li margini fan via, che non son arsi,  
> 142 e sopra loro ogni vapor si spegne.”  

the margins which are not burning, form a path and over them all fire is quenched.”

- *li margini fan via, che non son arsi* ‘the margins make a way, which are not burnt’ → “the margins which are not burning, form a path”: the relative clause moves immediately after its antecedent; ‘make a way’ becomes “form a path”, and the passive or resultant state ‘are not burnt’ becomes the active progressive “are not burning”.
- *ogni vapor* ‘every vapour’ → “all fire”: the distributive expression becomes a mass expression, and *vapor* is interpreted as fire rather than rendered by its direct lexical equivalent.
- *si spegne* ‘extinguishes itself’ → “is quenched”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes an explicit English passive.

## Summary — Close Echoes and Grammatical Recasting

Carlyle’s translation stays close to the Italian’s sequence of statements and often preserves its imagery and unusual phrasing: *dilatate falde* becomes “dilated flakes,” *la tresca delle misere mani* becomes “the dance of miserable hands,” and *maturi* remains “ripen.” Yet word order regularly changes. Adjectives and possessives move before their nouns, objects follow their verbs, and subjects often precede them. More extensive rearrangement brings *di giustizia orribil arte* into the order “[a] fearful [device] of justice,” while the question introduced by *qual sia quello stagno* moves after “thou shalt see.” Elsewhere, inversions such as “so fell the eternal heat” retain something of the Italian arrangement.

Changes of construction frequently make the English less literal without greatly altering the sense. *Poichè … mi strinse* becomes the participial “constraining me,” and *che si fue accorto* becomes “remarking,” leaving the recognition’s prior timing unexpressed. Finite clauses after *par* become infinitives after “seems”; *fu manifesto* becomes the passive “was revealed”; and *cosa non fu dagli tuoi occhi scorta* becomes the active “thy eyes have discerned nothing.” Other recastings are more interpretive: *avea la lingua sciolta* is expressed as “uttered louder cries of pain,” while *la colpa pentuta* becomes “their guilt,” with repentance transferred to the phrase “by penitence.”

Word choice ranges from close lexical echoes to clarification, generalization, or shifts of emphasis. *Landa* becomes the more general “plain,” *schiere* the more specific “legions,” and *vapore* is interpreted as “fire.” *Rame* (‘copper’) is rendered as “brass.” Additions make implicit references explicit: *quel grande* becomes “that great spirit,” *ad essa* becomes “to the wood,” and articles before body parts sometimes become possessives. Conversely, small elements such as *ben*, *poi*, and reinforcing *sì* sometimes receive no separate equivalent, as do the diminutive force of *fiammelle* and the uprightness expressed by *eretto*. Connectives and modals can also shift the relation between clauses: *Se* becomes “Though,” *o* becomes “and,” and *dovea* is rendered with “might.”

Two pervasive grammatical adjustments lie behind the individual examples. English regularly supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the person through the verb ending, and drops Italian articles before possessives, as in *il mio duca* → “my Guide.” These routine accommodations coexist with deliberately close diction and retained repetitions, comparisons, and images. The result is neither a word-for-word transcription nor a free paraphrase: its chief departures reshape Italian syntax for English prose, while its more conspicuous lexical choices sometimes clarify, narrow, or redirect the original expression.
