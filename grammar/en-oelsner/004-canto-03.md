# Inferno 3

## Lines 1–21 — The Gate of Hell

> 1 “Per me si va nella città dolente;  
> 2 per me si va nell’ eterno dolore;  
> 3 per me si va tra la perduta gente.  

“Through me is the way into the doleful city ; through me the way into the eternal pain; through me the way among the people lost.

- *si va … si va … si va* ‘one goes … one goes … one goes’ → “is the way”, “the way”, “the way”: the repeated impersonal verb becomes a noun construction; “is” appears only in the first clause.
- *nella città dolente* ‘into the city doleful’ → “into the doleful city”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *tra la perduta gente* ‘among the lost people’ → “among the people lost”: the adjective moves after the noun.

> 4 Giustizia mosse il mio alto Fattore;  

Justice moved my High Maker;

- Literal.

> 5 fecemi la divina Potestate,  
> 6 la somma Sapienza e il primo Amore.  

Divine Power made me, Wisdom Supreme, and Primal Love.

- *fecemi la divina Potestate* ‘made me the divine Power’ → “Divine Power made me”: the first subject moves before the verb and its object, and its definite article is omitted.
- *la somma Sapienza* ‘the supreme Wisdom’ → “Wisdom Supreme”: the article is omitted, and the adjective moves after the noun.
- *e il primo Amore* ‘and the first Love’ → “and Primal Love”: the article is omitted, and the ordinal ‘first’ becomes “Primal”.

> 7 Dinanzi a me non fur cose create,  
> 8 se non eterne, ed io eterno duro:  

Before me were no things created, but eternal; and eternal I endure:

- *non fur cose create* ‘not were things created’ → “were no things created”: negation moves from the verb to the noun phrase.
- *se non* ‘except’ → “but”: the exceptive phrase is rendered by exceptive “but”.
- *ed io eterno duro* ‘and I eternal endure’ → “and eternal I endure”: “eternal” moves before the subject.

> 9 lasciate ogni speranza, voi ch’ entrate.”  

leave all hope, ye that enter.”

- *ogni speranza* ‘every hope’ → “all hope”: the distributive ‘every’ becomes the collective “all”.

> 10 Queste parole di colore oscuro  
> 11 vid’ io scritte al sommo d’ una porta;  

These words, of colour obscure, saw I written above a gate;

- *al sommo d’* ‘at the top of’ → “above”: the phrase specifying the gate’s uppermost part becomes a preposition indicating a position above it.

> 12 per ch’io: “Maestro, il senso lor m’è duro.”  

whereat I: “Master, their meaning to me is hard.”

- *per ch’* ‘for which reason’ → “whereat”: the connective expressing a reason becomes one marking the occasion for the response.
- *il senso lor* ‘the meaning of them’ → “their meaning”: the postposed possessive becomes a possessive determiner before the noun, replacing the definite article.

> 13 Ed egli a me, come persona accorta:  

And he to me, as one experienced:

- *come persona accorta* ‘as a discerning person’ → “as one experienced”: the noun ‘person’ becomes the pronoun “one”; ‘discerning’ is interpreted as “experienced”, and the adjective follows its pronoun.

> 14 “Qui si convien lasciare ogni sospetto;  
> 15 ogni viltà convien che qui sia morta.  

“Here must all distrust be left; all cowardice must here be dead.

- *Qui si convien lasciare ogni sospetto* ‘Here it is necessary to leave every suspicion’ → “Here must all distrust be left”: the impersonal expression of necessity with an active infinitive becomes a modal passive, making the original object the subject. ‘Every’ becomes “all”, and ‘suspicion’ is rendered as “distrust”.
- *ogni viltà convien che qui sia morta* ‘every cowardice—it is necessary that here it be dead’ → “all cowardice must here be dead”: the impersonal expression governing a subjunctive clause becomes a direct modal construction with “must”; ‘every’ becomes “all”.

> 16 Noi siam venuti al luogo ov’ io t’ ho detto  
> 17 che tu vedrai le genti dolorose,  
> 18 ch’ hanno perduto il ben dello intelletto.”  

We are come to the place where I told thee thou shouldst see the wretched people, who have lost the good of the intellect.”

- *io t’ ho detto* ‘I to thee have told’ → “I told thee”: the present perfect becomes a simple past, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *che tu vedrai* ‘that thou wilt see’ → “thou shouldst see”: the conjunction is omitted, and the future indicative becomes a future-in-the-past construction following “told”.
- *le genti dolorose* ‘the sorrowful peoples’ → “the wretched people”: the plural noun becomes the unmarked collective “people”; ‘sorrowful’ becomes “wretched”, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 19 E poichè la sua mano alla mia pose,  
> 20 con lieto volto, ond’ io mi confortai,  
> 21 mi mise dentro alle segrete cose.  

And placing his hand on mine, with a cheerful countenance that comforted me, he led me into the secret things.

- *E poichè la sua mano alla mia pose* ‘And after he placed his hand to mine’ → “And placing his hand on mine”: the finite temporal clause becomes a participial phrase, without an explicit equivalent of ‘after’. The verb moves before its objects, and ‘to mine’ becomes “on mine”.
- *con lieto volto* ‘with cheerful countenance’ → “with a cheerful countenance”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *ond’ io mi confortai* ‘whereby I comforted myself’ → “that comforted me”: the connective becomes a relative pronoun referring to the countenance; the reflexive construction becomes transitive, with “me” as the object rather than the subject.
- *mi mise dentro alle segrete cose* ‘me he put within the secret things’ → “he led me into the secret things”: ‘put’ becomes “led”, specifying guided movement; ‘within’ becomes directional “into”, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.

## Lines 22–54 — The Lament of the Uncommitted

> 22 Quivi sospiri, pianti, e alti guai  
> 23 risonavan per l' aer senza stelle,  
> 24 per ch' io al cominciar ne lagrimai.  

Here sighs, plaints, and deep wailings resounded through the starless air : it made me weep at first.

- *Quivi* ‘there’ → “Here”: the spatial demonstrative changes.
- *pianti* ‘weepings’ → “plaints”: weeping becomes vocal lamentation.
- *alti guai* ‘loud lamentations’ → “deep wailings”: loudness is rendered as depth.
- *l' aer senza stelle* ‘the air without stars’ → “the starless air”: the following prepositional phrase becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *per ch' io al cominciar ne lagrimai* ‘because of which I at the beginning wept at it’ → “it made me weep at first”: the relative construction expressing consequence becomes an independent causative clause; the speaker becomes the object of “made”; *ne* has no separate equivalent; and the temporal phrase moves to the end.

> 25 Diverse lingue, orribili favelle,  
> 26 parole di dolore, accenti d' ira,  
> 27 voci alte e fioche, e suon di man con elle,  

Strange tongues, horrible outcries, words of pain, tones of anger, voices deep and hoarse, and sounds of hands amongst them,

- *Diverse* ‘various’ → “Strange”: diversity becomes unfamiliarity.
- *favelle* ‘utterances’ → “outcries”: speech is rendered specifically as cries.
- *accenti* ‘accents’ → “tones”: the rendering emphasizes vocal tone.
- *alte e fioche* ‘loud and faint’ → “deep and hoarse”: loudness becomes depth, and *fioche* is interpreted as hoarseness rather than weakness of sound.
- *suon* ‘sound’ → “sounds”: the singular becomes plural.
- *con elle* ‘with them’ → “amongst them”: accompaniment becomes intermixture.

> 28 facevano un tumulto, il qual s' aggira  
> 29 sempre in quell' aria senza tempo tinta,  
> 30 come la rena quando a turbo spira.  

made a tumult, which turns itself unceasing in that air for ever dyed, as sand when [it eddies in a whirlwind].

- *sempre* ‘always’ → “unceasing”: the adverb becomes an adjective describing the continuing motion.
- *senza tempo* ‘without time’ → “for ever”: the expression is interpreted as perpetual duration.
- *la rena* ‘the sand’ → “sand”: the definite article is omitted.
- *quando a turbo spira* ‘when it blows in a whirl’ → “when [it eddies in a whirlwind]”: *spira* becomes the sand’s eddying movement rather than blowing, and *a turbo* is expressed as movement within a whirlwind.

> 31 Ed io, ch' avea d' orror la testa cinta,  
> 32 dissi: “ Maestro, che è quel ch' i' odo?  
> 33 e che gente è, che par nel duol sì vinta? ”  

And I, my head begirt with horror, said : “Master, what is this that I hear? and who are these that seem so overcome with pain?”

- *ch' avea d' orror la testa cinta* ‘who had with horror the head girt’ → “my head begirt with horror”: the relative clause becomes an absolute construction without a finite verb; the definite article becomes a possessive; and the phrase expressing horror follows the participle.
- *quel* ‘that’ → “this”: the demonstrative changes.
- *che gente è* ‘what people is it’ → “who are these”: the collective noun disappears, the question becomes directly personal, the verb becomes plural, and a plural demonstrative is supplied.
- *che par nel duol sì vinta* ‘that seems in sorrow so overcome’ → “that seem so overcome with pain”: singular agreement becomes plural; “with pain” replaces the phrase introduced by *nel* and moves after “overcome”.

> 34 Ed egli a me: “ Questo misero modo  
> 35 tengon l' anime triste di coloro,  
> 36 che visser senza infamia e senza lodo.  

And he to me : “This miserable mode the dreary souls of those sustain, who lived without blame, and without praise.

- *tengon* ‘keep’ → “sustain”: maintaining a mode becomes sustaining it, and the verb moves after its expanded subject.
- *l' anime triste* ‘the souls sad’ → “the dreary souls”: “dreary” replaces ‘sad’, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *infamia* ‘infamy’ → “blame”: the stronger term for dishonour becomes blame.

> 37 Mischiate sono a quel cattivo coro  
> 38 degli angeli che non furon ribelli,  
> 39 nè fur fedeli a Dio, ma per sè foro.  

They are mixed with that caitiff choir of the angels, who were not rebellious, nor were faithful to God; but were for themselves.

- *Mischiate sono* ‘mixed are’ → “They are mixed”: the participle moves after the finite verb, with an explicit subject preceding both.
- *cattivo* ‘bad’ → “caitiff”: the adjective is rendered by an archaic term suggesting baseness or cowardice.
- *ma per sè foro* ‘but for themselves were’ → “but were for themselves”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb.

> 40 Cacciarli i ciel per non esser men belli,  
> 41 nè lo profondo inferno gli riceve,  
> 42 chè alcuna gloria i rei avrebber d' elli.”  

Heaven chased them forth to keep its beauty from impair; and the deep Hell receives them not, for the wicked would have some glory over them.”

- *Cacciarli i ciel* ‘chased them out the heavens’ → “Heaven chased them forth”: the subject moves before the verb, and the plural heavens become singular “Heaven”.
- *per non esser men belli* ‘in order not to be less beautiful’ → “to keep its beauty from impair”: the negative comparative construction becomes one of preventing impairment; the adjective becomes the noun “beauty”, with a possessive supplied.
- *nè* ‘nor’ → “and” and “not”: the negative conjunction is divided into a positive conjunction and a separate negative following the verb and object.
- *gli riceve* ‘them receives’ → “receives them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes coordinating.
- *alcuna gloria i rei avrebber d' elli* ‘some glory the guilty would have from them’ → “the wicked would have some glory over them”: subject–verb–object order replaces object–subject–verb order; ‘the guilty’ becomes “the wicked”; and *d' elli* is interpreted comparatively as “over them”, rather than as a source.

> 43 Ed io: “ Maestro, che è tanto greve  
> 44 a lor, che lamentar gli fa sì forte? ”  

And I : “Master what is so grievous to them, that makes them lament thus bitterly?”

- *greve* ‘heavy’ → “grievous”: physical weight is rendered directly as distress.
- *che lamentar gli fa sì forte* ‘that to lament them makes so loudly’ → “that makes them lament thus bitterly”: the causative verb precedes its object and infinitive; *sì* becomes “thus”; and loud lamentation becomes bitter lamentation.

> 45 Rispose: “ Dicerolti molto breve.  

He answered : “I will tell it to thee very briefly.

- *molto breve* ‘very brief’ → “very briefly”: the adjectival form used adverbially becomes an explicit English adverb.

> 46 Questi non hanno speranza di morte,  
> 47 e la lor cieca vita è tanto bassa,  
> 48 che invidiosi son d' ogni altra sorte.  

These have no hope of death; and their blind life is so mean, that they are envious of every other lot.

- *non hanno speranza* ‘do not have hope’ → “have no hope”: verbal negation becomes the negative determiner “no”.
- *bassa* ‘low’ → “mean”: lowness is interpreted as abjectness.
- *che invidiosi son* ‘that envious they are’ → “that they are envious”: the predicate adjective moves after the verb.

> 49 Fama di loro il mondo esser non lassa,  

Report of them the world permits not to exist;

- *Fama* ‘fame’ → “Report”: renown is rendered as report or mention.
- *esser non lassa* ‘to be does not let’ → “permits not to exist”: the infinitive moves after the governing verb; ‘let’ becomes “permits”, with a to-infinitive; and ‘be’ is expressed explicitly as “exist”.

> 50 misericordia e giustizia gli sdegna :  

Mercy and [Justice] disdains them

- *gli sdegna* ‘them disdains’ → “disdains them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 51 non ragioniam di lor, ma guarda e passa.”  

: let us not speak of them; but look, and pass.”

- *non ragioniam di lor* ‘we do not discourse of them’ → “let us not speak of them”: the first-person plural is read exhortatively and expressed through “let us”; ‘discourse’ becomes the less specific “speak”.

> 52 Ed io, che riguardai, vidi una insegna,  
> 53 che girando correva tanto ratta,  
> 54 che d' ogni posa mi pareva indegna ;  

And I, who looked, saw an ensign, which whirling ran so quickly that it seemed to scorn all pause;

- *riguardai* ‘looked attentively’ → “looked”: the suggestion of attentive looking is not separately expressed.
- *girando* ‘turning’ → “whirling”: the movement is rendered more vigorously.
- *tanto ratta* ‘so swift’ → “so quickly”: the adjective agreeing with *insegna* becomes an adverb modifying “ran”.
- *che d' ogni posa mi pareva indegna* ‘that of every rest to me it seemed unworthy’ → “that it seemed to scorn all pause”: *indegna* is interpreted as disdainful rather than unworthy, and the adjective becomes the verbal phrase “to scorn”; the preceding prepositional phrase becomes a following direct object; ‘every rest’ becomes “all pause”; and *mi* is omitted.

## Lines 55–87 — The Wretched And Acheron’s Ferryman

> 55 e dietro le venia sì lunga tratta  
> 56 di gente, ch’io non avrei mai creduto,  
> 57 che morte tanta n’avesse disfatta.  

and behind it came so long a train of people, that I should never have believed death had undone so many.

- *venia* ‘was coming’ → “came”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past.
- *sì lunga tratta* ‘so long train’ → “so long a train”: English supplies the indefinite article, placing it after the adjective in this construction.
- *non avrei mai creduto* ‘would not ever have believed’ → “should never have believed”: “should” serves as the first-person conditional auxiliary, and the negative expression becomes “never”.
- *che morte tanta n’avesse disfatta* ‘that death so many of them had undone’ → “death had undone so many”: the introductory conjunction and the partitive pronoun *ne* are omitted; the object follows the verb, and the Italian pluperfect subjunctive becomes an English pluperfect without a subjunctive marking.

> 58 Poscia ch’io v’ebbi alcun riconosciuto,  

After I had recognised some amongst them,

- *v’* ‘there’ → “amongst them”: the locative pronoun is interpreted as referring to the people and is placed after the object in English.
- *ebbi alcun riconosciuto* ‘had someone recognised’ → “had recognised some”: the object moves after the participle, and the singular indefinite *alcun* is rendered by the plural “some”.

> 59 vidi e conobbi l’ombra di colui  
> 60 che fece per viltate il gran rifiuto.  

I [saw and knew] the shadow of him who from cowardice made the great refusal.

- *colui* ‘that one’ → “him”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *per viltate* ‘through cowardice’ → “from cowardice”: the causal phrase moves from after the verb to before it.

> 61 Incontanente intesi, e certo fui,  
> 62 che quest’era la setta dei cattivi,  
> 63 a Dio spiacenti ed a’ nemici sui.  

Forthwith I understood and felt assured, that this was the crew of caitiffs, hateful to God and to his enemies.

- *certo fui* ‘certain I was’ → “felt assured”: being certain is expressed as feeling assured, and the adjective following the verb replaces the Italian adjective preceding it.
- *la setta dei cattivi* ‘the sect of the base’ → “the crew of caitiffs”: “crew” replaces ‘sect’, and the substantivized adjective becomes the noun “caitiffs”.
- *a Dio spiacenti* ‘to God displeasing’ → “hateful to God”: the adjective precedes its complement, and “hateful” is stronger than ‘displeasing’.
- *a’ nemici sui* ‘to the enemies his’ → “to his enemies”: the possessive moves before the noun, with the article omitted.

> 64 Questi sciaurati, che mai non fur vivi,  
> 65 erano ignudi e stimolati molto  
> 66 da mosconi e da vespe ch’eran ivi.  

These unfortunate, who never were alive, were naked, and sorely goaded by hornets and by wasps that were there.

- *sciaurati* ‘wretches’ → “unfortunate”: a substantivized adjective expresses the less strongly condemnatory sense of being unfortunate.
- *mai non* ‘never not’ → “never”: the Italian negative concord is expressed by a single English negative.
- *stimolati molto* ‘goaded much’ → “sorely goaded”: the adverb precedes the participle, and “sorely” gives an explicitly painful colouring to the degree expressed by *molto*.
- *mosconi* ‘large flies’ → “hornets”: a different kind of insect is named.

> 67 Elle rigavan lor di sangue il volto,  
> 68 che mischiato di lagrime a’ lor piedi  
> 69 da fastidiosi vermi era ricolto.  

These made their faces stream with blood, which mixed with tears was gathered at their feet by loathsome worms.

- *Elle* ‘They’ → “These”: the personal pronoun becomes a demonstrative.
- *rigavan lor di sangue il volto* ‘streaked for them with blood the face’ → “made their faces stream with blood”: the verb becomes a causative construction; the indirect pronoun becomes a possessive, and the distributive singular ‘face’ becomes plural. The Italian imperfect is expressed by a simple past.
- *mischiato di lagrime* ‘mixed of tears’ → “mixed with tears”: English uses “with” for the substance mixed in.
- *a’ lor piedi da fastidiosi vermi era ricolto* ‘at their feet by loathsome worms was gathered’ → “was gathered at their feet by loathsome worms”: the passive verb moves before both prepositional phrases.

> 70 E poi che a riguardare oltre mi diedi,  
> 71 vidi gente alla riva d’un gran fiume;  

And then, as I looked onwards, I saw people on the Shore of a great River;

- *poi che* ‘after’ → “then, as”: the temporal conjunction becomes an adverb followed by a conjunction expressing simultaneous action.
- *a riguardare oltre mi diedi* ‘to looking farther myself I gave’ → “I looked onwards”: the reflexive construction expressing application to an activity becomes a simple verb of looking.
- *alla riva* ‘at the shore’ → “on the Shore”: the relation to the riverbank is expressed with “on” rather than ‘at’.

> 72 perch’io dissi: “Maestro, or mi concedi,  
> 73 ch’io sappia quali sono, e qual costume  
> 74 le fa parer di trapassar sì pronte,  
> 75 com’io discerno per lo fioco lume.”  

whereat I said: “Master, now grant that I may know who these are; and what usage makes them seem so ready to pass over, as I discern by the faint light.”

- *perch’* ‘for which reason’ → “whereat”: an explicit relation of reason or consequence becomes a reference to the occasion of speaking.
- *mi concedi, ch’io sappia* ‘grant me that I know’ → “grant that I may know”: the indirect object *mi* is omitted, and the subjunctive is expressed with “may”.
- *quali sono* ‘which they are’ → “who these are”: the question is explicitly about personal identity, and “these” gives demonstrative force to the unexpressed Italian subject.
- *le fa parer di trapassar sì pronte* ‘them makes seem to cross so ready’ → “makes them seem so ready to pass over”: the object pronoun follows the finite verb, and the infinitive of crossing moves after “ready”.
- *per lo fioco lume* ‘through the faint light’ → “by the faint light”: the light is presented as the means of discernment rather than as what one sees through.

> 76 Ed egli a me: “Le cose ti fien conte,  
> 77 quando noi fermerem li nostri passi  
> 78 sulla trista riviera d’Acheronte.”  

And he [to me]: “The things shall be [known to] thee, when we stay our steps upon the joyless strand of Acheron.”

- *ti fien conte* ‘to thee shall be known’ → “shall be [known to] thee”: the pronoun moves from before the verb to after the adjective and becomes the complement of an explicit preposition.
- *fermerem li nostri passi* ‘we shall halt our steps’ → “we stay our steps”: the future becomes a present tense, as normally required in an English temporal clause referring to the future.
- *trista riviera* ‘sad riverbank’ → “joyless strand”: “joyless” replaces ‘sad’, and the riverbank is termed a “strand”.

> 79 Allor con gli occhi vergognosi e bassi,  
> 80 temendo no ’l mio dir gli fusse grave,  
> 81 infino al fiume dal parlar mi trassi.  

Then, with eyes ashamed and downcast, fearing my words might have offended him, I kept myself from speaking till we reached the stream.

- *con gli occhi* ‘with the eyes’ → “with eyes”: the definite article is omitted.
- *bassi* ‘low’ → “downcast”: the adjective is made specific to the direction of the eyes.
- *temendo no ’l mio dir gli fusse grave* ‘fearing lest my speaking were burdensome to him’ → “fearing my words might have offended him”: the conjunction introducing the feared possibility is omitted; the substantivized infinitive becomes the plural noun “words”; and being burdensome becomes having offended. The imperfect subjunctive is rendered by a modal perfect, while the indirect object becomes the direct object of “offended”.
- *infino al fiume* ‘as far as the river’ → “till we reached the stream”: the phrase becomes a temporal clause with an added verb of arrival, moves to the end of the sentence, and uses “stream” for *fiume*.
- *dal parlar mi trassi* ‘from speaking myself I withdrew’ → “I kept myself from speaking”: withdrawal is rendered as continued restraint, and the reflexive pronoun and prepositional phrase move after the verb.

> 82 Ed ecco verso noi venir per nave  
> 83 un vecchio bianco per antico pelo,  
> 84 gridando: “Guai a voi, anime prave!  

And lo! an old man, white with ancient hair, comes towards us in a bark, shouting: “Woe to you, depraved spirits!

- *verso noi venir per nave* ‘towards us to come by boat’ → “comes towards us in a bark”: the infinitive dependent on *ecco* becomes a finite present verb; the verb and its complements follow the subject, and travel ‘by boat’ is expressed as being “in a bark”.
- *bianco per antico pelo* ‘white because of ancient hair’ → “white with ancient hair”: the causal preposition becomes “with”, expressing the feature responsible for the whiteness.
- *anime* ‘souls’ → “spirits”: a different noun is used.

> 85 non isperate mai veder lo cielo:  

hope not ever to see Heaven:

- *non isperate mai* ‘not hope ever’ → “hope not ever”: the negative moves from before the imperative to after it.
- *lo cielo* ‘the heaven’ → “Heaven”: the definite article is omitted.

> 86 i’ vegno per menarvi all’altra riva,  
> 87 nelle tenebre eterne, in caldo e in gelo.  

I come to lead you to the other shore; into the eternal darkness; into fire and into ice.

- *nelle tenebre eterne* ‘into the darknesses eternal’ → “into the eternal darkness”: the Italian plural becomes an English mass singular, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *in caldo e in gelo* ‘into heat and into freezing cold’ → “into fire and into ice”: conditions of temperature are rendered as the concrete substances associated with them.

## Lines 88–120 — Charon Gathers the Damned

> 88 E tu che sei costì, anima viva,  
> 89 partiti da cotesti, che son morti.”  

And thou who art there, alive, depart thee from these who are dead."

- *costì* ‘there where you are’ → “there”: the distinction locating the place near the person addressed is not expressed.
- *anima viva* ‘living soul’ → “alive”: the noun of direct address is omitted, and its adjective becomes a description of “thou”.
- *cotesti* ‘those near you’ → “these”: the demonstrative changes from reference to those near the person addressed to the English proximal form.

> 90 Ma poi ch’ei vide, ch’io non mi partiva,  
> 91 disse: “Per altra via, per altri porti  
> 92 verrai a piaggia, non qui, per passare:  
> 93 più lieve legno convien che ti porti.”  

But when he saw that I departed not, he said: "By other ways, by other ferries, not here, shalt thou pass over: a lighter boat must carry thee."

- *poi ch’ei vide* ‘after he saw’ → “when he saw”: the explicit temporal succession becomes a more general temporal connection.
- *non mi partiva* ‘was not departing’ → “departed not”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, the negative follows the verb, and the Italian pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive verb.
- *Per altra via* ‘by another way’ → “By other ways”: the singular becomes plural.
- *per altri porti* ‘by other ports’ → “by other ferries”: places of passage or embarkation are rendered as ferries.
- *verrai a piaggia, non qui, per passare* ‘you will come to a shore, not here, in order to cross’ → “not here, shalt thou pass over”: coming to a shore is omitted, the purpose infinitive becomes the principal future verb, and the negative phrase moves before it.
- *più lieve legno convien che ti porti* ‘it is necessary that lighter wood carry you’ → “a lighter boat must carry thee”: “boat” makes the metonymical meaning of *legno* explicit; an indefinite article is supplied; and the impersonal expression with a subjunctive clause becomes a personal construction with “must”. The object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 94 E il duca a lui: “Caron, non ti crucciare:  
> 95 vuolsi così colà, dove si puote  
> 96 ciò che si vuole; e più non dimandare.”  

And my guide to him: "Charon, vex not thyself: thus it is willed there, where what is willed can be done; and ask no more."

- *il duca* ‘the leader’ → “my guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and leadership is expressed specifically as guidance.
- *non ti crucciare* ‘do not vex yourself’ → “vex not thyself”: the negative and reflexive pronoun both follow the verb rather than preceding it.
- *vuolsi così* ‘it is willed thus’ → “thus it is willed”: the adverb moves before the verb.
- *dove si puote ciò che si vuole* ‘where one can [do] what one wills’ → “where what is willed can be done”: the impersonal constructions become passives, “done” makes explicit the action left unstated after *puote*, and the relative clause moves before the expression of possibility.
- *più non dimandare* ‘no longer ask’ → “ask no more”: the negative and its accompanying adverb move after the verb.

> 97 Quinci fur quete le lanose gote  
> 98 al nocchier della livida palude,  
> 99 che intorno agli occhi avea di fiamme rote.  

Then the woolly cheeks were quiet of the steersman on the livid marsh, who round his eyes had wheels of flame.

- *Quinci* ‘hence’ → “Then”: the connection is expressed temporally rather than explicitly as a consequence.
- *fur quete le lanose gote* ‘were quiet the woolly cheeks’ → “the woolly cheeks were quiet”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *al nocchier* ‘to the steersman’ → “of the steersman”: the Italian dative of possession becomes an English possessive phrase.
- *della livida palude* ‘of the livid marsh’ → “on the livid marsh”: the relation expressed by *di* becomes a statement of location.
- *intorno agli occhi* ‘round the eyes’ → “round his eyes”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *di fiamme rote* ‘of flames wheels’ → “wheels of flame”: the noun precedes its modifier, and plural *fiamme* becomes singular mass “flame”.

> 100 Ma quell’ anime ch’eran lasse e nude,  
> 101 cangiâr colore e dibattero i denti,  
> 102 ratto che inteser le parole crude.  

But those spirits, who were foreworn and naked, changed colour and chattered with their teeth, soon as they heard the bitter words.

- *anime* ‘souls’ → “spirits”: a different noun is used for the disembodied persons.
- *dibattero i denti* ‘beat the teeth’ → “chattered with their teeth”: the transitive construction becomes an intransitive verb with an instrumental phrase, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *ratto che* ‘immediately when’ → “soon as”: the temporal expression is recast as a shortened English conjunction.
- *crude* ‘cruel’ → “bitter”: cruelty or harshness is expressed through a different figurative adjective.

> 103 Bestemmiavano Iddio e lor parenti,  
> 104 l’ umana specie, il luogo, il tempo, e il seme  
> 105 di lor semenza e di lor nascimenti.  

They blasphemed God and their parents; the human kind; the place, the time, and origin of their seed, and of their birth.

- *Bestemmiavano* ‘were blaspheming’ → “They blasphemed”: the imperfect, presenting continuing activity, becomes a simple past.
- *l’ umana specie* ‘the human species’ → “the human kind”: “kind” replaces ‘species’, and the adjective follows the article in the ordinary English order.
- *il seme* ‘the seed’ → “origin”: the concrete noun is replaced by an abstract interpretation, and its definite article is omitted.
- *nascimenti* ‘births’ → “birth”: the plural becomes singular.

> 106 Poi si ritrasser tutte quante insieme,  
> 107 forte piangendo, alla riva malvagia,  
> 108 che attende ciascun uom, che Dio non teme.  

Then all of them together, sorely weeping, drew to the accursed shore, which awaits every man that fears not God.

- *si ritrasser* ‘withdrew’ → “drew”: the sense of withdrawing or drawing back is reduced to movement towards the following destination.
- *tutte quante insieme* ‘all, every one, together’ → “all of them together”: the emphatic combination of quantifiers is compressed, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *forte piangendo* ‘strongly weeping’ → “sorely weeping”: intensity is expressed by an English adverb particularly associated with distress.
- *malvagia* ‘evil’ → “accursed”: the adjective describes a cursed condition rather than wickedness.
- *Dio non teme* ‘God [he] does not fear’ → “fears not God”: the object moves from before the verb to after it, while the negative also becomes postverbal.

> 109 Caron dimonio, con occhi di bragia  
> 110 loro accennando, tutte le raccoglie;  
> 111 batte col remo qualunque s’ adagia.  

Charon the demon, with eyes of glowing coal, beckoning them, collects them all; smites with his oar whoever lingers.

- *dimonio* ‘demon’ → “the demon”: a definite article is supplied.
- *di bragia* ‘of ember’ → “of glowing coal”: the single noun is expanded into a noun with an adjective making its burning condition explicit.
- *loro accennando* ‘to them beckoning’ → “beckoning them”: the indirect object becomes a direct object and follows the participle.
- *tutte le raccoglie* ‘all them he collects’ → “collects them all”: the object pronoun and its quantifier move after the verb, with their relative order reversed.
- *col remo* ‘with the oar’ → “with his oar”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *s’ adagia* ‘settles himself at ease’ → “lingers”: the pronominal expression is interpreted as delay and rendered by a simple intransitive verb.

> 112 Come d’autunno si levan le foglie  
> 113 l’ una appresso dell’ altra, infin che il ramo  
> 114 vede alla terra tutte le sue spoglie:  

As the leaves of autumn fall off one after the other, till the branch sees all its spoils upon the ground:

- *Come d’autunno si levan le foglie* ‘as in autumn the leaves detach themselves’ → “As the leaves of autumn fall off”: the temporal phrase becomes a modifier of “leaves”; the subject moves before the verb; and the pronominal expression of detachment becomes “fall off”, specifying downward movement.
- *l’ una appresso dell’ altra* ‘the one after the other’ → “one after the other”: the first definite article is omitted.
- *alla terra* ‘to the earth’ → “upon the ground”: the preposition is rendered as location rather than direction, and the phrase moves after the object of “sees”.

> 115 similemente il mal seme d’ Adamo  
> 116 gittansi di quel lito ad una ad una,  
> 117 per cenni, come augel per suo richiamo.  

so one by one the evil seed of Adam cast themselves from that shore at signals, as the bird at its call.

- *similemente* ‘similarly’ → “so”: the explicit adverb of similarity becomes the correlative answering the preceding “As”.
- *ad una ad una* ‘one at a time’ → “one by one”: the distributive expression is recast and moves from after the phrase identifying the shore to before the subject.
- *per cenni* ‘by gestures’ → “at signals”: gestures become the more general “signals”, and the preposition expresses response to them rather than means.
- *augel* ‘bird’ → “the bird”: a definite article is supplied.
- *per suo richiamo* ‘by its call’ → “at its call”: the causal or prompting relation is expressed with “at” rather than “by”.

> 118 Così sen vanno su per l’ onda bruna,  
> 119 ed avanti che sian di là discese,  
> 120 anche di qua nova schiera s’ aduna.  

Thus they depart on the brown water; and ere they have landed on the other shore, again a fresh crowd collects on this.

- *sen vanno* ‘go away’ → “depart”: the pronominal expression, including the particle of departure, becomes a single verb.
- *su per l’ onda bruna* ‘along over the dark wave’ → “on the brown water”: the compound preposition is reduced to “on”, singular ‘wave’ becomes mass “water”, and *bruna* is given the specific colour sense “brown” rather than the broader ‘dark’.
- *avanti che sian … discese* ‘before they have descended’ → “ere they have landed”: descent is interpreted specifically as disembarkation, and the Italian perfect subjunctive becomes an English indicative perfect.
- *di là* ‘on that side’ → “on the other shore”: the deictic expression is expanded to name the shore explicitly.
- *di qua* ‘on this side’ → “on this”: the place is expressed elliptically through the previously mentioned shore, and the phrase moves from before the subject to the end of the clause.
- *nova schiera s’ aduna* ‘a new band gathers itself’ → “a fresh crowd collects”: an indefinite article is supplied; “fresh” replaces ‘new’; *schiera*, a band or troop, becomes the less organized “crowd”; and the pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive verb.

## Lines 121–136 — Divine Justice and Dante’s Swoon

> 121 “Figliuol mio,” disse il maestro cortese,  
> 122 “quelli, che muoion nell’ira di Dio,  
> 123 tutti convegnon qui d’ogni paese;  

“My son,” said the courteous Master, “those who die under God’s wrath, all assemble here from every country;

- *Figliuol mio* ‘little son my’ → “My son”: the possessive precedes the noun, and the diminutive force of *Figliuol* is not separately expressed.
- *il maestro cortese* ‘the master courteous’ → “the courteous Master”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *nell’ira di Dio* ‘in the wrath of God’ → “under God’s wrath”: “under” replaces ‘in’, and the phrase specifying possession becomes a possessive before the noun.

> 124 e pronti sono a trapassar lo rio,  
> 125 chè la divina giustizia gli sprona  
> 126 sì che la tema si volge in disio.  

and they are prompt to pass the river, for Divine Justice spurs them so, that fear is changed into desire.

- *pronti sono* ‘ready are’ → “they are prompt”: the predicate adjective follows the verb rather than preceding it; *pronti* is expressed as “prompt”.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *gli sprona* ‘them spurs’ → “spurs them”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *la tema si volge in disio* ‘fear turns itself into desire’ → “fear is changed into desire”: the pronominal construction becomes a passive construction.

> 127 Quinci non passa mai anima buona:  
> 128 e però, se Caron di te si lagna,  
> 129 ben puoi saper omai, che il suo dir suona.”  

By this way no good spirit ever passes; and hence, if Charon complains of thee, thou easily now mayest know the import of his words.”

- *Quinci* ‘from here’ → “By this way”: the adverb is interpreted as indicating a route, expressed through a prepositional phrase, rather than a point of departure.
- *non passa mai anima buona* ‘never passes a good soul’ → “no good spirit ever passes”: the negative moves from the verb to the subject, with ‘never’ correspondingly becoming “ever”; the subject precedes the verb, the adjective precedes its noun, and ‘soul’ becomes “spirit”.
- *di te si lagna* ‘of thee complains’ → “complains of thee”: the prepositional complement follows the verb.
- *ben puoi saper omai* ‘well canst know by now’ → “thou easily now mayest know”: *ben* is interpreted as “easily”; ‘canst’ becomes “mayest”; and *omai* becomes “now”, placed before the modal and infinitive rather than after them.
- *che il suo dir suona* ‘what his saying signifies’ → “the import of his words”: the indirect question becomes a noun phrase. The meaning expressed by *suona* becomes “import”, while the substantivized infinitive *dir*, ‘saying’, becomes the plural noun “words”.

> 130 Finito questo, la buia campagna  
> 131 tremò sì forte, che dello spavento  
> 132 la mente di sudore ancor mi bagna.  

When he had ended, the dusky champaign trembled so violently, that the remembrance of my terror bathes me still with sweat.

- *Finito questo* ‘this finished’ → “When he had ended”: the absolute participial construction becomes a temporal clause with an explicit agent and a pluperfect verb; *questo* is not separately rendered.
- *buia* ‘dark’ → “dusky”: the adjective is rendered by a word suggesting dimness rather than unqualified darkness.
- *forte* ‘strongly’ → “violently”: the adverb describing strength is rendered as one describing violence.
- *dello spavento la mente* ‘of the fright the mind’ → “the remembrance of my terror”: *mente* is interpreted as ‘memory’ or ‘remembrance’, and the preceding phrase *dello spavento* is placed after it as its complement. “my” is supplied, and ‘fright’ becomes “terror”.
- *di sudore ancor mi bagna* ‘with sweat still me bathes’ → “bathes me still with sweat”: the verb precedes its object pronoun, while the adverb and the phrase specifying sweat move after the verb and object.

> 133 La terra lagrimosa diede vento,  
> 134 che balenò una luce vermiglia,  
> 135 la qual mi vinse ciascun sentimento;  

The tearful ground gave out wind, [which] flashed [forth] a crimson light that conquered all my senses;

- *La terra lagrimosa* ‘the ground tearful’ → “The tearful ground”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *diede vento* ‘gave wind’ → “gave out wind”: “out” is supplied, making the verb explicitly describe emission.
- *che balenò* ‘which flashed’ → “[which] flashed [forth]”: “[forth]” is supplied, giving the flashing an explicitly outward direction.
- *una luce vermiglia* ‘a light vermilion’ → “a crimson light”: the adjective precedes the noun, and the colour term ‘vermilion’ is rendered as “crimson”.
- *mi vinse ciascun sentimento* ‘for me conquered each sense’ → “conquered all my senses”: the affected-person pronoun *mi* becomes the possessive “my”; the distributive singular ‘each sense’ becomes the plural “all my senses”.

> 136 e caddi, come l’uom, cui sonno piglia.  

and I fell, like one who is seized with sleep.

- *come l’uom, cui sonno piglia* ‘like the man whom sleep seizes’ → “like one who is seized with sleep”: the generic definite noun ‘the man’ becomes the indefinite pronoun “one”. The active relative clause becomes passive, with sleep expressed through a phrase introduced by “with” rather than as the grammatical subject.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Constructions

Carlyle’s translation remains close to the Italian in its sequence of phrases, repeated expressions, and much of its vocabulary. It often preserves conspicuous inversions, as in “These words, of colour obscure, saw I written above a gate”, rather than consistently adopting ordinary English order. Elsewhere, subjects move before verbs, object pronouns follow them, and adjectives precede nouns. These adjustments are not uniform: *la somma Sapienza* becomes “Wisdom Supreme”, while *tra la perduta gente* becomes “among the people lost”, introducing a postposed adjective where the Italian has a preceding one.

The larger departures frequently involve changes of construction. The repeated impersonal *si va* becomes “is the way” and then “the way”; expressions of necessity with *convien* become constructions with “must”. Finite clauses may become participial phrases, as *E poichè la sua mano alla mia pose* becomes “And placing his hand on mine”; conversely, *Finito questo* becomes “When he had ended”. Reflexive or pronominal expressions are sometimes rendered transitively or passively: *ond’ io mi confortai* becomes “that comforted me”, and *la tema si volge in disio* becomes “fear is changed into desire”. Tenses are also adjusted to English usage, notably in reported speech and temporal clauses.

Word choice sometimes interprets or reshapes the Italian rather than reproducing its most immediate sense. *Diverse* becomes “Strange”, shifting from variety towards unfamiliarity; *alti guai* becomes “deep wailings”, exchanging loudness for depth. More concrete substitutions include *in caldo e in gelo*, ‘into heat and into freezing cold’, rendered as “into fire and into ice”; *mosconi*, ‘large flies’, becomes “hornets”. Yet many images and expressions remain closely represented, including “the good of the intellect”, “the secret things”, and “the evil seed of Adam”. Archaic vocabulary and phrasing help retain the marked character of the original even where the individual choices are interpretive.

Omissions and additions range from small grammatical adjustments to compression and expansion. *Mi* disappears from the rendering of *mi pareva indegna*, while *verrai a piaggia* receives no separate equivalent in the shortened account of passing over. Conversely, *di là* becomes “on the other shore”, explicitly naming what the Italian leaves implicit, and *infino al fiume* becomes “till we reached the stream”, supplying an action and its participants. Throughout, English also supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and normally omits the Italian definite article before possessives. Alongside these routine differences, shifts between singular and plural, verbal and nominal negation, and distributive and collective expressions—such as *ogni speranza* becoming “all hope”—produce a translation that follows the Italian closely without maintaining a word-for-word correspondence.
