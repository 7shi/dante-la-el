# Inferno 23

## Lines 1–21 — Silent Flight and Renewed Fear

> 1 Taciti, soli e senza compagnia  
> 2 n'andavam l' un dinanzi e l' altro dopo,  
> 3 come frati minor vanno per via.  

Silent, apart, and without escort we went on, the one before and the other after; as Minor Friars go their way.

- *soli* ‘alone’ → “apart”: being alone becomes being separate.
- *senza compagnia* ‘without company’ → “without escort”: the broader ‘company’ is narrowed to an accompanying escort.
- *n'andavam* ‘we were going away’ → “we went on”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the pronominal expression of departure becomes one of continuing onward.
- *frati minor* ‘friars minor’ → “Minor Friars”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *per via* ‘along the road’ → “their way”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverbial noun phrase, with a possessive supplied.

> 4 Volto era in su la favola d' Isopo  
> 5 lo mio pensier per la presente rissa,  
> 6 dov' ei parlò della rana e del topo:  

My thought was turned, by the present strife, to Æsop's fable where he [spoke] of the frog and mouse:

- *Volto era* ‘turned was’ → “was turned”: auxiliary and participle exchange places, and the predicate follows its subject rather than preceding it.
- *in su la favola d' Isopo* ‘upon the fable of Æsop’ → “to Æsop's fable”: the preposition changes, and the following possessive phrase becomes a possessive before the noun.
- *per la presente rissa* ‘because of the present brawl’ → “by the present strife”: the cause is expressed through a passive-agent construction; ‘brawl’ becomes the broader “strife”. The phrase also moves before the reference to the fable.
- *della rana e del topo* ‘of the frog and of the mouse’ → “of the frog and mouse”: the second preposition and article are omitted.

> 7 chè più non si pareggia mo ed issa,  
> 8 che l' un con l' altro fa se ben s' accoppia  
> 9 principio e fine con la mente fissa.  

for Ay and Yea pair not better, than does the one *case* with the other, if with attentive mind the beginning and end of *each* be well accoupled.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating causal conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *più non si pareggia* ‘more do not equal one another’ → “pair not better”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and degree is expressed by “better” rather than ‘more’. The predicate also moves after its subjects.
- *mo ed issa* ‘now and now’ → “Ay and Yea”: two synonymous expressions of time are replaced by two synonymous expressions of affirmation.
- *che l' un con l' altro fa* ‘than the one with the other does’ → “than does the one *case* with the other”: “does” moves before the subject, and “case” is supplied to identify what ‘the one’ refers to.
- *se ben s' accoppia principio e fine* ‘if well are coupled beginning and end’ → “if” … “the beginning and end of *each* be well accoupled”: the subject moves before the verb, and “well” moves after the auxiliary. The present indicative passive becomes an English subjunctive passive; the article and “of *each*” are supplied.
- *con la mente fissa* ‘with the mind fixed’ → “with attentive mind”: ‘fixed’ is interpreted as attentive, the adjective moves before the noun, and the article is omitted. The phrase moves from the end of the clause to its beginning.

> 10 E come l' un pensier dell' altro scoppia,  
> 11 così nacque di quello un altro poi,  
> 12 che la prima paura mi fe' doppia.  

And as one thought from the other springs, so arose from that another then, which made my first fear double.

- *l' un pensier* ‘the one thought’ → “one thought”: the definite article is omitted.
- *scoppia* ‘bursts forth’ → “springs”: the forceful verb of bursting becomes one of springing or arising.
- *nacque* ‘was born’ → “arose”: the metaphor of birth becomes a more general expression of emergence.
- *la prima paura mi fe' doppia* ‘the first fear for me made double’ → “made my first fear double”: the verb moves before its object, and the personal dative becomes the possessive “my”.

> 13 Io pensava così: “Questi per noi  
> 14 sono scherniti, e con danno e con beffa  
> 15 sì fatta, ch' assai credo che lor noi.  

I thus bethought me: "These through us are put to scorn, and with damage and mockery of such sort, as I believe must greatly vex them.

- *Io pensava così* ‘I was thinking thus’ → “I thus bethought me”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, the non-reflexive verb becomes the reflexive expression “bethought me”, and ‘thus’ moves before the verb.
- *per noi* ‘because of us’ → “through us”: responsibility or cause is expressed as agency.
- *sono scherniti* ‘are mocked’ → “are put to scorn”: the simple passive verb becomes a longer verbal expression.
- *e con danno e con beffa sì fatta* ‘and with harm and with mockery so fashioned’ → “and with damage and mockery of such sort”: the repeated preposition is omitted, and the participial description ‘so fashioned’ becomes a phrase describing kind. The Italian feminine singular participle specifically qualifies *beffa*.
- *ch' assai credo che lor noi* ‘that greatly I believe that it vexes them’ → “as I believe must greatly vex them”: the result construction becomes “such … as”; the second conjunction is omitted. “Greatly” moves from before ‘I believe’ to the verb of vexing, and the present subjunctive *noi* is rendered with the added modal “must”. The pronoun meaning ‘them’ moves after the verb.

> 16 Se l' ira sopra il mal voler s' aggueffa,  
> 17 ei ne verranno dietro più crudeli  
> 18 che il cane a quella lepre ch' egli acceffa.”  

If rage be added to their malice, they will pursue us, fiercer than [the] dog that leveret which he snaps."

- *l' ira* ‘the anger’ → “rage”: the noun is intensified, and the article is omitted.
- *sopra il mal voler* ‘upon the evil will’ → “to their malice”: ‘upon’ becomes “to”, the phrase ‘evil will’ becomes the single noun “malice”, and “their” is supplied in place of the definite article.
- *s' aggueffa* ‘winds itself’ → “be added”: the metaphor of winding together becomes a general expression of addition. The reflexive present indicative becomes an English subjunctive passive, and the verb moves before its prepositional complement.
- *ei ne verranno dietro* ‘they will come behind us’ → “they will pursue us”: the expression of following motion becomes a transitive verb of pursuit.
- *più crudeli* ‘more cruel’ → “fiercer”: cruelty is rendered as fierceness.
- *che il cane a quella lepre* ‘than the dog after that hare’ → “than [the] dog that leveret”: the preposition marking the object of pursuit is omitted, leaving an elliptical construction in English. ‘Hare’ becomes “leveret”, specifically a young hare.
- *ch' egli acceffa* ‘which he seizes with his muzzle’ → “which he snaps”: the verb’s explicit reference to seizing with the muzzle is compressed into “snaps”.

> 19 Già mi sentia tutti arricciar li peli  
> 20 della paura, e stava indietro intento,  
> 21 quando io dissi: “Maestro, se non celi  

Already I felt my hair all rise with fear; and was looking back intently, as I said: "Master, if thou do not hide

- *mi sentia* ‘I was feeling on myself’ → “I felt my”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the reflexive reference to the person affected becomes a possessive modifying the hair.
- *tutti arricciar li peli* ‘all bristle the hairs’ → “hair all rise”: the plural noun becomes a mass singular; it moves before the infinitive, while ‘all’ follows it. ‘Bristle’ becomes the less specific “rise”.
- *della paura* ‘from the fear’ → “with fear”: the causal preposition changes, and the article is omitted.
- *e stava indietro intento* ‘and remained intent behind’ → “and was looking back intently”: a continuing state of attention becomes an explicitly visual activity. “Looking” is supplied, and the adjective becomes an adverb.
- *quando* ‘when’ → “as”: the temporal connection is expressed as simultaneity.
- *se non celi* ‘if thou dost not conceal’ → “if thou do not hide”: the Italian present indicative is rendered with the English hypothetical subjunctive “do” rather than the indicative ‘dost’.

## Lines 22–54 — Escape From the Malebranche

> 22 te e me tostamente, i' ho pavento  
> 23 di Malebranche: noi gli avem già dietro;  
> 24 io gl' imagino sì che già gli sento."  

thyself and me speedily, I dread the Malebranche: they are already after us; I so imagine them that I hear them now.”

- *te* ‘thee’ → “thyself”: the simple personal pronoun becomes an emphatic form.
- *i' ho pavento / di Malebranche* ‘I have fear of the Malebranche’ → “I dread the Malebranche”: the noun phrase becomes a transitive verb, and the preposition disappears.
- *noi gli avem già dietro* ‘we have them already behind’ → “they are already after us”: the object becomes the subject; having someone behind becomes their being in pursuit.
- *io gl' imagino sì* ‘I them imagine so’ → “I so imagine them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and *sì* moves before it.
- *già gli sento* ‘already them I hear’ → “I hear them now”: the object pronoun follows the verb; *già* becomes “now” rather than ‘already’ and moves to the end.

> 25 E quei: "S' io fossi d' impiombato vetro,  
> 26 l' imagine di fuor tua non trarrei  
> 27 più tosto a me, che quella d' entro impetro.  

And he: “If I were of leaded glass, I should not draw thy outward image more quickly to me, than I impress that from within.

- *l' imagine di fuor tua non trarrei / più tosto a me* ‘the image from without of thee I should not draw more quickly to me’ → “I should not draw thy outward image more quickly to me”: the object moves after the verb; the postposed possessive becomes “thy” before the noun, and the adverbial phrase *di fuor* becomes the adjective “outward”.
- *quella d' entro impetro* ‘that from within I obtain’ → “I impress that from within”: the object moves after the verb. “Impress” interprets *impetro* as imprinting or receiving an impression, rather than its usual sense of obtaining.

> 28 Pur mo venian li tuoi pensier tra i miei  
> 29 con simile atto e con simile faccia,  
> 30 sì che d' intrambi un sol consiglio fei.  

Even now thy thoughts [were] enter[ing] among mine, with similar act and similar face; so that of both I have made one resolve.

- *Pur mo* ‘just now’ → “Even now”: the emphasis of *pur* is expressed by “Even” rather than ‘just’.
- *venian li tuoi pensier tra i miei* ‘were coming thy thoughts among mine’ → “thy thoughts [were] enter[ing] among mine”: the subject moves before the verb, and coming becomes entering.
- *con simile atto e con simile faccia* ‘with similar act and with similar face’ → “with similar act and similar face”: the second *con* is omitted.
- *d' intrambi un sol consiglio fei* ‘of both one sole counsel I made’ → “of both I have made one resolve”: the object follows the verb; the simple past becomes a present perfect; *consiglio* becomes “resolve”, and *un sol* is compressed into “one”.

> 31 S' egli è che sì la destra costa giaccia,  
> 32 che noi possiam nell' altra bolgia scendere,  
> 33 noi fuggirem l' imaginata caccia."  

In case the right coast so slopes, that we may descend into the other chasm, we shall escape the imagined chase.”

- *S' egli è che* ‘if it is that’ → “In case”: the conditional introductory clause is compressed into a conjunction.
- *sì la destra costa giaccia* ‘so the right side lies’ → “the right coast so slopes”: “coast” renders *costa* in its sense of a side or slope; lying in a particular position becomes sloping. The subjunctive *giaccia* becomes an English present indicative, and *sì* moves after the subject.
- *noi possiam nell' altra bolgia scendere* ‘we may into the other pouch descend’ → “we may descend into the other chasm”: the infinitive precedes the directional phrase, and the technical *bolgia*, literally ‘pouch’, becomes “chasm”.

> 34 Già non compiè di tal consiglio rendere,  
> 35 ch' io gli vidi venir con l' ali tese,  
> 36 non molto lungi, per volerne prendere.  

He had not ended giving this resolve, when I saw them come with wings extended, not far off, in will to seize us.

- *Già non compiè di tal consiglio rendere* ‘already he did not finish giving such counsel’ → “He had not ended giving this resolve”: the simple past becomes a past perfect, absorbing the temporal force of *già*. The construction *compiere di* becomes “ended” followed directly by a gerund; *tal* becomes “this”, *consiglio* becomes “resolve”, and the object moves after “giving”.
- *ch'* ‘that’ → “when”: the conjunction is rendered as the temporal link following the unfinished action.
- *io gli vidi venir* ‘I them saw come’ → “I saw them come”: the object pronoun follows the finite verb.
- *con l' ali tese* ‘with the wings extended’ → “with wings extended”: the definite article is omitted.
- *per volerne prendere* ‘for wishing to take us’ → “in will to seize us”: *volere* becomes the noun “will”, and the prepositional construction changes. The enclitic *-ne*, here meaning ‘us’, becomes the object after “seize”.

> 37 Lo duca mio di subito mi prese,  

My Guide suddenly took me,

- *Lo duca mio di subito mi prese* ‘the guide my suddenly me took’ → “My Guide suddenly took me”: the possessive moves before its noun, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 38 come la madre ch' al romore è desta,  
> 39 e vede presso a sè le fiamme accese,  

as a mother—that is awakened by the noise, and near her sees the kindled flames—

- *la madre* ‘the mother’ → “a mother”: the definite article becomes indefinite.
- *al romore è desta* ‘at the noise is awakened’ → “is awakened by the noise”: the prepositional phrase follows the verb, and the occasion or stimulus expressed by *al* becomes the cause introduced by “by”.
- *vede presso a sè* ‘sees near herself’ → “near her sees”: the phrase of proximity moves before the verb, and the reflexive pronoun becomes a simple personal pronoun.
- *le fiamme accese* ‘the flames kindled’ → “the kindled flames”: the participial adjective moves before its noun.

> 40 che prende il figlio e fugge e non s' arresta,  
> 41 avendo più di lui che di sè cura,  
> 42 tanto che solo una camicia vesta;  

who takes her child and flies, and caring more for him than for herself, pauses not so long as even to cast a shift about her;

- *il figlio* ‘the child’ → “her child”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *e non s' arresta, / avendo più di lui che di sè cura, / tanto che solo una camicia vesta* ‘and does not stop, having more care for him than for herself, so that she wears only a shirt’ → “and caring more for him than for herself, pauses not so long as even to cast a shift about her”: the participial clause moves before the main verb, and ‘having care’ becomes “caring”. The reflexive *s' arresta* becomes the intransitive “pauses”. More substantially, the translation takes *tanto che* as the extent of the denied pause, rather than as introducing the result that she wears only a shirt. Accordingly, *solo* becomes “even”, and *vesta* becomes the act of casting clothing about herself rather than wearing it.

> 43 e giù dal collo della ripa dura  
> 44 supin si diede alla pendente roccia,  
> 45 che l' un dei lati all' altra bolgia tura.  

and down from the ridge of the hard bank, supine he gave himself to the pendent rock, which dams up one side of the other chasm.

- *collo* ‘neck’ → “ridge”: the anatomical metaphor is replaced by a topographical term.
- *ripa dura* ‘bank hard’ → “hard bank”: the adjective moves before its noun.
- *si diede* ‘himself he gave’ → “he gave himself”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.
- *l' un dei lati all' altra bolgia tura* ‘one of the sides to the other pouch blocks’ → “dams up one side of the other chasm”: the verb precedes its object, and “dams up” gives *tura* a more specifically barrier-like expression. ‘One of the sides’ becomes “one side”; the relation expressed by *all' altra bolgia* becomes an “of” phrase, and *bolgia* becomes “chasm”.

> 46 Non corse mai sì tosto acqua per doccia  
> 47 a volger rota di molin terragno,  
> 48 quand' ella più verso le pale approccia,  

Never did water run so fast through spout to turn a land-mill’s wheel, when it approaches nearest to the ladles,

- *Non corse mai sì tosto acqua* ‘not ran ever so fast water’ → “Never did water run so fast”: *non … mai* is combined as initial “Never”, requiring auxiliary “did”; the subject moves between that auxiliary and the main verb.
- *per doccia* ‘through a channel’ → “through spout”: “spout” gives a more specific form to the water-conduit denoted by *doccia*.
- *a volger rota di molin terragno* ‘to turn wheel of ground-mill’ → “to turn a land-mill’s wheel”: the postposed *di* phrase becomes a possessive before “wheel”, and an indefinite article is supplied before “land-mill”.
- *più verso le pale approccia* ‘more towards the paddles approaches’ → “approaches nearest to the ladles”: the verb moves before its complements; the comparative adverb *più* is interpreted superlatively as “nearest”, *verso* becomes “to”, and the wheel’s *pale* are called “ladles” rather than ‘paddles’.

> 49 come il maestro mio per quel vivagno,  
> 50 portandosene me sopra il suo petto,  
> 51 come suo figlio, non come compagno.  

as my Master down that bank, carrying me away upon his breast, as his son and not as his companion.

- *il maestro mio* ‘the master my’ → “my Master”: the possessive moves before its noun.
- *per quel vivagno* ‘along that edge’ → “down that bank”: “down” specifies the direction, while the metaphorical *vivagno*, literally a cloth’s selvage or edge, becomes “bank”.
- *portandosene me* ‘carrying away with himself me’ → “carrying me away”: the reflexive element is not separately expressed, and the object comes before “away”.
- *come suo figlio, non come compagno* ‘as his son, not as companion’ → “as his son and not as his companion”: “and” is supplied, and the possessive is repeated before “companion”.

> 52 Appena fur li piè suoi giunti al letto  
> 53 del fondo giù, ch' ei furono in sul colle  
> 54 sopresso noi; ma non gli era sospetto:  

Scarcely had his feet reached the bed of the depth below, when they [were on] the height above us; but no fear it gave him:

- *Appena fur li piè suoi giunti al letto / del fondo giù* ‘scarcely were the feet his arrived at the bed of the bottom below’ → “Scarcely had his feet reached the bed of the depth below”: the Italian compound tense with *essere* becomes an English perfect with “had”; arriving at becomes the transitive “reached”, removing the preposition. The possessive moves before “feet”, and *fondo* becomes “depth” rather than ‘bottom’.
- *colle* ‘hill’ → “height”: a term for a particular landform becomes a more general term for elevated ground.
- *ma non gli era sospetto* ‘but to him there was no fear’ → “but no fear it gave him”: the construction with *essere* becomes an active construction with “gave”; “it” is supplied as its subject, and “no fear” is placed before the verb.

## Lines 55–87 — The Painted People’s Heavy Cloaks

> 55 chè l'alta Provvidenza, che lor volle  
> 56 porre ministri della fossa quinta,  
> 57 poder di partirs' indi a tutti tolle.  

for the high Providence, that willed to place them ministers of the fifth ditch, takes the power of leaving it from all.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *lor volle porre* ‘them willed to place’ → “willed to place them”: the object pronoun moves from before the finite verb to after the infinitive.
- *fossa quinta* ‘ditch fifth’ → “fifth ditch”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *poder ... a tutti tolle* ‘power ... from all takes’ → “takes the power” and “from all”: the main verb moves before its object, the indirect complement follows it, and an article is supplied before “power”.
- *partirs' indi* ‘departing thence’ → “leaving it”: the place expressed by the adverb *indi* becomes the direct object of “leaving”; the reflexive Italian verb becomes an English transitive verb.

> 58 Laggiù trovammo una gente dipinta,  
> 59 che giva intorno assai con lenti passi  
> 60 piangendo, e nel sembiante stanca e vinta.  

There beneath we found a painted people, who were going round with steps exceeding slow, weeping, and in their look tired and overcome.

- *Laggiù* ‘down there’ → “There beneath”: the single locative adverb is expanded, with the directional element following “There”.
- *una gente dipinta* ‘a people painted’ → “a painted people”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *giva* ‘went’ → “were going”: the imperfect becomes an explicitly progressive construction, with plural agreement for the collective “people”.
- *assai con lenti passi* ‘with very slow steps’ → “with steps exceeding slow”: the degree word moves into the phrase after “steps”, and the adjective follows rather than precedes the noun.
- *nel sembiante* ‘in the appearance’ → “in their look”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 61 Egli avean cappe con cappucci bassi  
> 62 dinanzi agli occhi, fatte della taglia  
> 63 che per li monaci in Cologna fassi.  

They had cloaks on, with deep hoods before their eyes, made in the shape that they make for the monks in Cologne.

- *avean cappe* ‘had cloaks’ → “had cloaks on”: “on” is supplied to make the sense of wearing explicit.
- *cappucci bassi* ‘hoods low’ → “deep hoods”: “deep” interprets *bassi*, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *dinanzi agli occhi* ‘before the eyes’ → “before their eyes”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *fatte della taglia* ‘made of the cut’ → “made in the shape”: the preposition changes, and the term for a garment’s cut becomes the more general “shape”.
- *che ... fassi* ‘that ... is made’ → “that they make”: the passive construction with *si* becomes active, with an indefinite “they”; the verb moves before the phrases specifying the monks and the place.

> 64 Di fuor dorate son sì ch' egli abbaglia,  
> 65 ma dentro tutte piombo, e gravi tanto,  
> 66 che Federico le mettea di paglia.  

Outward they are gilded, so that it dazzles; but within all lead, and so heavy, that Frederick's compared to them were straw.

- *Di fuor* ‘on the outside’ → “Outward”: the locative phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *dorate son* ‘gilded are’ → “they are gilded”: the predicate adjective moves after the verb, and the understood subject is expressed.
- *gravi tanto* ‘heavy so much’ → “so heavy”: the degree expression moves before the adjective.
- *che Federico le mettea di paglia* ‘that Frederick put them on of straw’ → “that Frederick's compared to them were straw”: the active construction becomes a comparison with a copular verb. Frederick, originally the subject, becomes a possessive standing for his cloaks; the cloaks become the subject of “were”. “compared to them” is supplied to express the comparison implicit in the Italian, and ‘of straw’ becomes the predicate noun “straw”.

> 67 O in eterno faticoso manto!  

O weary mantle for eternity!

- *in eterno* ‘for ever’ → “for eternity”: the adverbial expression becomes a phrase with a noun and moves from before the adjective to after “mantle”.
- *faticoso manto* ‘wearisome mantle’ → “weary mantle”: an adjective meaning that the mantle causes weariness becomes one ordinarily describing the condition of being weary.

> 68 Noi ci volgemmo ancor pure a man manca  
> 69 con loro insieme, intenti al tristo pianto;  

We turned again to the left hand, along with them, intent upon their dreary weeping;

- *Noi ci volgemmo* ‘We turned ourselves’ → “We turned”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive.
- *ancor pure* ‘again still’ → “again”: *pure*, reinforcing continuation, has no separate equivalent.
- *a man manca* ‘to left hand’ → “to the left hand”: a definite article is supplied.
- *con loro insieme* ‘with them together’ → “along with them”: the force of *insieme* is incorporated into “along with”, rather than expressed by a following adverb.
- *al tristo pianto* ‘upon the dreary weeping’ → “upon their dreary weeping”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 70 ma per lo peso quella gente stanca  
> 71 venia sì pian, che noi eravam nuovi  
> 72 di compagnia ad ogni muover d' anca.  

but that people, tired by their burden, came so slowly that our company was new at every movement of the hip.

- *per lo peso* ‘because of the weight’ → “by their burden”: the initial expression of cause moves after “tired” and becomes its complement. ‘weight’ is rendered as “burden”, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *che noi eravam nuovi di compagnia* ‘that we were new in company’ → “that our company was new”: “company” becomes the grammatical subject, while the original subject ‘we’ becomes its possessive; the plural predicate becomes singular.
- *ad ogni muover d' anca* ‘at every moving of hip’ → “at every movement of the hip”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun, and an article is supplied before “hip”.

> 73 Per ch' io al duca mio: "Fa che tu trovi  
> 74 alcun ch' al fatto o al nome si conosca,  
> 75 e gli occhi sì andando intorno movi."  

Wherefore I to my Guide: "See that thou find some one who may by deed or name be known; and move thy eyes around as we go on."

- *Per ch'* ‘for which reason’ → “Wherefore”: the connective phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *al duca mio* ‘to the guide my’ → “to my Guide”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *Fa che tu trovi* ‘Make that thou find’ → “See that thou find”: the Italian imperative construction becomes the English idiom of ensuring that something is done.
- *alcun ch' al fatto o al nome si conosca* ‘some one who by the deed or by the name be known’ → “some one who may by deed or name be known”: “may” expresses the subjunctive force of *si conosca* and precedes the intervening phrase; the definite articles and the repeated preposition are omitted.
- *gli occhi ... intorno movi* ‘the eyes ... around move’ → “move thy eyes around”: the verb moves before its object, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *sì andando* ‘thus going’ → “as we go on”: the gerund becomes a finite temporal clause with an explicit plural subject; “on” gives a continuing sense instead of separately rendering *sì* as ‘thus’.

> 76 Ed un che intese la parola Tosca,  
> 77 di retro a noi gridò: "Tenete i piedi,  
> 78 voi che correte sì per l' aura fosca;  

And one, who understood the Tuscan speech, cried after us: "Stay your feet, ye who run so fast through the brown air;

- *la parola Tosca* ‘the word Tuscan’ → “the Tuscan speech”: ‘word’ is rendered collectively as “speech”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *di retro a noi gridò* ‘from behind us cried’ → “cried after us”: the verb moves before the locative phrase, which is recast as the direction of the cry.
- *Tenete i piedi* ‘Hold the feet’ → “Stay your feet”: “Stay” expresses stopping rather than holding, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *sì* ‘so’ → “so fast”: “fast” is supplied to specify the degree of speed.
- *l' aura fosca* ‘the air dark’ → “the brown air”: the adjective moves before the noun, and the general darkness of *fosca* is rendered as a particular colour.

> 79 forse ch' avrai da me quel che tu chiedi."  

perhaps thou shalt obtain from me that which thou askest."

- *forse ch'* ‘perhaps that’ → “perhaps”: the connective *che* has no separate equivalent.
- *avrai* ‘thou wilt have’ → “thou shalt obtain”: “obtain” makes acquisition explicit rather than using the more general verb ‘have’.

> 80 Onde il duca si volse, e disse: "Aspetta,  
> 81 e poi secondo il suo passo procedi."  

Whereat my Guide turned round and said: "Wait, and then at his pace proceed."

- *Onde* ‘whence’ → “Whereat”: a connective marking consequence is rendered as a response to the preceding words.
- *il duca* ‘the guide’ → “my Guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *si volse* ‘turned himself’ → “turned round”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, with “round” supplied.
- *secondo il suo passo* ‘according to his pace’ → “at his pace”: the expression of conformity becomes the ordinary English prepositional phrase for matching a pace.

> 82 Ristetti, e vidi due mostrar gran fretta  
> 83 dell' animo, col viso, d' esser meco;  
> 84 ma tardavagli il carco e la via stretta.  

I stood still, and saw two, showing by their look great haste of mind to be with me; but the load and the narrow way retarded them.

- *Ristetti* ‘I stopped’ → “I stood still”: cessation of movement is expressed as a stationary posture.
- *mostrar* ‘show’ → “showing”: the infinitive dependent on a verb of perception becomes a present participle.
- *dell' animo* ‘of the mind’ → “of mind”: the definite article is omitted.
- *col viso* ‘with the face’ → “by their look”: the face is rendered as its expression, the article becomes a possessive, and “by” replaces the instrumental “with”. The phrase moves before “great haste of mind”.
- *tardavagli il carco e la via stretta* ‘retarded them the load and the way narrow’ → “the load and the narrow way retarded them”: the compound subject moves before the verb, the object pronoun follows it, and the adjective moves before “way”.

> 85 Quando fur giunti, assai con l'occhio bieco  
> 86 mi rimiraron senza far parola;  

When they came up, long with eye askance they viewed me, without uttering a word;

- *Quando fur giunti* ‘When they had arrived’ → “When they came up”: the compound past becomes a simple past, and arrival is expressed as coming up.
- *assai* ‘much’ → “long”: degree is interpreted specifically as duration.
- *con l'occhio bieco* ‘with the eye askance’ → “with eye askance”: the definite article is omitted.
- *mi rimiraron* ‘me they viewed’ → “they viewed me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *senza far parola* ‘without making word’ → “without uttering a word”: “uttering” replaces the general verb ‘making’, and an indefinite article is supplied.

> 87 poi si volsero in sè, e dicean seco:  

then they turned to one another, and said between them:

- *si volsero in sè* ‘turned themselves inward’ → “they turned to one another”: the reflexive expression is interpreted reciprocally, as turning towards each other rather than towards themselves.
- *e dicean seco* ‘and were saying with themselves’ → “and said between them”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and *seco* is interpreted reciprocally as “between them”.

## Lines 88–120 — The Hypocrites and the Crucified Man

> 88 “Costui par vivo all’ atto della gola;  

“This one seems alive by the action of his throat;

- *all’ atto della gola* ‘at the action of the throat’ → “by the action of his throat”: “by” presents the action as evidence; the definite article becomes the possessive “his”.

> 89 e s’ei son morti, per qual privilegio  
> 90 vanno scoperti della grave stola?”  

and if they are dead, by what privilege go they divested of the heavy stole?”

- *scoperti* ‘uncovered’ → “divested”: the absence of a covering is expressed as being stripped of it.

> 91 Poi disser me: “O Tosco, ch’al collegio  
> 92 degl’ ipocriti tristi se’ venuto,  
> 93 dir chi tu sei non avere in dispregio.”  

Then they said to me: “O Tuscan, that art come to the college of the sad hypocrites! to tell us who thou art disdain not.”

- *ch’al collegio degl’ ipocriti tristi se’ venuto* ‘that to the college of the hypocrites sad art come’ → “that art come to the college of the sad hypocrites”: the verb moves before the destination phrase, and the adjective moves before “hypocrites”.
- *dir* ‘to say’ → “to tell us”: “tell” replaces ‘say’, and “us” supplies an expressed recipient.
- *non avere in dispregio* ‘do not hold in disdain’ → “disdain not”: the phrase consisting of a verb and prepositional complement becomes a single verb; the negative follows it.

> 94 Ed io a loro: “Io fui nato e cresciuto  
> 95 sopra il bel fiume d’ Arno alla gran villa;  

And I to them: “On Arno’s beauteous river, in the great city I was born and grew;

- *fui nato e cresciuto* ‘was born and grown up’ → “was born and grew”: the two participles sharing an auxiliary become a passive construction followed by an independent finite verb.
- *sopra il bel fiume d’ Arno* ‘on the beautiful river of Arno’ → “On Arno’s beauteous river”: the location moves before the verbs; the following *d’ Arno* becomes a possessive preceding the noun.
- *alla gran villa* ‘at the great city’ → “in the great city”: ‘at’ becomes “in”, and this location also moves before the verbs.

> 96 e son col corpo ch’i’ ho sempre avuto.  

and I am with the body that I have always had.

- Literal.

> 97 Ma voi chi siete, a cui tanto distilla,  
> 98 quant’ io veggio, dolor giù per le guance,  
> 99 e che pena è in voi che sì sfavilla?”  

But you, who are ye from whom distils such sorrow as I see, down your cheeks? and what punishment is on ye that glitters so?”

- *voi chi siete* ‘you who are’ → “you, who are ye”: “ye” repeats the already expressed subject “you” after the interrogative verb.
- *a cui* ‘to whom’ → “from whom”: the relation is changed from recipient to source.
- *tanto distilla, quant’ io veggio, dolor* ‘so much distils, as I see, sorrow’ → “distils such sorrow as I see”: the quantitative correlation becomes “such … as”; “sorrow” moves before the intervening clause.
- *giù per le guance* ‘down along the cheeks’ → “down your cheeks”: “down” absorbs the separate expression of direction and passage, and the definite article becomes “your”.
- *in voi* ‘in you’ → “on ye”: an internal location becomes something borne upon the persons.
- *sì sfavilla* ‘so glitters’ → “glitters so”: the adverb moves after the verb.

> 100 E l’un rispose a me: “Le cappe rance  
> 101 son di piombo, sì grosse che li pesi  
> 102 fan così cigolar le lor bilance.  

And one of them replied to me: “Our orange mantles are of lead so thick, that the weights thus cause their scales to creak.

- *l’un* ‘the one’ → “one of them”: the definite article is replaced by an explicit partitive construction.
- *Le cappe rance* ‘The mantles orange’ → “Our orange mantles”: the definite article becomes “Our”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *fan così cigolar le lor bilance* ‘make thus creak their scales’ → “thus cause their scales to creak”: the adverb moves before the causative verb; “cause” takes a *to*-infinitive, and the scales move before that infinitive.

> 103 Frati Godenti fummo, e Bolognesi:  

We were Jovial Friars, and Bolognese:

- *Frati Godenti fummo* ‘Friars Jovial we were’ → “We were Jovial Friars”: the subject and verb precede the complement instead of following it, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 104 io Catalano e questi Loderingo  
> 105 nomati, e da tua terra insieme presi,  

I named Catalano, and Loderingo he; and by thy city chosen together,

- *io Catalano e questi Loderingo nomati* ‘I Catalano and this one Loderingo named’ → “I named Catalano, and Loderingo he”: the shared plural participle moves into the first member and is left understood in the second; the demonstrative ‘this one’ becomes “he” and moves after the name.
- *terra* ‘land’ → “city”: the translation selects the word’s urban sense.
- *insieme presi* ‘together taken’ → “chosen together”: ‘taken’ is interpreted as selection, and the adverb follows the participle.

> 106 come suole esser tolto un uom solingo  
> 107 per conservar sua pace; e fummo tali,  
> 108 ch’ancor si pare intorno dal Gardingo.”  

as usually one solitary man is chosen, to maintain its peace; and we were such, that it yet appears round the Gardingo.”

- *come suole esser tolto un uom solingo* ‘as is wont to be taken a man solitary’ → “as usually one solitary man is chosen”: the finite expression of customary action becomes the adverb “usually”; ‘taken’ becomes “chosen”; the subject moves before the passive verb, and the adjective moves before the noun. “One” gives *un* an explicitly numerical force.
- *ch’ancor si pare* ‘that still it appears’ → “that it yet appears”: the temporal adverb moves after “it”.
- *intorno dal Gardingo* ‘around by the Gardingo’ → “round the Gardingo”: the separate prepositional connection expressed by *dal* is absorbed into “round”.

> 109 Io cominciai: “O frati, i vostri mali . . .”  
> 110 ma più non dissi, chè all’ occhio mi corse  
> 111 un, crocifisso in terra con tre pali.  

I began: “O Friars, your evil”—but said no more, for to my eyes came one, cross-fixed in the ground with three stakes.

- *i vostri mali* ‘your evils’ → “your evil”: the plural becomes a collective singular.
- *ma più non dissi* ‘but more I did not say’ → “but said no more”: the separate adverb and verbal negative become “no more”, placed after the verb.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *all’ occhio mi corse* ‘to the eye to me ran’ → “to my eyes came”: the singular ‘eye’ becomes plural; the personal dative becomes a possessive; “came” replaces the more vigorous ‘ran’.
- *crocifisso in terra* ‘crucified on the ground’ → “cross-fixed in the ground”: ‘crucified’ is rendered by an explicitly compound expression, and “in” replaces ‘on’.

> 112 Quando mi vide, tutto si distorse,  
> 113 soffiando nella barba co’ sospiri;  

When he saw me, he writhed all over, blowing into his beard with sighs;

- *mi vide* ‘me he saw’ → “he saw me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *tutto si distorse* ‘all he twisted himself’ → “he writhed all over”: *tutto*, qualifying the whole person, becomes the adverbial “all over”; the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *nella barba* ‘into the beard’ → “into his beard”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 114 e il frate Catalan, ch’ a ciò s’ accorse,  
> 115 mi disse: “Quel confitto, che tu miri,  
> 116 consigliò i Farisei, che convenia  
> 117 porre un uom per lo popolo a’ martiri.  

and Friar Catalano, who perceived this, said to me: “That confixed one, on whom thou gazest, counselled the Pharisees that it was expedient to put one man to tortures for the people.

- *il frate Catalan* ‘the friar Catalano’ → “Friar Catalano”: the article is omitted before the title and name.
- *ch’ a ciò s’ accorse* ‘who of this became aware’ → “who perceived this”: the pronominal verb with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object; “this” moves after the verb.
- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *Quel confitto* ‘that transfixed’ → “That confixed one”: “one” supplies an explicit head for the substantivized participle.
- *che tu miri* ‘whom thou beholdest’ → “on whom thou gazest”: the Italian direct object becomes the complement of “on”, required by the chosen English verb.
- *convenia* ‘it was fitting’ → “it was expedient”: the translation selects expediency as the sense of what was fitting or advisable.
- *un uom* ‘a man’ → “one man”: “one” makes the numerical force explicit.
- *per lo popolo a’ martiri* ‘for the people to tortures’ → “to tortures for the people”: the two prepositional phrases exchange positions.

> 118 Attraversato e nudo è nella via,  
> 119 come tu vedi, ed è mestier ch’ ei senta  
> 120 qualunque passa com’ ei pesa pria;  

Traverse and naked he is upon the road, as thou seest; and has to feel the weight of every one that passes;

- *Attraversato* ‘laid crosswise’ → “Traverse”: the participle becomes an adjective describing transverse position.
- *nella via* ‘in the road’ → “upon the road”: “upon” replaces ‘in’.
- *ed è mestier ch’ ei senta* ‘and it is necessary that he feel’ → “and has to feel”: the impersonal expression followed by a subjunctive clause becomes a personal construction expressing necessity with an infinitive.
- *qualunque passa com’ ei pesa pria* ‘whoever passes, how he weighs first’ → “the weight of every one that passes”: the clause expressing how much someone weighs becomes the noun phrase “the weight”; *qualunque passa* becomes its following possessive complement, “of every one that passes”. *Pria*, ‘first’, is omitted.

## Lines 121–148 — The Broken Bridge and Virgil’s Anger

> 121 ed a tal modo il suocero si stenta  
> 122 in questa fossa, e gli altri del concilio  
> 123 che fu per li Giudei mala sementa.”  

and after the like fashion his father-in-law is racked in this ditch, and the others of that Council, which was a seed of evil for the Jews.”

- *a tal modo* ‘in such a manner’ → “after the like fashion”: the manner phrase is recast, with “like” replacing ‘such’.
- *il suocero* ‘the father-in-law’ → “his father-in-law”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *si stenta* ‘suffers’ → “is racked”: the pronominal verb becomes a passive expression, more specifically suggesting torment.
- *del concilio* ‘of the council’ → “of that Council”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative.
- *che fu per li Giudei mala sementa* ‘which was for the Jews evil seed’ → “which was a seed of evil for the Jews”: the phrase “for the Jews” moves after the predicate noun phrase; the adjective becomes an ‘of’ phrase, and an indefinite article is supplied.

> 124 Allor vid’ io maravigliar Virgilio  
> 125 sopra colui ch’ era disteso in croce  
> 126 tanto vilmente nell’ eterno esilio.  

Then I saw Virgil wonder over him that was distended on the cross so ignominiously in the eternal exile.

- *vid’ io maravigliar Virgilio* ‘saw I wonder Virgil’ → “I saw Virgil wonder”: the subject precedes the finite verb, and “Virgil” precedes the infinitive of which he is the subject.
- *in croce* ‘on a cross’ → “on the cross”: English supplies a definite article where Italian has none.
- *tanto vilmente* ‘so vilely’ → “so ignominiously”: the adverb is rendered specifically in terms of disgrace.

> 127 Poscia drizzò al frate cotal voce :  

Afterwards he to the Friar addressed these words:

- *drizzò al frate* ‘directed to the friar’ → “he to the Friar addressed”: ‘directed’ becomes “addressed,” and the recipient phrase moves before the verb.
- *cotal voce* ‘such a voice’ → “these words”: singular ‘voice’ becomes plural “words,” and ‘such’ becomes the demonstrative “these”.

> 128 “Non vi dispiaccia, se vi lece, dirci  
> 129 se alla man destra giace alcuna foce,  

“Let it not displease you, so it be lawful for you, to tell us if on the right hand lies any gap

- *Non vi dispiaccia* ‘may it not displease you’ → “Let it not displease you”: the independent subjunctive is expressed through “Let”.
- *se vi lece* ‘if it is permitted to you’ → “so it be lawful for you”: the conditional is recast with “so” and subjunctive “be”; permission is expressed as lawfulness.
- *alla man destra* ‘at the hand right’ → “on the right hand”: the preposition changes, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *alcuna foce* ‘any outlet’ → “any gap”: the noun for an opening or exit becomes the more general “gap”.

> 130 onde noi ambedue possiamo uscirci  
> 131 senza costringer degli angeli neri,  
> 132 che vegnan d’ esto fondo a dipartirci.”  

by which we both may go out hence, without constraining any of the Black Angels to come and extricate us from this bottom.”

- *onde* ‘whence’ → “by which”: the relative adverb becomes a preposition and relative pronoun, expressing the route rather than simply the point of departure.
- *degli angeli neri* ‘some of the angels black’ → “any of the Black Angels”: the partitive becomes “any of” after “without,” and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *che vegnan d’ esto fondo a dipartirci* ‘that they come from this bottom to take us away’ → “to come and extricate us from this bottom”: the subordinate subjunctive becomes an infinitive dependent on “constraining”; the purpose infinitive becomes a coordinated infinitive; and the phrase indicating removal moves to the end. ‘Take us away’ is rendered more specifically as “extricate us”.

> 133 Rispose adunque: “Più che tu non speri  
> 134 s’ appressa un sasso, che dalla gran cerchia  
> 135 si muove, e varca tutti i vallon feri,  
> 136 salvo ch’ a questo è rotto e nol coperchia :  

So he answered: “Nearer than thou dost hope, there is a stone that moves from the great circular wall, and bridges all the cruel valleys, save that in this ’tis broken and covers it not:

- *Rispose adunque* ‘answered therefore’ → “So he answered”: the connective moves before the verb and becomes “So”.
- *Più che tu non speri s’ appressa un sasso* ‘more than thou dost hope draws near a stone’ → “Nearer than thou dost hope, there is a stone”: the comparative and verb of approach become “Nearer” with an existential construction. The expletive *non*, which does not negate the comparison, is omitted.
- *dalla gran cerchia* ‘from the great circuit’ → “from the great circular wall”: the noun becomes an adjective and noun, making “wall” explicit; the phrase moves after “moves”.
- *varca* ‘crosses’ → “bridges”: the verb is rendered more specifically as spanning something like a bridge.
- *tutti i vallon feri* ‘all the valleys fierce’ → “all the cruel valleys”: ‘fierce’ becomes “cruel,” and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *a questo* ‘at this one’ → “in this”: the preposition changes.
- *nol coperchia* ‘does not cover it’ → “covers it not”: the Italian preverbal negative and object pronoun both follow the verb in English, with the negative last.

> 137 montar potrete su per la ruina,  
> 138 che giace in costa e nel fondo soperchia.”  

you [will be able to] mount up by its ruins, which slope down the side, and on the bottom make a heap.”

- *montar potrete* ‘mount you will be able’ → “you [will be able to] mount”: the infinitive moves after the expression of future ability.
- *su per la ruina* ‘up along the ruin’ → “up by its ruins”: the route preposition changes, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the singular noun becomes plural.
- *che giace in costa* ‘which lies on the slope’ → “which slope down the side”: lying on a slope becomes sloping down a side; “down” is supplied, and the verb agrees with the translated plural “ruins”.
- *soperchia* ‘rises above’ → “make a heap”: the verb is paraphrased with a noun phrase specifying the form of the accumulation, again with plural agreement.

> 139 Lo duca stette un poco a testa china,  
> 140 poi disse : “Mal contava la bisogna  
> 141 colui che i peccator di là uncina.”  

The Guide stood still a while with head bent down, then said: “Falsely did he tell the way, who hooks the sinners yonder.”

- *Lo duca* ‘the leader’ → “The Guide”: the title is rendered in terms of guidance.
- *stette un poco* ‘stood a little’ → “stood still a while”: “still” makes the lack of movement explicit, and “a while” supplies a temporal noun.
- *Mal* ‘badly’ → “Falsely”: the adverb is interpreted specifically as untruthfulness.
- *contava* ‘was recounting’ → “did” … “tell”: the imperfect becomes an emphatic or inverted simple-past construction.
- *la bisogna* ‘the matter’ → “the way”: the general noun is interpreted specifically as information about the route.
- *colui che* ‘the one who’ → “he” … “who”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun placed before “tell,” while the relative clause remains after its object.
- *i peccator di là uncina* ‘the sinners yonder he hooks’ → “hooks the sinners yonder”: the verb moves before its object and the adverb of place.

> 142 E il frate: “Io udi’ già dire a Bologna  
> 143 del diavol vizii assai, tra i quali udi’  
> 144 ch’ egli è bugiardo e padre di menzogna.”  

And the Friar: “I heard once at Bologna many of the Devil’s vices told; amongst which, I heard that he is a liar and the father of lies.”

- *Io udi’ già dire a Bologna* ‘I heard once tell at Bologna’ → “I heard once at Bologna” … “told”: the active infinitive becomes a passive participle placed after the things reported; the place phrase consequently precedes the reported material.
- *del diavol vizii assai* ‘of the devil vices many’ → “many of the Devil’s vices”: the possessive becomes an English possessive form, and the following quantifier becomes the preceding partitive expression “many of”.
- *bugiardo* ‘untruthful’ → “a liar”: a predicative adjective becomes a noun phrase.
- *padre di menzogna* ‘father of falsehood’ → “the father of lies”: a definite article is supplied, and the abstract singular becomes a plural count noun.

> 145 Appresso il duca a gran passi sen gì,  
> 146 turbato un poco d’ ira nel sembiante;  

Then with large steps my Guide went on, somewhat disturbed with anger in his look;

- *il duca* ‘the leader’ → “my Guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive, the title is rendered in terms of guidance, and the subject moves after the phrase describing his steps.
- *sen gì* ‘went away’ → “went on”: departure becomes onward movement.
- *turbato un poco* ‘disturbed a little’ → “somewhat disturbed”: the degree phrase becomes a single adverb placed before the participle.
- *d’ ira* ‘by anger’ → “with anger”: the cause is expressed through “with”.
- *nel sembiante* ‘in the countenance’ → “in his look”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘countenance’ becomes “look”.

> 147 ond’ io dagl’ incarcati mi parti’  
> 148 dietro alle poste delle care piante.  

whereat I from the laden *spirits* parted, following the prints of his beloved feet.

- *ond’* ‘wherefore’ → “whereat”: a connective expressing consequence becomes one expressing a response to what precedes.
- *dagl’ incarcati* ‘from the laden’ → “from the laden *spirits*”: the substantival participle is given an explicit noun.
- *dietro alle poste* ‘behind the footprints’ → “following the prints”: the prepositional expression becomes a participial phrase, and ‘footprints’ is shortened to “prints”.
- *delle care piante* ‘of the dear soles’ → “of his beloved feet”: the definite article becomes a possessive, ‘dear’ becomes “beloved,” and the soles are rendered as the feet themselves.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Recasting

Carlyle’s translation stays close to the Italian’s sequence of expressions, often preserving its imagery, repetitions, and unusual phrasing. “A painted people,” “O weary mantle for eternity!” and “the eternal exile” retain conspicuous features of Dante’s wording. Yet word order is regularly adjusted: subjects and objects move before and after their verbs respectively, adjectives generally precede nouns, and possessives follow English usage. Thus *dorate son* becomes “they are gilded,” while *il maestro mio* becomes “my Master”. More generally, subjects implicit in Italian verb endings are supplied as English pronouns, and Italian articles before possessives are omitted.

Changes of construction frequently make the English more direct or explicit. *I’ ho pavento* becomes “I dread,” *ei ne verranno dietro* becomes “they will pursue us,” and the impersonal *è mestier ch’ ei senta* becomes “has to feel”. Reflexive forms often become ordinary intransitive verbs, while participial or gerundial expressions may become finite clauses. Conversely, clauses can be compressed into noun phrases: *com’ ei pesa* is represented by “the weight”. Italian imperfects are sometimes rendered as simple pasts, and English perfect or subjunctive constructions occasionally replace the Italian tense or mood.

The vocabulary ranges from close equivalents to interpretative substitutions. *Scoppia*, ‘bursts forth’, becomes “springs,” and *nacque*, ‘was born’, becomes “arose,” softening or generalizing the original metaphors. Elsewhere the English narrows a meaning: *senza compagnia* becomes “without escort,” *lepre* becomes “leveret,” and *fosca* becomes “brown”. The substitution of “Ay and Yea” for *mo ed issa* preserves the relation between synonymous expressions but changes them from expressions of time to affirmations. A more substantial departure occurs in the mother’s clothing: the English describes her not pausing to put on a shift, rather than fleeing wearing only one.

Small omissions and additions also recur. Repeated prepositions and articles disappear; possessives replace articles with parts of the body or personal belongings; and explanatory nouns such as “case” and “spirits” give explicit heads to expressions left less fully specified in Italian. Some elements, including *pure* and *pria*, receive no separate equivalent. Such adjustments coexist with passages of nearly word-for-word correspondence: the translation generally preserves Dante’s verbal detail, while repeatedly reshaping its grammar and occasionally making a particular interpretation more explicit than the Italian itself.
