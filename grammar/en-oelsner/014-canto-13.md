# Inferno 13

## Lines 1–21 — The Wood of the Harpies

> 1 Non era ancor di là Nesso arrivato,  
> 2 quando noi ci mettemmo per un bosco,  
> 3 che da nessun sentiero era segnato.  

Nessus had not yet reached the other side, when we moved into a wood, which by no path was marked.

- *Non era ancor di là Nesso arrivato* ‘Not had yet on the other side Nessus arrived’ → “Nessus had not yet reached the other side”: the subject moves to the beginning, and the negative follows the auxiliary; arriving at a place becomes reaching it, with “the other side” as the direct object.
- *ci mettemmo per* ‘set ourselves through’ → “moved into”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and movement through the wood becomes movement into it.

> 4 Non frondi verdi, ma di color fosco;  

Not green the foliage, but of colour dusky;

- *frondi verdi* ‘leaves green’ → “green the foliage”: the plural noun becomes a singular collective noun, “the” is supplied, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 5 non rami schietti, ma nodosi e involti;  

not smooth the branches, but gnarled and warped;

- *rami schietti* ‘branches straight’ → “smooth the branches”: “smooth” replaces ‘straight’, “the” is supplied, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *nodosi e involti* ‘knotty and entangled’ → “gnarled and warped”: knotting and entanglement are rendered as irregular growth and distortion.

> 6 non pomi v' eran, ma stecchi con tosco.  

apples none were there, but withered sticks with poison.

- *non pomi v' eran* ‘no fruits there were’ → “apples none were there”: the general ‘fruits’ becomes the specific “apples”; the noun precedes the negative “none”, and the locative follows the verb.
- *stecchi* ‘sticks’ → “withered sticks”: “withered” is supplied.

> 7 Non han sì aspri sterpi nè sì folti  
> 8 quelle fiere selvagge, che in odio hanno  
> 9 tra Cecina e Corneto i luoghi colti.  

No holts so rough or dense have those wild beasts, that hate the cultivated tracts, between Cecina and Corneto.

- *Non han sì aspri sterpi nè sì folti* ‘have not so rough thickets nor so dense’ → “No holts so rough or dense have”: the negative is attached to the noun rather than the verb; the object moves before “have”; ‘thickets’ becomes “holts”; the first adjective follows rather than precedes the noun; and the second *sì* is omitted, with *nè* rendered as “or” under the opening negative.
- *fiere selvagge* ‘beasts wild’ → “wild beasts”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *in odio hanno* ‘in hatred have’ → “hate”: the phrase becomes a single verb.
- *i luoghi colti* ‘the places cultivated’ → “the cultivated tracts”: ‘places’ becomes “tracts”, the adjective moves before the noun, and the whole object moves before the phrase locating it between Cecina and Corneto.

> 10 Quivi le brutte Arpíe lor nidi fanno,  
> 11 che cacciar delle Strofade i Troiani  
> 12 con tristo annunzio di futuro danno.  

Here the unseemly Harpies make their nest[s], who chased the Trojans from the Strophades with dismal note of future woe.

- *brutte* ‘ugly’ → “unseemly”: physical ugliness is rendered by a word also suggesting unsightliness or impropriety.
- *lor nidi fanno* ‘their nests make’ → “make their nest[s]”: the verb moves before its object.
- *cacciar delle Strofade i Troiani* ‘drove from the Strophades the Trojans’ → “chased the Trojans from the Strophades”: ‘drove’ becomes “chased”, and the direct object moves before the phrase expressing origin.
- *tristo annunzio* ‘sad announcement’ → “dismal note”: the announcement becomes a “note”, with ‘sad’ rendered more strongly as “dismal”.
- *futuro danno* ‘future harm’ → “future woe”: harm or damage becomes suffering or misfortune.

> 13 Ale hanno late, e colli e visi umani,  
> 14 piè con artigli, e pennuto il gran ventre;  

Wide wings they have, and necks and faces human, feet with claws, and their large belly feathered;

- *Ale hanno late* ‘Wings have wide’ → “Wide wings they have”: the adjective moves before the noun, rather than following the verb; the subject implicit in the verb is also expressed.
- *e pennuto il gran ventre* ‘and feathered the large belly’ → “and their large belly feathered”: the noun phrase moves before “feathered”, and the definite article becomes the possessive “their”.

> 15 fanno lamenti in su gli alberi strani.  

they make rueful cries on the strange trees.

- *lamenti* ‘laments’ → “rueful cries”: one noun is expanded into an adjective and noun.
- *in su gli alberi strani* ‘upon the trees strange’ → “on the strange trees”: the compound preposition is represented by “on”, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 16 Lo buon maestro: "Prima che più entre  
> 17 sappi che se' nel secondo girone,"  
> 18 mi cominciò a dire, "e sarai, mentre  
> 19 che tu verrai nell' orribil sabbione.  

The kind Master began to say to me: “Before thou goest farther, know that thou art in the second round; and shalt be, until thou comest to the horrid sand.

- *Lo buon maestro* ‘The good master’ → “The kind Master”: the general ‘good’ is interpreted specifically as kindness.
- *mi cominciò a dire* ‘to me began to say’ → “began to say to me”: the reporting clause moves from within the direct speech to its beginning, and the indirect object follows rather than precedes the verbal phrase.
- *Prima che più entre* ‘Before that thou enter more’ → “Before thou goest farther”: the conjunction phrase becomes “Before”; entering becomes going; *più* is interpreted spatially as “farther”; and the Italian present subjunctive becomes an English present indicative.
- *mentre che tu verrai* ‘while thou wilt come’ → “until thou comest”: the conjunction is interpreted as marking an endpoint, rather than simultaneity, and the future becomes the present customary in English temporal clauses.
- *nell' orribil sabbione* ‘into the horrible expanse of sand’ → “to the horrid sand”: movement into becomes movement to, and the noun denoting a sandy expanse becomes the material noun “sand”.

> 20 Però riguarda bene, e sì vedrai  
> 21 cose che torrien fede al mio sermone."  

Therefore look well, and thou shalt see things which would take away belief from my speech.”

- *e sì vedrai* ‘and thus thou wilt see’ → “and thou shalt see”: the connective or emphatic *sì* is not separately represented.
- *al mio sermone* ‘to my discourse’ → “from my speech”: the Italian dative is rendered with “from” to express deprivation after “take away”.

## Lines 22–54 — The Wounded Tree Speaks

> 22 Io sentia da ogni parte traer guai,  
> 23 e non vedea persona che il facesse :  
> 24 per ch' io tutto smarrito m' arrestai.  

Already I heard wailings uttered on every side, and saw no one to make them : wherefore I, all bewildered, stood still.

- *Io sentia* ‘I heard’ → “Already I heard”: “Already” is supplied.
- *da ogni parte traer guai* ‘from every side utter laments’ → “wailings uttered on every side”: the active infinitive becomes a passive participle qualifying “wailings”; the expression of place moves after it, and ‘from’ becomes “on”.
- *e non vedea persona che il facesse* ‘and I saw no person who did it’ → “and saw no one to make them”: “no one” replaces ‘no person’; the finite subjunctive relative clause becomes an infinitive construction. The singular *il*, referring to the action, becomes “them”, referring to the wailings.
- *per ch'* ‘because of which’ → “wherefore”: the prepositional relative expression becomes a single connective adverb.
- *m' arrestai* ‘I stopped myself’ → “stood still”: the reflexive verb becomes an intransitive expression describing the resulting stillness.

> 25 Io credo ch' ei credette ch' io credesse  
> 26 che tante voci uscisser tra que' bronchi  
> 27 da gente che per noi si nascondesse.  

I think he thought that I was thinking so many voices came, amongst those stumps, from people who hid themselves on our account.

- *Io credo* ‘I believe’ → “I think”: belief is expressed as thought.
- *ch' ei credette* ‘that he believed’ → “he thought”: the conjunction is omitted, and ‘believed’ again becomes “thought”.
- *ch' io credesse* ‘that I believed’ → “that I was thinking”: the imperfect subjunctive becomes an English past progressive, again using ‘think’ rather than ‘believe’.
- *che tante voci uscisser* ‘that so many voices came out’ → “so many voices came”: the conjunction is omitted; “came” does not explicitly retain the outward movement of *uscisser*. The Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *tra que' bronchi* ‘among those rough bushes’ → “amongst those stumps”: *bronchi* is interpreted as stumps rather than rough, tangled bushes.
- *da gente che per noi si nascondesse* ‘from people who because of us hid themselves’ → “from people who hid themselves on our account”: the expression of cause moves after the verb; the subjunctive becomes an indicative.

> 28 Però disse il maestro : “ Se tu tronchi  
> 29 qualche fraschetta d' una d' este piante,  
> 30 li pensier ch' hai si faran tutti monchi.”  

Therefore the Master said : “If thou breakest off any little shoot from one of these plants, the thoughts, which thou hast, will all become defective.”

- *disse il maestro* ‘said the master’ → “the Master said”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *Se tu tronchi* ‘if thou cuttest off’ → “If thou breakest off”: cutting or truncating is rendered as breaking.
- *qualche fraschetta* ‘some little leafy branch’ → “any little shoot”: “any” replaces ‘some’ within the condition; “little” expresses the diminutive, while “shoot” does not explicitly retain the leafy sense of *frasca*.
- *d' una d' este piante* ‘of one of these plants’ → “from one of these plants”: the first *di* becomes “from”, expressing separation.
- *si faran tutti monchi* ‘will become all maimed’ → “will all become defective”: “all” moves before “become”; the image of mutilation in *monchi* becomes the more abstract “defective”.

> 31 Allor porsi la mano un poco avante,  
> 32 e colsi un ramicel da un gran pruno ;  
> 33 e il tronco suo gridò: “Perchè mi schiante?”  

Then I stretched my hand a little forward, and plucked a branchlet from a great thorn ; and the trunk of it cried, “Why dost thou rend me?”

- *Allor porsi la mano un poco avante* ‘then I put the hand a little forward’ → “Then I stretched my hand a little forward”: “stretched” specifies the movement more closely than *porsi*; the definite article before the body part becomes a possessive.
- *il tronco suo* ‘its trunk’ → “the trunk of it”: the postpositive possessive adjective becomes an *of*-phrase.
- *Perchè mi schiante* ‘why dost thou snap me’ → “Why dost thou rend me?”: “rend” expresses tearing rather than snapping; the object pronoun follows the verb rather than preceding it.

> 34 Da che fatto fu poi di sangue bruno,  
> 35 ricominciò a gridar: “Perchè mi scerpi?  
> 36 non hai tu spirto di pietate alcuno?  

And when it had grown dark with blood, it again began to cry : “Why dost thou tear me? hast thou no breath of pity?

- *Da che* ‘after that’ → “And when”: “And” is supplied, and the temporal conjunction becomes “when”.
- *fatto fu poi di sangue bruno* ‘it was then made brown with blood’ → “it had grown dark with blood”: the passive simple past becomes an intransitive past perfect; *poi* is not separately rendered. “Dark” is less specifically a colour than *bruno*.
- *Perchè mi scerpi* ‘why dost thou pluck me apart’ → “Why dost thou tear me?”: “tear” gives a more general action than the stripping or plucking expressed by *scerpi*; the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *non hai tu spirto di pietate alcuno* ‘hast thou not any spirit of pity’ → “hast thou no breath of pity?”: *non … alcuno* becomes “no”, placed before the noun rather than distributed around the clause. *Spirto* is rendered as “breath” rather than ‘spirit’.

> 37 Uomini fummo, ed or sem fatti sterpi:  
> 38 ben dovrebb’ esser la tua man più pia,  
> 39 se state fossim' anime di serpi.”  

Men we were, and now are turned to trees : truly thy hand should be more merciful, had we been souls of serpents.”

- *ed or sem fatti sterpi* ‘and now we are made shrubs’ → “and now are turned to trees”: “turned to” replaces ‘made’; “trees” generalizes the low or scrubby growth denoted by *sterpi*.
- *dovrebb’ esser la tua man più pia* ‘ought to be thy hand more compassionate’ → “thy hand should be more merciful”: the subject moves before the verb; ‘ought to’ becomes “should”, and *pia* is expressed as “merciful”.
- *se state fossim' anime di serpi* ‘if we had been souls of serpents’ → “had we been souls of serpents”: the conditional conjunction is replaced by subject–auxiliary inversion. The Italian participle precedes its auxiliary; the English participle follows it.

> 40 Come d' un stizzo verde, che arso sia  
> 41 dall' un de' capi, che dall' altro geme  
> 42 e cigola per vento che va via:  

As a green brand, that is burning at one end, at the other drops, and hisses with the wind which is escaping

- *Come d' un stizzo verde* ‘as from a brand green’ → “As a green brand”: *di*, ‘from’, is omitted, allowing the brand to become the subject of “drops” and “hisses”; the adjective moves before the noun.
- *che arso sia* ‘that be burnt’ → “that is burning”: a passive subjunctive becomes an active present progressive indicative.
- *dall' un de' capi* ‘at one of the ends’ → “at one end”: the partitive construction is condensed into a singular noun phrase.
- *che dall' altro geme* ‘which from the other drips’ → “at the other drops”: the repeated relative pronoun is omitted, and ‘from’ becomes “at”.
- *e cigola* ‘and squeaks’ → “and hisses”: a different kind of sound is specified.
- *per vento* ‘because of wind’ → “with the wind”: the causal preposition becomes “with”, and a definite article is supplied.
- *che va via* ‘which goes away’ → “which is escaping”: the verb and adverb become a single verb of escape, in the progressive rather than the simple present.

> 43 sì della scheggia rotta usciva insieme  
> 44 parole e sangue: ond' io lasciai la cima  
> 45 cadere, e stetti come l' uom che teme.  

: so from that broken splint, words and blood came forth together : whereat I let fall the top, and stood like one who is afraid.

- *della scheggia rotta usciva insieme parole e sangue* ‘from the splinter broken came out together words and blood’ → “from that broken splint, words and blood came forth together”: the definite article becomes the demonstrative “that”; the adjective moves before its noun. The coordinated subject moves before the verb, with “together” following the verb.
- *ond'* ‘whence’ → “whereat”: the connective presents the reaction as occurring at what has happened, rather than as proceeding from it.
- *lasciai la cima cadere* ‘I let the top fall’ → “let fall the top”: the object moves after the infinitive.
- *e stetti come l' uom che teme* ‘and I stood like the man who fears’ → “and stood like one who is afraid”: the generic definite noun becomes the indefinite pronoun “one”; the verb ‘fears’ becomes a copular construction with “afraid”.

> 46 “ S' egli avesse potuto creder prima,”  
> 47 rispose il savio mio, “anima lesa,  
> 48 ciò ch' ha veduto pur con la mia rima,  

“If he, O wounded Spirit!” my Sage replied, “could have believed before, what he has seen only in my verse,

- *avesse potuto creder prima* ‘had been able to believe before’ → “could have believed before”: the pluperfect subjunctive of ‘be able’ becomes a modal perfect. This predicate is separated from its subject by the vocative and reporting clause.
- *rispose il savio mio* ‘replied my sage’ → “my Sage replied”: the subject moves before the verb, and the possessive moves before its noun. The reporting clause also comes earlier within the interrupted speech.
- *anima lesa* ‘soul wounded’ → “O wounded Spirit!”: “O” is supplied; ‘soul’ becomes “Spirit”, and the adjective moves before the noun. The vocative moves before the reporting clause.
- *ciò ch' ha veduto* ‘that which he has seen’ → “what he has seen”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun are combined into “what”.
- *pur con la mia rima* ‘only through my rhyme’ → “only in my verse”: instrumental *con* becomes locative “in”, while “verse” generalizes *rima*. The English attaches the phrase to “has seen”; on the usual construction of the Italian, it qualifies believing beforehand through the verse alone.

> 49 non averebbe in te la man distesa;  
> 50 ma la cosa incredibile mi fece  
> 51 indurlo ad opra, che a me stesso pesa.  

he would not have stretched forth his hand against thee ; but the incredibility of the thing made me prompt him to do what grieves myself.

- *non averebbe in te la man distesa* ‘he would not have stretched the hand against thee’ → “he would not have stretched forth his hand against thee”: “forth” is supplied, and the definite article becomes a possessive. *In te* moves after the verb and object, while the participle moves before the object.
- *ma la cosa incredibile* ‘but the incredible thing’ → “but the incredibility of the thing”: the adjective becomes an abstract noun, with *la cosa* subordinated to it in an *of*-phrase.
- *mi fece indurlo ad opra* ‘made me induce him to an act’ → “made me prompt him to do”: *indurre* becomes “prompt”; the prepositional noun phrase *ad opra* becomes the infinitive “to do”. The object *mi* moves after *fece*.
- *che a me stesso pesa* ‘which weighs upon myself’ → “what grieves myself”: the relative referring to *opra* becomes “what”, supplying the object of “do”. The metaphor of weight becomes an explicit expression of grief, and the prepositional complement becomes a direct object.

> 52 Ma dilli chi tu fosti, sì che, in vece  
> 53 d' alcuna ammenda, tua fama rinfreschi  
> 54 nel mondo su, dove tornar gli lece.”  

But tell him who thou wast ; so that, to make thee some amends, he may refresh thy fame up in the world, to which he is permitted to return.”

- *in vece d' alcuna ammenda* ‘in place of some amends’ → “to make thee some amends”: a phrase expressing substitution becomes an infinitive expressing purpose; “make” and “thee” are supplied.
- *tua fama rinfreschi* ‘thy fame he may refresh’ → “he may refresh thy fame”: the object moves after the verb, and the subjunctive is expressed with “may”.
- *nel mondo su* ‘in the world above’ → “up in the world”: the locative adverb moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *dove tornar gli lece* ‘where to return is permitted to him’ → “to which he is permitted to return”: ‘where’ becomes the directional “to which”; the impersonal construction with a dative becomes a personal passive. The infinitive moves after the expression of permission.

## Lines 55–87 — A Plea for Vindication

> 55 E il tronco : “Sì con dolce dir m’adeschi,  
> 56 ch’ io non posso tacere ; e voi non gravi  
> 57 perch’ io un poco a ragionar m’inveschi.  

And the trunk: “Thou so allurest me with thy sweet words, that I cannot keep silent; and let it not seem burdensome to you, if I enlarge a little in discourse.

- *Sì con dolce dir m’adeschi* ‘so with sweet speech thou allurest me’ → “Thou so allurest me with thy sweet words”: the verb and its object precede the phrase describing the means; singular *dir*, a substantival infinitive, becomes plural “words”, and “thy” is supplied.
- *tacere* ‘be silent’ → “keep silent”: the infinitive is rendered by a phrase suggesting continued silence.
- *e voi non gravi* ‘and may it not burden you’ → “and let it not seem burdensome to you”: the subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”; the verb becomes an adjective with “seem”, which is supplied.
- *perch’ io un poco a ragionar m’inveschi* ‘because I entangle myself a little in speaking’ → “if I enlarge a little in discourse”: the causal conjunction is interpreted conditionally; the reflexive metaphor becomes an intransitive expression for speaking at greater length, and “a little” follows the verb.

> 58 Io son colui, che tenni ambo le chiavi  
> 59 del cor di Federico, e che le volsi  
> 60 serrando e disserrando sì soavi,  
> 61 che dal segreto suo quasi ogni uom tolsi;  

I am he, who held both keys of Frederick’s heart, and turned them, locking and unlocking so softly, that from his secrets I excluded almost every other man;

- *ambo le chiavi del cor di Federico* ‘both the keys of the heart of Frederick’ → “both keys of Frederick’s heart”: the article before “keys” is omitted, and the nested possessive construction is recast with “Frederick’s” before “heart”.
- *e che le volsi* ‘and who them turned’ → “and turned them”: the second relative pronoun is omitted, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *sì soavi* ‘so gentle’ → “so softly”: the Italian adjectival form, used adverbially, becomes an English adverb.
- *dal segreto suo* ‘from the secret his’ → “from his secrets”: singular *segreto* becomes plural, and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *quasi ogni uom tolsi* ‘almost every man I removed’ → “I excluded almost every other man”: the verb precedes its object; removal is rendered as exclusion, and “other” is supplied.

> 62 fede portai al glorioso ufizio,  
> 63 tanto ch’ io ne perdei lo sonno e i polsi.  

so great fidelity I bore to the glorious office, that I lost thereby both sleep and life.

- *fede portai al glorioso ufizio, tanto* ‘faith I bore to the glorious office, so much’ → “so great fidelity I bore to the glorious office”: *tanto*, measuring the extent of the action, becomes “so great”, modifying “fidelity” and moving to the beginning.
- *ne perdei lo sonno e i polsi* ‘through it I lost sleep and the pulses’ → “I lost thereby both sleep and life”: the causal pronoun becomes “thereby” after the verb; “both” is supplied, and plural *polsi* is rendered by the singular “life” that the pulses signify.

> 64 La meretrice, che mai dall’ ospizio  
> 65 di Cesare non torse gli occhi putti,  
> 66 morte comune e delle corti vizio,  
> 67 infiammò contra me gli animi tutti;  

The harlot, that never from Cæsar’s dwelling turned her adulterous eyes, common bane, and vice of courts, inflamed all minds against me;

- *che mai dall’ ospizio di Cesare non torse gli occhi putti* ‘that never from the lodging of Caesar turned the whorish eyes’ → “that never from Cæsar’s dwelling turned her adulterous eyes”: *ospizio* becomes “dwelling”; the possessive construction is reversed; the article before “eyes” becomes “her”; and *putti* is interpreted as “adulterous”. The reinforcing *non* is absorbed into the single English negative “never”.
- *morte comune e delle corti vizio* ‘death common and of the courts vice’ → “common bane, and vice of courts”: “bane” replaces ‘death’; the adjective precedes its noun, while the dependent phrase follows “vice”; the article before “courts” is omitted.
- *infiammò contra me gli animi tutti* ‘inflamed against me the minds all’ → “inflamed all minds against me”: the object moves before “against me”, and *tutti* becomes a preceding determiner, without a separate article.

> 68 e gl’ infiammati infiammâr sì Augusto,  
> 69 che i lieti onor tornaro in tristi lutti.  

and these, [being inflamed,] so inflamed Augustus, that my joyous honours were changed to dismal sorrows.

- *e gl’ infiammati* ‘and the inflamed’ → “and these, [being inflamed,]”: the substantivized participle becomes the demonstrative “these” followed by an explanatory participial phrase; “being” is supplied.
- *infiammâr sì Augusto* ‘inflamed so Augustus’ → “so inflamed Augustus”: the degree adverb moves before the verb.
- *i lieti onor* ‘the joyous honours’ → “my joyous honours”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *tornaro in tristi lutti* ‘turned into sad mournings’ → “were changed to dismal sorrows”: an intransitive active verb becomes passive; *lutti*, specifically mournings or griefs, is rendered by the broader “sorrows”.

> 70 L’ animo mio per disdegnoso gusto,  
> 71 credendo col morir fuggir disdegno,  
> 72 ingiusto fece me contra me giusto.  

My soul, in its disdainful mood, thinking to escape disdain by death, made me, *though* just, unjust against myself.

- *L’ animo mio* ‘the soul my’ → “My soul”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *per disdegnoso gusto* ‘through disdainful taste’ → “in its disdainful mood”: a phrase expressing cause or motive becomes one describing a state; ‘taste’ becomes “mood”, and “its” is supplied.
- *credendo col morir fuggir disdegno* ‘believing by dying to flee disdain’ → “thinking to escape disdain by death”: *credendo* is rendered as “thinking”; the substantival infinitive *morir* becomes the noun “death”, and the means of escape moves after its object.
- *ingiusto fece me contra me giusto* ‘unjust made me against me just’ → “made me, *though* just, unjust against myself”: the predicate “unjust” moves after the verb and object; the final adjective becomes an explicit concessive phrase through the supplied “though”; the second *me* becomes reflexive “myself”.

> 73 Per le nuove radici d’ esto legno  
> 74 vi giuro che giammai non ruppi fede  
> 75 al mio signor, che fu d’ onor sì degno.  

By the new roots of this tree, I swear to you, never did I break faith to my lord, who was so worthy of honour.

- *d’ esto legno* ‘of this wood’ → “of this tree”: the word for the material is rendered as the whole living form.
- *vi giuro* ‘to you I swear’ → “I swear to you”: the pronominal recipient becomes a prepositional phrase after the verb.
- *che giammai non ruppi fede* ‘that never I broke faith’ → “never did I break faith”: the introducing ‘that’ is omitted; initial “never” produces English inversion with auxiliary “did”. The Italian negative combination is represented by “never” alone.
- *che fu d’ onor sì degno* ‘who was of honour so worthy’ → “who was so worthy of honour”: the complement moves after the adjective.

> 76 E se di voi alcun nel mondo riede,  
> 77 conforti la memoria mia, che giace  
> 78 ancor del colpo che invidia le diede.”  

And if any of you return to the world, strengthen the memory of me, which still lies prostrate from the blow that envy gave it.”

- *di voi alcun* ‘of you anyone’ → “any of you”: the partitive phrase follows the pronoun rather than preceding it.
- *nel mondo riede* ‘into the world returns’ → “return to the world”: the verb precedes the destination phrase; *nel* becomes “to”, and English “any of you” takes “return” rather than the Italian singular verb form.
- *conforti la memoria mia* ‘may he strengthen the memory my’ → “strengthen the memory of me”: the third-person exhortative subjunctive becomes an English imperative; the possessive adjective is expanded into “of me”.
- *che giace ancor* ‘which lies still’ → “which still lies prostrate”: the adverb moves before the verb, and “prostrate” is supplied to make the force of *giace* explicit.
- *che invidia le diede* ‘that envy to it gave’ → “that envy gave it”: the indirect-object pronoun follows the verb and is expressed without a preposition.

> 79 Un poco attese, e poi : “Da ch’ ei si tace,”  
> 80 disse il poeta a me, “non perder l’ ora;  
> 81 ma parla, e chiedi a lui se più ti piace.”  

The Poet listened a while, and then said to me: “Since he is silent, lose not the hour; but speak, and ask him, if thou wouldst know more.”

- *Un poco attese, e poi … disse il poeta a me* ‘a little he waited, and then … said the poet to me’ → “The Poet listened a while, and then said to me”: the subject moves to the beginning and serves both verbs; the reporting clause is brought forward before the speech. *Attese* is interpreted as listening rather than waiting, and the duration follows the verb.
- *Da ch’ ei si tace* ‘since he keeps silent’ → “Since he is silent”: the pronominal verb becomes a copular construction with an adjective.
- *non perder l’ ora* ‘not lose the hour’ → “lose not the hour”: the infinitive used as a negative imperative becomes an English imperative, with the negative following the verb.
- *e chiedi a lui* ‘and ask of him’ → “and ask him”: the prepositional recipient becomes a direct object.
- *se più ti piace* ‘if more pleases thee’ → “if thou wouldst know more”: an impersonal expression of preference becomes a personal expression of willingness; “know” is supplied, and “more” becomes its object.

> 82 Ond’ io a lui : “Domandal tu ancora  
> 83 di quel che credi che a me satisfaccia;  
> 84 ch’ io non potrei, tanta pietà m’ accora.”  

Whereat I to him: “Do thou ask him farther, respecting what thou thinkest will satisfy me; for I could not, such pity is upon my heart.”

- *Domandal tu ancora* ‘ask him thou again’ → “Do thou ask him farther”: the imperative is expanded with “Do”; the expressed subject precedes “ask”, and the attached object follows it. *Ancora* is interpreted as continuation, “farther”, rather than repetition.
- *di quel che credi che a me satisfaccia* ‘of that which thou believest that to me may give satisfaction’ → “respecting what thou thinkest will satisfy me”: *di* becomes “respecting”, and *quel che* contracts to “what”; the second *che* is omitted. The present subjunctive becomes “will satisfy”, and the recipient becomes a direct object after the verb.
- *tanta pietà m’ accora* ‘so much pity grieves my heart’ → “such pity is upon my heart”: the verb expressing heart-pain becomes a construction with “is upon”; the emotional effect is conveyed through a spatial expression rather than directly named.

> 85 Però ricominciò : “Se l’ uom ti faccia  
> 86 liberamente ciò che il tuo dir prega,  
> 87 spirito incarcerato, ancor ti piaccia  

He therefore resumed: “So may the man do freely for thee what thy words entreat him, O imprisoned spirit, please thee

- *Però ricominciò* ‘therefore he began again’ → “He therefore resumed”: the verb is rendered as resuming an interrupted action rather than beginning again.
- *Se l’ uom ti faccia liberamente* ‘if the man may do for thee freely’ → “So may the man do freely for thee”: the conditional form with a subjunctive is rendered as an explicit wish with “So may”; the beneficiary moves from before the verb to after the adverb.
- *ciò che il tuo dir prega* ‘that which thy speech entreats’ → “what thy words entreat him”: *ciò che* becomes “what”; the substantival infinitive *dir* becomes plural “words”, and “him” is supplied as the person entreated.
- *spirito incarcerato* ‘spirit imprisoned’ → “O imprisoned spirit”: the adjective moves before the noun, and the vocative marker “O” is supplied.
- *ancor ti piaccia* ‘may it also please thee’ → “please thee”: *ancor* is omitted, and the subjunctive is represented by the bare verb without an expressed subject or modal auxiliary.

## Lines 88–120 — Souls Bound in Trees and Fleeing Spirits

> 88 di dirne come l’ anima si lega  
> 89 in questi nocchi; e dinne, se tu puoi,  
> 90 s’ alcuna mai da tai membra si spiega.”  

tell us farther, how the soul gets bound up in these knots; and tell us, if thou mayest, whether any ever frees itself from such members.”

- *di dirne* ‘to tell us’ → “tell us farther”: the dependent infinitive becomes an imperative, and “farther” is supplied.
- *si lega* ‘binds itself’ → “gets bound up”: the Italian reflexive construction is rendered as a passive, with “up” added.
- *se tu puoi* ‘if thou canst’ → “if thou mayest”: ability is expressed through a modal that can also suggest permission.
- *si spiega* ‘unfolds itself’ → “frees itself”: the figurative verb is rendered explicitly as release.

> 91 Allor soffiò lo tronco forte, e poi  
> 92 si convertì quel vento in cotal voce:  

Then the trunk blew strongly, and soon that wind was changed into these words:

- *soffiò lo tronco* ‘blew the trunk’ → “the trunk blew”: the subject is moved before the verb.
- *poi* ‘then’ → “soon”: succession becomes a statement about how quickly the change occurs.
- *si convertì quel vento* ‘changed itself that wind’ → “that wind was changed”: the reflexive construction becomes passive, and the subject precedes the verb.
- *cotal voce* ‘such a voice’ → “these words”: singular voice becomes plural verbal content, and the expression of kind becomes a demonstrative.

> 93 “Brevemente sarà risposto a voi.  

“Briefly shall you be answered.

- *sarà risposto a voi* ‘an answer will be given to you’ → “shall you be answered”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal passive, making the recipients its subject.

> 94 Quando si parte l’ anima feroce  
> 95 dal corpo ond’ ella stessa s’ è divelta,  
> 96 Minos la manda alla settima foce.  

When the fierce spirit quits the body, from which it has torn itself, Minos sends it to the seventh gulf.

- *l’ anima feroce* ‘the soul fierce’ → “the fierce spirit”: “spirit” replaces ‘soul’, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *si parte … dal corpo* ‘departs … from the body’ → “quits the body”: a reflexive verb with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object.
- *ond’ ella stessa s’ è divelta* ‘from which it itself has torn itself’ → “from which it has torn itself”: the emphatic *stessa* is omitted.
- *foce* ‘mouth’ → “gulf”: the image of an opening is replaced by that of a gulf.

> 97 Cade in la selva e non l’ è parte scelta;  
> 98 ma là dove fortuna la balestra,  
> 99 quivi germoglia come gran di spelta;  

It falls into the wood, and no place is chosen for it; but wherever fortune flings it, there it sprouts, like grain of spelt;

- *non l’ è parte scelta* ‘not to it is a part chosen’ → “no place is chosen for it”: the negation is attached to the noun, *parte* becomes “place”, and the recipient phrase moves to the end.
- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “wherever”: the two-part locative expression becomes a single indefinite relative adverb.

> 100 surge in vermena ed in pianta silvestra;  

shoots up to a sapling, and to a savage plant;

- *surge in vermena* ‘rises into a shoot’ → “shoots up to a sapling”: rising is expressed by the phrasal verb “shoots up”; *in* becomes “to”, and the young shoot becomes a sapling.
- *ed in pianta silvestra* ‘and into a woodland plant’ → “and to a savage plant”: “to” replaces ‘into’, an indefinite article is supplied, and the adjective is placed before the noun; “savage” gives *silvestra* a stronger sense than merely woodland or wild.

> 101 l’ Arpíe, pascendo poi delle sue foglie,  
> 102 fanno dolore, ed al dolor finestra.  

the Harpies, feeding then upon its leaves, give pain, and to the pain an outlet.

- *fanno dolore* ‘make pain’ → “give pain”: the verb of making becomes one of giving.
- *ed al dolor finestra* ‘and to the pain a window’ → “and to the pain an outlet”: the concrete ‘window’ becomes the functional “outlet”, with an indefinite article supplied.

> 103 Come l’ altre verrem per nostre spoglie,  
> 104 ma non però ch’ alcuna sen rivesta:  
> 105 chè non è giusto aver ciò ch’ uom si toglie.  

Like the others, we shall go for our spoils, [but not to the end that any may be] clothe[d] with them again: for it is not just that a man have what he takes from himself.

- *verrem* ‘we shall come’ → “we shall go”: the direction expressed by the verb is reversed.
- *ma non però ch’ alcuna sen rivesta* ‘but not for this, that any should reclothe itself in them’ → “[but not to the end that any may be] clothe[d] with them again”: the translation makes the purposive interpretation of *però … che* explicit. The reflexive subjunctive becomes a modal passive, ‘in them’ becomes “with them”, and the repeated action expressed by the verb’s prefix is rendered by “again” at the end.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *aver ciò ch’ uom si toglie* ‘to have that which one takes from oneself’ → “that a man have what he takes from himself”: the infinitive becomes a finite subjunctive clause. The generic *uom*, originally the subject within the relative clause, becomes “a man” in the main clause and “he” in the relative clause; ‘that which’ is compressed to “what”.

> 106 Qui le strascineremo, e per la mesta  
> 107 selva saranno i nostri corpi appesi,  
> 108 ciascuno al prun dell’ ombra sua molesta.”  

Hither shall we drag them, and through the mournful wood our bodies shall be suspended, each on the thorny tree of its tormented shade.”

- *Qui* ‘here’ → “Hither”: location is expressed as direction.
- *le strascineremo* ‘them we shall drag’ → “shall we drag them”: the object moves after the verb, while the auxiliary precedes the subject following the initial directional adverb.
- *saranno i nostri corpi appesi* ‘shall be our bodies suspended’ → “our bodies shall be suspended”: the subject moves before the complete verbal phrase.
- *al prun* ‘on the thorn-bush’ → “on the thorny tree”: a single noun becomes a noun qualified by an adjective, and the bush is called a tree.
- *dell’ ombra sua molesta* ‘of the shade its troubled’ → “of its tormented shade”: the possessive and adjective move before the noun. “Tormented” takes *molesta* in the sense of afflicted rather than troublesome, and strengthens that sense.

> 109 Noi eravamo ancora al tronco attesi,  
> 110 credendo ch’ altro ne volesse dire,  
> 111 quando noi fummo d’ un romor sorpresi,  

We still were listening to the trunk, thinking it would tell us more, when by a noise we were surprised;

- *Noi eravamo ancora al tronco attesi* ‘We were still intent on the trunk’ → “We still were listening to the trunk”: the construction expressing attentiveness becomes a progressive verb specifically denoting listening; “still” also moves before “were”.
- *credendo* ‘believing’ → “thinking”: belief is expressed as thought.
- *ch’ altro ne volesse dire* ‘that it wished to tell us something else’ → “it would tell us more”: the conjunction is omitted, the subjunctive expression of wishing becomes “would”, and ‘something else’ becomes “more”; the object also moves after the verb.
- *quando noi fummo d’ un romor sorpresi* ‘when we were by a noise surprised’ → “when by a noise we were surprised”: the phrase identifying the cause moves before the subject.

> 112 similemente a colui che venire  
> 113 sente il porco e la caccia alla sua posta,  
> 114 ch’ ode le bestie e le frasche stormire.  

like one who feels the boar and chase approaching to his stand, who hears the beasts and the branches crashing.

- *similemente a colui che* ‘similarly to the one who’ → “like one who”: the adverb and preposition become “like”, and the definite demonstrative expression becomes indefinite “one”.
- *venire sente il porco e la caccia alla sua posta* ‘hears coming the pig and the chase to his stand’ → “feels the boar and chase approaching to his stand”: *sente* is interpreted as “feels” rather than ‘hears’; the infinitive becomes a participle and moves after the objects. “Approaching” replaces ‘coming’, “boar” makes *porco* more specific, and the article before *caccia* is omitted.
- *le frasche* ‘the leafy boughs’ → “the branches”: the reference to foliage is not expressed.
- *stormire* ‘rustle’ → “crashing”: the infinitive becomes a participle, and the sound is made more violent.

> 115 Ed ecco duo dalla sinistra costa,  
> 116 nudi e graffiati, fuggendo sì forte,  
> 117 che della selva rompièno ogni rosta.  

And, lo! on the left hand, two *spirits*, naked and torn, fleeing so violently that they broke every fan of the wood.

- *duo* ‘two’ → “two *spirits*”: the noun is supplied.
- *dalla sinistra costa* ‘from the left side’ → “on the left hand”: origin becomes location, “hand” replaces ‘side’, and the phrase moves before the numeral.
- *graffiati* ‘scratched’ → “torn”: the specific kind of injury becomes a broader and stronger expression.
- *sì forte* ‘so strongly’ → “so violently”: force or speed is expressed specifically as violence.
- *della selva rompièno ogni rosta* ‘of the wood they broke every screen’ → “they broke every fan of the wood”: the phrase ‘of the wood’ moves from before the verb to after the noun it qualifies. The translation chooses the fan sense of *rosta* rather than its sense of a screen or barrier.

> 118 Quel dinanzi: “Ora accorri, accorri, morte!”  

The foremost: “Come now, come, O death!”

- *Quel dinanzi* ‘The one in front’ → “The foremost”: a demonstrative with a spatial adverb becomes a substantival superlative.
- *Ora accorri, accorri, morte!* ‘Now run to help, run to help, death!’ → “Come now, come, O death!”: “now” moves after the first imperative; “come” leaves the haste and assistance implicit in *accorri* unexpressed, and the vocative “O” is supplied.

> 119 E l’ altro, a cui pareva tardar troppo,  
> 120 gridava: “Lano sì non furo accorte  

And the other, who thought himself too slow, cried: “Lano, thy legs were not so ready

- *a cui pareva tardar troppo* ‘to whom it seemed to delay too much’ → “who thought himself too slow”: the impersonal construction of seeming becomes a personal judgment. The infinitive becomes an adjective, “himself” supplies its understood subject as an object of “thought”, and the expression of excessive delay becomes one of excessive slowness.
- *sì non furo accorte* ‘so were not nimble’ → “thy legs were not so ready”: “thy legs” supplies a subject not expressed within these lines; “so” moves into the negated adjective phrase, and “ready” renders *accorte* as readiness rather than nimbleness.

## Lines 121–151 — The Hounds and the Wounded Bush

> 121 le gambe tue alle giostre del Toppo.”  

at the jousts of Toppo.”

- *le gambe tue alle giostre del Toppo* ‘thy legs at the jousts of the Toppo’ → “at the jousts of Toppo.”: ‘thy legs’ has no equivalent in the supplied English excerpt; the article attached to the place-name is also omitted.

> 122 E poichè forse gli fallía la lena  
> 123 di sè e d’ un cespuglio fece groppo.  

And [since] his breath perhaps [was] failing him, of himself and of a bush he made one group.

- *forse gli fallía la lena* ‘perhaps to him was failing the breath’ → “his breath perhaps [was] failing him”: the subject moves before the verb, and ‘perhaps’ follows it; the Italian dative is represented by “him”, while “his” additionally replaces the definite article.
- *groppo* ‘knot’ → “one group”: the image of a knot becomes a grouping, with “one” supplied.

> 124 Di retro a loro era la selva piena  
> 125 di nere cagne bramose e correnti,  
> 126 come veltri che uscisser di catena.  

Behind them, the wood was filled with black braches, eager and fleet, as greyhounds that have escaped the leash.

- *Di retro a loro era la selva piena* ‘behind them was the wood full’ → “Behind them, the wood was filled”: the subject moves before the verb, and the adjective ‘full’ becomes the participle “filled”.
- *di* ‘of’ → “with”: the preposition changes to suit “filled”.
- *cagne* ‘she-dogs’ → “braches”: the English specifies female hunting dogs.
- *correnti* ‘running’ → “fleet”: an active participle becomes an adjective describing speed.
- *uscisser di catena* ‘should come out of a chain’ → “have escaped the leash”: the imperfect subjunctive becomes an indicative present perfect; coming out becomes escaping, and ‘chain’ becomes “the leash”, with an article supplied.

> 127 In quel che s’ appiattò miser li denti,  
> 128 e quel dilaceraro a brano a brano;  
> 129 poi sen portar quelle membra dolenti.  

Into him, who squatted, they thrust their teeth, and rent him piece by piece; then carried off his miserable limbs.

- *In quel che s’ appiattò* ‘into that one who hid himself’ → “Into him, who squatted”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the reflexive verb of concealment is rendered as a bodily posture.
- *miser li denti* ‘they put the teeth’ → “they thrust their teeth”: “thrust” strengthens ‘put’, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *quel dilaceraro* ‘that one they tore apart’ → “rent him”: the demonstrative object becomes “him” and moves after the verb.
- *sen portar* ‘they carried away with them’ → “carried off”: the pronominal construction is reduced to an active phrasal verb, without separately expressing the reflexive element.
- *quelle membra dolenti* ‘those limbs suffering’ → “his miserable limbs”: the demonstrative becomes a possessive, and the adjective moves before the noun; suffering is expressed more generally as misery.

> 130 Presemi allor la mia scorta per mano,  
> 131 e menommi al cespuglio che piangea,  
> 132 per le rotture sanguinenti, invano.  

My Guide now took me by the hand, and led me to the bush, which was lamenting through its bleeding fractures, in vain.

- *Presemi allor la mia scorta* ‘took me then my guide’ → “My Guide now took me”: the subject moves to the beginning, the attached object pronoun becomes a separate word, and ‘then’ becomes “now”.
- *per mano* ‘by hand’ → “by the hand”: English supplies the definite article.
- *piangea* ‘was weeping’ → “was lamenting”: weeping is rendered as lamentation.
- *per le rotture sanguinenti* ‘through the fractures bleeding’ → “through its bleeding fractures”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 133 “O Jacomo,” dicea, “da Sant’ Andrea,  
> 134 che t’ è giovato di me fare schermo?  

“O Jacomo da Sant’ Andrea!” it cried, “what hast thou gained by making me thy screen?

- *dicea* ‘it was saying’ → “it cried”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and neutral saying becomes crying; the reporting clause follows the complete name rather than interrupting it.
- *che t’ è giovato di me fare schermo* ‘what has it availed thee to make a screen of me’ → “what hast thou gained by making me thy screen”: the person benefited, expressed by an Italian dative, becomes the subject of “gained”. The infinitive construction becomes a phrase with “by making”; ‘of me’ becomes the object “me”, and “thy” is supplied before “screen”.

> 135 che colpa ho io della tua vita rea?”  

what blame have I of thy sinful life?”

- *vita rea* ‘life sinful’ → “sinful life”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 136 Quando il maestro fu sopr’ esso fermo,  
> 137 disse: “Chi fusti, che per tante punte  
> 138 soffi con sangue doloroso sermo?”  

When the Master had stopped beside it, he said: “Who wast thou, who, through so many wounds, blowest forth with blood thy dolorous speech?”

- *fu sopr’ esso fermo* ‘was above it still’ → “had stopped beside it”: a simple-past statement of position or stillness becomes a pluperfect action; ‘above’ becomes “beside”.
- *punte* ‘punctures’ → “wounds”: the more specific injuries are rendered by a general term.
- *soffi* ‘thou blowest’ → “blowest forth”: “forth” is supplied to express outward emission.
- *con sangue doloroso sermo* ‘with blood dolorous speech’ → “with blood thy dolorous speech”: the possessive “thy” is supplied.

> 139 E quegli a noi: “O anime, che giunte  
> 140 siete a veder lo strazio disonesto,  
> 141 ch’ ha le mie fronde sì da me disgiunte,  
> 142 raccoglietele al piè del tristo cesto.  

And he to us: “Ye spirits, who are come to see the ignominious mangling which has thus disjoined my leaves from me, O gather them to the foot of the dismal shrub!

- *O anime* ‘O souls’ → “Ye spirits”: the vocative particle is replaced by a personal pronoun, and ‘souls’ becomes “spirits”.
- *giunte siete* ‘arrived are’ → “are come”: the auxiliary moves before the participle, and ‘arrived’ becomes “come”.
- *lo strazio disonesto* ‘the mangling shameful’ → “the ignominious mangling”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *ch’ ha le mie fronde sì da me disgiunte* ‘which has my leaves so from me disjoined’ → “which has thus disjoined my leaves from me”: the participle moves forward from the end of the clause, preceding both its object and “from me”; ‘so’ is rendered as “thus”.
- *raccoglietele* ‘gather them’ → “O gather them”: the interjection “O” is supplied before the imperative.
- *al piè del tristo cesto* ‘at the foot of the sorrowful clump’ → “to the foot of the dismal shrub”: position is expressed as destination, ‘sorrowful’ becomes “dismal”, and the clump or stock is rendered as a shrub.

> 143 Io fui della città che nel Batista  
> 144 mutò ’l primo patrono: ond’ ei per questo  
> 145 sempre con l’ arte sua la farà trista;  

I was of the city that changed its first patron for the Baptist, on which account he with his art will always make it sorrowful;

- *che nel Batista mutò ’l primo patrono* ‘that into the Baptist changed the first patron’ → “that changed its first patron for the Baptist”: the verb and its object precede the phrase naming the replacement; ‘into’ becomes “for” in the English construction for exchange, and the definite article becomes “its”.
- *ond’ ei per questo* ‘whence he for this’ → “on which account he”: the two causal expressions are combined into one, placed before the pronoun.
- *sempre con l’ arte sua la farà trista* ‘always with the art his it will make sorrowful’ → “with his art will always make it sorrowful”: “always” moves between the auxiliary and main verb, the possessive precedes its noun, and the object pronoun follows “make”.

> 146 e se non fosse che in sul passo d’ Arno  
> 147 rimane ancor di lui alcuna vista,  

and were it not that at the passage of the Arno there yet remains some semblance of him,

- *e se non fosse che* ‘and if it were not that’ → “and were it not that”: subject–verb inversion replaces the explicit conditional conjunction.
- *in sul passo d’ Arno* ‘upon the passage of Arno’ → “at the passage of the Arno”: “at” replaces the compound preposition, and an article is supplied before the river-name.
- *rimane ancor di lui alcuna vista* ‘remains still of him some sight’ → “there yet remains some semblance of him”: existential “there” is supplied, and “of him” moves after the noun it qualifies; *vista* is interpreted as a visible likeness or “semblance”.

> 148 quei cittadin, che poi la rifondarno  
> 149 sopra il cener che d’ Attila rimase,  
> 150 avrebber fatto lavorare indarno.  

those citizens, who afterwards rebuilt it on the ashes left by Attila, would have laboured in vain.

- *la rifondarno* ‘it they refounded’ → “rebuilt it”: founding anew becomes rebuilding, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *sopra il cener che d’ Attila rimase* ‘upon the ash that from Attila remained’ → “on the ashes left by Attila”: singular ‘ash’ becomes plural “ashes”; the finite relative clause becomes a passive participial phrase, with ‘from Attila’ recast as “by Attila”.
- *avrebber fatto lavorare indarno* ‘would have caused work to be done in vain’ → “would have laboured in vain”: the causative construction with *fatto* is suppressed, making the grammatical subject perform the labour directly.

> 151 Io fei giubbetto a me delle mie case.”  

I made a gibbet for myself of my own dwelling.”

- *giubbetto* ‘gibbet’ → “a gibbet”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *a me* ‘for me’ → “for myself”: English uses an explicitly reflexive pronoun.
- *delle mie case* ‘of my houses’ → “of my own dwelling”: the plural becomes singular, ‘houses’ becomes “dwelling”, and “own” adds emphasis to the possessive.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Recasting

Carlyle’s translation remains close to the Italian in the sequence of its descriptions, its repetitions, and many of its images, but regularly adjusts their grammatical arrangement. Adjectives commonly move before their nouns, objects follow rather than precede verbs, and possessives move into their usual English position. Reporting clauses are sometimes brought forward so that they introduce speech instead of interrupting it. Yet the translation also preserves conspicuously Italianate inversions, as in “Men we were” and “No holts so rough or dense have”, together with parallel negatives and repeated words.

Choices of vocabulary sometimes narrow, broaden, or intensify the Italian. *Pomi*, ‘fruits’, becomes “apples”; *sterpi*, ‘shrubs’ or ‘scrub’, becomes “trees”; and *stormire*, ‘rustle’, becomes “crashing”. Figurative language is often retained, notably the keys of Frederick’s heart and the repeated language of inflaming. Elsewhere it is explained or made more abstract: *monchi*, ‘maimed’, becomes “defective”, *finestra*, ‘window’, becomes “outlet”, and *i polsi*, ‘the pulses’, becomes “life”. Such choices preserve the general force while changing the particular image or emphasis.

Changes of construction recur throughout. Reflexive or pronominal verbs become English intransitives or passives: *m’ arrestai* becomes “stood still”, and *si lega* becomes “gets bound up”. Italian subjunctives are variously represented by indicatives, modal expressions, or imperatives; infinitives and finite clauses also exchange roles. More substantial recasting occurs when *la cosa incredibile*, ‘the incredible thing’, becomes “the incredibility of the thing”, or when *in vece d’ alcuna ammenda*, ‘in place of some amends’, becomes “to make thee some amends”. These are departures not merely in order but in how the thought is grammatically presented.

Small additions and omissions further distinguish the prose from a word-for-word rendering. “Already”, “withered”, and “prostrate” supply temporal, descriptive, or explanatory detail; connective or emphatic words such as *sì*, *poi*, and *stessa* sometimes receive no separate equivalent. Conversely, Italian negative combinations are compressed into a single English negative. Two routine adjustments, generally left unrecorded in the comparison, are the supply of subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and the omission of definite articles before possessives. Alongside these ordinary accommodations to English, the translation retains much of the Italian’s verbal texture, producing prose that alternates between direct structural echoes and explanatory recasting.
