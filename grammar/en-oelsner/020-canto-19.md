# Inferno 19

## Lines 1–18 — The Third Chasm’s Round Holes

> 1 O Simon mago, o miseri seguaci,  
> 2 che le cose di Dio, che di bontate  
> 3 deono essere spose, e voi rapaci  
> 4 per oro e per argento adulterate;  

O Simon Magus! O wretched followers of his and robbers ye, who prostitute the things of God, that should be wedded unto righteousness, for gold and silver!

- *seguaci* ‘followers’ → “followers of his”: the possessive phrase is supplied.
- *che … adulterate* ‘who … adulterate’ → “who prostitute”: the verb is moved before its object, and adulteration is rendered as prostitution.
- *che di bontate deono essere spose* ‘that of goodness must be brides’ → “that should be wedded unto righteousness”: “should” replaces the stronger obligation of *deono*; the noun *spose* becomes the participle “wedded”; and ‘of goodness’ becomes “unto righteousness,” following the verb rather than preceding it.
- *e voi rapaci* ‘and you, rapacious’ → “and robbers ye”: the resumptive address becomes a coordinated vocative; the adjective becomes a noun, and the pronoun follows it. The phrase is also moved ahead of the relative clause.
- *per oro e per argento* ‘for gold and for silver’ → “for gold and silver”: the repeated preposition is omitted.

> 5 or convien che per voi suoni la tromba,  
> 6 però che nella terza bolgia state.  

now must the trump sound for you: for ye are in the third chasm.

- *convien che* ‘it is necessary that’ → “must”: an impersonal verb introducing a subordinate clause becomes a modal governing the main verb.
- *per voi suoni la tromba* ‘for you may sound the trumpet’ → “the trump sound for you”: the subject moves before the verb and the prepositional phrase after it; the subjunctive becomes a bare infinitive governed by “must.”
- *però che* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *state* ‘you stand’ → “ye are”: standing or remaining is rendered as being, and the verb moves before the phrase specifying location.
- *bolgia* ‘pouch’ → “chasm”: the Italian designation is rendered by a term describing the physical hollow.

> 7 Già eravamo alla seguente tomba  
> 8 montati, dello scoglio in quella parte  
> 9 che appunto sopra mezzo il fosso piomba.  

Already we had mounted to the following grave, on that part of the cliff which hangs right over the middle of the foss.

- *eravamo … montati* ‘we were … mounted’ → “we had mounted”: the Italian pluperfect, formed with ‘be,’ is rendered with the English auxiliary “had”; the intervening destination phrase follows the completed verb.
- *dello scoglio in quella parte* ‘of the rock in that part’ → “on that part of the cliff”: the modifying phrase follows “part” rather than preceding it; “on” replaces ‘in,’ and *scoglio* is rendered specifically as “cliff.”
- *che appunto sopra mezzo il fosso piomba* ‘which precisely over the middle of the ditch plunges’ → “which hangs right over the middle of the foss”: the downward action of *piomba* becomes the static “hangs,” and the verb moves from the end of the clause to immediately after its subject.

> 10 O somma Sapienza, quanta è l'arte  
> 11 che mostri in cielo, in terra e nel mal mondo,  
> 12 e quanto giusto tua virtù comparte!  

O Wisdom Supreme, what art thou shewest in heaven, on earth and in the evil world, and how justly thy Goodness dispenses!

- *somma Sapienza* ‘supreme Wisdom’ → “Wisdom Supreme”: the adjective follows the noun instead of preceding it.
- *quanta è l'arte che mostri* ‘how great is the art that thou showest’ → “what art thou shewest”: the construction with ‘is’ and a relative clause becomes a direct exclamation with “what”; the copula, definite article, and relative pronoun disappear.
- *in terra* ‘in earth’ → “on earth”: the preposition changes to the customary English expression.
- *giusto* ‘just’ → “justly”: an Italian adjective used adverbially becomes an explicitly formed English adverb.
- *tua virtù* ‘thy power’ → “thy Goodness”: the rendering interprets *virtù* as moral goodness rather than power or efficacy.

> 13 Io vidi per le coste e per lo fondo  
> 14 piena la pietra livida di fori  
> 15 d' un largo tutti, e ciascuno era tondo.  

I saw the livid stone, on the sides and on the bottom, full of holes, all of one breadth; and each was round.

- *per le coste e per lo fondo* ‘along the sides and along the bottom’ → “on the sides and on the bottom”: the prepositions express surface location rather than extension; the phrase moves after the object of “saw.”
- *piena la pietra livida di fori* ‘full the stone livid of holes’ → “the livid stone, on the sides and on the bottom, full of holes”: *piena* moves after the object noun, joining its complement instead of being separated from it by that noun; *livida* moves before the noun.
- *d'un largo tutti* ‘of one breadth all’ → “all of one breadth”: the quantifier moves before the phrase describing breadth.

> 16 Non mi parean meno ampi nè maggiori  
> 17 che quei che son nel mio bel San Giovanni  
> 18 fatti per loco de' battezzatori;  

Not less wide they seemed to me, nor larger, than those that are in my beauteous San Giovanni made for stands to the baptizers;

- *Non mi parean meno ampi* ‘they did not seem to me less wide’ → “Not less wide they seemed to me”: the negative and comparative adjective are brought together before the verb; the pronoun *mi* becomes the following phrase “to me.”
- *per loco de' battezzatori* ‘for a place of the baptizers’ → “for stands to the baptizers”: singular *loco* becomes plural “stands,” specifying its function; the possessive relation expressed by *de’* becomes “to,” presenting the stands as assigned to the baptizers.

## Lines 19–51 — Burning Soles and an Inverted Sinner

> 19 l' un delli quali, ancor non è molt' anni,  
> 20 rupp' io per un che dentro vi annegava :  

one of which, not many years ago, I broke to save one that was drowning in it:

- *l'un delli quali* ‘the one of which’ → “one of which”: the definite article is omitted.
- *ancor non è molt' anni* ‘it is not yet many years’ → “not many years ago”: the Italian present-tense construction measuring elapsed time becomes an English phrase with “ago”; *ancor* is not separately represented.
- *rupp' io* ‘broke I’ → “I broke”: the subject is placed before the verb.
- *per un che dentro vi annegava* ‘for one who inside there was drowning’ → “to save one that was drowning in it”: “to save” makes the purpose explicit; *dentro vi* becomes “in it” and moves after “was drowning”.

> 21 e questo fia suggel ch' ogni uomo sganni.  

and be this a seal to undeceive all men.

- *questo fia suggel* ‘this shall be a seal’ → “be this a seal”: the future indicative becomes an optative construction, with the verb before the subject.
- *ch' ogni uomo sganni* ‘that may undeceive every man’ → “to undeceive all men”: the relative clause with a subjunctive verb becomes an infinitive phrase; singular ‘every man’ becomes plural “all men”, and the object follows rather than precedes the verb.

> 22 Fuor della bocca a ciascun soperchiava  
> 23 d'un peccator li piedi, e delle gambe  
> 24 infino al grosso; e l'altro dentro stava.  

From the mouth of each emerged a sinner's feet, and legs up to the calf; and the rest remained within.

- *Fuor della bocca a ciascun* ‘out of the mouth for each’ → “From the mouth of each”: *fuor della* is compressed into “From the”, and the distributive *a ciascun* becomes a possessive construction.
- *soperchiava* ‘projected’ → “emerged”: projection beyond an opening is rendered as emergence from it.
- *d'un peccator li piedi* ‘of a sinner the feet’ → “a sinner's feet”: the preceding prepositional possessive becomes an English possessive determiner.
- *e delle gambe infino al grosso* ‘and of the legs up to the thick part’ → “and legs up to the calf”: the partitive construction is dropped, and *il grosso*, literally the thick part, is given the anatomical name “the calf”.
- *l'altro dentro stava* ‘the other part within remained’ → “the rest remained within”: *l'altro* becomes the collective “the rest”, and the verb precedes the adverb.

> 25 Le piante erano a tutti accese intrambe:  

The soles of all were both on fire:

- *Le piante erano a tutti accese intrambe* ‘the soles were for all alight both’ → “The soles of all were both on fire”: the distributive *a tutti* becomes “of all” beside the noun; “both” moves before the predicate, and *accese* becomes the prepositional phrase “on fire”.

> 26 per che sì forte guizzavan le giunte,  
> 27 che spezzate averian ritorte e strambe.  

wherefore the joints quivered so strongly, that they would have snapped in pieces withes and grass-ropes.

- *per che* ‘for which’ → “wherefore”: the preposition and relative pronoun become a single connective adverb.
- *sì forte guizzavan le giunte* ‘so strongly quivered the joints’ → “the joints quivered so strongly”: the subject moves before the verb, and the adverbial phrase moves after it.
- *spezzate averian* ‘broken they would have’ → “they would have snapped in pieces”: the participle moves after the conditional auxiliary, and *spezzate* is expanded into “snapped in pieces”.

> 28 Qual suole il fiammeggiar delle cose unte  
> 29 moversi pur su per l' estrema buccia,  
> 30 tal era lì da' calcagni alle punte.  

As the flaming of things oiled moves only on their outer surface: so was it there, from the heels to the points.

- *suole ... moversi* ‘is accustomed ... to move’ → “moves”: the habitual auxiliary is omitted, and the infinitive becomes the finite verb following its subject.
- *pur su per l' estrema buccia* ‘only up along the outermost skin’ → “only on their outer surface”: “on” replaces the directional phrase *su per*; “their” is supplied, the superlative becomes “outer”, and *buccia* is rendered less figuratively as “surface”.
- *tal era lì* ‘such was it there’ → “so was it there”: the predicative adjective *tal* becomes the adverb “so”.

> 31 “Chi è colui, maestro, che si cruccia,  
> 32 guizzando più che gli altri suoi consorti,”  
> 33 diss'io, “e cui più rossa fiamma succia?”  

"Master! who is that who writhes himself, quivering more than all his fellows," I said, "and sucked by ruddier flame?"

- *Chi è colui, maestro* ‘who is that, master’ → “Master! who is that”: the address moves from inside the question to its beginning.
- *si cruccia* ‘torments himself’ → “writhes himself”: the translation expresses torment through bodily movement.
- *gli altri suoi consorti* ‘his other companions’ → “all his fellows”: “all” replaces ‘other’.
- *diss'io* ‘said I’ → “I said”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *cui più rossa fiamma succia* ‘whom redder flame sucks’ → “sucked by ruddier flame”: the active relative clause becomes a passive participial phrase; the relative pronoun is omitted, and the flame becomes the agent introduced by “by”.

> 34 Ed egli a me: “Se tu vuoi ch'io ti porti  
> 35 laggiù per quella ripa che più giace,  
> 36 da lui saprai di sè e de' suoi torti.”  

And he to me: "If thou wilt have me carry thee down there, by that lower bank, thou shalt learn from him about himself and about his wrongs."

- *Se tu vuoi ch'io ti porti* ‘if thou wishest that I carry thee’ → “If thou wilt have me carry thee”: the finite subordinate clause becomes “have” followed by an object and a bare infinitive; the object corresponding to *ti* follows “carry”.
- *per quella ripa che più giace* ‘by that bank which lies more nearly flat’ → “by that lower bank”: the relative clause becomes an attributive adjective; “lower” expresses relative height rather than the gentler inclination conveyed by *più giace*.
- *da lui saprai di sè e de' suoi torti* ‘from him thou shalt know of himself and of his wrongs’ → “thou shalt learn from him about himself and about his wrongs”: “from him” moves after the verb; *saprai* becomes “shalt learn”, emphasizing the acquisition of knowledge, and *di* is rendered as “about”.

> 37 Ed io: “Tanto m'è bel, quanto a te piace:  

And I: "Whatever pleases thee, to me is grateful:

- *Tanto m'è bel, quanto a te piace* ‘as much is pleasing to me as pleases thee’ → “Whatever pleases thee, to me is grateful”: the correlative construction expressing equal degree becomes a clause introduced by “Whatever”; the two clauses reverse order, and *m'è bel* is rendered by “to me is grateful”.

> 38 tu se' signore, e sai ch' io non mi parto  
> 39 dal tuo volere, e sai quel che si tace.”  

thou art my lord, and knowest that I depart not from thy will; also thou knowest what is not spoken."

- *tu se' signore* ‘thou art lord’ → “thou art my lord”: “my” is supplied.
- *ch' io non mi parto* ‘that I do not withdraw myself’ → “that I depart not”: the pronominal Italian verb becomes an intransitive English verb without a reflexive pronoun, and the negative follows the verb.
- *e sai quel che si tace* ‘and knowest that which is kept silent’ → “also thou knowest what is not spoken”: *e* becomes “also”; *quel che* is compressed into “what”, and the affirmative expression for keeping something silent becomes a negative expression about speaking.

> 40 Allor venimmo in su l' argine quarto;  

Then we came upon the fourth bulwark;

- *in su l' argine quarto* ‘onto the embankment fourth’ → “upon the fourth bulwark”: the prepositional expression becomes “upon”, *argine* is rendered as “bulwark”, and the ordinal moves before the noun.

> 41 volgemmo, e discendemmo a mano stanca  
> 42 laggiù nel fondo foracchiato ed arto.  

we turned and descended, on the left hand, down there into the perforated and narrow bottom.

- *a mano stanca* ‘on the tired hand’ → “on the left hand”: the idiomatic *stanca* is rendered by its intended directional meaning rather than its literal adjective.
- *fondo foracchiato ed arto* ‘bottom perforated and narrow’ → “perforated and narrow bottom”: both adjectives move before the noun.

> 43 Lo buon maestro ancor della sua anca  
> 44 non mi dipose, sì mi giunse al rotto  
> 45 di quei che sì piangeva con la zanca.  

The kind Master did not yet depose me from his side, till he brought me to the cleft of him who so lamented with his legs.

- *Lo buon maestro* ‘the good master’ → “The kind Master”: the broader *buon* is rendered specifically as “kind”.
- *ancor ... non mi dipose* ‘still ... did not set me down’ → “did not yet depose me”: *ancor ... non* is reorganized as “not yet”; the object pronoun follows the verb, and “depose” renders the act of setting down.
- *della sua anca* ‘from his hip’ → “from his side”: the specific anatomical term becomes the broader “side”.
- *mi giunse* ‘me he brought’ → “he brought me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *piangeva* ‘wept’ → “lamented”: the translation uses a broader expression of grief rather than the specific act of weeping.
- *con la zanca* ‘with the shank’ → “with his legs”: the singular becomes plural, “his” replaces the definite article, and “legs” is less anatomically specific than *zanca*.

> 46 “O qual che se', che 'l di su tien di sotto,  
> 47 anima trista, come pal commessa,”  
> 48 comincia' io a dir, “se puoi, fa motto.”  

"O whoe'er thou be that hast thy upper part beneath, unhappy spirit, planted like a stake!" I began to say; "if thou art able, speak."

- *O qual che se'* ‘O whoever thou art’ → “O whoe'er thou be”: the indicative becomes the English subjunctive.
- *che 'l di su tien di sotto* ‘who keepest the above below’ → “that hast thy upper part beneath”: the substantivized *il di su* is expanded into “thy upper part”, with a possessive supplied; *tien* becomes “hast”.
- *anima trista* ‘soul sad’ → “unhappy spirit”: the adjective precedes the noun, and “unhappy spirit” replaces ‘sad soul’.
- *come pal commessa* ‘like a stake set’ → “planted like a stake”: the participle moves before the comparison, and *commessa* is rendered more specifically as “planted”.
- *comincia' io a dir* ‘began I to say’ → “I began to say”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *se puoi, fa motto* ‘if thou canst, make an utterance’ → “if thou art able, speak”: the modal verb becomes “art able”, and the verb–noun expression *fa motto* becomes the single verb “speak”.

> 49 Io stava come il frate che confessa  
> 50 lo perfido assassin, che poi ch' è fitto  
> 51 richiama lui, per che la morte cessa;  

I stood, like the friar who is confessing a treacherous assassin that, after being fixed, recalls him and thus delay[s] the death;

- *confessa* ‘confesses’ → “is confessing”: the simple present becomes the present progressive.
- *lo perfido assassin* ‘the treacherous assassin’ → “a treacherous assassin”: the definite article becomes indefinite.
- *poi ch' è fitto* ‘after he is fixed’ → “after being fixed”: the finite temporal clause becomes a nonfinite construction with “being”.
- *per che la morte cessa* ‘whereby he delays death’ → “and thus delay[s] the death”: the relative connective becomes the coordinating “and” with “thus”. The translation takes *cessa* transitively, meaning ‘delays’, with *la morte* as its preceding object; English places that object after the verb.

## Lines 52–84 — A Simonist’s Mistake and Prediction

> 52 ed ei gridò: “Sei tu già costì ritto,  
> 53 sei tu già costì ritto, Bonifazio?  

and he cried: "Art thou there already standing, Boniface? art thou there already standing?

- *Sei tu già costì ritto, sei tu già costì ritto, Bonifazio* ‘Art thou already there upright, art thou already there upright, Boniface’ → “Art thou there already standing, Boniface? art thou there already standing?”: “already” follows rather than precedes the indication of place; the adjective *ritto* becomes the participle “standing.” “There” does not retain *costì*’s specific reference to the hearer’s location. The address moves from the second question to the first.

> 54 Di parecchi anni mi mentì lo scritto.  

By several years the writ has lied to me.

- *mi mentì lo scritto* ‘to me lied the writing’ → “the writ has lied to me”: the subject moves before the verb and the indirect object after it; the simple past becomes a present perfect.

> 55 Se’ tu sì tosto di quell’ aver sazio,  
> 56 per lo qual non temesti torre a inganno  
> 57 la bella Donna, e di poi farne strazio?”  

Art thou so quickly sated with that wealth, for which thou didst not fear to seize the comely Lady by deceit, and then make havoc of her?"

- *torre a inganno la bella Donna* ‘to take by deceit the beautiful Lady’ → “to seize the comely Lady by deceit”: “seize” gives *torre* a more forceful sense, “comely” renders *bella*, and the phrase expressing means moves after the object.
- *di poi* ‘afterwards’ → “then”: the temporal phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *farne strazio* ‘make of her havoc’ → “make havoc of her”: the pronominal *ne* becomes a prepositional phrase placed after the noun.

> 58 Tal mi fec’ io, quai son color che stanno,  
> 59 per non intender ciò ch’ è lor risposto,  
> 60 quasi scornati, e risponder non sanno.  

I became like those who stand as if bemocked, not comprehending what is answered to them, and unable to reply.

- *Tal mi fec’ io, quai son color che stanno* ‘Such I made myself, as are those who stand’ → “I became like those who stand”: the reflexive construction becomes “became,” the expressed subject moves before the verb, and the correlative *tal … quai* becomes “like”; *son* is not separately rendered.
- *per non intender ciò ch’ è lor risposto* ‘because of not understanding that which is answered to them’ → “not comprehending what is answered to them”: the causal preposition is omitted, the infinitive becomes a participle, and ‘that which’ becomes “what.”
- *quasi scornati* ‘as if put to shame’ → “as if bemocked”: the rendering emphasizes being mocked rather than the resulting shame; the phrase moves before the explanation of their incomprehension.
- *e risponder non sanno* ‘and to reply they know not how’ → “and unable to reply”: a finite verb expressing lack of knowledge becomes an adjective expressing inability, and “to reply” moves after it.

> 61 Allor Virgilio disse: “Digli tosto,  
> 62 ‘Non son colui, non son colui che credi.’ ”  

Then Virgil said: "Say to him quickly, ‘I am not he, I am not he whom thou thinkest.'"

- *Non son colui, non son colui* ‘I am not that one, I am not that one’ → “I am not he, I am not he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.

> 63 Ed io risposi come a me fu imposto.  

And I replied as was enjoined me.

- *come a me fu imposto* ‘as to me was enjoined’ → “as was enjoined me”: the indirect object moves after the passive verb, with “me” used without a preposition.

> 64 Per che lo spirto tutti storse i piedi;  

Whereat the spirit quite wrenched his feet;

- *Per che* ‘because of which’ → “Whereat”: the causal connection becomes a compact expression of reaction to the preceding words.
- *tutti storse i piedi* ‘twisted his feet all over’ → “quite wrenched his feet”: *tutti*, grammatically agreeing with the feet, is rendered as an adverb intensifying the action; “wrenched” gives a forceful rendering of *storse*, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 65 poi sospirando e con voce di pianto,  
> 66 mi disse: “Dunque che a me richiedi?  

thereafter, sighing and with voice of weeping, he said to me: "Then what askest thou of me?

- *mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “he said to me”: the indirect object follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *che a me richiedi* ‘what of me dost thou ask’ → “what askest thou of me”: “of me” moves from before the verb to the end of the question.

> 67 Se di saper chi io sia ti cal cotanto,  
> 68 che tu abbi però la ripa corsa,  
> 69 sappi ch’ io fui vestito del gran manto;  

If to know who I am concerneth thee so much, that thou hast therefore passed the bank, learn that I was clothed with the Great Mantle;

- *chi io sia* ‘who I be’ → “who I am”: the subjunctive in the indirect question becomes an English indicative.
- *ti cal cotanto* ‘matters to thee so much’ → “concerneth thee so much”: the construction with an indirect object becomes a transitive verb with a direct object.
- *che tu abbi però la ripa corsa* ‘that thou have therefore the bank traversed’ → “that thou hast therefore passed the bank”: the perfect subjunctive becomes an indicative present perfect, and the participle moves before its object. *Corsa* is understood as traversing or passing the bank, without retaining the literal suggestion of running.
- *sappi* ‘know’ → “learn”: the imperative denotes acquiring knowledge rather than knowing.

> 70 e veramente fui figliuol dell’ orsa,  
> 71 cupido sì, per avanzar gli orsatti,  
> 72 che su l’ avere, e qui me misi in borsa.  

and verily I was a son of the She-bear, so eager to advance the Whelps, that I pursed wealth above, and here myself.

- *cupido sì* ‘eager so’ → “so eager”: the degree adverb moves before the adjective.
- *per avanzar gli orsatti* ‘to advance the little bears’ → “to advance the Whelps”: the diminutive noun specifying bears becomes a more general noun for young animals.
- *che su l’ avere, e qui me misi in borsa* ‘that above wealth, and here myself, I put in a purse’ → “that I pursed wealth above, and here myself”: “pursed” compresses the verb and prepositional phrase into a single verb, which moves before the objects. “Above” moves after “wealth,” and the article before *avere* is omitted.

> 73 Di sotto al capo mio son gli altri tratti  
> 74 che precedetter me simoneggiando,  
> 75 per le fessure della pietra piatti.  

Beneath my head are dragged the others who preceded me in simony, cowering [within] the fissure[s] of the stone.

- *Di sotto al capo mio* ‘beneath the head mine’ → “Beneath my head”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *son gli altri tratti* ‘are the others dragged’ → “are dragged the others”: the participle moves beside its auxiliary, ahead of the subject.
- *simoneggiando* ‘practising simony’ → “in simony”: the verbal gerund becomes a prepositional phrase containing a noun.
- *per* ‘through’ → “[within]”: the preposition is interpreted as indicating location within the openings rather than extension through them.
- *piatti* ‘flat’ → “cowering”: an adjective describing position becomes a participle suggesting an attitude or action; it moves before the phrase specifying location.

> 76 Laggiù cascherò io altresì, quando  
> 77 verrà colui ch’ io credea che tu fossi,  
> 78 allor ch’ io feci il subito dimando.  

I too shall fall down thither, when he comes for whom I took thee when I put the sudden question.

- *Laggiù cascherò io altresì* ‘Down there shall fall I also’ → “I too shall fall down thither”: the subject and additive adverb move before the verb, while the indication of place moves to the end. “Thither” makes the direction of movement explicit.
- *quando verrà colui* ‘when will come that one’ → “when he comes”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun placed before the verb; the future becomes the present normally used in an English future-time clause.
- *ch’ io credea che tu fossi* ‘whom I believed that thou wert’ → “for whom I took thee”: the construction with an embedded clause becomes “took” with an object and a “for” complement.
- *allor ch’ io feci il subito dimando* ‘then when I made the sudden question’ → “when I put the sudden question”: *allor che* is compressed to “when,” and English “put” replaces ‘made’ in the expression for asking a question.

> 79 Ma più è il tempo già che i piè mi cossi  
> 80 e ch’ io son stato così sottosopra,  
> 81 ch’ ei non starà piantato coi piè rossi:  

But longer is the time already, that I have baked my feet and stood inverted thus, than he shall stand planted with glowing feet:

- *più* ‘more’ → “longer”: the comparative is made specific to duration.
- *che i piè mi cossi* ‘that the feet for myself I cooked’ → “that I have baked my feet”: the simple past becomes a present perfect; the object moves after the verb, and the personal pronoun indicating whose feet are affected becomes “my.” “Baked” specifies a kind of cooking.
- *e ch’ io son stato così sottosopra* ‘and that I have been thus upside down’ → “and stood inverted thus”: the repeated relative conjunction is omitted, and the auxiliary is shared with the preceding verb. ‘Been’ becomes “stood,” “inverted” renders *sottosopra*, and “thus” moves after it.
- *ch’ ei non starà* ‘than he will not stand’ → “than he shall stand”: the Italian comparative construction’s expletive negative is omitted.
- *coi piè rossi* ‘with the feet red’ → “with glowing feet”: “glowing” interprets rather than directly reproduces ‘red’; the adjective moves before the noun, and the definite article is omitted.

> 82 chè dopo lui verrà, di più laid’ opra,  
> 83 di ver ponente un pastor senza legge,  
> 84 tal che convien che lui e me ricopra.  

for after him, from westward, there shall come a lawless Shepherd, of uglier deeds, fit to cover him and me.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating causal conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *verrà* ‘will come’ → “there shall come”: introductory “there” is supplied before the verb.
- *di più laid’ opra* ‘of fouler work’ → “of uglier deeds”: the singular collective *opra* becomes plural “deeds”; the phrase moves from before the indication of origin and the subject to after the subject.
- *di ver ponente* ‘from towards the west’ → “from westward”: the directional expression is compressed, and the phrase moves before the verb.
- *un pastor senza legge* ‘a shepherd without law’ → “a lawless Shepherd”: the postposed prepositional phrase becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *tal che convien che lui e me ricopra* ‘such that it is necessary that he cover him and me’ → “fit to cover him and me”: the consecutive construction with impersonal *convien* becomes an adjective followed by an infinitive. “Fit” interprets the requirement as fitness or suitability rather than explicitly stating necessity.

## Lines 85–117 — A Rebuke of Avaricious Shepherds

> 85 Nuovo Jason sarà, di cui si legge  
> 86 ne' 'Maccabei'; e come a quel fu molle  
> 87 suo re, così fia a lui chi Francia regge."  

A new Jason will it be, of whom we read in Maccabees; and as to that *high priest* his king was pliant, so to this shall be he who governs France."

- *Nuovo Jason sarà* ‘a new Jason he will be’ → “A new Jason will it be”: the supplied subject is “it,” rather than a personal pronoun, and follows the verb.
- *di cui si legge* ‘of whom one reads’ → “of whom we read”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal construction with “we”.
- *ne' 'Maccabei'* ‘in the Maccabees’ → “in Maccabees”: the definite article is omitted.
- *a quel* ‘to that one’ → “to that *high priest*”: “high priest” is supplied to identify the demonstrative’s referent.
- *fu molle suo re* ‘was soft his king’ → “his king was pliant”: the subject moves before the verb, and figurative ‘softness’ becomes pliancy.
- *fia a lui* ‘shall be to him’ → “to this shall be”: the complement moves before the verb, and the personal pronoun becomes a demonstrative.
- *chi Francia regge* ‘who France rules’ → “he who governs France”: the independent relative becomes “he who,” and the object moves after the verb.

> 88 Io non so s'io mi fui qui troppo folle,  
> 89 ch' io pur risposi lui a questo metro :  

I know not if here I was too hardy, for I answered him in this strain:

- *Io non so* ‘I not know’ → “I know not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *s'io mi fui qui* ‘if I myself was here’ → “if here I was”: “here” moves before the subject, and the pronominal *mi* is not separately represented.
- *troppo folle* ‘too foolish’ → “too hardy”: foolishness is rendered as excessive boldness.
- *ch'* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *io pur risposi lui* ‘I indeed answered him’ → “I answered him”: the emphatic *pur* is omitted.
- *a questo metro* ‘to this measure’ → “in this strain”: the preposition changes, and the figurative expression is recast.

> 90 "Deh or mi di', quanto tesoro volle  
> 91 nostro Signore in prima da san Pietro,  
> 92 che gli ponesse le chiavi in balìa?  

"Ah! now tell me how much treasure our Lord required of St Peter, before he put the keys into his keeping?

- *mi di'* ‘to me tell’ → “tell me”: the object pronoun moves after the imperative.
- *quanto tesoro volle nostro Signore* ‘how much treasure wanted our Lord’ → “how much treasure our Lord required”: the subject moves before the verb, and wanting becomes requiring.
- *da san Pietro* ‘from St Peter’ → “of St Peter”: the preposition changes to suit “required”.
- *in prima … che* ‘first … that’ → “before”: the separated elements of the temporal construction become a single conjunction introducing the subordinate clause.
- *gli ponesse le chiavi in balìa* ‘to him he should put the keys in control’ → “he put the keys into his keeping”: the indirect-object pronoun becomes possessive “his”; *balìa* is rendered as “keeping”; and the imperfect subjunctive becomes an English simple past.

> 93 Certo non chiese se non : 'viemmi retro.'  

Surely he demanded nought but ‘Follow me!'

- *non chiese se non* ‘he did not ask except’ → “he demanded nought but”: the negative-and-exception construction becomes a verb with “nought but,” and asking becomes demanding.
- *viemmi retro* ‘come behind me’ → “Follow me!”: the verb, pronoun, and directional expression become a transitive verb with its object.

> 94 Nè Pier nè gli altri chiesero a Mattia  
> 95 oro od argento, quando fu sortito  
> 96 al loco che perdè l' anima ria.  

Nor did Peter, nor the others, ask of Matthias gold or silver, when he was chosen for the office which the guilty soul had lost.

- *Nè Pier nè gli altri chiesero* ‘neither Peter nor the others asked’ → “Nor did Peter, nor the others, ask”: the first negative becomes “Nor,” with auxiliary “did” and inversion.
- *a Mattia* ‘to Matthias’ → “of Matthias”: the Italian construction for asking someone is replaced by the English construction with “of”.
- *fu sortito* ‘he was selected by lot’ → “he was chosen”: the reference to selection by lot is not expressed.
- *al loco* ‘to the place’ → “for the office”: ‘place’ is interpreted as an office, and the preposition changes.
- *che perdè l' anima ria* ‘which lost the wicked soul’ → “which the guilty soul had lost”: the subject moves before the verb, the adjective moves before its noun, “guilty” replaces ‘wicked,’ and the simple past becomes a past perfect.

> 97 Però ti sta, chè tu se' ben punito ;  
> 98 e guarda ben la mal tolta moneta,  
> 99 ch' esser ti fece contra Carlo ardito.  

Therefore stay thou *here*, for thou art justly punished; and keep well the ill-got money, which against Charles made thee be bold.

- *ti sta* ‘stay’ → “stay thou *here*”: the pronominal imperative becomes an imperative with an explicit subject, and “here” is supplied.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *ben punito* ‘well punished’ → “justly punished”: *ben* is interpreted as expressing the justice of the punishment.
- *la mal tolta moneta* ‘the wrongly taken coin’ → “the ill-got money”: the participial phrase becomes the compound adjective “ill-got,” and *moneta* is rendered collectively as “money”.
- *ch' esser ti fece contra Carlo ardito* ‘which to be thee made against Charles bold’ → “which against Charles made thee be bold”: the infinitive moves after the causative verb and its object, while “against Charles” moves before them.

> 100 E se non fosse che ancor lo mi vieta  
> 101 la riverenza delle somme chiavi,  
> 102 che tu tenesti nella vita lieta,  
> 103 i' userei parole ancor più gravi :  

And were it not that reverence for the Great Keys thou heldest in the glad life yet hinders me, I should use still heavier words:

- *E se non fosse* ‘and if it were not’ → “And were it not”: conditional inversion replaces the conjunction *se*.
- *che ancor lo mi vieta* ‘that still it to me forbids’ → “that” and “yet hinders me”: the verb and its modifiers move after the subject and its relative clause; *lo* is omitted, *mi* becomes a direct object, and forbidding something becomes hindering someone.
- *la riverenza delle somme chiavi* ‘the reverence of the highest keys’ → “reverence for the Great Keys”: the initial article is omitted, “for” replaces ‘of,’ and the superlative *somme* becomes the positive adjective “Great”.
- *che tu tenesti* ‘which thou heldest’ → “thou heldest”: the object relative pronoun is omitted.
- *nella vita lieta* ‘in the life glad’ → “in the glad life”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *i' userei parole ancor più gravi* ‘I would use words still more weighty’ → “I should use still heavier words”: the conditional is expressed with “should,” and the adjective with its modifiers moves before the noun.

> 104 chè la vostra avarizia il mondo attrista,  
> 105 calcando i buoni e sollevando i pravi.  

for your avarice grieves the world, trampling on the good, and raising up the wicked.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *la vostra avarizia il mondo attrista* ‘your avarice the world saddens’ → “your avarice grieves the world”: the object moves after the verb, and saddening is expressed as grieving.
- *calcando i buoni* ‘trampling the good’ → “trampling on the good”: the direct-object construction becomes a construction with “on”.
- *sollevando* ‘raising’ → “raising up”: the particle “up” is supplied.

> 106 Di voi pastor s' accorse il Vangelista,  
> 107 quando colei, che siede sopra l' acque,  
> 108 puttaneggiar co' regi a lui fu vista ;  

Shepherds such as ye the Evangelist perceived, when she, that sitteth on the waters, was seen by him committing fornication with the kings;

- *Di voi pastor s' accorse il Vangelista* ‘of you shepherds took notice the Evangelist’ → “Shepherds such as ye the Evangelist perceived”: the prepositional complement of ‘took notice’ becomes the direct object of “perceived”; the address to ‘you shepherds’ becomes “Shepherds such as ye”; and the subject moves before the verb.
- *colei* ‘that woman’ → “she”: the demonstrative expression becomes a personal pronoun.
- *puttaneggiar co' regi a lui fu vista* ‘to fornicate with the kings to him was seen’ → “was seen by him committing fornication with the kings”: the passive verb moves first; the infinitive becomes a participial phrase using a verb and noun; and *a lui* is rendered as an agent phrase with “by”.

> 109 quella che con le sette teste nacque,  
> 110 e dalle dieci corna ebbe argomento,  
> 111 fin che virtute al suo marito piacque.  

she that was born with seven heads, and in her ten horns had a witness so long as virtue pleased her spouse.

- *quella* ‘that one’ → “she”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *con le sette teste nacque* ‘with the seven heads was born’ → “was born with seven heads”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase, and the definite article is omitted.
- *dalle dieci corna ebbe argomento* ‘from the ten horns had support’ → “in her ten horns had a witness”: ‘from’ becomes “in,” the definite article becomes possessive “her,” and *argomento* is interpreted as evidence or testimony rather than support.
- *virtute al suo marito piacque* ‘virtue to her husband was pleasing’ → “virtue pleased her spouse”: the verb moves before its complement, the Italian indirect object becomes an English direct object, and “spouse” replaces the gender-specific ‘husband’.

> 112 Fatto v' avete Dio d' oro e d' argento ;  

Ye have made you a god of gold and silver;

- *Fatto v' avete Dio* ‘made for yourselves you have God’ → “Ye have made you a god”: the participle moves after the auxiliary; the reflexive-benefactive pronoun becomes “you” after the verb; and an indefinite article is supplied before “god”.
- *d' oro e d' argento* ‘of gold and of silver’ → “of gold and silver”: the repeated preposition is omitted.

> 113 e che altro è da voi all' idolatre,  
> 114 se non ch' egli uno, e voi n' orate cento?  

and wherein do ye differ from the idolater, save that he worships one, and ye a hundred?

- *e che altro è da voi all' idolatre* ‘and what other thing is there from you to the idolater’ → “and wherein do ye differ from the idolater”: the impersonal question about a difference becomes a question with “ye” as subject and “differ” as verb; the paired prepositions become a single “from”.
- *se non* ‘if not’ → “save”: the expression of exception is condensed into one word.
- *ch' egli uno, e voi n' orate cento* ‘that he one, and you of them worship a hundred’ → “that he worships one, and ye a hundred”: the verb, elliptical in the first Italian clause and explicit in the second, is supplied in the first English clause and omitted in the second; the partitive pronoun *n'* is not separately represented.

> 115 Ahi, Constantin, di quanto mal fu matre,  
> 116 non la tua conversion, ma quella dote  
> 117 che da te prese il primo ricco patre!"  

Ah Constantine! to how much ill gave birth, not thy conversion, but that dower which the first rich Father took from thee!"

- *di quanto mal fu matre* ‘of how much ill was mother’ → “to how much ill gave birth”: the noun predicate ‘was mother’ becomes the verbal expression “gave birth,” with a corresponding change of preposition.
- *che da te prese il primo ricco patre* ‘which from thee took the first rich father’ → “which the first rich Father took from thee”: the subject moves before the verb, and “from thee” moves after it.

## Lines 118–133 — Rebuke and Return to the Bridge

> 118 E mentre io gli cantava cotai note,  
> 119 o ira o coscienza che il mordesse,  
> 120 forte springava con ambo le piote.  

And whilst I sung these notes to him, whether it was rage or conscience gnawed him, he violently sprawled with both his feet.

- *io gli cantava* ‘I to him was singing’ → “I sung” and “to him”: the indirect object moves after the verb and its direct object; the Italian imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *cotai* ‘such’ → “these”: a description of the kind of notes becomes a demonstrative reference to them.
- *o ira o coscienza che il mordesse* ‘whether anger or conscience bit him’ → “whether it was rage or conscience gnawed him”: “it was” is supplied, recasting the alternatives in a cleft construction without an expressed relative pronoun; “gnawed” replaces ‘bit’, and the Italian subjunctive is not separately marked.
- *forte springava* ‘strongly he was kicking’ → “he violently sprawled”: the adverb follows the subject rather than preceding it; kicking becomes sprawling, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *con ambo le piote* ‘with both the soles’ → “with both his feet”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the soles are rendered more generally as feet.

> 121 Io credo ben che al mio duca piacesse,  
> 122 con sì contenta labbia sempre attese  
> 123 lo suon delle parole vere espresse.  

And indeed I think it pleased my Guide, with so satisfied a look did he keep listening to the sound of the true words uttered.

- *Io credo ben che* ‘I believe indeed that’ → “And indeed I think”: “And” is supplied, ‘believe’ becomes “think”, the reinforcing adverb moves before the subject, and the conjunction introducing the following clause is omitted.
- *al mio duca piacesse* ‘to my leader it was pleasing’ → “it pleased my Guide”: the recipient moves after the verb and becomes its direct object; ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”, and the imperfect subjunctive following *credo* becomes an ordinary English past.
- *con sì contenta labbia* ‘with so contented a countenance’ → “with so satisfied a look”: the words for contentment and countenance are rendered by the related expressions “satisfied” and “look”.
- *sempre attese lo suon* ‘always he attended to the sound’ → “did he keep listening to the sound”: the adverb becomes the continuative construction “keep listening”; attending becomes specifically listening, and the auxiliary precedes the subject.
- *delle parole vere espresse* ‘of the words true expressed’ → “of the true words uttered”: “true” moves before its noun, and ‘expressed’ becomes “uttered”.

> 124 Però con ambo le braccia mi prese,  
> 125 e poi che tutto su mi s' ebbe al petto,  
> 126 rimontò per la via onde discese;  

Therefore with both his arms he took me; and, when he had me quite upon his breast, remounted by the path where he had descended;

- *con ambo le braccia* ‘with both the arms’ → “with both his arms”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *mi prese* ‘me he took’ → “he took me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *poi che* ‘after that’ → “when”: the explicit indication of subsequent action becomes a general temporal conjunction.
- *tutto su mi s' ebbe al petto* ‘all up he had me to himself at the breast’ → “he had me quite upon his breast”: the object moves after the verb, and *tutto* becomes the following adverb “quite”; *su … al* is compressed into “upon”, while the reflexive *si* is not separately expressed and the definite article becomes “his”.
- *onde discese* ‘by which he descended’ → “where he had descended”: the expression of route becomes a relative adverb of place, and the simple past becomes a past perfect.

> 127 nè si stancò d' avermi a sè distretto,  
> 128 sì mi portò sopra il colmo dell' arco,  
> 129 che dal quarto al quinto argine è tragetto.  

nor did he weary in holding me clasped to him, till he bore me away to the summit of the arch which is a crossway from the fourth to the fifth rampart.

- *si stancò* ‘he wearied himself’ → “did he weary”: the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb, with auxiliary inversion after “nor”.
- *d' avermi a sè distretto* ‘of having me to himself clasped’ → “in holding me clasped to him”: ‘of having’ becomes “in holding”; the phrase referring back to the subject follows the participle and uses “him” rather than an explicit reflexive.
- *sì* ‘so’ → “till”: the connective is interpreted temporally, as marking the endpoint of the preceding action.
- *mi portò* ‘me he carried’ → “he bore me away”: the object follows the verb, and “away” is supplied.
- *sopra* ‘onto’ → “to”: the translation expresses the destination without separately expressing position upon it.
- *dal quarto al quinto argine è tragetto* ‘from the fourth to the fifth embankment is a crossing’ → “is a crossway from the fourth to the fifth rampart”: the verb and its complement move before the phrase of extent; ‘crossing’ becomes “crossway”, and the embankment is termed a “rampart”.

> 130 Quivi soavemente spose il carco,  
> 131 soave per lo scoglio sconcio ed erto,  
> 132 che sarebbe alle capre duro varco;  

Here he placidly set down the burden [placidly] on the rough steep cliff, which to the goats would be a painful passage;

- *soavemente … soave* ‘gently … gentle’ → “placidly” and “[placidly]”: gentleness is rendered as placidity; the second Italian form, an adjective used adverbially, becomes an explicit English adverb.
- *per lo scoglio sconcio ed erto* ‘because of the rock ill-shaped and steep’ → “on the rough steep cliff”: the translation takes *per* locally rather than causally; ‘rock’ becomes “cliff”, ‘ill-shaped’ becomes “rough”, the adjectives move before the noun, and the conjunction between them is omitted.
- *che sarebbe alle capre duro varco* ‘which would be for the goats a hard passage’ → “which to the goats would be a painful passage”: the phrase referring to the goats moves before the verb and uses “to” rather than ‘for’; ‘hard’ becomes “painful”.

> 133 indi un altro vallon mi fu scoperto.  

thence another valley was discovered to me.

- *mi fu scoperto* ‘to me was uncovered’ → “was discovered to me”: the recipient moves after the passive verb, and ‘uncovered’ becomes “discovered”, here used in the sense of being disclosed to view.

## Summary — Close Echoes and Prose Recasting

Carlyle’s translation stays close to the Italian’s sequence of statements, addresses, comparisons, and repetitions, while frequently rearranging words within them. Subjects generally move before verbs, objects and complements after them, and descriptive adjectives before their nouns: *piena la pietra livida di fori* becomes “the livid stone” followed by “full of holes.” Yet the English also preserves conspicuous inversions, as in “Not less wide they seemed to me” and “Beneath my head are dragged the others.” Its prose thus combines ordinary English ordering with constructions that echo the Italian’s emphasis.

Changes of construction often make the rendering more compact or more idiomatic. *Convien che* becomes “must,” *fa motto* becomes “speak,” and *misi in borsa* becomes “pursed.” Elsewhere, a clause becomes an adjective or phrase: *senza legge* is rendered as “lawless,” while *simoneggiando* becomes “in simony.” The active relative clause *cui più rossa fiamma succia* becomes the passive “sucked by ruddier flame.” Italian subjunctives often become English indicatives, and past tenses are adjusted to English temporal usage. Such changes preserve the broad relation between the statements without reproducing their grammatical form.

Word choice sometimes specifies or interprets what the Italian leaves more general, and sometimes changes the force of an image. *Il grosso*, ‘the thick part,’ becomes “the calf”; *virtù*, ‘power’ or ‘efficacy,’ becomes “Goodness”; and *piomba*, ‘plunges,’ becomes the static “hangs.” Other choices broaden or redirect the expression: *piangeva* becomes “lamented,” *folle* becomes “hardy,” and *piatti*, ‘flat,’ becomes “cowering.” Alongside these departures, many concrete details and figurative expressions remain closely recognizable, including the round holes, burning soles, “Great Mantle,” and “She-bear.”

Small omissions and additions recur throughout. Repeated prepositions and relative conjunctions are sometimes dropped, as are elements such as the habitual *suole* and emphatic *pur*. Conversely, “to save” makes the purpose of *per un* explicit, and “followers of his” supplies a possessive relation. The revised text likewise includes explanatory wording such as “high priest” and the bracketed “[within].” More generally, beyond the individual changes recorded in the comparison, English regularly supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits Italian articles before possessives. These routine adjustments help make the prose readable while leaving much of the Italian’s verbal texture, repetition, and rhetorical shape intact.
