# Inferno 21

## Lines 1–18 — Boiling Pitch in Malebolge

> 1 Così di ponte in ponte altro parlando,  
> 2 che la mia Commedìa cantar non cura,  
> 3 venimmo, e tenevamo il colmo, quando  

THus from bridge to bridge we came, with other talk which my Comedy cares not to recite ; and held the summit, when

- *Così di ponte in ponte altro parlando, che la mia Commedìa cantar non cura, venimmo* ‘thus from bridge to bridge, speaking of other things which my Comedy to sing does not care, we came’ → “THus from bridge to bridge we came, with other talk which my Comedy cares not to recite”: the main verb is moved forward, before the phrase about talking and its relative clause. The participial construction ‘speaking of other things’ becomes the prepositional phrase “with other talk”, and the unspecified things discussed become “talk”. “recite” replaces ‘sing’, and the infinitive follows rather than precedes the governing verb.

> 4 ristemmo per veder l' altra fessura  
> 5 di Malebolge, e gli altri pianti vani;  
> 6 e vidila mirabilmente oscura.  

we stood still to see the other cleft of Malebolge and [the] other vain lamentings; and I found it marvellously dark.

- *ristemmo* ‘we stopped’ → “we stood still”: the act of stopping is expressed as standing motionless.
- *gli altri pianti vani* ‘the other lamentations vain’ → “[the] other vain lamentings”: the final adjective moves before the noun.
- *vidila* ‘I saw it’ → “I found it”: seeing becomes finding or discovering; the attached object pronoun becomes a separate word after the verb.

> 7 Quale nell' arzanà de' Viniziani  
> 8 bolle l' inverno la tenace pece  
> 9 a rimpalmar li lor legni non sani,  

As in the arsenal of the Venetians boils the clammy pitch in winter, to caulk their damaged ships,

- *l' inverno* ‘the winter’ → “in winter”: the Italian temporal noun phrase becomes an English prepositional phrase and moves from before the subject to after it.
- *la tenace pece* ‘the tenacious pitch’ → “the clammy pitch”: resistance to separation or adhesiveness is expressed as clamminess.
- *a rimpalmar* ‘to caulk again’ → “to caulk”: the repeated action suggested by the prefix is not separately expressed.
- *li lor legni non sani* ‘their timbers not sound’ → “their damaged ships”: the metonymic ‘timbers’ becomes the explicit “ships”; the negative description becomes the positive adjective “damaged”, placed before the noun.

> 10 che navicar non ponno, e in quella vece  
> 11 chi fa suo legno nuovo, e chi ristoppa  
> 12 le coste a quel che più viaggi fece;  

[which] they cannot navigate ; and, instead thereof, one builds his ship anew, one plugs the ribs of that which hath made many voyages

- *che navicar non ponno* ‘for they cannot sail’ → “[which] they cannot navigate”: the translation reads *che* as an object relative referring to the ships rather than as a causal conjunction. It makes *navicar* transitive, with the ships as its object, and places the infinitive after the negative modal.
- *in quella vece* ‘in that stead’ → “instead thereof”: the demonstrative construction becomes an adverbial expression with a backward-looking pronominal complement.
- *chi fa suo legno nuovo* ‘who makes his timber new’ → “one builds his ship anew”: distributive *chi* becomes “one”; “builds” specifies the general ‘makes’, and “ship” makes the metonymy explicit. The adjective *nuovo* becomes the adverb “anew”, describing the building rather than the ship.
- *chi ristoppa* ‘who stuffs again’ → “one plugs”: distributive *chi* becomes “one”, while “plugs” does not explicitly retain either repetition or the use of stuffing.
- *le coste a quel* ‘the ribs to that’ → “the ribs of that”: the Italian construction identifying the vessel to which the ribs belong becomes an English possessive construction.
- *che più viaggi fece* ‘which more voyages made’ → “which hath made many voyages”: *più* is taken as ‘many’ rather than comparative ‘more’; the simple past becomes a present perfect, and the verb moves before its object.

> 13 chi ribatte da proda, e chi da poppa;  

; some hammer at the prow, some at the stern

- *chi ribatte da proda* ‘who hammers again at prow’ → “some hammer at the prow”: the singular distributive pronoun becomes plural “some”; the repetitive force of *ri-* is not separately expressed, and an article is supplied before “prow”.
- *e chi da poppa* ‘and who at stern’ → “some at the stern”: the conjunction is omitted, the singular distributive pronoun becomes plural “some”, and an article is supplied before “stern”.

> 14 altri fa remi, ed altri volge sarte;  

; some make oars, and some twist ropes

- *altri fa* ‘another makes’ → “some make”: the singular distributive pronoun and verb become plural.
- *altri volge sarte* ‘another turns rigging’ → “some twist ropes”: the singular construction becomes plural; ‘turns’ is interpreted specifically as twisting, and the nautical ‘rigging’ becomes the more general “ropes”.

> 15 chi terzeruolo ed artimon rintoppa:  

; one mends the jib, and one the mainsail

- *chi terzeruolo ed artimon rintoppa* ‘who storm sail and mainsail patches again’ → “one mends the jib, and one the mainsail”: distributive *chi* becomes “one”, and the verb moves before its objects. A second “one” is supplied, dividing between two workers the objects governed by one subject in Italian. *Terzeruolo* is rendered as “jib”, a different sail designation; “mends” generalizes ‘patches’ and leaves repetition unexpressed. Articles are supplied before both sails.

> 16 tal, non per foco, ma per divina arte  
> 17 bollia laggiuso una pegola spessa  
> 18 che inviscava la ripa da ogni parte.  

: so, not by fire but by art Divine, a dense pitch boiled down there, and overglued the banks on every side.

- *tal* ‘such’ → “so”: the expression describing the pitch becomes an adverbial counterpart to the opening ‘as’.
- *divina arte* ‘divine art’ → “art Divine”: the adjective is moved after the noun.
- *bollia laggiuso una pegola spessa* ‘boiled down there a pitch thick’ → “a dense pitch boiled down there”: the subject moves before the verb and place adverb; its adjective moves before the noun, and “dense” replaces ‘thick’.
- *che* ‘which’ → “and”: the relative clause becomes a coordinated predicate.
- *inviscava* ‘made sticky’ → “overglued”: the verb is rendered by an expression emphasizing a coating of adhesive.
- *la ripa* ‘the bank’ → “the banks”: the singular becomes plural.
- *da ogni parte* ‘from every part’ → “on every side”: the Italian expression becomes an English idiom of surrounding position.

## Lines 19–51 — A Sinner Cast Into the Pitch

> 19 Io vedea lei, ma non vedeva in essa  
> 20 ma' che le bolle che il bollor levava,  
> 21 e gonfiar tutta, e riseder compressa.  

It I saw; but saw nought therein, except the bubbles which the boiling raised, and the heaving and compressed subsiding of the whole.

- *Io vedea lei* ‘I saw her’ → “It I saw”: the object moves before the subject; the feminine pronoun referring to the pitch becomes neuter in English.
- *ma non vedeva in essa ma' che* ‘but did not see in it other than’ → “but saw nought therein, except”: the negative verb becomes an affirmative verb with a negative object, “nought”; ‘in it’ becomes “therein”.
- *e gonfiar tutta, e riseder compressa* ‘and all of it swell, and subside compressed’ → “and the heaving and compressed subsiding of the whole”: the infinitives governed by ‘saw’ become verbal nouns; ‘all of it’ becomes “of the whole”, and ‘compressed’ moves from a description of the pitch to a modifier of “subsiding”.

> 22 Mentr' io laggiù fissamente mirava,  
> 23 lo duca mio, dicendo: “Guarda, guarda!”  
> 24 mi trasse a sè del loco dov' io stava.  

Whilst I was gazing fixtly down on it, my Guide, saying, "Take care, take care!" drew me to him from the place where I was standing.

- *laggiù fissamente mirava* ‘down there fixedly I gazed’ → “was gazing fixtly down on it”: the imperfect becomes an explicitly progressive construction; the verb precedes its adverbs, and “on it” supplies the object of the gaze while ‘there’ is not separately rendered.
- *lo duca mio* ‘the leader mine’ → “my Guide”: “Guide” renders ‘leader’, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *Guarda, guarda!* ‘Look, look!’ → “Take care, take care!”: the commands to look become warnings.
- *mi trasse a sè* ‘me drew to himself’ → “drew me to him”: the object follows the verb, and the reflexive becomes an ordinary personal pronoun.
- *dov' io stava* ‘where I stood’ → “where I was standing”: the imperfect is rendered by an explicitly progressive construction.

> 25 Allor mi volsi come l' uom cui tarda  
> 26 di veder quel che gli convien fuggire,  
> 27 e cui paura subita sgagliarda,  
> 28 che per veder non indugia il partire;  

Then I turned round, like one who longs to see what he must shun, and who is dashed with sudden fear, so that he puts not off his flight to look;

- *mi volsi* ‘I turned myself’ → “I turned round”: English replaces the reflexive construction with an intransitive verb and an adverb.
- *l' uom* ‘the man’ → “one”: a general pronoun replaces the noun and article.
- *cui tarda di veder* ‘to whom it is slow to see’ → “who longs to see”: the Italian impersonal expression of impatience becomes a personal verb expressing desire.
- *quel che gli convien fuggire* ‘that which it behoves him to flee’ → “what he must shun”: “what” contracts ‘that which’, and the impersonal construction becomes a personal verb with “must”; “shun” renders ‘flee’.
- *e cui paura subita sgagliarda* ‘and whom sudden fear weakens’ → “and who is dashed with sudden fear”: the active construction becomes passive, with fear expressed through “with”; “dashed” renders ‘weakened’, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *che per veder non indugia il partire* ‘who, to see, does not delay departing’ → “so that he puts not off his flight to look”: the clause is interpreted as a consequence; the purpose phrase moves to the end, “puts not off” replaces ‘does not delay’, and the substantivized infinitive becomes “his flight”.

> 29 e vidi dietro a noi un diavol nero  
> 30 correndo su per lo scoglio venire.  

and behind us I saw a black Demon come running up the cliff.

- *vidi dietro a noi* ‘I saw behind us’ → “behind us I saw”: the place phrase moves before the verb.
- *un diavol nero* ‘a devil black’ → “a black Demon”: “Demon” replaces ‘devil’, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *correndo su per lo scoglio venire* ‘running up along the cliff to come’ → “come running up the cliff”: the infinitive moves before the participial and directional phrases and is bare after “saw”; “up” absorbs the combined directional force of *su per*.

> 31 Ahi quanto egli era nell' aspetto fiero!  

Ah, how ferocious was his aspect!

- *quanto egli era nell' aspetto fiero* ‘how he was fierce in appearance’ → “how ferocious was his aspect”: the description of how he appeared becomes a statement whose subject is “his aspect”; “ferocious” renders ‘fierce’.

> 32 e quanto mi parea nell' atto acerbo,  
> 33 con l' ale aperte, e sopra il piè leggiero!  

and how bitter he seemed to me in gesture, with his wings outspread, and light of foot!

- *quanto mi parea nell' atto acerbo* ‘how to me he seemed in bearing harsh’ → “how bitter he seemed to me in gesture”: the predicative adjective moves forward; “bitter” renders ‘harsh’, “gesture” renders ‘bearing’, and the indirect object follows the verb.
- *con l' ale aperte* ‘with the wings open’ → “with his wings outspread”: the article becomes a possessive, and “outspread” specifies the manner in which the wings are open.
- *sopra il piè leggiero* ‘upon the foot light’ → “light of foot”: the adjective moves first, and the Italian prepositional phrase becomes an English idiom with “of”.

> 34 L' omero suo, ch' era acuto e superbo,  
> 35 carcava un peccator con ambo l' anche,  
> 36 e quei tenea de' piè ghermito il nerbo.  

His shoulders that were sharp and high, a sinner with both haunches laded; and of each foot he held the sinew grasped.

- *L' omero suo, ch' era acuto e superbo* ‘his shoulder, which was sharp and lofty’ → “His shoulders that were sharp and high”: the singular shoulder becomes plural, with a corresponding change in the verb; the possessive moves before the noun.
- *carcava un peccator con ambo l' anche* ‘laded a sinner with both the haunches’ → “a sinner with both haunches laded”: the Italian’s postverbal subject, ‘a sinner’, precedes the verb in English, while the object remains at the beginning of the sentence; the article before ‘haunches’ is omitted.
- *e quei tenea de' piè ghermito il nerbo* ‘and that one held of the feet grasped the sinew’ → “and of each foot he held the sinew grasped”: the demonstrative becomes “he”; plural ‘feet’ becomes distributive “each foot”; the possessive phrase moves before the subject, and “grasped” follows rather than precedes “the sinew”.

> 37 “Del nostro ponte,” disse “o Malebranche,  
> 38 ecco un degli anzian di santa Zita;  

"Ye Malebranche of our bridge!" he said, "lo! one of Santa Zita's Elders;

- *Del nostro ponte, disse o Malebranche* ‘Of our bridge, he said, O Malebranche’ → “Ye Malebranche of our bridge!” and “he said”: the vocative is placed before its modifying phrase, the reporting clause follows the complete address, and “Ye” replaces the vocative particle.
- *un degli anzian di santa Zita* ‘one of the elders of Santa Zita’ → “one of Santa Zita's Elders”: the possessive construction replaces the phrase with ‘of’, placing the name before “Elders” and removing the separate article.

> 39 mettetel sotto, ch' io torno per anche  
> 40 a quella terra ch' i' n' ho ben fornita:  

thrust him under, while I return for others to that city which [I have] provided well with them:

- *mettetel sotto* ‘put him under’ → “thrust him under”: “thrust” gives the action greater force than ‘put’.
- *ch' io torno per anche* ‘for I return for more’ → “while I return for others”: the explanatory conjunction becomes temporal, and ‘more’ is expressed as “others”.
- *ch' i' n' ho ben fornita* ‘which I have with them well supplied’ → “which [I have] provided well with them”: the pronominal complement becomes a following prepositional phrase, and “well” moves after the participle.

> 41 ognun v' è barattier, fuor che Bonturo;  

every [one] there is a barrator, except Bonturo;

- Literal.

> 42 del no per li denar vi si fa ita.”  

there they make 'Ay' of 'No' for money."

- *del no per li denar vi si fa ita* ‘of no, for the money, there one makes yes’ → “there they make 'Ay' of 'No' for money”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal one with indefinite “they”; the place adverb moves first, while the material and payment phrases follow the verb and its object; plural, articulated ‘the moneys’ becomes unarticulated mass “money”.

> 43 Laggiù il buttò, e per lo scoglio duro  
> 44 si volse, e mai non fu mastino sciolto  
> 45 con tanta fretta a seguitar lo furo.  

Down he threw him, then wheeled along the flinty cliff; and never was mastiff loosed with such a haste to follow thief.

- *Laggiù il buttò* ‘down there him he threw’ → “Down he threw him”: ‘there’ is not separately rendered, and the object follows the verb.
- *e per lo scoglio duro si volse* ‘and along the hard cliff turned himself’ → “then wheeled along the flinty cliff”: “then” replaces the conjunction, the verb precedes the place phrase, the reflexive becomes intransitive “wheeled”, and “flinty” is more specific than ‘hard’.
- *e mai non fu mastino sciolto* ‘and never was a mastiff loosed’ → “and never was mastiff loosed”: English omits the indefinite article needed in an ordinary prose rendering of the singular count noun.
- *con tanta fretta* ‘with so much haste’ → “with such a haste”: the expression of quantity becomes one of degree or kind, with an indefinite article added.
- *a seguitar lo furo* ‘to follow the thief’ → “to follow thief”: the definite article is omitted.

> 46 Quei s' attuffò, e tornò su convolto;  

The sinner plunged in, and came up again writhing convolved;

- *Quei s' attuffò* ‘that one plunged himself’ → “The sinner plunged in”: the demonstrative is replaced by an identifying noun phrase, and the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb with “in”.
- *e tornò su convolto* ‘and returned up contorted’ → “and came up again writhing convolved”: ‘returned’ is expanded into “came” and “again”; “writhing” is supplied alongside the rendering of *convolto*.

> 47 ma i demon, che del ponte avean coperchio,  
> 48 gridar: “Qui non ha loco il santo volto;  
> 49 qui si nuota altrimenti che nel Serchio:  

but the Demons, who were under cover of the bridge, cried: "Here the Sacred Face besteads not; here swim ye otherwise than in the Serchio:

- *che del ponte avean coperchio* ‘who of the bridge had cover’ → “who were under cover of the bridge”: possession of cover becomes a construction describing position under it, and the modifying phrase follows “cover”.
- *Qui non ha loco il santo volto* ‘here the holy face has no place’ → “Here the Sacred Face besteads not”: ‘has no place’ is interpreted as ‘is of no assistance’; the subject moves before the verb, and its adjective precedes the noun.
- *qui si nuota* ‘here one swims’ → “here swim ye”: the impersonal construction becomes a direct second-person statement, with the verb before the subject.

> 50 però, se tu non vuoi de' nostri graffi,  
> 51 non far sopra la pegola soverchio.”  

therefore, unless thou wishest to make trial of our drags, come not out above the pitch."

- *se tu non vuoi de' nostri graffi* ‘if you do not want any of our scratches’ → “unless thou wishest to make trial of our drags”: “unless” absorbs the negative condition; “to make trial” is supplied, and “drags” renders the threatening scratches by naming the hooked implements.
- *non far sopra la pegola soverchio* ‘do not make a projection above the pitch’ → “come not out above the pitch”: the expression for protruding is rendered as movement out of the pitch, and the verb phrase is brought together before the prepositional phrase.

## Lines 52–84 — Virgil Confronts the Demons

> 52 Poi l' addentar con più di cento raffi ;  
> 53 disser: “Coperto convien che qui balli,  
> 54 sì che, se puoi, nascosamente accaffi.”  

Then they struck him with more than a hundred prongs, *and* said: “Covered thou must dance thee here; so that, if thou canst, thou mayest pilfer privately.”

- *l' addentar* ‘him they bit’ → “they struck him”: the metaphor of biting becomes striking, and the object follows the verb.
- *più di cento* ‘more than hundred’ → “more than a hundred”: English supplies the article before “hundred”.
- *disser* ‘they said’ → “*and* said”: a conjunction is supplied to coordinate the verbs.
- *Coperto convien che qui balli* ‘covered it is necessary that here thou dance’ → “Covered thou must dance thee here”: the impersonal construction becomes the personal modal “must”; “thee” is supplied reflexively, and “here” moves after the verb.
- *nascosamente accaffi* ‘secretly thou pilfer’ → “thou mayest pilfer privately”: the subjunctive is represented by “mayest”, and the adverb moves after the verb.

> 55 Non altrimenti i cuochi ai lor vassalli  
> 56 fanno atuffare in mezzo la caldaia  
> 57 la carne con gli uncin, perchè non galli.  

Not otherwise do the cooks make their vassals dip the flesh into the middle of the boiler with their hooks, to hinder it from floating.

- *i cuochi ai lor vassalli fanno atuffare* ‘the cooks cause their vassals to plunge’ → “do the cooks make their vassals dip”: English supplies the auxiliary “do” and inverts auxiliary and subject after the opening negative phrase; the Italian causative’s *ai lor vassalli* becomes the direct object of “make”.
- *in mezzo la caldaia la carne* ‘into the middle of the boiler the flesh’ → “the flesh into the middle of the boiler”: the object moves before the expression of place.
- *con gli uncin* ‘with the hooks’ → “with their hooks”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *perchè non galli* ‘so that it may not float’ → “to hinder it from floating”: the negative finite purpose clause becomes an infinitive expressing prevention, with “floating” dependent on the supplied verb “hinder”.

> 58 Lo buon maestro: “Acciocchè non si paia  
> 59 che tu ci sii,” mi disse, “giù t' acquatta  
> 60 dopo uno scheggio che alcun schermo t' haia;  

The kind Master said to me: “That it may not be seen that thou art here, cower down behind a jagg, so that thou mayest have some screen for thyself;

- *Lo buon maestro* ‘the good master’ → “The kind Master”: “kind” selects a more specific sense of *buon*.
- *Acciocchè non si paia che tu ci sii* ‘so that it may not appear that thou art here’ → “That it may not be seen that thou art here”: the impersonal construction with *parere*, ‘appear’, becomes a passive construction with “seen”.
- *mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “said to me”: the reporting clause moves from within the speech to before it, and the indirect object follows the verb.
- *giù t' acquatta* ‘down crouch thyself’ → “cower down”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, and “down” follows the verb.
- *uno scheggio* ‘a crag’ → “a jagg”: the English describes the rock as a jagged projection.
- *che alcun schermo t' haia* ‘that may afford thee some shelter’ → “so that thou mayest have some screen for thyself”: the relative clause describing the rock becomes a purpose clause; the person sheltered becomes its subject, and the Italian indirect object is recast as “for thyself”.

> 61 e per nulla offension che mi sia fatta,  
> 62 non temer tu, ch' io ho le cose conte,  
> 63 perchè altra volta fui a tal baratta.”  

and whatever outrage may be done to me, fear not thou: for I know these matters, having once before been in the like affray.”

- *per nulla offension che mi sia fatta* ‘for no injury that may be done to me’ → “whatever outrage may be done to me”: the prepositional construction, whose negative belongs with the following prohibition, becomes a concessive clause with “whatever”; “outrage” gives *offension* a stronger colouring than ‘injury’.
- *non temer tu* ‘not fear thou’ → “fear not thou”: the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *ch' io ho le cose conte* ‘because I have the things known’ → “for I know these matters”: causal subordination becomes coordination with “for”; ‘have … known’ becomes the simple verb “know”, and the definite article becomes the demonstrative “these”.
- *perchè altra volta fui a tal baratta* ‘because another time I was at such an affray’ → “having once before been in the like affray”: the finite causal clause becomes a perfect participial clause; “once before” makes the earlier occurrence explicit, and “the like” replaces ‘such’.

> 64 Poscia passò di là dal co' del ponte,  
> 65 e com' ei giunse in su la ripa sesta,  
> 66 mestier gli fu d' aver sicura fronte.  

Then he passed beyond the head of the bridge; and when he arrived on the sixth bank, it was needful for him to have a steadfast front.

- *com'* ‘as’ → “when”: the conjunction is rendered explicitly temporally.
- *in su la ripa sesta* ‘upon the bank sixth’ → “on the sixth bank”: the ordinal moves before the noun.
- *mestier gli fu d' aver* ‘need to him it was to have’ → “it was needful for him to have”: the noun *mestier* becomes the predicate adjective “needful”, with an anticipatory “it”; the expression referring to him follows the predicate.
- *sicura fronte* ‘a confident brow’ → “a steadfast front”: “steadfast” emphasizes firmness, while “front” gives the bodily expression a broader sense of outward bearing.

> 67 Con quel furor e con quella tempesta  
> 68 ch' escono i cani addosso al poverello,  
> 69 che di subito chiede ove s' arresta  
> 70 usciron quei di sotto il ponticello,  

With that fury and that storm, wherewith the dogs rush forth upon the poor man who where he stops suddenly seeks *alms*, rushed those *Demons* from beneath the bridge,

- *e con quella tempesta* ‘and with that storm’ → “and that storm”: the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *ch' escono i cani* ‘with which come out the dogs’ → “wherewith the dogs rush forth”: the subject moves before the verb, and “rush forth” adds speed and force to ‘come out’.
- *addosso al poverello* ‘upon the poor little man’ → “upon the poor man”: the diminutive or pitying force of *poverello* is not separately expressed.
- *che di subito chiede ove s' arresta* ‘who suddenly asks where he stops’ → “who where he stops suddenly seeks *alms*”: the clause of place moves before the verb; ‘asks’ becomes “seeks”, with its object “*alms*” supplied; the reflexive *s' arresta* becomes intransitive “stops”.
- *usciron quei* ‘came out those’ → “rushed those *Demons*”: “rushed” again intensifies the motion, and “*Demons*” supplies a noun for the demonstrative pronoun.
- *di sotto il ponticello* ‘from beneath the little bridge’ → “from beneath the bridge”: the diminutive is omitted.

> 71 e volser contra lui tutti i roncigli;  
> 72 ma ei gridò: “Nessun di voi sia fello!  

and turned against him all their crooks; but he cried: “Be none of ye outrageous!

- *tutti i roncigli* ‘all the hooks’ → “all their crooks”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *Nessun di voi sia fello* ‘none of you be wicked’ → “Be none of ye outrageous”: the verb moves before the subject, and the exhortatory subjunctive becomes an English imperative; “outrageous” renders *fello* in terms of violent conduct rather than wickedness generally.

> 73 Innanzi che l' uncin vostro mi pigli,  
> 74 traggasi avanti alcun di voi che m'oda,  
> 75 e poi d' arroncigliarmi si consigli.”  

Before ye touch me with your forks, let one of you come forth to hear me, and then take counsel about hooking me.”

- *Innanzi che l' uncin vostro mi pigli* ‘before your hook seizes me’ → “Before ye touch me with your forks”: the hook, originally the subject, becomes an instrument wielded by “ye”; singular ‘hook’ becomes plural “forks”, and ‘seizes’ is softened to “touch”.
- *traggasi avanti alcun di voi* ‘let someone of you draw himself forward’ → “let one of you come forth”: the reflexive verb becomes intransitive “come”, and the subject precedes the main verb rather than following it.
- *che m'oda* ‘who may hear me’ → “to hear me”: the relative clause with a subjunctive expressing purpose becomes a purpose infinitive.
- *e poi d' arroncigliarmi si consigli* ‘and then about hooking me let counsel be taken’ → “and then take counsel about hooking me”: the impersonal construction becomes a direct imperative, and the subject of deliberation moves after the verb.

> 76 Tutti gridaron: “Vada Malacoda ;”  
> 77 per che un si mosse, e gli altri stetter fermi;  
> 78 e venne a lui dicendo: “Che gli approda?”  

All cried: “Let Malacoda go”; thereat one moved himself, the others standing firm, and came to him, saying: “What will this avail him?”

- *Vada Malacoda* ‘may Malacoda go’ → “Let Malacoda go”: the exhortatory subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”, placing the subject before the main verb.
- *per che* ‘because of which’ → “thereat”: the connective relative phrase becomes a single adverb, expressing the immediate response.
- *e gli altri stetter fermi* ‘and the others stood firm’ → “the others standing firm”: the coordinated finite clause becomes an absolute participial construction, and the conjunction is omitted.
- *Che gli approda* ‘what does it avail him’ → “What will this avail him”: the present becomes future, and “this” explicitly identifies the action whose usefulness is questioned.

> 79 “Credi tu, Malacoda, qui vedermi  
> 80 esser venuto,” disse il mio maestro,  
> 81 “sicuro già da tutti vostri schermi,  
> 82 senza voler divino e fato destro?  

“Dost thou expect, Malacoda,” said my Master, “to find I have come here, secure already against all your hindrances, without will Divine and fate propitious?

- *Credi tu* ‘believest thou’ → “Dost thou expect”: belief becomes expectation, and English uses an interrogative auxiliary.
- *qui vedermi esser venuto* ‘here to see me to have come’ → “to find I have come here”: “find” replaces ‘see’; the infinitive *esser venuto*, with *mi* as its subject, becomes the finite clause “I have come”; “here” moves from before the verb of seeing to after the verb of coming.
- *da tutti vostri schermi* ‘from all your defences’ → “against all your hindrances”: “against” replaces ‘from’, and *schermi* is interpreted as obstructive measures rather than defences.

> 83 Lasciami andar, chè nel cielo è voluto  
> 84 ch' io mostri altrui questo cammin silvestro.”  

Let me pass on: for it is willed in Heaven that I shew another this savage way.”

- *andar* ‘go’ → “pass on”: the translation makes continued passage explicit.
- *chè nel cielo è voluto* ‘because in heaven it is willed’ → “for it is willed in Heaven”: causal subordination becomes coordination with “for”, and the expression of place moves after the verb.
- *altrui* ‘to another’ → “another”: English expresses the recipient as an indirect object without a preposition.
- *questo cammin silvestro* ‘this way woodland’ → “this savage way”: *silvestro* is rendered figuratively as “savage” rather than as ‘woodland’, and the adjective moves before the noun.

## Lines 85–117 — Safe Conduct Among the Devils

> 85 Allor gli fu l' orgoglio sì caduto  
> 86 che si lasciò cascar l' uncino ai piedi,  
> 87 e disse agli altri: "Omai non sia feruto."  

Then was his pride so fallen, that he let the hook drop at his feet, and said to the others: "Now strike him not!"

- *gli fu l' orgoglio sì caduto* ‘to him was pride so fallen’ → “was his pride so fallen”: the possessive dative *gli* becomes “his”, attached to “pride”.
- *si lasciò cascar l' uncino ai piedi* ‘let the hook fall at the feet’ → “he let the hook drop at his feet”: the pronominal *si* is not separately represented; the definite article with the body part becomes “his”, and *cascar* is rendered by “drop”.
- *non sia feruto* ‘let him not be wounded’ → “strike him not”: a passive third-person injunction becomes an active command to the others, with “him” as its object; wounding is rendered as striking.

> 88 E il duca mio a me: "O tu, che siedi  
> 89 tra gli scheggion del ponte quatto quatto,  
> 90 sicuramente omai a me ti riedi."  

And my Guide to me: "O thou that sittest cowering, cowering amongst the great splinters of the bridge, securely now return to me!"

- *il duca mio* ‘the leader my’ → “my Guide”: *duca* becomes “Guide”, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *quatto quatto* ‘crouched, crouched’ → “cowering, cowering”: the English suggests fear as well as a lowered posture; the phrase moves from after the description of the hiding place to immediately after “sittest”.
- *a me ti riedi* ‘to me get yourself back’ → “return to me”: the destination follows the verb, and the pronominal *ti* is not separately expressed.

> 91 Per ch' io mi mossi, ed a lui venni ratto;  
> 92 e i diavoli si fecer tutti avanti,  
> 93 sì ch' io temetti ch' ei tenesser patto.  

Whereat I moved, and quickly came to him; and the Devils all pressed forward, so that I feared they might not hold the compact.

- *Per ch'* ‘for which reason’ → “Whereat”: the explicit causal connection becomes a relative adverb marking the occasion of the action.
- *io mi mossi* ‘I moved myself’ → “I moved”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *a lui venni ratto* ‘to him I came quickly’ → “quickly came to him”: the adverb moves before the verb and the destination after it.
- *si fecer tutti avanti* ‘made themselves all forward’ → “all pressed forward”: the reflexive idiom becomes an intransitive expression suggesting forceful movement, and “all” moves before the verb.
- *ch' ei tenesser patto* ‘that they should keep agreement’ → “they might not hold the compact”: “that” is omitted, the subjunctive is represented by “might”, and an article is supplied before “compact”. The added “not” makes explicit the feared failure to keep the agreement, rather than reproducing the Italian clause’s affirmative form.

> 94 E così vid' io già temer li fanti  
> 95 ch' uscivan patteggiati di Caprona,  
> 96 veggendo sè tra nimici cotanti.  

And thus once I saw the footmen, who marched out under treaty from Caprona, fear at seeing themselves among so many enemies.

- *vid' io* ‘saw I’ → “I saw”: subject and verb exchange places.
- *già* ‘formerly’ → “once”: the general reference to an earlier time becomes a reference to one occasion, and moves before “I saw”.
- *temer li fanti* ‘fear the footmen’ → “the footmen” … “fear”: the subject of the infinitive moves before it; its relative clause consequently intervenes between “the footmen” and “fear”.
- *uscivan* ‘were going out’ → “marched out”: the English adds the manner of movement and uses a simple past for the imperfect.
- *patteggiati* ‘having made terms’ → “under treaty”: a participial description of the men becomes a prepositional phrase describing the conditions of their departure.
- *veggendo* ‘seeing’ → “at seeing”: “at” is supplied to connect the sight explicitly with the fear.
- *nimici cotanti* ‘enemies so many’ → “so many enemies”: the expression of quantity moves before the noun.

> 97 Io m' accostai con tutta la persona  
> 98 lungo il mio duca, e non torceva gli occhi  
> 99 dalla sembianza lor ch' era non buona.  

I drew near my Guide with my whole body, and turned not away my eyes from the look of them, which was not good.

- *Io m' accostai con tutta la persona lungo il mio duca* ‘I drew myself close with all the person alongside my leader’ → “I drew near my Guide with my whole body”: the reflexive becomes intransitive; “near my Guide” absorbs the separate phrase introduced by *lungo* and moves before the bodily description. *Persona* becomes “body”, its article becomes “my”, and *duca* becomes “Guide”.
- *non torceva gli occhi* ‘was not turning the eyes’ → “turned not away my eyes”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, “away” makes the direction explicit, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *sembianza* ‘appearance’ → “look”: the English uses a more immediate term for the visible expression.
- *lor* ‘their’ → “of them”: the possessive becomes a prepositional phrase.

> 100 Ei chinavan gli raffi, e "Vuoi che 'l tocchi,"  
> 101 diceva l' un con l' altro, "in sul groppone?"  
> 102 e rispondean: "Sì, fa che gliele accocchi."  

They lowered their drag-hooks, and kept saying to one another: "Shall I touch him on the rump?" and answering: "Yes, see thou nick it for him."

- *Ei chinavan gli raffi* ‘They were lowering the hooks’ → “They lowered their drag-hooks”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, the article becomes “their”, and “drag-hooks” specifies the kind of hooks.
- *e … diceva l' un con l' altro* ‘and … the one was saying with the other’ → “and kept saying to one another”: the conjunction and reporting clause are brought together before the quotation. The singular distributive construction becomes plural and reciprocal; *con* becomes “to”, and “kept saying” brings out the repeated action of the imperfect.
- *Vuoi che 'l tocchi* ‘Do you want me to touch him’ → “Shall I touch him”: a question about the other’s wishes, expressed through a subordinate clause, becomes a modal question offering an action.
- *e rispondean* ‘and they were answering’ → “and answering”: the finite imperfect becomes a participle coordinated with “saying” under “kept”.
- *fa che gliele accocchi* ‘see that you deal it to him’ → “see thou nick it for him”: the clause introduced by *che* loses its explicit conjunction; the idiomatic *accocchi*, suggesting landing a blow, becomes the more specific “nick”, and the recipient is expressed by “for him” rather than ‘to him’.

> 103 Ma quel demonio, che tenea sermone  
> 104 col duca mio, si volse tutto presto  
> 105 e disse: "Posa, posa, Scarmiglione."  

But that Demon, who [was speaking] with my Guide, turned instant round, and said: "Quiet, quiet, Scarmiglione!"

- *tenea sermone* ‘was holding discourse’ → “[was speaking]”: a verb-and-noun expression becomes a single verbal expression.
- *duca mio* ‘leader my’ → “my Guide”: the noun changes, and the possessive moves before it.
- *si volse tutto presto* ‘turned himself very quickly’ → “turned instant round”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, with “round” expressing the turn; the emphatic *tutto presto* becomes the compressed adverbial “instant”.
- *Posa, posa* ‘Stop, stop’ → “Quiet, quiet”: verbal commands become elliptical calls for quiet.

> 106 Poi disse a noi: "Più oltre andar per questo  
> 107 iscoglio non si può, però che giace  
> 108 tutto spezzato al fondo l' arco sesto;  

Then he said to us: "To go farther by this cliff will not be possible: for the sixth arch lies all in fragments at the bottom;

- *Più oltre andar* ‘farther to go’ → “To go farther”: the infinitive moves before its adverbial expression.
- *per questo iscoglio* ‘along this rock’ → “by this cliff”: “by” presents the rock as a route, and “cliff” gives a more specific shape to *iscoglio*.
- *non si può* ‘one cannot’ → “will not be possible”: the impersonal modal construction becomes a statement of possibility, and the present becomes future.
- *però che* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *giace tutto spezzato al fondo l' arco sesto* ‘lies all broken at the bottom the arch sixth’ → “the sixth arch lies all in fragments at the bottom”: the subject moves before the verb, the ordinal moves before its noun, and the participle *spezzato* becomes the prepositional phrase “in fragments”.

> 109 e se l' andare avanti pur vi piace,  
> 110 andatevene su per questa grotta;  
> 111 presso è un altro scoglio che via face.  

and if it please you still to go onward, go along this [ridge]: near at hand is another cliff which forms a path.

- *se l' andare avanti pur vi piace* ‘if the going onward still pleases you’ → “if it please you still to go onward”: the substantival infinitive serving as subject becomes a postponed infinitive introduced by anticipatory “it”. The indicative *piace* becomes the conditional subjunctive “please”, and “still” moves after the verb and its object.
- *andatevene su per questa grotta* ‘go away up along this rocky slope’ → “go along this [ridge]”: the pronominal departure expressed by *-vene* and the upward direction of *su* are not separately rendered. “[ridge]” interprets *grotta* as the raised rocky route rather than simply a slope or bank.
- *presso* ‘near’ → “near at hand”: the English expands the adverb.
- *scoglio* ‘rock’ → “cliff”: the English again specifies the form of the rock.
- *che via face* ‘which a way makes’ → “which forms a path”: the object moves after the verb; “forms a path” replaces ‘makes a way’.

> 112 Ier, più oltre cinqu' ore che quest' otta,  
> 113 mille dugento con sessanta sei  
> 114 anni compiè che qui la via fu rotta.  

Yesterday, five hours later than this hour, [completed] a thousand two hundred and sixty-six years since the way here was broken.

- *più oltre cinqu' ore che quest' otta* ‘farther on five hours than this hour’ → “five hours later than this hour”: the measure of time moves before the comparative expression, and *più oltre* is compressed into “later”.
- *mille dugento con sessanta sei anni compiè* ‘a thousand two hundred with sixty-six years completed’ → “[completed] a thousand two hundred and sixty-six years”: the verb moves before the numerical expression, and *con* becomes “and”.
- *che qui la via fu rotta* ‘that here the way was broken’ → “since the way here was broken”: *che* is rendered as a temporal conjunction marking the starting point of the interval, and “here” moves after “the way”.

> 115 Io mando verso là di questi miei  
> 116 a riguardar s' alcun se ne sciorina;  

Thitherward I send some of these my men, to look if any one be out airing himself;

- *Io mando verso là* ‘I send toward there’ → “Thitherward I send”: the directional phrase becomes one adverb and moves before the subject and verb.
- *di questi miei* ‘some of these of mine’ → “some of these my men”: “men” supplies the noun left implicit by the substantival possessive.
- *se ne sciorina* ‘airs himself out of it’ → “be out airing himself”: the present indicative becomes subjunctive “be” with a participial expression. “Out” retains the outward movement of *ne*, but its reference to a place left behind is not separately expressed.

> 117 gite con lor, ch' ei non saranno rei."  

go with them, for they will not be treacherous."

- *rei* ‘wicked’ → “treacherous”: the English narrows a general term for badness to a particular kind of misconduct.

## Lines 118–139 — The Devils’ Escort and Signal

> 118 “Tratti avanti, Alichino e Calcabrina,”  
> 119 cominciò egli a dire, “e tu, Cagnazzo;  
> 120 e Barbariccia guidi la decina.  

"Draw forward, Alichino and Calcabrina," he then began to say, "and thou, Cagnazzo; and let Barbariccia lead the ten.

- *Tratti avanti* ‘Draw yourselves forward’ → “Draw forward”: the reflexive element is not separately expressed.
- *cominciò egli a dire* ‘began he to say’ → “he then began to say”: the subject moves before the verb, and “then” is supplied.
- *Barbariccia guidi la decina* ‘Barbariccia guide the group of ten’ → “let Barbariccia lead the ten”: the command expressed by the subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”; the collective noun *decina* becomes the substantival numeral “ten”.

> 121 Libicocco vegna oltre, e Draghignazzo,  
> 122 Ciriatto sannuto, e Graffiacane,  
> 123 e Farfarello, e Rubicante il pazzo.  

Let Libicocco come besides, and Draghignazzo, tusked Ciriatto, and Graffiacane, and Farfarello, and furious Rubicante.

- *Libicocco vegna oltre* ‘Libicocco come forward’ → “Let Libicocco come besides”: “Let” expresses the commanding subjunctive; the directional *oltre* becomes the additive “besides”.
- *Ciriatto sannuto* ‘Ciriatto tusked’ → “tusked Ciriatto”: the adjective moves before the name.
- *Rubicante il pazzo* ‘Rubicante the madman’ → “furious Rubicante”: the substantival epithet becomes an adjective preceding the name, and madness is rendered as fury.

> 124 Cercate intorno le boglienti pane;  

Search around the boiling glue;

- *le boglienti pane* ‘the boiling birdlimes’ → “the boiling glue”: the plural becomes a singular mass noun, and birdlime is rendered by the more general “glue”.

> 125 costor sien salvi insino all’ altro scheggio,  
> 126 che tutto intero va sopra le tane.”  

be these two safe [so far as] the other crag, which all unbroken goes across the dens."

- *costor sien salvi* ‘these be safe’ → “be these two safe”: the verb moves before the subject, and “two” is supplied.
- *tutto intero* ‘wholly entire’ → “all unbroken”: entirety is expressed specifically as freedom from breaks.
- *sopra* ‘above’ → “across”: the translation expresses traversal rather than position overhead.

> 127 “O me! maestro, che è quel che io veggio?”  
> 128 diss’ io; “deh! senza scorta andiamci soli,  
> 129 se tu sai ir, ch’ io per me non la chieggio.  

"Oh me! Master, what is this that I see?" said I; "ah, without escort let us go alone, if thou knowest the way; for as to me, I seek it not!

- *quel* ‘that’ → “this”: the demonstrative changes from distant to near.
- *andiamci* ‘let us go away’ → “let us go”: the pronominal element *ci*, giving the expression the force of going away or on one’s way, is not separately rendered.
- *se tu sai ir* ‘if thou knowest how to go’ → “if thou knowest the way”: the infinitive expressing knowledge of how to proceed becomes a noun denoting the route.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *io per me non la chieggio* ‘I for myself do not ask for it’ → “as to me, I seek it not”: *per me* becomes a phrase of reference placed before the subject; asking for becomes seeking; the object follows the verb, and the negative follows the object.

> 130 Se tu sei sì accorto come suoli,  
> 131 non vedi tu ch’ ei digrignan li denti,  
> 132 e con le ciglia ne minaccian duoli?”  

If thou beest so wary, as thou art wont, dost thou not see how they grin[d] their teeth, and with their brows threaten mischief to us?"

- *accorto* ‘discerning’ → “wary”: perceptiveness is rendered as cautious alertness.
- *non vedi tu* ‘not seest thou’ → “dost thou not see”: English supplies the auxiliary “dost”, placing the subject and negative between it and the main verb.
- *ch’* ‘that’ → “how”: a statement of what is perceived becomes a clause introduced as manner.
- *li denti* ‘the teeth’ → “their teeth”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *le ciglia* ‘the eyebrows’ → “their brows”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “brows” renders eyebrows.
- *ne minaccian duoli* ‘to us they threaten pains’ → “threaten mischief to us”: the preceding object pronoun becomes a following prepositional phrase; plural pains become the singular mass noun “mischief”.

> 133 Ed egli a me: “Non vo’ che tu paventi;  
> 134 lasciali digrignar pure a lor senno,  
> 135 ch’ ei fanno ciò per li lessi dolenti.”  

And he to me: "I would not have thee be afraid; let them grin[d] on at their will: for they do it at the boiled wretches."

- *Non vo’ che tu paventi* ‘I do not wish that thou fear’ → “I would not have thee be afraid”: the present expression of wishing becomes “would not have”; the subordinate subjunctive clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction, and the verb of fearing becomes “be afraid”.
- *pure* ‘just’ → “on”: the permissive emphasis is expressed through an adverb of continuation.
- *a lor senno* ‘at their judgment’ → “at their will”: judgment or discretion is rendered as will.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *ciò* ‘that’ → “it”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *per* ‘for’ → “at”: the translation explicitly treats the following people as the targets of the gesture.
- *li lessi dolenti* ‘the suffering boiled ones’ → “the boiled wretches”: substantival *lessi* becomes the adjective “boiled”, while adjectival *dolenti* becomes the noun “wretches”; their order is reversed.

> 136 Per l’ argine sinistro volta dienno;  

By the sinister bank they turned;

- *Per* ‘Along’ → “By”: movement along a route is expressed as movement beside it.
- *volta dienno* ‘a turn they gave’ → “they turned”: the noun-and-verb expression becomes a single verb, preceded by its subject.

> 137 ma prima avea ciascun la lingua stretta  
> 138 coi denti, verso lor duca per cenno,  

but first each of them had pressed his tongue between the teeth toward their Captain, as a signal;

- *avea ciascun* ‘had each’ → “each of them had”: the subject moves before the auxiliary, and “of them” is supplied.
- *la lingua stretta coi denti* ‘the tongue gripped with the teeth’ → “pressed his tongue between the teeth”: the participle moves before the object; the definite article becomes a possessive; gripping becomes pressing, and the instrumental ‘with’ becomes the positional “between”.
- *duca* ‘leader’ → “Captain”: a general designation of leadership becomes a military title.
- *per cenno* ‘for a sign’ → “as a signal”: the phrase is rendered as an explicit statement of function.

> 139 ed egli avea del cul fatto trombetta.  

and he of his — had made a trumpet.

- *avea del cul fatto trombetta* ‘had of the arse made a little trumpet’ → “of his — had made a trumpet”: the prepositional phrase moves before the auxiliary; the definite article becomes a possessive, while the anatomical noun is suppressed; the diminutive force of *trombetta* is lost, and an indefinite article is supplied.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Recasting

Carlyle’s translation stays close to the Italian’s sequence of statements, repetitions, and concrete imagery, while frequently rearranging individual clauses. Subjects and verbs move ahead of intervening phrases, objects follow their verbs, and adjectives generally precede their nouns: *bollia laggiuso una pegola spessa* becomes “a dense pitch boiled down there”. Yet the English also preserves conspicuously marked ordering, as in “It I saw”, and sometimes introduces its own, as in “art Divine”. Repeated commands and parallel constructions remain prominent, while archaic pronouns and verbal forms give the prose a deliberately non-colloquial texture.

Changes of construction regularly make Italian expressions more explicit or more idiomatic in English. Impersonal necessity becomes a personal modal in *Coperto convien che qui balli*, “Covered thou must dance thee here”; reflexive verbs often become intransitive verbs, as *mi volsi* becomes “I turned round”. Elsewhere, finite clauses become participial phrases, infinitives become verbal nouns, and relative clauses become coordinated predicates or purpose infinitives. Thus *perchè altra volta fui a tal baratta* becomes “having once before been in the like affray”, while *gonfiar* and *riseder* become “the heaving” and “subsiding”. More generally, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits the articles that Italian places before possessives.

Vocabulary ranges from close equivalents to interpretive substitutions. *Legni*, ‘timbers’, becomes “ships”, making the metonymy explicit; *Guarda* becomes “Take care”, selecting the warning implicit in the command. *Rei*, ‘wicked’, is narrowed to “treacherous”, whereas *sarte*, ‘rigging’, becomes the more general “ropes”. Some choices change the force or emphasis: *addentar*, ‘bite’, becomes “struck”, losing the metaphor, while “rushed” gives *usciron*, ‘came out’, greater urgency. Number and grammatical category also shift, as singular *omero* becomes “shoulders” and *Rubicante il pazzo*, ‘Rubicante the madman’, becomes “furious Rubicante”.

Additions clarify reference, connect clauses, or spell out implications: “men” supplies the noun implicit in *di questi miei*, and “two” specifies *costor*. The added negative in “they might not hold the compact” makes the feared breach explicit rather than preserving the Italian clause’s affirmative wording. Conversely, diminutives and the apparent repetitive force of several prefixed verbs are often left unexpressed, and some conjunctions and directional particles disappear. The final “his —” suppresses *cul* altogether. These departures coexist with considerable verbal fidelity: many phrases retain their original images and syntactic outlines, and the translation often prefers an unusual English expression to a smoother paraphrase.
