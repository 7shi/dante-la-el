# Inferno 18

## Lines 1–15 — The Layout of Malebolge

> 1 Loco è in inferno detto Malebolge,  
> 2 tutto di pietra e di color ferrigno,  
> 3 come la cerchia che d' intorno il volge.  

There is a place in Hell called Malebolge, all of stone, and of an iron colour, like the barrier which winds round it.

- *Loco è* ‘A place is’ → “There is a place”: the subject follows the verb in an existential construction with supplied “There”.
- *di color ferrigno* ‘of colour iron-like’ → “of an iron colour”: the adjective becomes the attributive noun “iron”, placed before “colour”, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *la cerchia* ‘the encircling enclosure’ → “the barrier”: the noun is rendered by a word that does not itself express enclosure or circularity.
- *d' intorno il volge* ‘round it winds’ → “winds round it”: the verb moves before the adverbial expression and pronoun.

> 4 Nel dritto mezzo del campo maligno  
> 5 vaneggia un pozzo assai largo e profondo,  
> 6 di cui suo loco dicerò l' ordigno.  

Right in the middle of the malignant field yawns a well exceeding wide and deep, whose structure [I] shall tell [in] its own place.

- *Nel dritto mezzo* ‘In the exact middle’ → “Right in the middle”: the adjective qualifying ‘middle’ becomes an adverb placed before the prepositional phrase.
- *campo maligno* ‘field malignant’ → “malignant field”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *di cui suo loco dicerò l' ordigno* ‘of which its place I shall say the structure’ → “whose structure [I] shall tell [in] its own place”: the possessive relative “whose” replaces ‘of which’, and the object moves before the verb. The unmarked locative *suo loco* receives an explicit preposition and moves after the verb; “own” is added. ‘Say’ is rendered as “tell”.

> 7 Quel cinghio che rimane adunque è tondo,  
> 8 tra il pozzo e il piè dell' alta ripa dura,  
> 9 ed ha distinto in dieci valli il fondo.  

The border therefore that remains, between the well and the foot of the high rocky bank, is round; and it has its bottom divided into ten valleys.

- *Quel cinghio* ‘That belt’ → “The border”: the demonstrative becomes a definite article, and the noun denoting an encircling belt is rendered as “border”.
- *che rimane adunque* ‘that remains therefore’ → “therefore that remains”: “therefore” moves before the relative clause.
- *tra il pozzo e il piè dell' alta ripa dura* ‘between the well and the foot of the high hard bank’ → “between the well and the foot of the high rocky bank”: the phrase moves before “is round”. ‘Hard’ becomes “rocky”, specifying material rather than hardness, and this adjective moves before the noun.
- *ed ha distinto in dieci valli il fondo* ‘and has divided into ten valleys the bottom’ → “and it has its bottom divided into ten valleys”: the object moves before the participle and its accompanying phrase; the definite article becomes the possessive “its”.

> 10 Quale, dove per guardia delle mura  
> 11 più e più fossi cingon li castelli,  
> 12 la parte dov' ei son rende figura :  

As is the form that ground presents, where to defend the walls successive ditches begird a castle:

- *Quale … la parte dov' ei son rende figura* ‘What … form the tract where they are presents’ → “As is the form that ground presents”: the Italian object, ‘what form’, becomes the subject of a comparison with supplied “is”, while ‘presents’ becomes the verb of a clause qualifying “form”. ‘The tract where they are’ is compressed into “that ground”, with the explicit relative description left unexpressed.
- *dove per guardia delle mura* ‘where for protection of the walls’ → “where to defend the walls”: the purpose phrase with a noun becomes an infinitive clause, and ‘of the walls’ becomes the verb’s direct object. The clause introduced by “where” follows the main comparison rather than interrupting it.
- *più e più fossi* ‘more and more ditches’ → “successive ditches”: the repeated expression of multiplicity becomes an adjective expressing succession.
- *li castelli* ‘the castles’ → “a castle”: the definite plural becomes an indefinite singular.

> 13 tale imagine quivi facean quelli;  

such image these made here;

- *quivi facean quelli* ‘there made those’ → “these made here”: the subject moves before the verb, and the adverb follows it. The distal expressions ‘those’ and ‘there’ become the proximal “these” and “here”.

> 14 e come a tai fortezze dai lor sogli  
> 15 alla ripa di fuor son ponticelli,  

and as, from the thresholds of the fortress, there are bridges to the outward bank:

- *a tai fortezze dai lor sogli* ‘at such fortresses from their thresholds’ → “from the thresholds of the fortress”: the locative phrase is absorbed into the possessive construction “of the fortress”, replacing ‘their’. The plural becomes singular, and ‘such’ is replaced by a definite article.
- *alla ripa di fuor* ‘to the bank outside’ → “to the outward bank”: the postposed expression ‘outside’ becomes an adjective before the noun; the whole phrase moves after the clause about the bridges.
- *son ponticelli* ‘are little bridges’ → “there are bridges”: existential “there” is supplied, and the diminutive force of *ponticelli* is not expressed.

## Lines 16–48 — Scourged Sinners in the First Chasm

> 16 così da imo della roccia scogli  
> 17 movien, che ricidean gli argini e fossi  
> 18 infino al pozzo, che i tronca e raccogli.  

so from the basis of the rock proceeded cliffs that crossed the embankments and the ditches, down to the well which truncates and collects them.

- *da imo della roccia* ‘from the bottom of the rock’ → “from the basis of the rock”: the rock’s lowest part is expressed as its “basis”.
- *scogli movien* ‘rock spurs extended’ → “proceeded cliffs”: the subject follows the verb, and projecting rocks are described as “cliffs” that “proceeded”.
- *ricidean* ‘cut across’ → “crossed”: the image of cutting through is reduced to crossing.
- *gli argini e fossi* ‘the embankments and ditches’ → “the embankments and the ditches”: the article is repeated before the second noun.
- *infino al pozzo* ‘as far as the well’ → “down to the well”: “down” supplies a direction not separately expressed in the Italian.
- *i tronca e raccogli* ‘them truncates and gathers’ → “truncates and collects them”: the shared object pronoun moves after both verbs.

> 19 In questo loco, dalla schiena scossi  
> 20 di Gerion, trovammoci; e il poeta  
> 21 tenne a sinistra, ed io retro mi mossi.  

In this place, shaken from the back of Geryon, we found ourselves ; and the Poet kept to the left, and I moved behind.

- *dalla schiena scossi di Gerion* ‘from the back shaken of Geryon’ → “shaken from the back of Geryon”: the participle precedes the prepositional phrase, bringing “of Geryon” immediately beside “the back”.
- *ed io retro mi mossi* ‘and I behind moved myself’ → “and I moved behind”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and the adverb follows it.

> 22 Alla man destra vidi nuova pieta,  
> 23 nuovi tormenti e nuovi frustatori,  
> 24 di che la prima bolgia era repleta.  

On the right hand I saw new misery, new torments, and new tormenters, wherewith the first chasm was filled.

- *Alla man destra* ‘at the right hand’ → “On the right hand”: the spatial preposition changes.
- *nuova pieta* ‘new pity’ → “new misery”: the translation names the suffering that provokes pity rather than pity itself.
- *nuovi frustatori* ‘new scourgers’ → “new tormenters”: the specific reference to whipping becomes a general term for inflicting suffering.
- *di che* ‘of which’ → “wherewith”: the complement is expressed with ‘with’ rather than ‘of’, using a relative adverb.
- *la prima bolgia* ‘the first ditch’ → “the first chasm”: “chasm” replaces the more specific designation of a ditch.

> 25 Nel fondo erano ignudi i peccatori;  

In its bottom the sinners were naked;

- *Nel fondo* ‘in the bottom’ → “In its bottom”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *erano ignudi i peccatori* ‘were naked the sinners’ → “the sinners were naked”: the subject moves before the verb and its complement.

> 26 dal mezzo in qua ci venian verso il volto,  
> 27 di là con noi, ma con passi maggiori:  

[on our side of the middle] they came facing us ; and, on the other side, along with us, but with larger steps:

- *dal mezzo in qua* ‘from the middle hither’ → “[on our side of the middle]”: the directional expression becomes a description of position relative to the observers.
- *ci venian verso il volto* ‘to us they came towards the face’ → “they came facing us”: the two expressions of direction towards the observers become the participial phrase “facing us”; the explicit reference to the face disappears.
- *di là* ‘on that side’ → “on the other side”: the demonstrative becomes a relational description.
- *con noi* ‘with us’ → “along with us”: “along” makes the shared direction of movement explicit.
- *con passi maggiori* ‘with steps larger’ → “with larger steps”: the adjective moves before its noun.

> 28 come i Roman, per l' esercito molto,  
> 29 l' anno del Giubbileo, su per lo ponte  
> 30 hanno a passar la gente modo colto,  

thus the Romans, because of the great throng, in the year of Jubilee, upon the bridge have taken means to pass the people over

- *come* ‘as’ → “thus”: the comparative conjunction becomes a demonstrative adverb.
- *per l' esercito molto* ‘because of the host great’ → “because of the great throng”: “throng” renders *esercito* as a crowd rather than an army or host, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *l' anno del Giubbileo* ‘the year of the Jubilee’ → “in the year of Jubilee”: “in” makes the temporal construction explicit, while the article before *Giubbileo* is omitted.
- *su per lo ponte* ‘along the bridge’ → “upon the bridge”: the expression of passage along a surface becomes one of location upon it.
- *hanno a passar la gente modo colto* ‘have, to pass the people, a method chosen’ → “have taken means to pass the people over”: the object follows the completed verb phrase, and the infinitive follows the object; choosing a method becomes taking means, and “over” is supplied.

> 31 che dall' un lato tutti hanno la fronte  
> 32 verso il castello e vanno a Santo Pietro,  
> 33 dall' altra sponda vanno verso il monte.  

; so that, on the one side, all have their faces towards the Castle, and go to St Peter's ; at the other ledge, they go towards the Mount.

- *che* ‘that’ → “so that”: “so” explicitly marks the resulting arrangement.
- *tutti hanno la fronte verso il castello* ‘all have the forehead towards the castle’ → “all have their faces towards the Castle”: the singular body part becomes plural, the article becomes possessive, and “faces” replaces ‘forehead’.
- *a Santo Pietro* ‘to Saint Peter’ → “to St Peter's”: the possessive makes the destination an implicitly named place associated with the saint.
- *dall' altra sponda* ‘on the other bank’ → “at the other ledge”: both the spatial preposition and the term for the bordering edge change.

> 34 Di qua, di là, su per lo sasso tetro  
> 35 vidi demon cornuti con gran ferze,  
> 36 che li battean crudelmente di retro.  

On this side, on that, along the hideous stone, I saw horned Demons with large scourges, who smote them fiercely from behind.

- *Di qua, di là* ‘here, there’ → “On this side, on that”: the deictic adverbs are expanded into references to opposite sides.
- *su per lo sasso tetro* ‘along the stone gloomy’ → “along the hideous stone”: “hideous” replaces the suggestion of darkness or gloom, and the adjective moves before its noun.
- *demon cornuti* ‘demons horned’ → “horned Demons”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *li battean* ‘them beat’ → “smote them”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *crudelmente* ‘cruelly’ → “fiercely”: fierceness replaces the explicit characterization of cruelty.

> 37 Ahi come facean lor levar le berze  
> 38 alle prime percosse! già nessuno  
> 39 le seconde aspettava nè le terze.  

Ah! how they made them lift their legs at the first strokes ! truly none waited for the second or the third.

- *le berze* ‘the heels’ → “their legs”: the translation takes *berze* as the legs rather than, more narrowly, the heels; a possessive replaces the article.
- *già* ‘already’ → “truly”: the word is interpreted emphatically rather than temporally.
- *nessuno le seconde aspettava nè le terze* ‘no one the second awaited nor the third’ → “none waited for the second or the third”: the first object moves after the verb; transitive ‘awaited’ becomes “waited for”; and ‘nor’ becomes “or” under the preceding negative “none”.

> 40 Mentr' io andava, gli occhi miei in uno  
> 41 furo scontrati; ed io sì tosto dissi:  
> 42 "Di già veder costui non son digiuno."  

As I went on, my eyes were met by one, and instantly I said : “This one I have seen before.”

- *Mentr' io andava* ‘while I went’ → “As I went on”: “on” is supplied to express continuing movement.
- *gli occhi miei* ‘the eyes my’ → “my eyes”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *in uno furo scontrati* ‘upon one were encountered’ → “were met by one”: the encounter with someone is recast as a passive construction in which that person becomes the agent, expressed by “by one”; the complement follows the verb.
- *ed io sì tosto dissi* ‘and I so soon said’ → “and instantly I said”: the adverbial expression becomes “instantly” and moves before the subject.
- *Di già veder costui non son digiuno* ‘of already seeing this man I am not fasting’ → “This one I have seen before.”: the metaphor of not fasting from an experience becomes a direct affirmative statement; the infinitive becomes a present-perfect verb, “before” expresses prior experience, and “This one” replaces the explicitly masculine demonstrative.

> 43 Perciò a figurarlo i piedi affissi;  
> 44 e il dolce duca meco si ristette,  
> 45 ed assentì ch' alquanto indietro gissi.  

I therefore stayed my feet to recognize him ; and the kind Guide stood still with me, and allowed me to go back a little.

- *Perciò a figurarlo i piedi affissi* ‘therefore, to discern him, the feet I fixed’ → “I therefore stayed my feet to recognize him”: the verb precedes its object, and the purpose phrase moves to the end; fixing the feet becomes staying them, discerning becomes recognizing, and the article before the body part becomes possessive.
- *il dolce duca* ‘the sweet leader’ → “the kind Guide”: affectionate ‘sweet’ becomes “kind”, and ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”.
- *meco si ristette* ‘with me stopped himself’ → “stood still with me”: the reflexive verb becomes the intransitive phrase “stood still”, and “with me” moves after it.
- *ed assentì ch' alquanto indietro gissi* ‘and assented that a little backward I should go’ → “and allowed me to go back a little”: assent becomes permission; the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object-plus-infinitive construction; and the expressions of direction and extent follow the verb.

> 46 E quel frustato celar si credette  
> 47 bassando il viso, ma poco gli valse;  
> 48 ch' io dissi: "Tu che l' occhio a terra gette,  

And that scourged _spirit_ thought to hide himself, lowering his face ; but little it availed him, for I said : “Thou, that dost cast thy eye upon the ground!

- *E quel frustato* ‘and that scourged one’ → “And that scourged _spirit_”: the substantival participle is expanded with the supplied noun “_spirit_”.
- *celar si credette* ‘to hide himself he thought’ → “thought to hide himself”: the governing verb moves before the infinitive.
- *il viso* ‘the face’ → “his face”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *ma poco gli valse* ‘but little to him it availed’ → “but little it availed him”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *ch' io dissi* ‘because I said’ → “for I said”: the subordinating causal conjunction becomes the coordinating “for”.
- *Tu che l' occhio a terra gette* ‘thou who the eye to earth castest’ → “Thou, that dost cast thy eye upon the ground!”: “that” replaces the personal relative; the verb precedes its object and takes the auxiliary “dost”; the article becomes possessive; and direction ‘to earth’ becomes “upon the ground”.

## Lines 49–81 — Venedico’s Confession and the Bridge

> 49 se le fazion che porti non son false,  
> 50 Venedico se’ tu Caccianimico;  
> 51 ma che ti mena a sì pungenti salse?”  

If the features which thou wearest be not false, thou art Venedico Caccianimico; but what brings thee to such a biting pickle?”

- *non son false* ‘are not false’ → “be not false”: the Italian indicative becomes an English subjunctive in the conditional clause.
- *Venedico se’ tu Caccianimico* ‘Venedico art thou Caccianimico’ → “thou art Venedico Caccianimico”: subject and verb precede the name, whose two parts are brought together.
- *mena* ‘leads’ → “brings”: the verb of leading becomes one of bringing.
- *sì pungenti salse* ‘so pungent sauces’ → “such a biting pickle”: the plural becomes singular, with an indefinite article; “such” replaces the degree adverb, and “biting pickle” recasts the culinary expression.

> 52 Ed egli a me: “Mal volentier lo dico;  
> 53 ma sforzami la tua chiara favella,  
> 54 che mi fa sovvenir del mondo antico.  

And he to me, “Unwillingly I tell it; but thy clear speech, that makes me recollect the former world, compels me.

- *Mal volentier* ‘ill willingly’ → “Unwillingly”: the two-word expression becomes a single negative adverb.
- *lo dico* ‘it I say’ → “I tell it”: the object follows the verb, and ‘say’ becomes “tell”.
- *sforzami la tua chiara favella* ‘compels me thy clear speech’ → “thy clear speech, that makes me recollect the former world, compels me”: the subject moves before the verb; its relative clause consequently intervenes between subject and predicate.
- *sovvenir del mondo antico* ‘remember the ancient world’ → “recollect the former world”: the complement introduced by *di* becomes a direct object, and “former” renders *antico* as belonging to an earlier life or time rather than simply being ancient.

> 55 Io fui colui, che la Ghisola bella  
> 56 condussi a far la voglia del Marchese,  
> 57 come che suoni la sconcia novella.  

It was I who led the fair Ghisola to do the Marquis' will, however the unseemly tale may sound.

- *Io fui colui, che* ‘I was the one who’ → “It was I who”: the identification is recast as an emphatic cleft construction.
- *la Ghisola bella* ‘the Ghisola fair’ → “the fair Ghisola”: the adjective moves before the name.
- *far la voglia del Marchese* ‘do the will of the Marquis’ → “do the Marquis' will”: the possessive construction replaces the phrase with *di* and moves the possessor before “will”.
- *come che suoni la sconcia novella* ‘however sound the unseemly tale’ → “however the unseemly tale may sound”: the subject moves before the verb, and “may” expresses the Italian subjunctive.

> 58 E non pur io qui piango Bolognese:  

And I am not the only Bolognese that weeps here:

- *E non pur io qui piango Bolognese* ‘And not only I here weep as a Bolognese’ → “And I am not the only Bolognese that weeps here”: the main assertion of weeping becomes a construction with “am” and a relative clause; *Bolognese* becomes the noun qualified by “only”, and the locative moves after the verb.

> 59 anzi n’è questo loco tanto pieno,  
> 60 che tante lingue non son ora apprese  
> 61 a dicer ‘sipa’ tra Savena e Reno;  

nay, this place is so filled with us, that as many tongues are not now taught to say *sipa* between Savena and Reno;

- *n’è questo loco tanto pieno* ‘of them is this place so full’ → “this place is so filled with us”: subject–verb order replaces the inversion; *ne* becomes an explicit first-person phrase, and the adjective ‘full’ becomes the participial “filled”.
- *tante* ‘so many’ → “as many”: the comparison is made explicit.

> 62 e se di ciò vuoi fede o testimonio,  
> 63 recati a mente il nostro avaro seno.”  

and if thou desirest assurance and testimony thereof, recall to thy memory our avaricious heart.”

- *di ciò* ‘of this’ → “thereof”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb and moves from before the verb to after “testimony”.
- *o* ‘or’ → “and”: the alternative becomes a conjunction.
- *recati a mente* ‘bring to mind for thyself’ → “recall to thy memory”: the reflexive dative becomes the possessive “thy”, and the idiom is recast with “recall”.
- *seno* ‘breast’ → “heart”: one bodily term replaces another in the figurative expression.

> 64 Così parlando il percosse un demonio  
> 65 della sua scuriada, e disse: “Via,  
> 66 ruffian, qui non son femmine da conio.”  

And as he thus spake, a Demon smote him with his lash, and said: “Away! [pandar] there are no women here to coin.”

- *Così parlando* ‘thus speaking’ → “And as he thus spake”: “And” is supplied, and the gerund becomes a finite temporal clause with an explicit subject.
- *il percosse un demonio* ‘him smote a demon’ → “a Demon smote him”: subject–verb–object order replaces object–verb–subject order.
- *della sua scuriada* ‘of his lash’ → “with his lash”: instrumental *di* is rendered by “with”.
- *qui non son femmine* ‘here are no women’ → “there are no women here”: existential “there” is supplied, and “here” moves after the noun.
- *da conio* ‘for coining’ → “to coin”: the noun construction becomes an active infinitive. The translation adopts the monetary sense of *conio*, rather than the alternative sense ‘deception’.

> 67 Io mi raggiunsi con la scorta mia;  

I rejoined my Escort;

- *mi raggiunsi con* ‘joined myself again with’ → “rejoined”: the pronominal verb and its preposition become a directly transitive verb.
- *la scorta mia* ‘the escort mine’ → “my Escort”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 68 poscia con pochi passi divenimmo  
> 69 là dove un scoglio della ripa uscia.  

then, with a few steps, we came to where a cliff proceeded from the bank.

- *con pochi passi* ‘with few steps’ → “with a few steps”: “a” is supplied, giving the phrase the sense of a small number rather than emphasizing scarcity.
- *divenimmo là dove* ‘we came there where’ → “we came to where”: the locative *là* is absorbed into a directional construction with “to”.
- *un scoglio* ‘a rock’ → “a cliff”: the more general rocky feature is rendered as a cliff.
- *della ripa uscia* ‘from the bank came out’ → “proceeded from the bank”: the verb moves before its complement, and ‘came out’ becomes “proceeded”.

> 70 Assai leggeramente quel salimmo,  
> 71 e volti a destra su per la sua scheggia,  
> 72 da quelle cerchie eterne ci partimmo.  

This we very easily ascended; and, turning to the right upon its jagged ridge, we quitted those eternal circles.

- *Assai leggeramente quel salimmo* ‘very lightly that we climbed’ → “This we very easily ascended”: the object moves to the beginning; ‘that’ becomes “This”, and *leggeramente* is understood as ease rather than lightness of movement.
- *volti* ‘turned’ → “turning”: the past participle becomes a present participle, presenting the turn as accompanying the action rather than already accomplished.
- *su per la sua scheggia* ‘up along its splinter’ → “upon its jagged ridge”: the directional expression becomes “upon”, while *scheggia* is expanded into a descriptive phrase naming a ridge.
- *da quelle cerchie eterne ci partimmo* ‘from those circles eternal we departed’ → “we quitted those eternal circles”: the pronominal intransitive construction with *da* becomes a transitive one; the complement follows the verb, and the adjective precedes its noun.

> 73 Quando noi fummo là, dov’ei vaneggia  
> 74 di sotto, per dar passo agli sferzati,  

When we reached the part where it yawns beneath to leave a passage for the scourged,

- *noi fummo là* ‘we were there’ → “we reached the part”: being at a place becomes reaching it, and the locative adverb becomes the noun phrase “the part”.
- *vaneggia* ‘is hollow’ → “yawns”: a spatial condition is expressed through a figurative verb.
- *per dar passo agli sferzati* ‘to give passage to the scourged’ → “to leave a passage for the scourged”: ‘give’ becomes “leave”, an indefinite article is supplied, and the recipient construction with ‘to’ becomes one with “for”.

> 75 lo duca disse: “Attienti, e fa che feggia  
> 76 lo viso in te di questi altri mal nati,  
> 77 a’ quali ancor non vedesti la faccia,  
> 78 però che son con noi insieme andati.”  

my Guide said: “Stay, and let the look strike on thee of these other ill-born *spirits,* whose faces thou hast not yet seen, for they have gone along with us.”

- *lo duca* ‘the leader’ → “my Guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”.
- *Attienti* ‘hold thyself’ → “Stay”: the pronominal command becomes a simple intransitive imperative.
- *fa che feggia lo viso in te* ‘make the face strike upon thee’ → “let the look strike on thee”: the causative construction with a subjunctive becomes “let” with a bare infinitive; the subject of ‘strike’ moves before that verb, and *viso* is interpreted as “look” rather than ‘face’.
- *mal nati* ‘ill-born’ → “ill-born *spirits,*”: a noun is supplied where the Italian adjective stands substantivally.
- *a’ quali ancor non vedesti la faccia* ‘to whom thou didst not yet see the face’ → “whose faces thou hast not yet seen”: the dative relative becomes possessive “whose”; the singular becomes plural, and the simple past becomes a present perfect. The object moves before the subject and verb, while *ancor non* is reordered as “not yet”.
- *però che* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *con noi insieme* ‘with us together’ → “along with us”: ‘together’ becomes “along” and precedes “with us”; the whole expression moves after the participle “gone”.

> 79 Dal vecchio ponte guardavam la traccia,  
> 80 che venia verso noi dall’ altra banda,  
> 81 e che la ferza similmente scaccia.  

From the ancient bridge we viewed the train, who were coming towards us, on the other side, chased likewise by the scourge.

- *vecchio* ‘old’ → “ancient”: the English gives a stronger expression of age.
- *guardavam* ‘we were watching’ → “we viewed”: the imperfect is rendered by a simple past rather than an explicitly ongoing construction.
- *traccia* ‘track’ → “train”: the translation takes the noun in its sense of a moving file rather than a track left behind.
- *che venia verso noi* ‘which was coming towards us’ → “who were coming towards us”: the singular collective antecedent is treated as plural persons, producing “who” and “were”.
- *dall’ altra banda* ‘from the other side’ → “on the other side”: origin or direction becomes location.
- *e che la ferza similmente scaccia* ‘and which the lash likewise drives away’ → “chased likewise by the scourge”: the coordinated active relative clause becomes a passive participial phrase, without an expressed conjunction or relative pronoun. The subject becomes the agent introduced by “by”, and ‘drives away’ becomes “chased”.

## Lines 82–114 — Jason’s Deceit and the Filthy Ditch

> 82 Il buon maestro, senza mia domanda,  
> 83 mi disse: "Guarda quel grande che viene,  
> 84 e per dolor non par lagrima spanda:  

The kind Master, without my asking, said to me: “Look at that great *soul* who comes, and seems to shed no tear for pain:

- *buon* ‘good’ → “kind”: goodness is rendered specifically as kindness.
- *senza mia domanda* ‘without my question’ → “without my asking”: the noun becomes a gerund naming the act of asking.
- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *Guarda* ‘Behold’ → “Look at”: the directly transitive verb becomes a verb with a preposition.
- *quel grande* ‘that great one’ → “that great *soul*”: the substantival adjective is given the explicit noun “*soul*”.
- *per dolor non par lagrima spanda* ‘for pain it does not seem that he sheds a tear’ → “seems to shed no tear for pain”: the subordinate finite clause becomes an infinitive; the negative moves from seeming to the tear; and the phrase expressing cause moves to the end.

> 85 quanto aspetto reale ancor ritiene!  

what a *regal* aspect he yet retains!

- *quanto aspetto reale* ‘how much aspect royal’ → “what a *regal* aspect”: the quantitative exclamation becomes the English construction with “what a”, and the adjective precedes its noun.

> 86 Quelli è Jason, che per core e per senno  
> 87 li Colchi del monton privati fene.  

That is Jason, who, by courage and by counsel, bereft the Colchians of the ram.

- *Quelli* ‘that one’ → “That”: the substantival demonstrative is rendered without ‘one’.
- *per core e per senno* ‘through heart and through judgment’ → “by courage and by counsel”: the prepositions express means; ‘heart’ is rendered as courage, and practical judgment as counsel.
- *li Colchi del monton privati fene* ‘the Colchians of the ram deprived made’ → “bereft the Colchians of the ram”: the construction with *fare* and a participle becomes a single verb, which precedes its object and complement.

> 88 Egli passò per l' isola di Lenno,  
> 89 poi che le ardite femmine spietate  
> 90 tutti li maschi loro a morte dienno.  

He passed, by the isle of Lemnos, after the bold merciless women had given all their males to death.

- *passò per l' isola* ‘passed through the island’ → “passed, by the isle”: *per* is interpreted as passage by the island rather than through it.
- *le ardite femmine spietate* ‘the bold women merciless’ → “the bold merciless women”: the second adjective moves before the noun.
- *tutti li maschi loro a morte dienno* ‘all the males their to death gave’ → “had given all their males to death”: the simple past becomes a past perfect, making the anterior action explicit; the verb precedes its object and complement, and the possessive precedes the noun.

> 91 Ivi con segni e con parole ornate  
> 92 Isifile ingannò, la giovinetta  
> 93 che prima avea tutte l'altre ingannate.  

There, with tokens and fair words, did he deceive the young Hypsipyle, who had before deceived all the rest.

- *con segni e con parole ornate* ‘with signs and with words adorned’ → “with tokens and fair words”: ‘signs’ becomes “tokens”, adorned language becomes “fair” language, the repeated preposition is omitted, and the adjective moves before its noun.
- *Isifile ingannò, la giovinetta* ‘Hypsipyle he deceived, the young girl’ → “did he deceive the young Hypsipyle”: the object moves after the verb; the appositional noun phrase becomes an adjective attached to the name; and the simple past is expanded with “did”, followed by the subject.
- *che prima avea tutte l'altre ingannate* ‘who before had all the others deceived’ → “who had before deceived all the rest”: “before” moves after the auxiliary, the participle precedes its object, and ‘the others’ becomes “the rest”.

> 94 Lasciolla quivi gravida e soletta:  

He left her there pregnant and forlorn:

- *soletta* ‘quite alone’ → “forlorn”: the diminutive’s emphasis on being alone becomes a word suggesting abandonment and distress.

> 95 tal colpa a tal martiro lui condanna;  
> 96 ed anche di Medea si fa vendetta.  

such guilt condemns him to such torment; and also for Medea vengeance is taken.

- *a tal martiro lui condanna* ‘to such torment him condemns’ → “condemns him to such torment”: the verb moves before the object, and the prepositional complement follows it.
- *di Medea* ‘of Medea’ → “for Medea”: the preposition is interpreted as identifying the person on whose behalf vengeance is taken.
- *si fa vendetta* ‘vengeance is made’ → “vengeance is taken”: the Italian passive construction is retained, but the expression with ‘make’ becomes the English idiom with “taken”.

> 97 Con lui sen va chi da tal parte inganna;  
> 98 e questo basti della prima valle  
> 99 sapere, e di color che in sè assanna."  

With him go all who practise the like deceit; and let this suffice to know respecting the first valley, and those whom it devours.”

- *Con lui sen va chi* ‘with him goes away whoever’ → “With him go all who”: singular *chi* becomes plural “all who”, with a corresponding change in the verb; the departure expressed by *se ne va* is reduced to “go”.
- *da tal parte inganna* ‘in such a respect deceives’ → “practise the like deceit”: the qualification of the verb becomes a modifier of the noun “deceit”, and ‘deceives’ becomes a verb-and-object expression.
- *questo basti* ‘may this suffice’ → “let this suffice”: the subjunctive is expressed through an English “let” construction.
- *della prima valle sapere, e di color* ‘of the first valley to know, and of those’ → “to know respecting the first valley, and those”: the infinitive moves before its first complement; “respecting” renders the first *di* and also governs the second complement, without a repeated preposition.
- *che in sè assanna* ‘whom within itself it grips with its teeth’ → “whom it devours”: gripping with the teeth becomes devouring, and ‘within itself’ is not separately expressed.

> 100 Già eravam là 've lo stretto calle  
> 101 con l' argine secondo s' incrocicchia,  
> 102 e fa di quello ad un altro arco spalle.  

We had already come to where the narrow pathway crosses the second bank, and makes of it a buttress for another arch.

- *Già eravam là 've* ‘already we were there where’ → “We had already come to where”: an imperfect expressing location becomes a past perfect expressing arrival; “already” moves inside the verb phrase, and ‘there where’ becomes the destination “to where”.
- *con l' argine secondo s' incrocicchia* ‘with the bank second intersects itself’ → “crosses the second bank”: the pronominal construction with *con* becomes a directly transitive verb, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *di quello* ‘of that’ → “of it”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *ad un altro arco spalle* ‘for another arch shoulders’ → “a buttress for another arch”: plural ‘shoulders’ becomes a singular architectural noun, with an added indefinite article; the noun moves before the phrase identifying what it supports.

> 103 Quindi sentimmo gente, che si nicchia  
> 104 nell' altra bolgia e che col muso isbuffa,  
> 105 e sè medesma con le palme picchia.  

Here we heard people whining in the other chasm, and puffing with mouth and nostrils, and knocking on themselves with their palms.

- *Quindi* ‘from there’ → “Here”: the indication of a point from which something is perceived becomes a simple indication of present location.
- *che si nicchia* ‘who whines’ → “whining”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial complement to “heard”.
- *nell' altra bolgia* ‘in the other pouch’ → “in the other chasm”: the pouch metaphor is replaced by a term for the physical hollow.
- *e che col muso isbuffa* ‘and who with the muzzle snorts’ → “and puffing with mouth and nostrils”: the relative clause becomes a participial phrase, ‘snorts’ becomes “puffing”, and the single animal term *muso* is expanded into two human organs; the verb also moves before the accompanying prepositional phrase.
- *e sè medesma con le palme picchia* ‘and itself with the palms beats’ → “and knocking on themselves with their palms”: the finite verb becomes a participle and moves before its complements; direct beating becomes “knocking on”; the singular reflexive referring to *gente* becomes plural “themselves”; and the definite article before “palms” becomes a possessive.

> 106 Le ripe eran grommate d' una muffa  
> 107 per l' alito di giù che vi si appasta,  
> 108 che con gli occhi e col naso facea zuffa.  

The banks were crusted over with a mould from the vapour below, which concretes upon them, which did battle with the eyes and with the nose.

- *per l' alito di giù* ‘because of the breath from below’ → “from the vapour below”: cause is expressed as source, ‘breath’ becomes “vapour”, and *di giù* loses its explicit indication of origin.
- *che vi si appasta* ‘which there sticks itself’ → “which concretes upon them”: sticking or adhering becomes congealing; the pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive verb, and the locative *vi* is expanded into “upon them”.
- *che con gli occhi e col naso facea zuffa* ‘which with the eyes and with the nose was making a brawl’ → “which did battle with the eyes and with the nose”: the verbal expression moves before its complements; the idiom becomes “did battle”, and the imperfect is rendered by a simple past.

> 109 Lo fondo è cupo sì che non ci basta  
> 110 loco a veder senza montare al dosso  
> 111 dell' arco, ove lo scoglio più soprasta.  

The bottom is so deep, that we could see *it* nowhere without mounting to the ridge of the arch, where the cliff stands highest.

- *cupo sì* ‘deep so’ → “so deep”: the degree adverb moves before the adjective.
- *che non ci basta loco a veder* ‘that no place suffices us for seeing’ → “that we could see *it* nowhere”: the construction about an adequate viewing-place becomes one about the observers’ ability to see; present ‘suffices’ becomes past modal “could”, ‘no place’ becomes “nowhere”, and the object “*it*” is supplied.
- *al dosso* ‘to the back’ → “to the ridge”: the bodily metaphor becomes a topographical term.
- *ove lo scoglio più soprasta* ‘where the rock rises above most’ → “where the cliff stands highest”: ‘rock’ becomes “cliff”, and the expression of greatest elevation above something becomes “stands highest”, without an expressed relation to what lies below.

> 112 Quivi venimmo, e quindi giù nel fosso  
> 113 vidi gente attuffata in uno sterco,  
> 114 che dagli uman privati parea mosso.  

We got upon it; and [thence] in the ditch beneath, I saw a people dipped in excrement, that seemed as *it had* flowed from human privies.

- *Quivi venimmo* ‘there we came’ → “We got upon it”: the initial locative becomes a following prepositional phrase specifying arrival upon something, and ‘came’ becomes “got”.
- *giù nel fosso* ‘down in the ditch’ → “in the ditch beneath”: the initial adverb becomes a following qualification of the ditch’s position.
- *gente* ‘people’ → “a people”: an indefinite article makes the collective noun an explicitly countable group.
- *uno sterco* ‘an excrement’ → “excrement”: the indefinite article is omitted with the English mass noun.
- *che dagli uman privati parea mosso* ‘which from the human privies seemed moved’ → “that seemed as *it had* flowed from human privies”: the source phrase moves after the verb; the construction with a participle becomes an “as” clause with a supplied subject and past-perfect auxiliary; passive or resultative movement becomes active flowing; and the definite article before “human privies” is omitted.

## Lines 115–136 — Flatterers in Filth

> 115 E mentre ch' io laggiù con l'occhio cerco,  
> 116 vidi un col capo sì di merda lordo,  
> 117 che non parea s' era laico o cherco.  

And whilst I was searching with my eyes, down amongst it, I beheld one with a head so smeared in filth, that it did not appear whether he was layman or clerk.

- *laggiù* ‘down there’ → “down amongst it”: the simple indication of place becomes a prepositional phrase with a supplied pronoun, and moves after the phrase about the eyes.
- *con l'occhio* ‘with the eye’ → “with my eyes”: the singular becomes plural, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves after the verb.
- *cerco* ‘I search’ → “I was searching”: the present becomes a past progressive.
- *col capo* ‘with the head’ → “with a head”: the definite article becomes indefinite.
- *sì di merda lordo* ‘so with shit befouled’ → “so smeared in filth”: the complement follows rather than precedes the adjective; “smeared” makes the coating more explicit, while “filth” softens the explicit noun.

> 118 Quei mi sgridò: “Perchè se’ tu sì ingordo  
> 119 di riguardar più me, che gli altri brutti?”  

He bawled to me: "Why art thou so eager in gazing at me, more than the others in their nastiness?"

- *Quei* ‘that one’ → “He”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *mi sgridò* ‘rebuked me’ → “bawled to me”: a verb of reprimand becomes a verb emphasizing loud speech, and the direct object becomes a prepositional complement following the verb.
- *sì ingordo* ‘so greedy’ → “so eager”: the translation weakens the suggestion of voracious appetite.
- *di riguardar* ‘to gaze at’ → “in gazing at”: the infinitive becomes a gerund introduced by “in”.
- *più me, che gli altri brutti* ‘more me than the other filthy ones’ → “me, more than the others in their nastiness”: “more” moves after “me”, and the adjective describing the others becomes a prepositional phrase containing an abstract noun and a supplied possessive.

> 120 Ed io a lui: “Perchè, se ben ricordo,  
> 121 già t' ho veduto coi capelli asciutti,  
> 122 e sei Alessio Interminei da Lucca:  
> 123 però t' adocchio più che gli altri tutti.”  

And I to him: "Because, if I rightly recollect, I have seen thee before with thy hair dry; and thou art Alessio Interminei of Lucca: therefore do I eye thee more than all the rest."

- *se ben ricordo* ‘if I remember well’ → “if I rightly recollect”: good recollection is expressed as correct recollection.
- *già t' ho veduto* ‘already thee I have seen’ → “I have seen thee before”: ‘already’ becomes “before” and moves to the end of the clause; the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *coi capelli asciutti* ‘with the hairs dry’ → “with thy hair dry”: the plural noun becomes an English mass noun, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *t' adocchio* ‘thee I eye’ → “do I eye thee”: the object follows the verb, and the translation introduces “do” with subject–auxiliary inversion.
- *più che gli altri tutti* ‘more than the others all’ → “more than all the rest”: “all” precedes rather than follows the nominal expression, and ‘the others’ becomes the collective “the rest”.

> 124 Ed egli allor, battendosi la zucca:  
> 125 “Quaggiù m' hanno sommerso le lusinghe,  
> 126 ond' io non ebbi mai la lingua stucca.”  

And he then, beating his pate: "Down to this, the flatteries wherewith my tongue was never weary have sunk me!"

- *battendosi la zucca* ‘beating himself the gourd’ → “beating his pate”: the reflexive construction becomes a possessive construction, and the figurative ‘gourd’ is replaced by a colloquial word for the head.
- *Quaggiù* ‘down here’ → “Down to this”: location becomes an endpoint expressed through the supplied demonstrative “this”.
- *m' hanno sommerso le lusinghe* ‘me have submerged the flatteries’ → “the flatteries” and “have sunk me”: the subject moves before the verb and the object after it; the verb phrase is postponed until after the relative clause. ‘Submerged’ becomes “sunk”.
- *ond' io non ebbi mai la lingua stucca* ‘of which I never had the tongue sated’ → “wherewith my tongue was never weary”: the construction with ‘I had’ becomes one with “my tongue” as subject and “was” as verb. The article becomes a possessive, ‘sated’ becomes “weary”, and the relative connection is expressed through “wherewith”.

> 127 Appresso ciò lo duca: “Fa che pinghe,”  
> 128 mi disse, “il viso un poco più avante,  
> 129 sì che la faccia ben con gli occhi attinghe  
> 130 di quella sozza e scapigliata fante,  

Thereupon my Guide said to me: "Stretch thy face a little forwards, that thy eyes may fully reach the visage of that unclean and dishevelled strumpet,

- *Appresso ciò* ‘after this’ → “Thereupon”: the prepositional phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *lo duca* ‘the guide’ → “my Guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Fa che pinghe* ‘make that thou push’ → “Stretch”: the imperative governing a subordinate subjunctive becomes a single direct imperative, and ‘push’ becomes “Stretch”.
- *mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object follows the verb, and the reporting clause moves from within the quoted command to before it.
- *il viso* ‘the face’ → “thy face”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *un poco più avante* ‘a little further forward’ → “a little forwards”: the explicit comparative *più* is omitted.
- *sì che* ‘so that’ → “that”: the connective is reduced by omitting the explicit equivalent of *sì*.
- *la faccia ben con gli occhi attinghe* ‘the face well with the eyes thou mayest reach’ → “thy eyes may fully reach the visage”: the instrumental ‘with the eyes’ becomes the subject, with a possessive replacing the article. The object moves after the verb, *ben* becomes “fully”, and the subjunctive is expressed with “may”.
- *fante* ‘servant-girl’ → “strumpet”: the translation adopts a sexually derogatory interpretation rather than the noun’s literal occupational sense.

> 131 che là si graffia con l'unghie merdose,  
> 132 ed or s' accoscia, ed ora è in piede stante.  

who yonder with her filthy nails scratches herself, now cowering low, now standing on her feet.

- *si graffia con l'unghie merdose* ‘scratches herself with the shitty nails’ → “with her filthy nails scratches herself”: the instrumental phrase moves before the verb, while the reflexive pronoun follows it. The article becomes a possessive, the adjective precedes its noun, and “filthy” softens the explicit adjective.
- *ed or s' accoscia* ‘and now squats down’ → “now cowering low”: ‘and’ is omitted, the finite reflexive verb becomes a participle, and the physical action is rendered as “cowering”, with “low” making the lowered position explicit.
- *ed ora è in piede stante* ‘and now is on foot standing’ → “now standing on her feet”: ‘and’ and the finite verb ‘is’ are omitted. The phrase about the feet follows “standing”; singular ‘foot’ becomes plural “feet”, and a possessive is supplied.

> 133 Taide è, la puttana che rispose  
> 134 al drudo suo, quando disse: ‘Ho io grazie  
> 135 grandi appo te?’ ‘Anzi, meravigliose.’  

It is Thais, the harlot, who answered her paramour, when he said: ‘Dost thou thank me much?’ ‘Nay, wondrously.’

- *Taide è* ‘Thais is’ → “It is Thais”: the translation supplies an introductory “It” and places the name after the verb.
- *al drudo suo* ‘to the paramour her’ → “her paramour”: the Italian prepositional complement becomes the direct object of “answered”, and the possessive precedes rather than follows the noun.
- *Ho io grazie grandi appo te* ‘Have I great thanks with thee?’ → “Dost thou thank me much?”: possession of thanks becomes the action of thanking. The Italian subject becomes “me”, the person addressed becomes the subject “thou”, the noun *grazie* becomes the verb “thank”, and the adjective *grandi* becomes the adverb “much”.
- *Anzi, meravigliose* ‘rather, marvellous’ → “Nay, wondrously”: the corrective ‘rather’ becomes “Nay”, and the plural adjective agreeing with the understood ‘thanks’ becomes an adverb modifying the understood act of thanking.

> 136 E quinci sien le nostre viste sazie.”  

And herewith let our view rest sated."

- *quinci* ‘from here’ → “herewith”: the translation takes the adverb as ‘with this’ rather than giving its literal spatial sense.
- *sien le nostre viste sazie* ‘may our sights be sated’ → “let our view rest sated”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”; ‘be’ becomes “rest”, and the plural *viste* becomes the singular “view”.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Reshaping

Carlyle’s translation remains close to the Italian’s sequence of description, repeated expressions, and many of its images, but regularly rearranges its syntax. Subjects and verbs move before objects placed first in Italian; adjectives generally precede their nouns; and separated elements are brought together. Thus *Venedico se’ tu Caccianimico* becomes “thou art Venedico Caccianimico”. Purpose phrases and other complements often move after the expressions they qualify. Yet the English retains some marked inversions, as in “yawns a well” and “such image these made here”, preserving something of the Italian’s ordering rather than consistently adopting ordinary English prose order.

Changes of construction often go beyond rearrangement. Reflexive verbs become intransitive or directly transitive expressions: *mi raggiunsi con* becomes “rejoined”. Finite relative clauses become participial phrases, as *che si nicchia* becomes “whining”; elsewhere, a gerund becomes a finite clause, as *Così parlando* becomes “And as he thus spake”. The translation also redistributes grammatical roles. In *Ho io grazie grandi appo te*, having thanks becomes receiving the action of thanking: “Dost thou thank me much?” Tenses sometimes change to clarify temporal relations, while Italian subjunctives receive English auxiliaries or constructions with “let”.

Word choice ranges from close correspondence to interpretation and paraphrase. “truncates and collects” closely follows *tronca e raccogli*, and “did battle with the eyes and with the nose” preserves the personification in *facea zuffa*. By contrast, *frustatori*, ‘scourgers’, becomes the broader “tormenters”; *ripa dura*, ‘hard bank’, becomes “rocky bank”; and *soletta*, ‘quite alone’, becomes “forlorn”, emphasizing abandonment and distress. Some metaphors disappear: *non son digiuno*, ‘I am not fasting’, is absorbed into “This one I have seen before.” Others are retained but recast, notably *pungenti salse*, ‘pungent sauces’, as “a biting pickle”. The explicit coarseness of *merda* and *merdose* is softened to “filth” and “filthy”.

Additions frequently make implicit relations explicit. Substantival adjectives and participles receive nouns such as “soul” and “spirits”; “there” supplies an English existential construction; and possessives replace definite articles before body parts. Conversely, the diminutive force of *ponticelli* is lost in “bridges”, and *in sè* is not separately represented in “devours”. Number and deixis also shift: *li castelli* becomes “a castle”, while *quivi* and *quelli* become “here” and “these”. Alongside these individually noted departures are two pervasive grammatical adjustments: English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits the Italian definite article before possessives. These routine accommodations help make the prose intelligible in English without erasing its frequently close verbal and rhetorical attachment to the original.
