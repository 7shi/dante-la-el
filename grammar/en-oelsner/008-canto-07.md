# Inferno 7

## Lines 1–15 — Plutus Silenced and Felled

> 1 “Pape Satan, pape Satan aleppe,”  
> 2 cominciò Pluto colla voce chioccia ;  

“Pape Satan! pape Satan, aleppe!” began Plutus, with clucking voice;

- *colla voce chioccia* ‘with the hoarse voice’ → “with clucking voice”: the article is omitted, the adjective precedes the noun, and “clucking” gives a more particular sound than ‘hoarse’.

> 3 e quel Savio gentil, che tutto seppe,  
> 4 disse per confortarmi : “Non ti noccia  
> 5 la tua paura, chè, poder ch’ egli abbia,  
> 6 non ti torrà lo scender questa roccia.”  

and that gentle Sage, who knew all, said, comforting me: “Let not thy fear hurt thee: for, whatever power he have, he shall not hinder [thee] from descending this rock.”

- *quel Savio gentil* ‘that Sage gentle’ → “that gentle Sage”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *che tutto seppe* ‘who all knew’ → “who knew all”: the object moves after the verb.
- *disse per confortarmi* ‘said to comfort me’ → “said, comforting me”: the purpose expressed by *per* with an infinitive becomes an accompanying action expressed by a participle.
- *Non ti noccia la tua paura* ‘may thy fear not hurt thee’ → “Let not thy fear hurt thee”: the optative subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”; the subject moves before the verb expressing harm, and its object follows it.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *poder ch’ egli abbia* ‘power that he may have’ → “whatever power he have”: the concessive force of the Italian construction is expressed by “whatever”.
- *non ti torrà lo scender* ‘will not take from thee the descending’ → “he shall not hinder [thee] from descending”: taking an action away from someone becomes hindering that person from performing it; the indirect object becomes a direct object, and the substantival infinitive becomes a gerund governed by “from”.

> 7 Poi si rivolse a quella enfiata labbia,  
> 8 e disse : “Taci, maledetto lupo!  
> 9 consuma dentro te con la tua rabbia.  

Then he turned himself to that inflated visage, and said: “Peace, cursed Wolf! consume thyself internally with thy greedy rage.

- *si rivolse* ‘himself he turned’ → “he turned himself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Taci* ‘be silent’ → “Peace”: a verbal imperative becomes a noun used as a command.
- *consuma* ‘waste away’ → “consume thyself”: the verb used absolutely becomes an explicitly reflexive construction.
- *dentro te* ‘within thee’ → “internally”: a prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *con la tua rabbia* ‘with thy rage’ → “with thy greedy rage”: “greedy” is supplied.

> 10 Non è senza cagion l’ andare al cupo:  
> 11 vuolsi nell’ alto, là dove Michele  
> 12 fe’ la vendetta del superbo strupo.”  

Not without cause is our journey to the deep: it is willed on high, [there] where Michael took vengeance of the proud adultery.”

- *Non è senza cagion* ‘is not without cause’ → “Not without cause is”: the whole negative phrase moves before the verb.
- *l’ andare* ‘the going’ → “our journey”: the substantival infinitive becomes a noun, and the definite article is replaced by a supplied possessive.
- *nell’ alto* ‘in the height’ → “on high”: the preposition changes, and the article and substantival construction give way to an idiomatic adverbial phrase.
- *fe’ la vendetta* ‘made the vengeance’ → “took vengeance”: the verb changes to suit the English idiom, and the article is omitted.
- *strupo* ‘violation’ → “adultery”: the translation adopts the more specific interpretation of sexual infidelity.

> 13 Quali dal vento le gonfiate vele  
> 14 caggiono avvolte, poichè l’ alber fiacca :  
> 15 tal cadde a terra la fiera crudele.  

As sails, swelled by the wind, fall entangled when the mast breaks: so fell that cruel monster to the ground.

- *Quali* ‘such as’ → “As”: the inflected comparative form agreeing with the sails becomes an English conjunction.
- *dal vento le gonfiate vele* ‘by the wind the swollen sails’ → “sails, swelled by the wind”: the article is omitted; the participle moves after the noun, and the phrase expressing its agent moves after the participle.
- *avvolte* ‘wrapped’ → “entangled”: the translation specifies tangling rather than wrapping.
- *poichè* ‘after’ → “when”: the temporal relation is expressed as coincidence rather than explicitly as succession.
- *cadde a terra la fiera crudele* ‘fell to earth the beast cruel’ → “fell that cruel monster to the ground”: the subject moves before the phrase of destination, its adjective moves before the noun, the definite article becomes a demonstrative, and ‘beast’ becomes “monster”; ‘to earth’ is rendered by “to the ground”, with an article supplied.

## Lines 16–48 — The Clash of Hoarders and Spendthrifts

> 16 Così scendemmo nella quarta lacca,  
> 17 prendendo più della dolente ripa,  
> 18 che il mal dell' universo tutto insacca.  

[Thus] we descended into the fourth concavity, taking in more of the dismal bank, which shuts up all the evil of the universe.

- *prendendo* ‘taking’ → “taking in”: the particle “in” is supplied.
- *dolente* ‘sorrowful’ → “dismal”: an adjective expressing sorrow or suffering becomes one describing a gloomy character.
- *il mal dell' universo tutto* ‘the evil of the universe all’ → “all the evil of the universe”: the quantifier moves before the noun phrase.
- *insacca* ‘bags up’ → “shuts up”: the image of putting something into a sack becomes a general expression for enclosing it; the verb moves before its object.

> 19 Ahi giustizia di Dio! tante chi stipa  
> 20 nuove travaglie e pene, quante io viddi?  

Ah, Justice Divine! who shall tell in few the many fresh pains and travails that I saw?

- *giustizia di Dio* ‘justice of God’ → “Justice Divine”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adjective.
- *chi stipa* ‘who packs together’ → “who shall tell in few”: the present becomes a future construction with “shall”; packing together is interpreted as compressed narration, rather than the heaping together of pains.
- *tante nuove travaglie e pene, quante* ‘as many new travails and pains as’ → “the many fresh pains and travails that”: the correlative construction becomes a definite noun phrase followed by a relative clause; the order of the two nouns is reversed. The object also follows the question’s verb, whereas *tante* precedes *chi stipa*.

> 21 e perchè nostra colpa sì ne scipa?  

and why does guilt of ours thus waste us?

- *nostra colpa* ‘our guilt’ → “guilt of ours”: the possessive becomes a following prepositional phrase.
- *ne scipa* ‘us wastes’ → “waste us”: the object pronoun follows the verb, and the present-tense question is formed with the auxiliary “does” earlier in the clause.

> 22 Come fa l' onda là sovra Cariddi,  
> 23 che si frange con quella in cui s' intoppa:  
> 24 così convien che qui la gente riddi.  

As does the surge, there above Charybdis, that breaks itself against the surge wherewith it meets: so have the people here to counter-dance.

- *con quella* ‘with that one’ → “against the surge”: “against” makes the collision explicit, and the demonstrative pronoun becomes a repeated noun.
- *in cui s' intoppa* ‘into which it bumps’ → “wherewith it meets”: the preposition and relative pronoun become a relative adverb expressing association rather than motion into something; the reflexive verb is rendered by the intransitive “meets”.
- *così convien che qui la gente riddi* ‘so it is necessary that here the people dance in a ring’ → “so have the people here to counter-dance”: the impersonal construction with a subjunctive becomes a personal construction of obligation with an infinitive. The singular collective *gente* is construed as plural; “here” follows the subject; and “counter-dance” specifies opposing movement rather than simply dancing in a ring.

> 25 Qui vidi gente più che altrove troppa,  
> 26 e d' una parte e d' altra, con grandi urli,  
> 27 voltando pesi per forza di poppa;  

Here saw I too many more than elsewhere, both on the one side and on the other, with loud howlings, rolling weights by force of chests;

- *gente più che altrove troppa* ‘people, more than elsewhere, too many’ → “too many more than elsewhere”: *gente* is left implicit in “many”, and the expression of excessive number moves before the comparison.
- *e d' una parte e d' altra* ‘and on one side and on another’ → “both on the one side and on the other”: the paired conjunctions become “both … and”, and definite articles are supplied for the two sides.
- *grandi urli* ‘great howls’ → “loud howlings”: the general adjective of magnitude is rendered specifically as loudness.
- *voltando* ‘turning’ → “rolling”: the translation specifies the manner of movement.
- *poppa* ‘breast’ → “chests”: the singular becomes a distributive plural.

> 28 percotevansi incontro, e poscia pur lì  
> 29 si rivolgea ciascun, voltando a retro,  
> 30 gridando: "Perchè tieni?" e "Perchè burli?"  

they smote against each other, and then each wheeled round just there, rolling aback, shouting, “Why holdest thou?” and “Why throwest thou away?”

- *percotevansi incontro* ‘they struck one another against’ → “they smote against each other”: the reciprocal pronoun becomes “each other” after “against”, which renders the directional adverb *incontro*.
- *si rivolgea ciascun* ‘turned himself each’ → “each wheeled round”: the subject moves before the verb, and the reflexive construction becomes the intransitive phrase “wheeled round”.
- *voltando a retro* ‘turning backward’ → “rolling aback”: “rolling” again specifies the kind of turning.
- *burli* ‘squanderest’ → “throwest thou away”: the single verb becomes an idiomatic phrasal verb.

> 31 Così tornavan per lo cerchio tetro,  
> 32 da ogni mano all' opposito punto,  
> 33 gridandosi anche loro ontoso metro.  

Thus they returned along the gloomy circle, on either hand, to the opposite point, [again] shouting [at each other] their reproachful measure.

- *per lo cerchio tetro* ‘through the circle gloomy’ → “along the gloomy circle”: “along” presents the circle as a route, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *da ogni mano* ‘from every hand’ → “on either hand”: ‘from’ becomes “on”, and ‘every’ becomes “either”, specifying two alternatives.
- *gridandosi anche* ‘shouting to one another also’ → “[again] shouting [at each other]”: *anche* is interpreted temporally as repetition and moved before the participle; the reciprocal pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase with “at” rather than “to”.

> 34 Poi si volgea ciascun, quand' era giunto,  
> 35 per lo suo mezzo cerchio, all' altra giostra.  

Then every one, when he had reached it, turned through his half-circle towards the other joust.

- *si volgea ciascun, quand' era giunto* ‘turned himself each, when he had arrived’ → “every one, when he had reached it, turned”: the subject and temporal clause move before the main verb; the reflexive pronoun is not separately represented. Intransitive *era giunto* becomes transitive “had reached”, with “it” supplied as its object.
- *all' altra giostra* ‘to the other joust’ → “towards the other joust”: the preposition is rendered as direction towards a destination.

> 36 Ed io che avea lo cor quasi compunto,  
> 37 dissi: "Maestro mio, or mi dimostra  
> 38 che gente è questa; e se tutti fur cherci  
> 39 questi chercuti alla sinistra nostra."  

And I, who felt my heart as it were stung, said: “My Master, now shew me what people these are; and whether all those tonsured on our left were of the clergy.”

- *che avea lo cor quasi compunto* ‘who had the heart almost pricked’ → “who felt my heart as it were stung”: possession or condition becomes perception through “felt”; the definite article becomes the possessive “my”; and *quasi* becomes the qualifying phrase “as it were” rather than a straightforward approximation.
- *Maestro mio* ‘Master my’ → “My Master”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *mi dimostra* ‘to me show’ → “shew me”: the indirect-object pronoun follows the imperative.
- *che gente è questa* ‘what people is this’ → “what people these are”: the singular demonstrative and verb become plural, and the subject precedes the verb in the English indirect question.
- *se tutti fur cherci questi chercuti alla sinistra nostra* ‘if all were clerics these tonsured ones at the left our’ → “whether all those tonsured on our left were of the clergy”: *se* introduces the indirect question as “whether”; the subject phrase moves before the verb; and *questi* becomes the more distant “those”. The adjective *chercuti*, used substantivally, becomes “those tonsured”; *cherci* becomes a phrase expressing membership, “of the clergy”; and the final possessive moves before “left”.

> 40 Ed egli a me: "Tutti quanti fur guerci  
> 41 sì della mente in la vita primaia,  
> 42 che con misura nullo spendio ferci.  

And he to me: “In their first life, all were so squint-eyed in mind, that they made no expenditure in it with moderation.

- *Tutti quanti* ‘all of them together’ → “all”: the reinforcing *quanti* is not separately represented.
- *guerci sì della mente* ‘squint-eyed so of the mind’ → “so squint-eyed in mind”: the degree adverb moves before the adjective, and ‘of the mind’ becomes “in mind”.
- *in la vita primaia* ‘in the life first’ → “In their first life”: the phrase moves to the beginning of the speech; the article becomes a possessive, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *con misura* ‘with measure’ → “with moderation”: the figurative sense of measure is made explicit, and the phrase moves to the end of the clause.
- *nullo spendio ferci* ‘no expenditure they made there’ → “they made no expenditure in it”: the object follows the verb; the enclitic *ci* becomes “in it”, explicitly referring back to the preceding life.

> 43 Assai la voce lor chiaro l' abbaia,  
> 44 quando vengono a' duo punti del cerchio,  
> 45 ove colpa contraria li dispaia.  

Most clearly do their voices bark out this, when they come to the two points of the circle, where contrary guilt divides them.

- *Assai … chiaro* ‘very … clearly’ → “Most clearly”: the separated modifiers are brought together at the beginning of the clause, with “Most” used intensively. The English also introduces emphatic “do” before the subject.
- *la voce lor* ‘the voice their’ → “their voices”: the singular becomes plural, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *l' abbaia* ‘it barks’ → “bark out this”: *abbaia* becomes the phrasal verb “bark out”; the object pronoun becomes the demonstrative “this” and follows the verb.
- *colpa contraria* ‘guilt contrary’ → “contrary guilt”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *li dispaia* ‘them sets apart’ → “divides them”: the object pronoun follows the verb, and the expression for setting apart becomes the more general “divides”.

> 46 Questi fur cherci, che non han coperchio  
> 47 piloso al capo, e Papi e Cardinali,  
> 48 in cui usa avarizia il suo soperchio."  

These were Priests, that have not hairy covering on their heads, and Popes and Cardinals, in whom avarice does its utmost.”

- *cherci* ‘clerics’ → “Priests”: the translation uses a narrower designation.
- *non han coperchio piloso al capo* ‘have not covering hairy on the head’ → “have not hairy covering on their heads”: the adjective moves before the noun; the definite article becomes a possessive; and the singular ‘head’ becomes a distributive plural.
- *in cui usa avarizia il suo soperchio* ‘in whom exercises avarice its excess’ → “in whom avarice does its utmost”: the subject moves before the verb. The expression for exercising excess becomes the idiom “does its utmost”, rendering *soperchio* as the greatest extent of activity rather than excess.

## Lines 49–81 — Misused Wealth and Fortune’s Rule

> 49 Ed io : " Maestro, tra questi cotali  
> 50 dovrei io ben riconoscere alcuni,  
> 51 che furo immondi di cotesti mali."  

And I: "Master, among this set, I surely ought to recognise some that were defiled by these evils."

- *tra questi cotali* ‘among these such ones’ → “among this set”: the plural demonstrative and its accompanying description become a singular collective noun phrase.
- *dovrei io ben riconoscere alcuni* ‘ought I indeed to recognise some’ → “I surely ought to recognise some”: the subject moves before the modal verb, and the emphatic adverb moves before “ought”.
- *furo immondi di* ‘were unclean with’ → “were defiled by”: an adjective describing a condition becomes a passive participle, with “by” replacing the preposition expressing the source of that condition.
- *cotesti mali* ‘those evils’ → “these evils”: the demonstrative changes its deictic reference.

> 52 Ed egli a me: "Vani pensieri aduni:  

And he to me: "Vain thoughts combinest thou:

- *aduni* ‘you gather’ → “combinest thou”: gathering or accumulating becomes combining.

> 53 la sconoscente vita, che i fe' sozzi,  
> 54 ad ogni conoscenza or li fa bruni.  

their undiscerning life, which made them sordid, now makes them too obscure for any recognition.

- *la sconoscente vita* ‘the undiscerning life’ → “their undiscerning life”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *che i fe' sozzi* ‘which them made sordid’ → “which made them sordid”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *ad ogni conoscenza or li fa bruni* ‘to every recognition now them makes dark’ → “now makes them too obscure for any recognition”: the opening prepositional phrase moves to the end, and the object pronoun follows the verb. “Obscure” renders figurative darkness; the supplied “too” creates a degree construction with “for”, and ‘every’ becomes “any” within that construction.

> 55 In eterno verranno agli due cozzi;  

To all eternity they shall continue butting one another;

- *In eterno* ‘for eternity’ → “To all eternity”: the temporal expression is recast, with “all” added.
- *verranno agli due cozzi* ‘will come to the two collisions’ → “they shall continue butting one another”: future coming becomes continued action; the noun becomes a participle, ‘two’ disappears, and the reciprocal object “one another” is supplied.

> 56 questi risurgeranno del sepulcro  
> 57 col pugno chiuso, e questi co' crin mozzi.  

these shall arise from their graves with closed fists; and these with hair shorn off.

- *risurgeranno* ‘will rise again’ → “shall arise”: the sense of rising again is not separately expressed.
- *del sepulcro* ‘from the tomb’ → “from their graves”: the singular becomes plural, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *col pugno chiuso* ‘with the fist closed’ → “with closed fists”: the distributive singular becomes plural, the article disappears, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *co' crin mozzi* ‘with the hairs shorn’ → “with hair shorn off”: the plural becomes the mass noun “hair”, the article disappears, and “off” explicitly expresses removal.

> 58 Mal dare, e mal tener lo mondo pulcro  
> 59 ha tolto loro, e posti a questa zuffa;  
> 60 qual ella sia, parole non ci appulcro.  

Ill-giving, and ill-keeping, has deprived them of the bright world, and put them to this conflict; what a conflict it is, I adorn no words to tell.

- *Mal dare, e mal tener* ‘to give badly, and to keep badly’ → “Ill-giving, and ill-keeping”: substantivized infinitives with adverbs become compound verbal nouns.
- *lo mondo pulcro ha tolto loro* ‘the world beautiful has taken away from them’ → “has deprived them of the bright world”: taking something from someone becomes depriving someone of something. The Italian indirect object becomes the English direct object, and the preceding Italian direct object becomes a following prepositional complement. “Bright” replaces ‘beautiful’, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *posti a questa zuffa* ‘placed at this brawl’ → “put them to this conflict”: the object “them” is supplied, and the broader “conflict” replaces ‘brawl’.
- *qual ella sia* ‘of what sort it may be’ → “what a conflict it is”: the subjunctive becomes indicative, “conflict” is supplied, and the wording takes an exclamatory form.
- *parole non ci appulcro* ‘words I do not embellish about it’ → “I adorn no words to tell”: the object moves after the verb, verbal negation becomes the determiner “no”, and *ci*, referring to the matter being described, is recast as the infinitive of purpose “to tell”.

> 61 Or puoi, figliuol, veder la corta buffa  
> 62 de' ben, che son commessi alla Fortuna,  
> 63 per che l' umana gente si rabbuffa.  

But thou, my Son, mayest [now] see the brief mockery of the goods that are committed unto Fortune, for which the human kind contend with one another.

- *Or puoi, figliuol, veder* ‘now you can, son, see’ → “But thou, my Son, mayest [now] see”: “But” and the possessive “my” are supplied; “now” moves from the beginning to immediately before “see”. “Mayest” renders the modal *puoi*.
- *l'umana gente* ‘the human people’ → “the human kind”: the collective noun is rendered as a designation of a class or kind.
- *si rabbuffa* ‘quarrels’ → “contend with one another”: “contend” gives a less forceful expression, the reciprocal force is made explicit, and the singular Italian verb becomes plural in agreement with the English collective subject.

> 64 Chè tutto l'oro, ch' è sotto la luna,  
> 65 e che già fu, di queste anime stanche  
> 66 non poterebbe farne posar una.  

For all the gold that is beneath the moon, or ever was, could not give rest to a single one of these weary souls.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “For”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *e che già fu* ‘and which formerly was’ → “or ever was”: “or” replaces ‘and’, the relative pronoun is omitted, and “ever” replaces the reference to former existence.
- *di queste anime stanche non poterebbe farne posar una* ‘of these souls weary could not make one of them rest’ → “could not give rest to a single one of these weary souls”: the opening partitive phrase moves to the end, and the adjective precedes its noun. The resumptive *ne* is not separately represented. The causative construction ‘make … rest’ becomes “give rest to”, with the verb ‘rest’ becoming a noun, and “single” is added to emphasize ‘one’.

> 67 " Maestro," dissi lui, "or mi di' anche:  
> 68 questa Fortuna, di che tu mi tocche,  
> 69 che è, che i ben del mondo ha sì tra branche? "  

"Master," I said to him, "now tell me also: this Fortune, of which thou hintest to me; what is she, that has the good things of the world thus within her clutches?"

- *mi di'* ‘to me say’ → “tell me”: ‘say’ becomes “tell”, and the pronoun moves after the verb.
- *tu mi tocche* ‘you to me touch upon’ → “thou hintest to me”: touching upon a subject becomes hinting at it, and the pronoun moves into a following prepositional phrase.
- *che i ben del mondo ha sì tra branche* ‘that the goods of the world has thus between claws’ → “that has the good things of the world thus within her clutches”: the object moves after the verb, and ‘goods’ becomes “good things”. “Within” replaces ‘between’, “clutches” renders the bodily image of ‘claws’, and “her” is supplied.

> 70 E quegli a me: " O creature sciocche,  
> 71 quanta ignoranza è quella che vi offende!  

And he to me: "O foolish creatures, how great is this ignorance that falls upon ye!

- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *creature sciocche* ‘creatures foolish’ → “foolish creatures”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *quanta ignoranza è quella* ‘how much ignorance is that’ → “how great is this ignorance”: the quantity expression becomes the predicative “how great”, and ‘that’ becomes “this”. The demonstrative, originally standing as a pronoun, now determines “ignorance”.
- *che vi offende* ‘which you harms’ → “that falls upon ye”: harming becomes falling upon; the preceding direct-object pronoun becomes the complement of a following preposition.

> 72 Or vo' che tu mia sentenza ne imbocche.  

Now I wish thee to receive my judgment of her.

- *vo' che tu mia sentenza ne imbocche* ‘I wish that you my judgment of it take into your mouth’ → “I wish thee to receive my judgment of her”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction. Taking something into the mouth becomes the abstract “receive”; the object moves after that verb, and *ne* becomes the following “of her”, explicitly referring to Fortune.

> 73 Colui, lo cui saver tutto trascende,  
> 74 fece li cieli, e diè lor chi conduce,  

He whose wisdom is transcendent over all, made the heavens and gave them guides,

- *Colui* ‘that one’ → “He”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *lo cui saver tutto trascende* ‘whose knowing surpasses everything’ → “whose wisdom is transcendent over all”: the substantivized infinitive *saver* becomes “wisdom”. The transitive verb becomes a copular construction with an adjective, and its preceding object becomes the following prepositional complement “over all”.
- *diè lor chi conduce* ‘gave them whoever leads’ → “gave them guides”: a free relative clause with a singular verb becomes a plural noun.

> 75 sì ch' ogni parte ad ogni parte splende,  
> 76 distribuendo ugualmente la luce;  

so that every part shines to every part, equally distributing the light;

- *ad ogni parte splende* ‘to every part shines’ → “shines to every part”: the verb moves before its prepositional complement.
- *distribuendo ugualmente* ‘distributing equally’ → “equally distributing”: the adverb moves before the participle.

> 77 similemente agli splendor mondani  
> 78 ordinò general ministra e duce,  

in like manner, for worldly splendours, he ordained a general minister and guide,

- *similemente* ‘similarly’ → “in like manner”: an adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *agli splendor mondani* ‘to the splendours worldly’ → “for worldly splendours”: “for” replaces ‘to’, the definite article is omitted, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *general ministra e duce* ‘general minister and guide’ → “a general minister and guide”: English supplies an indefinite article before the singular count noun.

> 79 che permutasse a tempo li ben vani,  
> 80 di gente in gente, e d' uno in altro sangue,  
> 81 oltre la difension de' senni umani:  

to change betimes the vain possessions, from people to people, and from one kindred to another, beyond the hindrance of human wisdom:

- *che permutasse* ‘who should exchange’ → “to change”: a relative clause with a subjunctive verb becomes an infinitive expressing purpose; “change” is less specifically reciprocal than ‘exchange’.
- *a tempo* ‘at the proper time’ → “betimes”: a prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *li ben vani* ‘the goods vain’ → “the vain possessions”: ‘goods’ becomes “possessions”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *di gente in gente* ‘from people into people’ → “from people to people”: “to” replaces the directional ‘into’.
- *e d'uno in altro sangue* ‘and from one into another blood’ → “and from one kindred to another”: “kindred” renders ‘blood’ metonymically and moves from after ‘another’ to after ‘one’; “to” again replaces ‘into’.
- *oltre la difension de' senni umani* ‘beyond the defence of the human understandings’ → “beyond the hindrance of human wisdom”: defensive resistance is rendered as “hindrance”. The plural *senni* becomes the mass noun “wisdom”, its definite article disappears, and the adjective moves before the noun.

## Lines 82–114 — Fortune and the Marsh of Styx

> 82 per ch' una gente impera, e l' altra langue,  
> 83 seguendo lo giudicio di costei,  
> 84 che è occulto, come in erba l' angue.  

hence one people commands, another languishes; obeying her sentence, which is hidden like the serpent in the grass.

- *per ch'* ‘through which’ → “hence”: the connective phrase becomes an adverb expressing consequence.
- *una gente impera* ‘a people rules’ → “one people commands”: the indefinite article becomes numerical “one,” and ruling is expressed as commanding.
- *e l' altra langue* ‘and the other languishes’ → “another languishes”: the conjunction is omitted, and “another” replaces the definite expression.
- *seguendo* ‘following’ → “obeying”: following a judgment is expressed more specifically as obedience.
- *lo giudicio di costei* ‘the judgment of this woman’ → “her sentence”: the demonstrative becomes a possessive placed before the noun; “sentence” gives the judgment a judicial sense.
- *come in erba l' angue* ‘like in grass the serpent’ → “like the serpent in the grass”: the serpent precedes the phrase describing its location, and an article is supplied before “grass.”

> 85 Vostro saver non ha contrasto a lei :  

Your knowledge cannot understand her:

- *non ha contrasto a lei* ‘has no power of resistance against her’ → “cannot understand her”: inability to resist becomes inability to comprehend, and the construction with ‘has’ becomes a modal verb followed by an infinitive.

> 86 ella provvede, giudica, e persegue  
> 87 suo regno, come il loro gli altri Dei.  

she provides, judges, and maintains her kingdom, as the other Gods do theirs.

- *persegue suo regno* ‘pursues her kingdom’ → “maintains her kingdom”: pursuing is interpreted as sustaining or administering.
- *come il loro gli altri Dei* ‘as theirs the other Gods’ → “as the other Gods do theirs”: the subject precedes the possessive object, and “do” supplies the verb left implicit in Italian.

> 88 Le sue permutazion non hanno triegue ;  

Her permutations have no truce;

- *non hanno triegue* ‘have no truces’ → “have no truce”: the plural noun becomes singular.

> 89 necessità la fa esser veloce ;  

necessity makes her be swift;

- Literal.

> 90 sì spesso vien chi vicenda consegue.  

[thus he comes] oft [who doth a] change [obtain].

- *sì spesso vien* ‘so often comes’ → “[thus he comes] oft”: the degree adverb becomes an adverb of consequence, and “oft” follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *chi vicenda consegue* ‘who obtains a turn’ → “[who doth a] change [obtain]”: a turn in succession becomes a “change”; the relative is attached to the preceding “he,” and the added auxiliary “doth” allows the object to precede “obtain.”

> 91 Quest' è colei, ch' è tanto posta in croce  
> 92 pur da color, che le dovrian dar lode,  
> 93 dandole biasmo a torto e mala voce.  

This is she, who is so much reviled, even by those who ought to praise her, *when* blaming her wrongfully, and with evil words.

- *posta in croce* ‘put on a cross’ → “reviled”: the figurative expression is replaced by its sense of being verbally attacked.
- *le dovrian dar lode* ‘ought to give praise to her’ → “ought to praise her”: the noun becomes a verb, and the indirect object becomes a direct object.
- *dandole biasmo* ‘giving her blame’ → “*when* blaming her”: the noun becomes a verb, the indirect object becomes its direct object, and a temporal conjunction is supplied before the participle.
- *e mala voce* ‘and bad repute’ → “and with evil words”: bad reputation becomes abusive speech, and “with” makes the phrase describe the means of blaming rather than a second thing given.

> 94 Ma ella s' è beata, e ciò non ode :  

But she is in bliss, and hears it not:

- *s' è beata* ‘is blessed in herself’ → “is in bliss”: the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase, and the pronominal *s'* is not separately represented.
- *ciò non ode* ‘that she does not hear’ → “hears it not”: the demonstrative becomes “it,” and both object and negation follow the verb.

> 95 con l' altre prime creature lieta  
> 96 volve sua spera, e beata si gode.  

with the other Primal Creatures joyful, she wheels her sphere, and tastes her blessedness.

- *beata si gode* ‘blessed, enjoys herself’ → “tastes her blessedness”: the adjective describing the subject becomes the verb’s object; the reflexive becomes a possessive, and enjoyment is expressed through the metaphor of tasting.

> 97 Or discendiamo omai a maggior pieta ;  

*But* let us now descend to greater misery;

- *Or* ‘now’ → “*But*”: the transitional adverb becomes an adversative conjunction.
- *discendiamo omai* ‘let us descend now’ → “let us now descend”: the exhortative verb is expressed with “let us,” and the adverb precedes “descend.”
- *a maggior pieta* ‘to greater piteousness’ → “to greater misery”: the quality of arousing pity is expressed as the suffering that arouses it.

> 98 già ogni stella cade, che saliva  
> 99 quando mi mossi, e il troppo star si vieta."  

already every star is falling, that was ascending when [I set out] and to stay too long is not permitted.”

- *cade, che saliva* ‘falls, which ascended’ → “is falling, that was ascending”: the present and imperfect are rendered with progressive forms, emphasizing ongoing movement.
- *mi mossi* ‘I moved myself’ → “[I set out]”: the pronominal verb is rendered by an idiom specifically expressing departure.
- *il troppo star* ‘the excessive staying’ → “to stay too long”: the nominalized infinitive becomes an English infinitival subject, and excess is specified as excessive duration.
- *si vieta* ‘is forbidden’ → “is not permitted”: the passive construction with *si* becomes an English passive, and prohibition is expressed by negating permission.

> 100 Noi ricidemmo il cerchio all' altra riva  
> 101 sopra una fonte, che bolle, e riversa  
> 102 per un fossato che da lei diriva.  

We crossed the circle, to the other bank, near a fount, that boils and pours down through a cleft, which it has formed.

- *ricidemmo* ‘cut across’ → “crossed”: the verb’s image of cutting across is reduced to crossing.
- *sopra una fonte* ‘above a fount’ → “near a fount”: the spatial relation is interpreted as proximity rather than position above.
- *riversa* ‘pours out’ → “pours down”: the English specifies downward movement.
- *per un fossato* ‘through a ditch’ → “through a cleft”: a ditch or channel is represented as a fissure.
- *che da lei diriva* ‘which derives from it’ → “which it has formed”: the channel’s derivation from the fount becomes its formation by the fount. The fount becomes the subject, the relative pronoun becomes the object, and the present becomes a present perfect.

> 103 L' acqua era buia assai vie che persa ;  

The water was darker far than perse;

- *buia assai vie che persa* ‘dark much more than perse’ → “darker far than perse”: the comparison is incorporated into “darker,” and the intensifying words are condensed into the following “far.”

> 104 e noi, in compagnia dell' onde bige,  
> 105 entrammo giù per una via diversa.  

and we, accompanying the dusky waves, entered down by a strange path.

- *in compagnia dell' onde bige* ‘in company with the waves grey’ → “accompanying the dusky waves”: the prepositional phrase becomes a participial construction with a direct object; the adjective moves before the noun, and greyness becomes duskiness.
- *per una via diversa* ‘through a path different’ → “by a strange path”: the route is introduced by “by,” the adjective precedes its noun, and difference is interpreted as strangeness.

> 106 Una palude fa, che ha nome Stige,  
> 107 questo tristo ruscel, quando è disceso  
> 108 al piè delle maligne piagge grige.  

This dreary streamlet makes a Marsh, that is named Styx, when it has descended to the foot of the grey malignant shores.

- *Una palude fa … questo tristo ruscel* ‘a marsh makes … this sad streamlet’ → “This dreary streamlet makes a Marsh”: the postponed subject moves to the beginning and the object follows the verb; “dreary” expresses the streamlet’s sadness as a quality of its appearance or atmosphere.
- *che ha nome Stige* ‘which has the name Styx’ → “that is named Styx”: possession of a name becomes a passive naming construction.
- *è disceso* ‘is descended’ → “has descended”: the Italian perfect auxiliary ‘is’ becomes English “has.”
- *delle maligne piagge grige* ‘of the malignant slopes grey’ → “of the grey malignant shores”: slopes become shores, and the final colour adjective moves before both the noun and its other adjective.

> 109 Ed io, che di mirar mi stava inteso,  
> 110 vidi genti fangose in quel pantano,  
> 111 ignude tutte, e con sembiante offeso.  

And I, who stood intent on looking, saw muddy people in that bog, all naked and with a look of anger.

- *che di mirar mi stava inteso* ‘who on looking remained intent’ → “who stood intent on looking”: the phrase specifying the object of attention moves after “intent”; *stava* is rendered as “stood,” and the pronominal *mi* is not separately expressed.
- *genti fangose* ‘people muddy’ → “muddy people”: the adjective precedes its noun.
- *ignude tutte* ‘naked all’ → “all naked”: the quantifier precedes the adjective.
- *con sembiante offeso* ‘with offended countenance’ → “with a look of anger”: the adjective becomes a phrase with an abstract noun, an indefinite article is supplied, and the offended or injured expression is interpreted specifically as anger.

> 112 Questi si percotean non pur con mano,  
> 113 ma con la testa, e col petto, e co' piedi,  
> 114 troncandosi coi denti a brano a brano.  

They were smiting each other, not with hands only, but with head, and with chest, and with feet; *maiming* one another with their teeth, piece by piece.

- *Questi* ‘these’ → “They”: the demonstrative becomes an ordinary personal pronoun.
- *si percotean* ‘struck one another’ → “were smiting each other”: the imperfect becomes a past progressive, and the preverbal reciprocal pronoun becomes a phrase following the verb.
- *non pur con mano* ‘not only with hand’ → “not with hands only”: “only” moves after the prepositional phrase, and singular ‘hand’ becomes plural.
- *con la testa, e col petto, e co' piedi* ‘with the head, and with the chest, and with the feet’ → “with head, and with chest, and with feet”: the definite articles before the body parts are omitted.
- *troncandosi* ‘cutting one another apart’ → “*maiming* one another”: the specific action of cutting or tearing apart becomes the more general infliction of disabling injury.
- *coi denti* ‘with the teeth’ → “with their teeth”: a possessive replaces the definite article.

## Lines 115–130 — Wrath and Sorrow in the Mire

> 115 Lo buon maestro disse: “ Figlio, or vedi  
> 116 l' anime di color cui vinse l' ira;  

The kind Master said: “Son, now see the souls of those whom anger overcame;

- *buon* ‘good’ → “kind”: general goodness is rendered more specifically as kindness.
- *cui vinse l' ira* ‘whom overcame anger’ → “whom anger overcame”: the subject is moved before the verb.

> 117 ed anche vo' che tu per certo credi,  
> 118 che sotto l' acqua ha gente che sospira,  
> 119 e fanno pullular quest' acqua al summo,  

and also I would have thee to believe for certain, that there are people underneath the water, who sob, and make it bubble at the surface;

- *vo' che tu per certo credi* ‘I wish that thou for certain believe’ → “I would have thee to believe for certain”: the finite subordinate clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction after “would have”; the expression of certainty moves after “believe”.
- *che sotto l' acqua ha gente* ‘that underneath the water there is people’ → “that there are people underneath the water”: existential *ha* becomes “there are”; the singular collective *gente* becomes plural “people”, and the phrase locating them moves after the noun.
- *sospira* ‘sighs’ → “sob”: sighing becomes sobbing, and the singular verb becomes plural to agree with “people”.
- *fanno pullular quest' acqua* ‘make bubble this water’ → “make it bubble”: “it” replaces ‘this water’ and precedes the infinitive rather than following it.
- *al summo* ‘at the top’ → “at the surface”: the general term for the uppermost part becomes the specific term for the water’s surface.

> 120 come l' occhio ti dice u' che s' aggira.  

as thy eye may tell thee, whichever way it turns.

- *l' occhio* ‘the eye’ → “thy eye”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *ti dice* ‘to thee says’ → “may tell thee”: “may” is supplied, “tell” replaces ‘say’, and the recipient follows the verb.
- *u' che s' aggira* ‘wherever it turns itself’ → “whichever way it turns”: place is expressed as direction, and the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.

> 121 Fitti nel limo dicon: ‘Tristi fummo  
> 122 nell' aer dolce che dal sol s' allegra,  
> 123 portando dentro accidioso fummo;  

Fixed in the slime, they say: ‘Sullen were we in the sweet air, that is gladdened by the Sun, carrying lazy smoke within our hearts;

- *Tristi* ‘sad’ → “Sullen”: sadness is interpreted as sullenness.
- *nell' aer dolce* ‘in the air sweet’ → “in the sweet air”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *che dal sol s' allegra* ‘that from the sun rejoices’ → “that is gladdened by the Sun”: the reflexive verb expressing rejoicing becomes a passive construction, with the sun expressed as its agent; the phrase naming the sun moves after the verb.
- *dentro* ‘within’ → “within our hearts”: “our hearts” is supplied, and the expression moves from before “lazy smoke” to after it.

> 124 or ci attristiam nella belletta negra.'  

now lie we sullen here in the black mire.’

- *ci attristiam* ‘we make ourselves sad’ → “lie we sullen here”: the reflexive verb becomes a description of a continuing state; “lie” and “here” are supplied, and sadness is again rendered as sullenness.
- *nella belletta negra* ‘in the mire black’ → “in the black mire”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 125 Quest' inno si gorgoglian nella strozza,  
> 126 chè dir nol posson con parola integra.  

This hymn they gurgle in their throats, for they cannot speak it in full words.”

- *si gorgoglian* ‘they gurgle to themselves’ → “they gurgle”: the reflexive pronoun is omitted.
- *nella strozza* ‘in the throat’ → “in their throats”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the distributive singular becomes plural.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *dir nol posson* ‘say it not they can’ → “they cannot speak it”: the modal precedes the infinitive, the negative is attached to the modal, and the object follows the infinitive; “speak” replaces ‘say’.
- *con parola integra* ‘with word whole’ → “in full words”: ‘with’ becomes “in”, the singular becomes plural, and the adjective moves before the noun; ‘whole’ is rendered as “full”.

> 127 Così girammo della lorda pozza  
> 128 grand' arco, tra la ripa secca e il mezzo,  
> 129 con gli occhi volti a chi del fango ingozza;  

Thus, between the dry bank and the putrid fen, we compassed a large arc of that loathly slough, with eyes turned towards those that swallow of its filth;

- *della lorda pozza* ‘of the foul pool’ → “of that loathly slough”: the definite article becomes demonstrative; “loathly slough” replaces ‘foul pool’, making the description more strongly expressive of disgust and mire. The phrase follows “arc” rather than preceding it.
- *grand' arco* ‘great arc’ → “a large arc”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *tra la ripa secca* ‘between the bank dry’ → “between the dry bank”: the adjective moves before the noun; the phrase beginning here is moved before the main clause.
- *il mezzo* ‘the wet [ground]’ → “the putrid fen”: the translation takes *mezzo* as a substantivized adjective meaning wet or sodden, not as the noun ‘middle’; “fen” supplies an explicit noun, while “putrid” adds the sense of decay.
- *gli occhi* ‘the eyes’ → “eyes”: the definite article is omitted.
- *a chi* ‘towards whoever’ → “towards those that”: the singular indefinite relative becomes a plural demonstrative followed by a relative clause.
- *del fango* ‘of the mud’ → “of its filth”: the definite article becomes a possessive, ‘mud’ becomes the more evaluative “filth”, and the phrase moves after the verb.
- *ingozza* ‘gulps’ → “swallow”: the stronger suggestion of gulping or swallowing greedily is reduced.

> 130 Venimmo al piè d' una torre al dassezzo.  

we came to the foot of a tower at last.

- Literal.

## Summary — Close Structure, Interpretive Detail

The translation remains close to the Italian’s sequence of statements, comparisons, and speeches, often retaining its images and even its unusual phrasing. Yet word order regularly changes: adjectives move before nouns, objects follow verbs, and postponed subjects move forward. Thus *che tutto seppe* becomes “who knew all”, while *Una palude fa … questo tristo ruscel* is reordered as “This dreary streamlet makes a Marsh”. Some inversions survive, however, as in “Here saw I” and “Sullen were we”, preserving something of the Italian’s marked arrangement.

Changes of construction often make the English more idiomatic or explicit. *disse per confortarmi* becomes “said, comforting me”, replacing purpose with an accompanying action; *l’ andare* becomes “our journey”, replacing a substantivized infinitive with a noun. Elsewhere, *le dovrian dar lode* becomes “ought to praise her”, turning a noun into a verb, and *non ti torrà lo scender* becomes “he shall not hinder [thee] from descending”, recasting both the action and its grammatical objects. Reflexive constructions may disappear, become explicitly reciprocal, or take another form: *si rivolgea ciascun* becomes “each wheeled round”, whereas *si percotean* becomes “were smiting each other”. Singular collectives and distributive singulars also frequently become plurals, as with *la voce lor*, “their voices”, and *col pugno chiuso*, “with closed fists”.

Vocabulary ranges from close equivalents to interpretive substitutions. “Clucking” makes *chioccia* more acoustically specific; “Priests” narrows *cherci*; and “Sullen” interprets *Tristi* rather than simply rendering it as ‘sad’. Some departures change the proposition more substantially: *chi stipa*, ‘who packs together’, becomes “who shall tell in few”, while *non ha contrasto a lei*, ‘has no power of resistance against her’, becomes “cannot understand her”. Figurative language is sometimes reduced, as when *posta in croce* becomes “reviled”, but elsewhere English introduces an image of its own, notably “tastes her blessedness” for *beata si gode*.

Additions and omissions contribute to this mixture of closeness and interpretation. “Greedy” in “thy greedy rage”, “our hearts” after “within”, and “single” in “a single one” supply characterization, location, or emphasis not separately expressed in the Italian. Conversely, the two collisions specified by *agli due cozzi* lose their number in “continue butting one another”. Beyond the individual changes catalogued above, English regularly supplies subject pronouns where Italian identifies the subject through the verb ending, and omits Italian articles before possessives. Such routine adjustments coexist with passages of almost word-for-word correspondence: *necessità la fa esser veloce* becomes “necessity makes her be swift”. Overall, the translation preserves much of the Italian’s verbal texture while repeatedly reshaping its syntax and making particular interpretations explicit.
