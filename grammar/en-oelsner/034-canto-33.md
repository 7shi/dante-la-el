# Inferno 33

## Lines 1–18 — Ugolino Begins His Tale

> 1 La bocca sollevò dal fiero pasto  
> 2 quel peccator, forbendola ai capelli  
> 3 del capo, ch’ egli avea di retro guasto.  

From the fell repast that sinner raised his mouth, wiping it upon the hair of the head he had laid waste behind.

- *La bocca sollevò dal fiero pasto quel peccator* ‘The mouth raised from the fell repast that sinner’ → “From the fell repast that sinner raised his mouth”: the prepositional phrase moves to the beginning, the subject precedes the verb, and the object follows it; the definite article before ‘mouth’ becomes a possessive.
- *ai capelli* ‘at the hair’ → “upon the hair”: the preposition is changed.
- *ch’ egli avea di retro guasto* ‘which he had behind damaged’ → “he had laid waste behind”: the relative pronoun is omitted, ‘damaged’ becomes “laid waste”, and the adverbial phrase moves after the participle.

> 4 Poi cominciò: “Tu vuoi ch' io rinnovelli  
> 5 disperato dolor che il cor mi preme,  
> 6 già pur pensando, pria ch' io ne favelli.  

Then he began: “Thou willest that I renew desperate grief, which wrings my heart, even at the very thought, before I tell thereof.

- *che il cor mi preme* ‘which the heart to me presses’ → “which wrings my heart”: the object moves after the verb, the personal pronoun becomes a possessive, and ‘presses’ is rendered more forcefully as “wrings”.
- *già pur pensando* ‘already merely thinking’ → “even at the very thought”: the gerund becomes a noun in a prepositional phrase; ‘already’ and ‘merely’ are recast as the emphasis of “even” and “very”.
- *pria ch’ io ne favelli* ‘before I of it speak’ → “before I tell thereof”: ‘speak’ becomes “tell”, and the preverbal pronoun meaning ‘of it’ becomes the following adverb “thereof”.

> 7 Ma se le mie parole esser den seme,  
> 8 che frutti infamia al traditor ch' io rodo,  
> 9 parlare e lagrimar vedrai insieme.  

But if my words are to be a seed, that may bear fruit of infamy to the traitor whom I gnaw, thou shalt see me speak and weep at the same time.

- *le mie parole esser den seme* ‘my words be must seed’ → “my words are to be a seed”: the modal precedes the infinitive rather than following it; obligation or destined consequence is expressed by “are to”, and an indefinite article is supplied before “seed”.
- *che frutti infamia* ‘that should fruit infamy’ → “that may bear fruit of infamy”: the verb meaning ‘produce as fruit’ is expanded into “bear fruit”, with the subjunctive expressed through “may”; its direct object becomes an “of” phrase.
- *parlare e lagrimar vedrai insieme* ‘speak and weep thou wilt see together’ → “thou shalt see me speak and weep at the same time”: the governing verb moves before the infinitives, their understood subject is made explicit as “me”, and ‘together’ becomes “at the same time”.

> 10 I' non so chi tu sei, nè per che modo  
> 11 venuto se' quaggiù; ma Fiorentino  
> 12 mi sembri veramente, quand' io t' odo.  

I know not who thou mayest be, nor by what mode thou hast come down here; but, when I hear thee, in truth thou seemest to me a Florentine.

- *chi tu sei* ‘who thou art’ → “who thou mayest be”: the indicative becomes a modal expression of uncertainty.
- *venuto se’ quaggiù* ‘come art down here’ → “thou hast come down here”: the auxiliary precedes the participle, and English uses ‘have’ where Italian uses ‘be’.
- *Fiorentino mi sembri veramente* ‘Florentine to me thou seemest truly’ → “in truth thou seemest to me a Florentine”: the predicate moves after the verb, as does ‘to me’; the adverb becomes an introductory prepositional phrase, and “a” makes the predicate explicitly nominal.
- *quand’ io t’ odo* ‘when I thee hear’ → “when I hear thee”: the object follows the verb, and the whole subordinate clause moves before the main clause.

> 13 Tu dei saper ch' io fui Conte Ugolino,  
> 14 e questi è l' Arcivescovo Ruggieri;  
> 15 or ti dirò perchè i son tal vicino.  

Thou hast to know that I was Count Ugolino, and this the Archbishop Ruggieri; now I will tell thee why I am such a neighbour to him.

- *Tu dei saper* ‘Thou must know’ → “Thou hast to know”: the modal verb is rendered by the construction ‘have to’ with an infinitive.
- *questi è* ‘this one is’ → “this”: the personal demonstrative is rendered by “this”, and the copula is omitted.
- *or ti dirò* ‘now to thee I shall tell’ → “now I will tell thee”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *perchè i son tal vicino* ‘why to him I am such neighbour’ → “why I am such a neighbour to him”: the Old Italian dative pronoun *i* (‘to him’) moves to the end as “to him”, and an indefinite article is supplied before “neighbour”.

> 16 Che per l' effetto de' suo' ma' pensieri,  
> 17 fidandomi di lui, io fossi preso  
> 18 e poscia morto, dir non è mestieri.  

That by the effect of his ill devices I, confiding in him, was taken and thereafter put to death, it is not necessary to say.

- *de’ suo’ ma’ pensieri* ‘of his evil thoughts’ → “of his ill devices”: ‘thoughts’ is interpreted as schemes or contrivances.
- *fidandomi di lui* ‘trusting myself to him’ → “confiding in him”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb with “in”; the phrase moves from before the explicit subject to after it.
- *fossi preso* ‘were taken’ → “was taken”: the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *morto* ‘killed’ → “put to death”: the single participle is expanded into a verbal phrase.
- *dir non è mestieri* ‘to say is not need’ → “it is not necessary to say”: anticipatory “it” is supplied, the infinitive moves to the end, and the noun expressing necessity becomes the adjective “necessary”.

## Lines 19–51 — Ugolino’s Dream And Imprisonment

> 19 Però quel che non puoi avere inteso,  
> 20 ciò è come la morte mia fu cruda,  
> 21 udirai, e saprai se m' ha offeso.  

But that which thou canst not have learnt, that is, how cruel was my death, thou shalt hear—and know if he has offended me.

- *non puoi avere inteso* ‘thou canst not have heard’ → “thou canst not have learnt”: hearing is rendered as acquiring knowledge.
- *come la morte mia fu cruda* ‘how my death was cruel’ → “how cruel was my death”: the predicate adjective moves immediately after “how”, the subject follows the verb, and the possessive precedes its noun.
- *m' ha offeso* ‘me he has offended’ → “he has offended me”: the object pronoun follows the verb instead of preceding it.

> 22 Breve pertugio dentro dalla muda,  
> 23 la qual per me ha il titol della fame  
> 24 e in che convien ancor ch' altri si chiuda,  

A narrow hole within the mew, which from me has the title of Famine, and in which others yet must be shut up,

- *Breve pertugio* ‘a small opening’ → “A narrow hole”: smallness is specified as narrowness, and “hole” replaces ‘opening’.
- *dentro dalla muda* ‘inside of the mew’ → “within the mew”: the Italian compound prepositional expression becomes a single preposition.
- *per me* ‘because of me’ → “from me”: the cause of the naming is expressed as its source.
- *e in che convien ancor ch' altri si chiuda* ‘and in which it is necessary yet that another be shut up’ → “and in which others yet must be shut up”: the impersonal construction with a subordinate subjunctive becomes a personal construction with “must”; singular indefinite *altri* becomes plural “others”, and “yet” follows that subject.

> 25 m' avea mostrato per lo suo forame  
> 26 più lune già, quand' io feci il mal sonno,  
> 27 che del futuro mi squarciò il velame.  

had through its opening already shewn me several moons, when I slept the evil sleep that rent for me the curtain of the future.

- *m' avea mostrato per lo suo forame più lune già* ‘to me had shown through its opening several moons already’ → “had through its opening already shewn me several moons”: the recipient pronoun follows the participle; the prepositional phrase and “already” move between the auxiliary and participle.
- *quand' io feci il mal sonno* ‘when I made the evil sleep’ → “when I slept the evil sleep”: the Italian construction with ‘make’ becomes an English verb with a cognate object.
- *che del futuro mi squarciò il velame* ‘that of the future for me tore the veil’ → “that rent for me the curtain of the future”: the phrase modifying ‘veil’ follows its noun, the beneficiary follows the verb, and “curtain” replaces ‘veil’.

> 28 Questi pareva a me maestro e donno,  
> 29 cacciando il lupo e i lupicini al monte,  
> 30 per che i Pisan veder Lucca non ponno.  

This *man* seemed to me lord and master, chasing the wolf and his whelps, upon the mountain for which the Pisans cannot see Lucca.

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “This *man*”: the masculine demonstrative pronoun is expanded with an explicit noun.
- *maestro e donno* ‘master and lord’ → “lord and master”: the two titles exchange places.
- *i lupicini* ‘the little wolves’ → “his whelps”: the diminutive is rendered as a term for offspring, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *al monte* ‘at the mountain’ → “upon the mountain”: the preposition specifies a position on the mountain.
- *i Pisan veder Lucca non ponno* ‘the Pisans see Lucca cannot’ → “the Pisans cannot see Lucca”: the modal verb and negative precede the infinitive and its object.

> 31 Con cagne magre, studiose e conte,  
> 32 Gualandi con Sismondi e con Lanfranchi  
> 33 s' avea messi dinanzi dalla fronte.  

With hounds meagre, keen, and dexterous, he had put in front of him Gualandi with Sismondi, and with Lanfranchi.

- *cagne* ‘female dogs’ → “hounds”: the translation specifies hunting dogs but does not retain their sex.
- *studiose e conte* ‘eager and skilled’ → “keen, and dexterous”: eagerness becomes keenness, and general skill becomes dexterity.
- *s' avea messi dinanzi dalla fronte* ‘he had placed for himself before the forehead’ → “he had put in front of him”: the anatomical expression becomes an ordinary spatial phrase, with the reflexive reference expressed by “him”; the verbal phrase moves before its named objects.

> 34 In picciol corso mi pareano stanchi  
> 35 lo padre e i figli, e con l' acute scane  
> 36 mi parea lor veder fender li fianchi.  

After short course, the father and his sons seemed to me weary; and methought I saw their flanks torn by the sharp teeth.

- *In picciol corso* ‘in a short course’ → “After short course”: the preposition makes the short course something completed before the weariness appears.
- *mi pareano stanchi lo padre e i figli* ‘to me seemed weary the father and the sons’ → “the father and his sons seemed to me weary”: the subject moves before the verb, the experiencer follows it, and the article before ‘sons’ becomes “his”.
- *e con l' acute scane mi parea lor veder fender li fianchi* ‘and with the sharp fangs it seemed to me to see someone cleave their flanks’ → “and methought I saw their flanks torn by the sharp teeth”: the instrumental phrase moves to the end and uses “by”; ‘fangs’ becomes “teeth”. The impersonal construction with an infinitive becomes “methought I saw”, and the active infinitive ‘cleave’ becomes the passive participle “torn”. The Italian dative *lor*, identifying whose flanks are affected, becomes possessive “their”.

> 37 Quando fui desto innanzi la dimane,  
> 38 pianger senti' fra il sonno i miei figliuoli,  
> 39 ch' eran con meco, e domandar del pane.  

When I awoke before the dawn, I heard my sons [who were with me, weeping in their sleep, and] asking for bread.

- *fui desto* ‘I was awake’ → “I awoke”: an adjective with ‘be’ becomes a verb expressing the transition into wakefulness.
- *innanzi la dimane* ‘before the morning’ → “before the dawn”: “dawn” gives a more specific time than ‘morning’.
- *pianger senti' fra il sonno i miei figliuoli* ‘weep I heard amid sleep my sons’ → “I heard my sons” and “weeping in their sleep”: the perceived action moves after the verb of hearing and its object, and its infinitive becomes a participle. ‘Amid sleep’ becomes “in their sleep”, with a possessive supplied; the intervening relative clause now precedes the description of weeping.
- *e domandar del pane* ‘and ask some bread’ → “and] asking for bread”: the infinitive becomes a participle, English ‘ask’ takes “for”, and the partitive force of *del* is left unexpressed.

> 40 Ben se' crudel, se tu già non ti duoli,  
> 41 pensando ciò ch' al mio cor s' annunziava ;  
> 42 e se non piangi, di che pianger suoli?  

Thou art right cruel, if thou dost not grieve already at the thought of what my heart foreboded; and if thou weepest not, at what art thou used to weep?

- *Ben se' crudel* ‘indeed thou art cruel’ → “Thou art right cruel”: the opening affirmation becomes an intensifier immediately before the adjective.
- *se tu già non ti duoli* ‘if thou already dost not grieve’ → “if thou dost not grieve already”: “already” moves after the verb; the Italian pronominal verb is rendered by non-pronominal “grieve”.
- *pensando* ‘thinking’ → “at the thought of”: the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase with a noun.
- *ciò ch' al mio cor s' annunziava* ‘that which was announcing itself to my heart’ → “what my heart foreboded”: the recipient ‘my heart’ becomes the subject, and what announced itself becomes the object of foreboding.
- *di che pianger suoli* ‘for what to weep art thou accustomed’ → “at what art thou used to weep”: the preposition changes, and the infinitive follows rather than precedes the expression of habitual action.

> 43 Già eran desti, e l'ora s' appressava  
> 44 che il cibo ne soleva essere addotto,  
> 45 e per suo sogno ciascun dubitava ;  

They were now awake, and the hour approaching at which our food used to be brought us, and each was anxious from his dream,

- *Già eran desti* ‘already they were awake’ → “They were now awake”: “now” replaces ‘already’ and follows the verb.
- *e l'ora s' appressava* ‘and the hour was drawing near’ → “and the hour approaching”: the finite imperfect becomes a participle with an understood auxiliary.
- *che il cibo ne soleva essere addotto* ‘at which the food used to be brought to us’ → “at which our food used to be brought us”: the temporal relation expressed by *che* is made explicit with “at which”; the definite article before ‘food’ becomes “our”, while *ne* is also retained as “us”.
- *e per suo sogno ciascun dubitava* ‘and because of his dream each was afraid’ → “and each was anxious from his dream”: the causal phrase moves after the subject and predicate; apprehension is expressed through an adjective with “was”, and “from” presents the dream as its source.

> 46 ed io sentii chiavar l' uscio di sotto  
> 47 all' orribile torre : ond' io guardai  
> 48 nel viso a' miei figliuoi senza far motto.  

and below I heard the outlet of the horrible tower locked up: whereat I looked into the faces of my sons, without uttering a word.

- *chiavar l' uscio* ‘nail shut the door’ → “the outlet” and “locked up”: the translation takes *chiavar* as locking rather than nailing shut. The active infinitive becomes a passive participle following the affected object, and “outlet” replaces ‘door’.
- *ed io sentii … di sotto* ‘and I heard … below’ → “and below I heard”: the phrase moves from after ‘door’ to before “I heard”, making its attachment less specifically to the doorway.
- *all' orribile torre* ‘to the horrible tower’ → “of the horrible tower”: the Italian preposition becomes the English possessive or relational “of”.
- *ond' io guardai* ‘wherefore I looked’ → “whereat I looked”: a connective expressing consequence becomes one expressing a response to what has just happened.
- *nel viso a' miei figliuoi* ‘in the face to my sons’ → “into the faces of my sons”: ‘in’ becomes “into”, singular ‘face’ becomes plural, and the Italian dative becomes an English possessive construction with “of”.
- *senza far motto* ‘without making a word’ → “without uttering a word”: the Italian idiom with ‘make’ is rendered with the verb ‘utter’.

> 49 Io non piangeva, sì dentro impietrai ;  
> 50 piangevan elli ; ed Anselmuccio mio  
> 51 disse : 'Tu guardi sì, padre, che hai?'  

I did not weep: so stony grew I within; they wept; and my little Anselm said: ‘Thou lookest so, father, what ails thee?’

- *sì dentro impietrai* ‘so within I turned to stone’ → “so stony grew I within”: the verb meaning ‘turn to stone’ becomes “grew” with an adjective, and “within” moves to the end.
- *piangevan elli* ‘wept they’ → “they wept”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *ed Anselmuccio mio disse* ‘and little Anselm my said’ → “and my little Anselm said”: the diminutive suffix is expressed by the separate adjective “little”, and the possessive moves before the name.
- *che hai* ‘what hast thou’ → “what ails thee”: the idiomatic question with ‘have’ becomes one with “ails”, changing the person addressed from subject to object.

## Lines 52–84 — Starvation and the Curse on Pisa

> 52 Però non lagrimai, nè rispos’ io  
> 53 tutto quel giorno, nè la notte appresso,  
> 54 infin che l’ altro sol nel mondo uscio.  

But I shed no tear, nor answered all that day, nor the next night, till another sun came forth upon the world.

- *Però* ‘therefore’ → “But”: the connective is rendered adversatively rather than as expressing a consequence.
- *non lagrimai* ‘I did not weep’ → “I shed no tear”: the verb becomes a verb-and-noun expression, with the negation attached to the noun.
- *nè rispos’ io* ‘nor answered I’ → “nor answered”: the explicitly stated subject is omitted, shared instead with the preceding verb.
- *nè la notte appresso* ‘nor the night following’ → “nor the next night”: the following night becomes the next night, with the modifier placed before the noun.
- *l’altro sol* ‘the other sun’ → “another sun”: the definite expression becomes indefinite.
- *nel mondo uscio* ‘into the world came forth’ → “came forth upon the world”: the verb precedes the prepositional phrase, and “upon” replaces the Italian expression of entry into the world.

> 55 Come un poco di raggio si fu messo  
> 56 nel doloroso carcere, ed io scorsi  
> 57 per quattro visi il mio aspetto stesso,  
> 58 ambo le mani per dolor mi morsi.  

When a small ray was sent into the doleful prison, and I discerned in their four faces the aspect of my own, I bit on both my hands for grief.

- *Come* ‘as soon as’ → “When”: the explicit sense of immediate succession is weakened.
- *un poco di raggio* ‘a little of a ray’ → “a small ray”: a noun expressing quantity becomes an adjective qualifying the ray.
- *si fu messo* ‘had placed itself’ → “was sent”: the reflexive construction becomes passive, introducing the suggestion of an external agency; the anterior past becomes a simple past.
- *per quattro visi* ‘through four faces’ → “in their four faces”: the preposition changes, and the possessive is supplied.
- *il mio aspetto stesso* ‘my very own appearance’ → “the aspect of my own”: the directly possessed appearance becomes an “of” construction, with “my own” implicitly referring to the speaker’s face.
- *ambo le mani per dolor mi morsi* ‘both the hands for grief I bit myself’ → “I bit on both my hands for grief”: the verb moves before its object, the reflexive becomes a possessive, “on” is supplied, and the phrase expressing the cause follows the object.

> 59 Ed ei, pensando ch’ io ’l fessi per voglia  
> 60 di manicar, di subito levorsi,  

And they, thinking that I did it from desire of eating, of a sudden rose up,

- *ch’ io ’l fessi* ‘that I it did’ → “that I did it”: the object follows the verb, and the Italian subjunctive expressing their supposition becomes an English indicative.
- *per voglia di manicar* ‘for desire of eating’ → “from desire of eating”: the causal relation is expressed through “from”.
- *di subito* ‘suddenly’ → “of a sudden”: the adverbial expression is rendered by a different idiomatic phrase.
- *levorsi* ‘they rose’ → “rose up”: “up” is supplied.

> 61 e disser: ‘Padre, assai ci fia men doglia,  
> 62 se tu mangi di noi: tu ne vestisti  
> 63 queste misere carni, e tu le spoglia.’  

and said: 'Father, it will give us much less pain, if thou wilt eat of us: thou didst put upon us this miserable flesh, and do thou strip it off.'

- *assai ci fia men doglia* ‘much to us will be less pain’ → “it will give us much less pain”: the construction with ‘be’ becomes one with “give”, an anticipatory “it” is supplied, and the degree word is placed directly before “less”.
- *se tu mangi di noi* ‘if thou eatest of us’ → “if thou wilt eat of us”: the present indicative becomes a construction with “wilt”, suggesting willingness or prospective action.
- *tu ne vestisti* ‘thou clothedst us’ → “thou didst put upon us”: the verb of clothing becomes a construction describing the putting on of something.
- *queste misere carni* ‘these miserable fleshes’ → “this miserable flesh”: the plural demonstrative and noun become singular.
- *e tu le spoglia* ‘and thou strip them off’ → “and do thou strip it off”: emphatic “do” is supplied, the subject follows it, and the plural object becomes singular to agree with “flesh”.

> 64 Queta’ mi allor per non farli più tristi;  
> 65 lo dì e l’altro stemmo tutti muti.  

Then I calmed myself, in order not to make them more unhappy; that day and the next we all were mute.

- *Queta’ mi allor* ‘I quieted myself then’ → “Then I calmed myself”: the temporal adverb moves to the beginning, and ‘quieted’ becomes “calmed”.
- *per non farli più tristi* ‘to not make them sadder’ → “in order not to make them more unhappy”: the purpose construction is expanded, and ‘sadder’ becomes “more unhappy”.
- *lo dì e l’altro* ‘the day and the other’ → “that day and the next”: the first article becomes a demonstrative, and ‘the other’ is explicitly interpreted as the next day.
- *stemmo tutti muti* ‘we remained all mute’ → “we all were mute”: remaining in a condition becomes simply being in it, and “all” precedes the verb.

> 66 Ahi dura terra, perchè non t’ apristi?  

Ah, hard earth! why didst thou not open?

- *perchè non t’ apristi* ‘why didst thou not open thyself’ → “why didst thou not open”: the reflexive verb becomes intransitive.

> 67 Poscia che fummo al quarto dì venuti,  
> 68 Gaddo mi si gittò disteso a’ piedi,  
> 69 dicendo: ‘Padre mio, chè non m’ aiuti?’  

When we had come to the fourth day, Gaddo threw himself stretched out at my feet, saying: 'My father! why don't you help me?'

- *Poscia che* ‘after’ → “When”: explicit temporal succession becomes a more general temporal relation.
- *fummo al quarto dì venuti* ‘we were to the fourth day come’ → “we had come to the fourth day”: English uses ‘have’ rather than ‘be’ as the perfect auxiliary, and the participle precedes the phrase specifying the day.
- *mi si gittò disteso a’ piedi* ‘to me himself threw stretched at the feet’ → “threw himself stretched out at my feet”: the reflexive follows the verb, the personal dative becomes a possessive attached to “feet”, and “out” makes the sense of ‘stretched’ explicit.
- *Padre mio* ‘father my’ → “My father”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *non m’ aiuti* ‘not me thou helpest’ → “don't you help me”: English supplies the auxiliary for the negative question and places the object after the main verb.

> 70 Quivi morì; e come tu mi vedi,  
> 71 vid’ io cascar li tre ad uno ad uno  

There he died; and even as thou seest me, saw I the three fall one by one,

- *e come tu mi vedi* ‘and as thou me seest’ → “and even as thou seest me”: “even” is supplied, and the object follows the verb.
- *vid’ io cascar li tre* ‘saw I fall the three’ → “saw I the three fall”: the subject of the infinitive precedes it rather than following it.

> 72 tra il quinto dì e il sesto: ond’ io mi diedi  
> 73 già cieco a brancolar sopra ciascuno,  
> 74 e due dì li chiamai poi che fur morti;  
> 75 poscia, più che il dolor, potè il digiuno.”  

between the fifth day and the sixth: whence I betook me, already blind, to groping over each, and for [two] days called them, after they were dead; then fasting had more power than grief."

- *ond’ io mi diedi* ‘whence I myself gave’ → “whence I betook me”: the idiom of giving oneself to an activity becomes one of betaking oneself to it, and the reflexive follows the verb.
- *a brancolar* ‘to grope’ → “to groping”: the infinitive becomes a gerund governed by a preposition.
- *e due dì li chiamai* ‘and two days them I called’ → “and for [two] days called them”: “for” explicitly marks duration, and the object follows the verb.
- *più che il dolor, potè il digiuno* ‘more than grief, had power fasting’ → “fasting had more power than grief”: the subject moves before the predicate, and the comparative phrase follows it; the force of the Italian verb is expressed through the noun “power”.

> 76 Quand’ ebbe detto ciò, con gli occhi torti  
> 77 riprese il teschio misero coi denti,  
> 78 che furo all’ osso, come d’ un can, forti.  

When he had spoken this, with eyes distorted he seized the miserable skull again with his teeth, which as a dog's were strong upon the bone.

- *ebbe detto ciò* ‘he had said this’ → “he had spoken this”: ‘say’ is rendered by ‘speak’, retaining the direct object.
- *con gli occhi torti* ‘with the eyes distorted’ → “with eyes distorted”: the definite article is omitted.
- *riprese il teschio misero* ‘retook the skull miserable’ → “he seized the miserable skull again”: the verb becomes “seized”, its repeated action is separately expressed by “again”, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *coi denti* ‘with the teeth’ → “with his teeth”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *che furo all’ osso, come d’ un can, forti* ‘which were upon the bone, as of a dog, strong’ → “which as a dog's were strong upon the bone”: the comparison moves before the verb, the adjective moves directly after it, and the phrase with ‘of’ becomes a possessive.

> 79 Ahi Pisa, vituperio delle genti  
> 80 del bel paese là dove il “sì” suona,  
> 81 poi che i vicini a te punir son lenti,  

Ah, Pisa! scandal to the people of the beauteous land where “_sì_” is heard, since thy neighbours are slow to punish thee,

- *vituperio delle genti* ‘disgrace of the peoples’ → “scandal to the people”: ‘disgrace’ becomes “scandal”, the relation expressed by ‘of’ becomes one expressed by “to”, and the plural becomes a collective singular.
- *là dove il ‘sì’ suona* ‘there where the yes sounds’ → “where “_sì_” is heard”: ‘there’ and the article are omitted, *sì* is retained untranslated, and the active verb describing sound becomes a passive verb describing its reception.
- *i vicini* ‘the neighbours’ → “thy neighbours”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *a te punir son lenti* ‘to thee punish are slow’ → “are slow to punish thee”: the infinitival phrase follows the predicate, and its object follows the infinitive.

> 82 movasi la Caprara e la Gorgona,  
> 83 e faccian siepe ad Arno in su la foce,  
> 84 sì ch’ egli anneghi in te ogni persona.  

let the _Caprara_ and _Gorgona_ move, and hedge up the _Arno_ at its mouth, that it may drown in thee every living soul.

- *movasi la Caprara e la Gorgona* ‘may move itself the Caprara and the Gorgona’ → “let the _Caprara_ and _Gorgona_ move”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”, the reflexive becomes intransitive, the subjects precede “move”, and the second article is omitted.
- *e faccian siepe ad Arno* ‘and may they make a hedge for Arno’ → “and hedge up the _Arno_”: the verb-and-noun expression becomes a phrasal verb, Arno becomes its direct object, and the article is supplied; the exhortation continues under the preceding “let”.
- *in su la foce* ‘upon the mouth’ → “at its mouth”: the preposition changes, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *sì ch’* ‘so that’ → “that”: ‘so’ is omitted.
- *ogni persona* ‘every person’ → “every living soul”: ‘person’ becomes “soul”, and “living” is supplied.

## Summary — Close Echoes in English Prose

The translation stays close to the Italian’s phrasing and imagery, but regularly rearranges its words into English prose order. Subjects move before verbs, objects follow them, and adjectives and possessives generally precede their nouns. Thus *piangevan elli* becomes “they wept”, while *più che il dolor, potè il digiuno* becomes “fasting had more power than grief”. Longer phrases and clauses also change position to bring related elements together. Yet inversion is sometimes retained, as in “saw I the three fall”, and expressions such as “lord and master”, “crystal vizors”, and “dates for my figs” preserve much of the original’s verbal texture.

Changes of construction often accompany this reordering. Italian personal datives become English possessives: *che il cor mi preme* becomes “which wrings my heart”, and *aprimi gli occhi* becomes “open my eyes”. Reflexive or pronominal verbs frequently become English intransitives, as when *si volve in entro* becomes “turns inward”. Elsewhere a verb becomes a noun phrase, or a phrase becomes a verb: *pensando* is rendered as “at the thought of”, whereas *faccian siepe* becomes “hedge up”. Impersonal constructions are recast with personal subjects or anticipatory ‘it’, and Italian subjunctives are variously represented by indicatives or by auxiliaries such as “may”. English also uses ‘have’ where Italian compound tenses use *essere*.

The vocabulary ranges from close equivalents to interpretative substitutions. *Ma’ pensieri* becomes “ill devices”, making the thoughts into schemes; *croce* becomes “torture”, replacing the figurative object with its meaning; and *vapore* becomes “heat”. Some choices strengthen or specify the expression, as “wrings” does for *preme*; others generalize it, as “teeth” does for *scane*, ‘fangs’. Grammatical number sometimes changes too: *carni* becomes “flesh”, while *dattero per figo* becomes “dates for my figs”.

Words are supplied to clarify references, location, or emphasis: *questi* becomes “This *man*”, *di sopra* becomes “above on earth”, and *l’occhio* becomes “thine eye *itself*”. Conversely, some elements receive no separate equivalent, including *mio* in the address rendered “Master” and *oramai* before “reach hither thy hand”. Beyond the individual departures recorded in the comparison, English routinely supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending, and omits the articles that Italian normally places before possessives. These adjustments make the rendering less than word-for-word without generally loosening its close attachment to the Italian’s sequence of expressions, repetitions, and concrete vocabulary.
