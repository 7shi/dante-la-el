# Inferno 30

## Lines 1–21 — The Madness of Athamas and Hecuba

> 1 Nel tempo che Giunone era crucciata  
> 2 per Semelè contra il sangue tebano,  
> 3 come mostrò una ed altra fiata,  

At the time that Juno was incensed for Semele against the Theban blood, as she already more than once had shewn,

- *il sangue tebano* ‘the blood Theban’ → “the Theban blood”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *mostrò* ‘she showed’ → “she already more than once had shewn”: the simple past becomes past perfect, and “already” is supplied.
- *una ed altra fiata* ‘one and another time’ → “more than once”: the coordinated expression becomes an idiomatic phrase expressing repetition, placed before the main verb.

> 4 Atamante divenne tanto insano  
> 5 che, veggendo la moglie con due figli  
> 6 andar carcata da ciascuna mano,  

Athamas grew so insane, that he, seeing his wife, with two sons, [go] laden on either hand,

- *divenne* ‘became’ → “grew”: becoming is expressed as growth into a condition.
- *la moglie* ‘the wife’ → “his wife”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *da ciascuna mano* ‘from each hand’ → “on either hand”: “on” replaces the Italian preposition, and “either” renders the distributive *ciascuna* with reference to the two hands.

> 7 gridò: “Tendiam le reti, sì ch’ io pigli  
> 8 la leonessa e i leoncini al varco”;  

cried: "Spread we the nets, that I may take the lioness and her young lions at the pass";

- *Tendiam le reti* ‘Let us stretch the nets’ → “Spread we the nets”: “Spread” replaces ‘stretch’, and the exhortation takes an inverted English form with “we” following the verb.
- *sì ch’ io pigli* ‘so that I may catch’ → “that I may take”: *sì* is absorbed into the conjunction “that”; “take” renders ‘catch’, and the subjunctive is expressed with “may”.
- *i leoncini* ‘the little lions’ → “her young lions”: the article becomes a possessive, and the diminutive is expressed by the separate adjective “young”.

> 9 e poi distese i dispietati artigli,  
> 10 prendendo l’ un che avea nome Learco,  
> 11 e rotollo, e percosselo ad un sasso;  

and then stretched out his pitiless talons, grasping the one who had the name Learchus; and whirled him, and dashed him on a rock;

- *i dispietati artigli* ‘the pitiless talons’ → “his pitiless talons”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *prendendo* ‘taking’ → “grasping”: the English verb makes the physical grip more explicit.
- *avea nome* ‘had name’ → “had the name”: English supplies the definite article.
- *percosselo ad un sasso* ‘struck him against a rock’ → “dashed him on a rock”: “dashed” gives a more forceful rendering of ‘struck’, and “on” expresses the contact conveyed by *ad*.

> 12 e quella s’ annegò con l’ altro carco.  

and she with her other burden drowned herself.

- *quella* ‘that woman’ → “she”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *s’ annegò* ‘herself drowned’ → “drowned herself”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb in English.
- *con l’ altro carco* ‘with the other burden’ → “with her other burden”: the article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves before the verb.

> 13 E quando la fortuna volse in basso  
> 14 l’ altezza de’ Troian che tutto ardiva,  
> 15 sì che insieme col regno il re fu casso,  

And when Fortune brought low the all-daring pride of the Trojans, so that the King together with his kingdom was blotted out;

- *volse in basso* ‘turned downward’ → “brought low”: the phrase is replaced by an English idiom for lowering or overthrowing.
- *l’ altezza de’ Troian che tutto ardiva* ‘the height of the Trojans which dared everything’ → “the all-daring pride of the Trojans”: “pride” interprets the figurative *altezza*; the relative clause becomes the compound adjective “all-daring” and moves before the noun.
- *insieme col regno il re fu casso* ‘together with the kingdom the king was effaced’ → “the King together with his kingdom was blotted out”: the subject moves before the accompanying phrase; the article before ‘kingdom’ becomes “his”; and “blotted out” renders *casso* with a phrasal verb.

> 16 Ecuba trista, misera e cattiva,  
> 17 poscia che vide Polissena morta,  
> 18 e del suo Polidoro in su la riva  
> 19 del mar si fu la dolorosa accorta,  

Hecuba, sad, miserable, and captive, after she had seen Polyxena slain, and, forlorn, discerned her Polydorus, on the sea-strand,

- *poscia che vide* ‘after she saw’ → “after she had seen”: the simple past becomes past perfect.
- *Polissena morta* ‘Polyxena dead’ → “Polyxena slain”: “slain” specifies death through killing rather than simply the condition of being dead.
- *del suo Polidoro … si fu … accorta* ‘of her Polydorus she had become aware’ → “discerned her Polydorus”: the reflexive construction governing *di* becomes a transitive verb with a direct object, which follows rather than precedes the verb. The earlier “had” supplies the shared auxiliary for the perfect construction.
- *in su la riva del mar* ‘on the shore of the sea’ → “on the sea-strand”: the noun and its dependent phrase become a compound noun.
- *la dolorosa* ‘the sorrowful woman’ → “forlorn”: the substantival adjective serving as subject becomes a parenthetical adjective describing Hecuba, and moves before the verb. “Forlorn” also shifts the emphasis from sorrow to desolation.

> 20 forsennata latrò sì come cane:  

she, out of her senses, barked like a dog:

- *forsennata* ‘mad’ → “out of her senses”: the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *sì come cane* ‘just as dog’ → “like a dog”: the comparison becomes a phrase introduced by “like”, without a separate equivalent for *sì*, and English supplies an indefinite article.

> 21 tanto il dolor le fe’ la mente torta.  

to such a degree had [the] sorrow wrung her soul.

- *tanto* ‘so much’ → “to such a degree”: the adverb becomes a prepositional phrase expressing extent.
- *il dolor le fe’ la mente torta* ‘the sorrow made her mind twisted’ → “had [the] sorrow wrung her soul”: the causative construction becomes the transitive verb “wrung”; the simple past becomes past perfect, with the auxiliary placed before the subject. The indirect pronoun *le* becomes the possessive “her”, and “soul” replaces ‘mind’.

## Lines 22–54 — The Biting Shadows and the Dropsied Spirit

> 22 Ma nè di Tebe furie nè Troiane  
> 23 si vider mai in alcun tanto crude,  
> 24 non punger bestie, non che membra umane,  

But neither Theban Furies nor Trojan were ever seen in aught so cruel—not in stinging brutes, and much less human limbs;

- *nè di Tebe furie nè Troiane* ‘neither of Thebes furies nor Trojan’ → “neither Theban Furies nor Trojan”: the place-name phrase becomes an adjective placed before the noun.
- *si vider mai* ‘ever showed themselves’ → “were ever seen”: the Italian pronominal construction is rendered as an English passive.
- *in alcun* ‘in anyone’ → “in aught”: an expression referring to a person becomes one referring to anything.
- *non punger bestie* ‘not to sting beasts’ → “not in stinging brutes”: the infinitive becomes a gerund governed by the supplied “in”; “brutes” replaces ‘beasts’.
- *non che membra umane* ‘let alone human limbs’ → “and much less human limbs”: the idiom becomes an explicit comparative phrase, with “and” supplied.

> 25 quant' io vidi in due ombre smorte e nude,  
> 26 che mordendo correvan di quel modo,  
> 27 che il porco quando del porcil si schiude.  

as I saw [in] two shadows, pale and naked, which ran biting in the manner that a hungry swine does when he is thrust out from his sty.

- *quant' io vidi in* ‘as much as I saw in’ → “as I saw [in]”: the quantitative comparison is shortened to “as”.
- *smorte* ‘deathly pale’ → “pale”: the suggestion of deathlike pallor is reduced.
- *che mordendo correvan* ‘which biting ran’ → “which ran biting”: the participle follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *di quel modo, che* ‘in that manner in which’ → “in the manner that”: the demonstrative becomes the definite article; the English subsequently supplies “does” to stand for the repeated action.
- *il porco* ‘the pig’ → “a hungry swine”: the generic definite article becomes indefinite, and “hungry” is added.
- *del porcil* ‘from the sty’ → “from his sty”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *si schiude* ‘gets out’ → “he is thrust out”: emergence becomes a passive action imposed from outside, with the additional suggestion of force.

> 28 L' una giunse a Capocchio, ed in sul nodo  
> 29 del collo l' assannò sì che tirando  
> 30 grattar gli fece il ventre al fondo sodo.  

The one came to Capocchio, and fixed its tusks on his neck-joint, so that, dragging him, it made the solid bottom claw his belly.

- *L' una* ‘one’ → “The one”: English adds the definite article to the Italian pronominal expression.
- *giunse a* ‘arrived at’ → “came to”: arrival is expressed as coming.
- *ed in sul nodo del collo l' assannò* ‘and on the joint of the neck it tusked him’ → “and fixed its tusks on his neck-joint”: the verb becomes a verb-and-object phrase; the location moves after it, the neck phrase becomes a compound noun, and the object pronoun becomes the possessive “his”.
- *tirando* ‘dragging’ → “dragging him”: the object is supplied.
- *grattar gli fece il ventre al fondo sodo* ‘made him scrape his belly along the solid bottom’ → “it made the solid bottom claw his belly”: the bottom becomes the agent of the infinitive rather than the surface against which the belly is scraped; “claw” replaces ‘scrape’.

> 31 E l' Aretin, che rimase tremando,  
> 32 mi disse : “Quel folletto è Gianni Schicchi,  
> 33 e va rabbioso altrui così conciando.”  

And the Aretine, who remained trembling, said to me: “That goblin is Gianni Schicchi; and, rabid, he goes thus mangling others.”

- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *va rabbioso altrui così conciando* ‘goes rabid, others thus treating’ → “rabid, he goes thus mangling others”: “rabid” moves before the subject and verb, and the object moves after the participle; “mangling” makes explicit the damaging sense of *conciando*.

> 34 “O,” diss' io lui, “se l' altro non ti ficchi  
> 35 li denti addosso, non ti sia fatica  
> 36 a dir chi è, pria che di qui si spicchi.”  

“Oh!” said I to him, “so may the other not plant its teeth on thee, be pleased to tell us who it is, ere it snatch itself away.”

- *se l' altro non ti ficchi li denti addosso* ‘if the other does not stick the teeth onto you’ → “so may the other not plant its teeth on thee”: the conditional form of the wish becomes an explicit optative with “may”; “plant” replaces ‘stick’, and “its” replaces the definite article.
- *non ti sia fatica a dir chi è* ‘may it not be a trouble to you to say who it is’ → “be pleased to tell us who it is”: the negative request becomes a positive imperative formula, and “us” is supplied.
- *pria che di qui si spicchi* ‘before it tears itself away from here’ → “ere it snatch itself away”: ‘from here’ has no separate equivalent, and ‘tearing itself away’ becomes “snatch itself away”.

> 37 Ed egli a me : “Quell' è l' anima antica  
> 38 di Mirra scellerata, che divenne  
> 39 al padre, fuor del dritto amore, amica.  

And he to me: “That is the ancient spirit of flagitious Myrrha, who loved her father with more than rightful love.

- *l' anima antica* ‘the spirit ancient’ → “the ancient spirit”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *di Mirra scellerata* ‘of Myrrha flagitious’ → “of flagitious Myrrha”: the adjective moves before the name.
- *che divenne al padre, fuor del dritto amore, amica* ‘who became her father’s lover, outside rightful love’ → “who loved her father with more than rightful love”: becoming someone’s lover is recast as loving him, without the change of state expressed by *divenne*; ‘outside’ becomes the quantitative “more than”.

> 40 Questa a peccar con esso così venne,  
> 41 falsificando sè in altrui forma,  

She came to sin with him disguised in alien form;

- *Questa* ‘this one’ → “She”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *a peccar con esso così venne* ‘to sin with him thus came’ → “came to sin with him”: the main verb moves before its infinitive phrase; *così* has no separate equivalent.
- *falsificando sè in altrui forma* ‘falsifying herself in another’s form’ → “disguised in alien form”: an active reflexive gerund becomes a passive participle describing her condition; another person’s form becomes “alien form”.

> 42 come l' altro, che là sen va, sostenne,  
> 43 per guadagnar la donna della torma,  
> 44 falsificare in sè Buoso Donati,  
> 45 testando, e dando al testamento norma.”  

even as the other who there is going away, undertook, that he might gain the Lady of the troop, to disguise himself as Buoso Donati, making a testament and giving to it legal form.”

- *come* ‘as’ → “even as”: “even” is supplied to emphasize the comparison.
- *che là sen va* ‘who there goes away’ → “who there is going away”: the simple present becomes a progressive.
- *sostenne* ‘bore’ → “undertook”: the verb is interpreted as undertaking an action rather than bearing or sustaining something.
- *per guadagnar* ‘in order to gain’ → “that he might gain”: the purpose infinitive becomes a finite clause with “might”.
- *falsificare in sè Buoso Donati* ‘to counterfeit Buoso Donati in himself’ → “to disguise himself as Buoso Donati”: the person counterfeited ceases to be the direct object; “himself” becomes the object, and “as” introduces the assumed identity.
- *testando* ‘making a will’ → “making a testament”: the single Italian verb is expanded into a verb-and-object phrase.
- *dando al testamento norma* ‘giving to the testament a rule’ → “giving to it legal form”: the repeated noun becomes a pronoun, “form” replaces ‘rule’, and “legal” is supplied.

> 46 E poi che i due rabbiosi fur passati,  
> 47 sopra cui io avea l' occhio tenuto,  
> 48 rivolsilo a guardar gli altri mal nati.  

And when the furious two, on whom I had kept my eye, were passed, I turned it to observe the other ill-born spirits.

- *poi che* ‘after’ → “when”: the explicitly successive relation becomes a more general temporal one.
- *i due rabbiosi* ‘the two furious ones’ → “the furious two”: the numeral and adjective exchange positions, and “two” becomes the nominal head.
- *sopra cui io avea l' occhio tenuto* ‘on whom I had the eye kept’ → “on whom I had kept my eye”: the participle moves before its object, and the definite article becomes “my”.
- *rivolsilo* ‘I turned it back’ → “I turned it”: the backward or renewed direction of the turning is not separately expressed.
- *gli altri mal nati* ‘the other ill-born ones’ → “the other ill-born spirits”: “spirits” supplies a noun for the substantival Italian expression.

> 49 Io vidi un, fatto a guisa di liuto,  
> 50 pur ch' egli avesse avuta l' anguinaia  
> 51 tronca dal lato che l' uomo ha forcuto.  

I saw one shapen like a lute, if he had only had his groin cut short at the part where man is forked.

- *fatto* ‘made’ → “shapen”: the translation specifies shape rather than making generally.
- *a guisa di liuto* ‘in the manner of a lute’ → “like a lute”: the nominal expression becomes a simple comparison.
- *pur ch' egli avesse avuta* ‘provided only that he had had’ → “if he had only had”: the proviso becomes an “if” clause, with “only” placed within the verb phrase.
- *l' anguinaia tronca* ‘the groin cut off’ → “his groin cut short”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and complete truncation is expressed as being “cut short”.
- *dal lato che l' uomo ha forcuto* ‘at the part which man has forked’ → “at the part where man is forked”: the relative object construction becomes a relative clause of place, and possessing a forked part becomes being forked there.

> 52 La grave idropisì, che sì dispaia  
> 53 le membra con l' umor che mal converte  
> 54 che il viso non risponde alla ventraia,  

The heavy dropsy, which with its ill-digested humour so disproportions the limbs, that the visage corresponds not to the paunch,

- *che sì dispaia le membra* ‘which so makes the limbs unequal’ → “which” … “so disproportions the limbs”: “disproportions” renders the making unequal as a distortion of bodily proportions; the intervening phrase about the humour precedes the verb in English.
- *con l' umor che mal converte* ‘with the humour that it badly converts’ → “with its ill-digested humour”: the relative clause becomes a passive adjectival expression, interpreting faulty conversion as faulty digestion; “its” replaces the definite article.
- *non risponde* ‘does not answer’ → “corresponds not”: figurative ‘answering’ becomes explicit correspondence, and the negative follows the verb.

## Lines 55–87 — Master Adam’s Thirst and Vengeance

> 55 faceva a lui tener le labbra aperte,  
> 56 come l' etico fa, che per la sete  
> 57 l' un verso il mento e l' altro in su rinverte.  

made him hold his lips apart, as does the hectic patient, who for thirst curls the one lip towards the chin, and the other upwards.

- *faceva a lui* ‘made to him’ → “made him”: the Italian indirect object becomes the direct object of the English causative construction.
- *tener le labbra aperte* ‘hold the lips open’ → “hold his lips apart”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “apart” replaces ‘open’.
- *come l' etico fa* ‘as the consumptive does’ → “as does the hectic patient”: the substantivized adjective becomes an adjective and noun, and the verb moves before its subject.
- *per la sete* ‘for the thirst’ → “for thirst”: the definite article is omitted.
- *l' un verso il mento e l' altro in su rinverte* ‘the one towards the chin and the other upwards turns back’ → “curls the one lip towards the chin, and the other upwards”: the verb moves before its objects; ‘turns back’ becomes “curls”; and “lip,” understood in the Italian, is supplied.

> 58 "O voi, che senza alcuna pena siete,  
> 59 (e non so io perchè), nel mondo gramo,"  
> 60 diss' egli a noi, "guardate ed attendete  
> 61 alla miseria del maestro Adamo:  

“O ye! who are exempt from every punishment (and why I know not), in this grim world,” said he to us, “look and attend to the misery of Master Adam

- *che senza alcuna pena siete* ‘who without any punishment are’ → “who are exempt from every punishment”: the verb moves before the complement, and being ‘without any punishment’ becomes being “exempt from every punishment”.
- *e non so io perchè* ‘and not know I why’ → “and why I know not”: “why” moves to the beginning of the embedded question, the subject precedes the verb, and the negative follows it.
- *nel mondo gramo* ‘in the world wretched’ → “in this grim world”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative, the adjective precedes the noun, and ‘wretched’ becomes “grim”.
- *del maestro Adamo* ‘of the master Adam’ → “of Master Adam”: the article before the title is omitted.

> 62 io ebbi, vivo, assai di quel ch' io volli,  
> 63 ed ora, lasso! un gocciol d' acqua bramo.  

: when alive, I had enough of what I wished; and now, alas! I crave one little drop of water.

- *vivo* ‘alive’ → “when alive”: “when” makes the temporal relation explicit, and the phrase moves before “I had”.
- *assai* ‘much’ → “enough”: abundance is rendered as sufficiency.
- *di quel ch' io volli* ‘of that which I wished’ → “of what I wished”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun become the single relative “what”.
- *lasso* ‘weary’ → “alas!”: an adjective describing the speaker becomes an interjection.
- *un gocciol d' acqua bramo* ‘one little drop of water I crave’ → “I crave one little drop of water”: the verb moves before its object.

> 64 Li ruscelletti, che dei verdi colli  
> 65 del Casentin discendon giuso in Arno,  
> 66 facendo i lor canali freddi e molli,  
> 67 sempre mi stanno innanzi, e non indarno:  

The rivulets that from the verdant hills of Casentino descend into the Arno, making their channels cool and moist, stand constantly before me, and not in vain:

- *verdi* ‘green’ → “verdant”: the adjective of colour becomes one describing green vegetation.
- *discendon giuso in Arno* ‘descend down into Arno’ → “descend into the Arno”: the explicit downward adverb is omitted, and an article is supplied before the river’s name.
- *freddi e molli* ‘cold and wet’ → “cool and moist”: both qualities are expressed in milder terms.
- *sempre mi stanno innanzi* ‘always to me stand before’ → “stand constantly before me”: “constantly” replaces ‘always’ and follows the verb; the Italian pronoun preceding the verb becomes the object of “before”.

> 68 chè l' imagine lor vie più m' asciuga,  
> 69 che il male ond' io nel volto mi discarno.  

for the image of them dries me up far more than the disease which from my visage wears the flesh.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *l' imagine lor* ‘their image’ → “the image of them”: the possessive becomes an “of” phrase following the noun.
- *vie più m' asciuga* ‘far more me dries’ → “dries me up far more”: the object pronoun and the expression of degree move after the verb, and “up” is supplied.
- *ond' io nel volto mi discarno* ‘through which I in the face lose my flesh’ → “which from my visage wears the flesh”: the Italian speaker is the subject of a reflexive verb, with the disease expressed as its cause; the translation makes the disease the subject of a transitive verb. The meaning contained in *discarno* is distributed between “wears” and “the flesh,” while ‘in the face’ becomes “from my visage”.

> 70 La rigida giustizia, che mi fruga,  
> 71 tragge cagion del loco ov' io peccai  
> 72 a metter più li miei sospiri in fuga.  

The rigid Justice, which searches me, takes occasion from the place at which I sinned, to give my sighs a quicker flight.

- *che mi fruga* ‘which me searches’ → “which searches me”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *tragge cagion* ‘draws occasion’ → “takes occasion”: the English uses a different verb in the idiom.
- *ov' io peccai* ‘where I sinned’ → “at which I sinned”: the relative adverb becomes a preposition and relative pronoun.
- *a metter più li miei sospiri in fuga* ‘to put my sighs more to flight’ → “to give my sighs a quicker flight”: ‘put to flight’ becomes “give … a … flight,” and the adverb *più* is interpreted as greater speed and expressed by the adjective “quicker”.

> 73 Ivi è Romena, là dov' io falsai  
> 74 la lega sigillata del Batista:  
> 75 per ch' io il corpo suso arso lasciai.  

There is Romena where I falsified the alloy, sealed with the Baptist's image : for which on earth I left my body burnt.

- *là dov' io falsai* ‘there where I falsified’ → “where I falsified”: the second locative expression, *là*, is omitted.
- *sigillata del Batista* ‘sealed with the Baptist’ → “sealed with the Baptist's image”: “image” is supplied, and the Baptist becomes its possessor.
- *io il corpo suso arso lasciai* ‘I the body up above burnt left’ → “on earth I left my body burnt”: *suso* is interpreted as “on earth” and moved to the beginning of the clause; the verb moves before its object; and the definite article before ‘body’ becomes a possessive.

> 76 Ma s' io vedessi qui l' anima trista  
> 77 di Guido o d' Alessandro o di lor frate,  
> 78 per fonte Branda non darei la vista.  

But if I could see the miserable soul of Guido here, or of Alessandro, or their brother, for Branda's fount I would not give the sight.

- *s' io vedessi* ‘if I saw’ → “if I could see”: the subjunctive is rendered with the modal “could,” explicitly expressing possibility.
- *qui l' anima trista di Guido* ‘here the soul sad of Guido’ → “the miserable soul of Guido here”: the adverb moves from immediately after the verb to after “of Guido”; the adjective moves before the noun, and ‘sad’ becomes “miserable”.
- *o di lor frate* ‘or of their brother’ → “or their brother”: the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *per fonte Branda* ‘for fount Branda’ → “for Branda's fount”: the name following the noun becomes a possessive preceding it.

> 79 Dentro c' è l' una già, se l' arrabbiate  
> 80 ombre che vanno intorno dicon vero;  
> 81 ma che mi val, ch' ho le membra legate?  

One is in already, if the mad shadows that are going round speak true; but what avails it me whose limbs are tied?

- *Dentro c' è l' una già* ‘within there is the one already’ → “One is in already”: the existential construction becomes a statement with “One” as its subject; the definite article is omitted, and the initial locative becomes the complement “in”.
- *l' arrabbiate ombre* ‘the enraged shadows’ → “the mad shadows”: ‘enraged’ becomes “mad”.
- *che vanno intorno* ‘that go around’ → “that are going round”: the simple present becomes a progressive present.
- *che mi val* ‘what to me avails’ → “what avails it me”: “it” is supplied as the subject, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *ch' ho le membra legate* ‘for I have the limbs bound’ → “whose limbs are tied”: the explanatory clause becomes a possessive relative clause attached to “me”; the speaker’s having bound limbs becomes a passive statement with “limbs” as its subject.

> 82 S' io fossi pur di tanto ancor leggiero,  
> 83 ch' io potessi in cent' anni andare un' oncia,  
> 84 io sarei messo già per lo sentiero,  

Were I only still so light, that I could move one inch in a hundred years, I had already put myself upon the road,

- *S' io fossi pur di tanto ancor leggiero* ‘If I were only to such a degree still light’ → “Were I only still so light”: conditional inversion replaces ‘if’; the expression of degree becomes “so” and moves after “still”.
- *ch' io potessi in cent' anni andare un' oncia* ‘that I could in a hundred years go one inch’ → “that I could move one inch in a hundred years”: the time phrase moves after the verb and its object, and ‘go’ becomes “move”.
- *io sarei messo già per lo sentiero* ‘I would be set already upon the path’ → “I had already put myself upon the road”: the conditional construction becomes a pluperfect with counterfactual force, and “myself” makes the reflexive sense explicit. “Already” moves between the auxiliary and participle, and ‘path’ becomes “road”.

> 85 cercando lui tra questa gente sconcia,  
> 86 con tutto ch' ella volge undici miglia,  
> 87 e men d' un mezzo di traverso non ci ha.  

to seek him among this disfigured people, though it winds round eleven miles, and is not less than half a mile across.

- *cercando lui* ‘seeking him’ → “to seek him”: the gerund becomes an infinitive expressing purpose.
- *con tutto ch'* ‘with all that’ → “though”: the Italian concessive phrase becomes a single conjunction.
- *ella volge* ‘it turns’ → “it winds round”: the verb is expanded into a phrase making the circuit explicit.
- *e men d' un mezzo di traverso non ci ha* ‘and less than a half across there is not’ → “and is not less than half a mile across”: the existential construction becomes a statement of measurement with an understood subject carried over from the preceding clause. The negative precedes the comparison, “across” moves to the end, and “mile,” understood after *mezzo*, is supplied.

## Lines 88–120 — Master Adam and Sinon Quarrel

> 88 Io son per lor tra sì fatta famiglia:  

Through them am I in such a crew:

- *Io son per lor* ‘I am through them’ → “Through them am I”: the causal phrase comes first, and subject and verb are inverted.
- *tra sì fatta famiglia* ‘among such a family’ → “in such a crew”: “in” replaces ‘among’, and “crew” gives *famiglia* a disparaging collective sense.

> 89 ei m’ indussero a battere i fiorini,  
> 90 che avean ben tre carati di mondiglia.”  

they induced me to stamp the florins that had three carats of alloy.”

- *ei m’ indussero* ‘they me induced’ → “they induced me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *a battere* ‘to strike’ → “to stamp”: the verb is rendered specifically in its coin-making sense.
- *ben tre carati* ‘fully three carats’ → “three carats”: the intensifying *ben* is omitted.
- *mondiglia* ‘dross’ → “alloy”: the derogatory term for impurities becomes a technical term for the mixed-in metal.

> 91 Ed io a lui: “Chi son li due tapini,  
> 92 che fuman come man bagnate il verno,  
> 93 giacendo stretti a’ tuoi destri confini?”  

And I to him: “Who are the abject two, lying close to thy right confines, and smoking like a hand bathed in winter-time?”

- *Chi son li due tapini* ‘Who are the two wretches’ → “Who are the abject two”: the noun *tapini* becomes the adjective “abject”, while “two” becomes the substantive.
- *che fuman* ‘who smoke’ → “and smoking”: the relative clause becomes a coordinated participial phrase and moves after the phrase describing their position.
- *come man bagnate* ‘like wet hands’ → “like a hand bathed”: plural hands become a singular hand, and ‘wet’ is rendered as “bathed”.
- *il verno* ‘the winter’ → “in winter-time”: the temporal expression receives an explicit preposition.
- *giacendo stretti a’ tuoi destri confini* ‘lying close at thy right boundaries’ → “lying close to thy right confines”: the phrase moves before the description of their smoking, and the spatial relation is expressed with “to”.

> 94 “Qui li trovai, e poi volta non dierno,”  
> 95 rispose, “quand’ io piovvi in questo greppo,  
> 96 e non credo che dieno in sempiterno.  

“Here I found them, when I rained into this pinfold,” he answered; “and since then they have not given a turn, and may not give, I think, to all eternity.

- *Qui li trovai* ‘Here them I found’ → “Here I found them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *e poi volta non dierno* ‘and afterwards a turn they did not give’ → “and since then they have not given a turn”: *poi* becomes a period extending from that time to the present; the simple past becomes a present perfect; and the object follows the verb. The clause also moves after the arrival clause and the reporting phrase.
- *rispose* ‘answered’ → “he answered”: the reporting phrase moves from before the arrival clause to after it.
- *in questo greppo* ‘into this rocky slope’ → “into this pinfold”: the terrain word is replaced by a word for an enclosure.
- *e non credo che dieno in sempiterno* ‘and I do not believe that they give for ever’ → “and may not give, I think, to all eternity”: the governing clause becomes the parenthesis “I think”; its negation is transferred to “give”; and the subordinate subjunctive becomes “may not give”. *In sempiterno* is expanded into “to all eternity”.

> 97 L’ una è la falsa che accusò Joseppo,  
> 98 l’ altro è il falso Sinon greco da Troia;  
> 99 per febbre acuta gittan tanto leppo.”  

One is the false *wife* who accused Joseph; the other is false Sinon, the Greek from Troy; burning fever makes them reek so strongly.”

- *L’ una* ‘The one’ → “One”: the definite article is omitted.
- *la falsa* ‘the false woman’ → “the false *wife*”: the substantivized feminine adjective becomes an adjective modifying the supplied, more specific noun “wife”.
- *il falso Sinon* ‘the false Sinon’ → “false Sinon”: the definite article is omitted.
- *greco* ‘Greek’ → “the Greek”: an article is supplied, making the nationality an explicit noun phrase in apposition.
- *per febbre acuta gittan tanto leppo* ‘through acute fever they throw out so much stench’ → “burning fever makes them reek so strongly”: the causal prepositional phrase becomes the subject of a causative construction. “Burning” replaces ‘acute’ and precedes “fever”; the noun *leppo* becomes the verb “reek”; and the quantity of stench becomes the intensity expressed by “so strongly”.

> 100 E l’ un di lor, che si recò a noia  
> 101 forse d’ esser nomato sì oscuro,  
> 102 col pugno gli percosse l’ epa croia;  
> 103 quella sonò, come fosse un tamburo;  

And one of them, who took offence perhaps at being named thus darkly, smote the rigid belly of him with his fist; it sounded like a drum;

- *E l’ un di lor* ‘And the one of them’ → “And one of them”: the definite article is omitted.
- *che si recò a noia forse d’ esser nomato sì oscuro* ‘who took it as an annoyance perhaps to be named so obscurely’ → “who took offence perhaps at being named thus darkly”: the reflexive idiom becomes “took offence”; the infinitive becomes a gerund after “at”; and *sì oscuro*, used adverbially, is rendered as “thus darkly”.
- *col pugno gli percosse l’ epa croia* ‘with the fist he struck him the hard belly’ → “smote the rigid belly of him with his fist”: the instrumental phrase moves to the end, its definite article becomes “his”, and the dative *gli* becomes the possessive phrase “of him”. “Rigid” renders *croia* and precedes the noun.
- *quella sonò* ‘that sounded’ → “it sounded”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *come fosse un tamburo* ‘as if it were a drum’ → “like a drum”: the hypothetical clause is reduced to a prepositional comparison.

> 104 e mastro Adamo gli percosse il volto  
> 105 col braccio suo che non parve men duro,  

and Master Adam smote him in the face with his arm, that did not seem less hard,

- *e mastro Adamo gli percosse il volto* ‘and Master Adam struck to him the face’ → “and Master Adam smote him in the face”: the Italian dative becomes the direct object “him”, while the Italian direct object becomes “in the face”.
- *col braccio suo* ‘with the arm his’ → “with his arm”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 106 dicendo a lui: “Ancor che mi sia tolto  
> 107 lo mover, per le membra che son gravi,  
> 108 ho io il braccio a tal mestiere sciolto.”  

saying to him: “Though I am kept from moving by my weighty limbs, I have an arm free for such necessity.”

- *Ancor che mi sia tolto lo mover* ‘Although moving be taken from me’ → “Though I am kept from moving”: the person deprived of movement becomes the subject of the passive construction, and the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *per le membra che son gravi* ‘because of the limbs that are heavy’ → “by my weighty limbs”: the causal phrase is recast with “by”; the definite article becomes “my”; and the relative clause becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *ho io il braccio a tal mestiere sciolto* ‘have I the arm for such a task free’ → “I have an arm free for such necessity”: subject and verb return to ordinary English order; the definite article becomes indefinite; “free” moves before the phrase expressing purpose; and *mestiere* is interpreted as ‘need’ rather than ‘task’.

> 109 Ond’ ei rispose: “Quando tu andavi  
> 110 al foco non l’ avei tu così presto;  
> 111 ma sì e più l’ avei quando coniavi.”  

Thereat he answered: “When thou wast going to the fire, thou hadst it not so ready; but as ready, and more, when thou wast coining.”

- *Ond’ ei rispose* ‘Whence he answered’ → “Thereat he answered”: the connective is rendered as a response to the preceding words rather than literally as ‘whence’.
- *non l’ avei tu così presto* ‘not it hadst thou so ready’ → “thou hadst it not so ready”: the subject moves before the verb, and the object and negation follow it.
- *ma sì e più l’ avei* ‘but so and more thou hadst it’ → “but as ready, and more”: “ready” is supplied from the preceding clause, while *l’ avei*, ‘thou hadst it’, is left implicit.

> 112 E l’ idropico: “Tu di’ ver di questo;  
> 113 ma tu non fosti sì ver testimonio,  
> 114 là ’ve del ver a Troia fosti richiesto.”  

And he of the dropsy: “In this thou sayest true; but thou wast not so true a witness there, when questioned of the truth at Troy.”

- *E l’ idropico* ‘And the dropsical man’ → “And he of the dropsy”: the substantivized adjective becomes a pronoun with a prepositional phrase naming the condition.
- *Tu di’ ver di questo* ‘Thou sayest truth about this’ → “In this thou sayest true”: the qualifying phrase moves to the beginning and changes from ‘about’ to “In”; the noun ‘truth’ becomes adverbial “true”.
- *sì ver testimonio* ‘so true witness’ → “so true a witness”: English supplies an indefinite article before the singular predicate noun.
- *là ’ve del ver a Troia fosti richiesto* ‘there where about the truth at Troy thou wast questioned’ → “there, when questioned of the truth at Troy”: spatial ‘where’ becomes temporal “when”; the finite passive is reduced to a participial clause; and the phrases concerning truth and Troy move after “questioned”.

> 115 “S’ io dissi ’l falso, tu falsasti il conio,”  
> 116 disse Sinone, “e son qui per un fallo,  
> 117 e tu per più che alcun altro demonio.”  

“If I spoke false, thou too didst falsify the coin,” said Sinon; “and I am here for one crime, and thou for more than any other Demon.”

- *S’ io dissi ’l falso* ‘If I said the false’ → “If I spoke false”: ‘say’ becomes “spoke”, and the substantivized adjective becomes adverbial “false”.
- *tu falsasti il conio* ‘thou falsified the coinage’ → “thou too didst falsify the coin”: “too” is supplied; the simple past is expressed with “didst”; and *conio*, literally the stamp or die and hence the coinage, is rendered as “coin”.
- *un fallo* ‘one fault’ → “one crime”: the general word for a fault or transgression receives a specifically criminal rendering.

> 118 “Ricorditi, spergiuro, del cavallo,”  
> 119 rispose quel ch’ avea enfiata l’ epa;  
> 120 “e siati reo che tutto il mondo sallo.”  

“Bethink thee, perjurer, of the horse,” answered he who had the inflated paunch; “and be it a torture to thee that all the world knows thereof.”

- *quel* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *ch’ avea enfiata l’ epa* ‘who had the paunch swollen’ → “who had the inflated paunch”: the adjective describing the paunch’s condition becomes an attributive adjective within the noun phrase.
- *e siati reo* ‘and be it grievous to thee’ → “and be it a torture to thee”: the predicate adjective becomes a noun phrase, and the attached pronoun *ti* is expressed after that phrase as “to thee”.
- *che tutto il mondo sallo* ‘that all the world knows it’ → “that all the world knows thereof”: the direct-object pronoun attached to *sa* becomes “thereof”, meaning ‘of it’.

## Lines 121–148 — A Quarrel and a Rebuke

> 121 “A te sia rea la sete onde ti crepa,”  
> 122 disse il Greco, “la lingua, e l’ acqua marcia  
> 123 che il ventre innanzi gli occhi sì t’ assiepa.”  

“To thee be torture the thirst that cracks thy tongue,” replied the Greek, “and the foul water which makes that belly such a hedge before thy eyes.”

- *A te sia rea la sete* ‘To thee be harmful the thirst’ → “To thee be torture the thirst”: the adjective describing the thirst’s harmfulness becomes the predicate noun “torture”.
- *onde ti crepa ... la lingua* ‘from which thy tongue cracks’ → “that cracks thy tongue”: the relative expression of cause becomes the subject of a transitive verb; the tongue becomes its object. The tongue’s cracking is placed entirely before the reporting clause.
- *disse* ‘said’ → “replied”: the translation specifies that the speech is an answer.
- *acqua marcia* ‘water rotten’ → “foul water”: “foul” replaces ‘rotten’, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *che il ventre innanzi gli occhi sì t’assiepa* ‘which so hedges thy belly before thy eyes’ → “which makes that belly such a hedge before thy eyes”: the verb becomes the construction “makes ... a hedge”, and *sì* becomes “such”. The article before *ventre* becomes the demonstrative “that”; the phrase locating the belly follows the new predicate noun.

> 124 Allora il monetier: “Così si squarcia  
> 125 la bocca tua per mal dir come suole:  

Then the coiner : “Thus thy jaw gapes wide, as usual, to speak ill

- *Così si squarcia / la bocca tua* ‘Thus thy mouth is torn open’ → “Thus thy jaw gapes wide”: the reflexive or passive construction becomes an intransitive verb with an adverb; ‘mouth’ becomes “jaw”. The subject precedes the verb instead of following it, and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *per mal dir come suole* ‘to speak ill as it is wont’ → “as usual, to speak ill”: the finite clause becomes an adverbial phrase, which is moved before the infinitive.

> 126 chè s’ i’ ho sete ed umor mi rinfarcia,  
> 127 tu hai l’ arsura e il capo che ti duole,  
> 128 e per leccar lo specchio di Narcisso,  
> 129 non vorresti a invitar molte parole.”  

: for if I have thirst, and moisture stuffs me, thou hast the burning, and the head that pains thee; and to make thee lap the mirror of Narcissus thou wouldst not require many words of invitation.”

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the causal subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *umor mi rinfarcia* ‘moisture me stuffs’ → “moisture stuffs me”: the object pronoun follows the verb instead of preceding it.
- *che ti duole* ‘which aches for thee’ → “that pains thee”: the Italian construction with a dative sufferer becomes a transitive construction with “thee” as its object.
- *per leccar* ‘to lick’ → “to make thee lap”: “lap” replaces ‘lick’, and the causative “make thee” is supplied.
- *non vorresti* ‘thou wouldst not want’ → “thou wouldst not require”: wanting is interpreted as needing rather than desiring.
- *a invitar molte parole* ‘to invite [thee], many words’ → “many words of invitation”: the infinitive becomes a prepositional phrase containing a noun, and follows “many words” instead of preceding it.

> 130 Ad ascoltarli er’ io del tutto fisso,  
> 131 quando il maestro mi disse: “Or pur mira!  
> 132 che per poco è che teco non mi risso.”  

I was standing all intent to hear them, when the Master said to me : “Now keep looking, a little longer and I quarrel with thee!”

- *Ad ascoltarli* ‘To listen to them’ → “to hear them”: ‘listen to’ becomes “hear”, and the infinitive phrase moves from the beginning to the end of its clause.
- *er’ io del tutto fisso* ‘was I wholly fixed’ → “I was standing all intent”: “standing” is supplied, ‘fixed’ becomes “intent”, and *del tutto* is compressed into “all”. The subject precedes the verb.
- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *Or pur mira* ‘Now just look’ → “Now keep looking”: “keep” makes the continuation of the looking explicit.
- *che per poco è che teco non mi risso* ‘for by little it is that with thee I do not quarrel’ → “a little longer and I quarrel with thee”: the expression of narrowly avoiding a quarrel becomes a conditional warning about continuing longer. The causal connective and explicit negative disappear, a temporal sense is supplied by “longer”, and “with thee” moves after the verb.

> 133 Quand’ io ‘l senti’ a me parlar con ira,  
> 134 volsimi verso lui con tal vergogna,  
> 135 ch’ ancor per la memoria mi si gira.  

When I heard him speak to me in anger, I turned towards him with such shame, that it comes over me again as I but think of it.

- *a me parlar* ‘to me speak’ → “speak to me”: the infinitive precedes its indirect object.
- *con ira* ‘with anger’ → “in anger”: the preposition changes.
- *volsimi verso lui* ‘I turned myself towards him’ → “I turned towards him”: the explicit reflexive pronoun is omitted.
- *ch’ ancor per la memoria mi si gira* ‘that still through memory it turns itself within me’ → “that it comes over me again as I but think of it”: the reflexive image of turning becomes “comes over me”, and *ancor* is taken as ‘again’ rather than ‘still’. The prepositional phrase about memory becomes a finite clause, with “but” supplied.

> 136 E quale è quei che suo dannaggio sogna,  
> 137 che sognando desidera sognare,  
> 138 sì che quel ch’ è, come non fosse, agogna:  

And as one who dreams of something hurtful to him, and dreaming wishes it a dream, so that he longs for that which is, as if it were not

- *E quale è quei* ‘And as is he’ → “And as one”: the copula is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes the indefinite “one”.
- *che suo dannaggio sogna* ‘who his own harm dreams’ → “who dreams of something hurtful to him”: the object moves after the verb and is introduced by “of”. The noun ‘harm’ becomes “something hurtful”, and the possessive becomes “to him”.
- *che sognando desidera sognare* ‘who dreaming desires to dream’ → “and dreaming wishes it a dream”: the relative clause becomes coordinated with the preceding clause. The infinitive ‘to dream’ becomes the object-and-complement construction “it a dream”.
- *quel ch’è, come non fosse, agogna* ‘that which is, as if it were not, he longs for’ → “he longs for that which is, as if it were not”: the verb moves before its object and the intervening comparison.

> 139 tal mi fec’ io, non potendo parlare,  
> 140 che desiava scusarmi, e scusava  
> 141 me tuttavia, e nol mi credea fare.  

: such grew I, who, without power to speak, wished to excuse myself and all the while excused, and did not think that I was doing it.

- *tal mi fec’ io* ‘such I made myself’ → “such grew I”: the reflexive expression of becoming is rendered by the intransitive “grew”.
- *non potendo parlare, / che desiava scusarmi* ‘not being able to speak, who wished to excuse myself’ → “who, without power to speak, wished to excuse myself”: the relative pronoun moves before the intervening phrase. The negative gerund becomes a prepositional phrase, with verbal ability expressed by the noun “power”.
- *e scusava / me tuttavia* ‘and excused myself all the while’ → “and all the while excused”: the explicit reflexive object is omitted, and the adverbial expression moves before the verb.
- *e nol mi credea fare* ‘and did not believe myself to do it’ → “and did not think that I was doing it”: ‘believe’ becomes “think”, and the infinitival construction becomes a finite clause. The infinitive *fare* becomes past progressive “was doing”, with its object placed after it.

> 142 “Maggior difetto men vergogna lava,”  
> 143 disse il maestro, “che il tuo non è stato :  
> 144 però d’ ogni tristizia ti disgrava;  

“Less shame washes off a greater fault than thine has been,” said the Master : “therefore unload thee of all sorrow

- *Maggior difetto men vergogna lava* ‘A greater fault less shame washes’ → “Less shame washes off a greater fault”: the object–subject–verb order becomes subject–verb–object, and “off” is supplied.
- *che il tuo non è stato* ‘than thine has not been’ → “than thine has been”: the Italian comparative construction’s non-negative *non* is omitted.
- *d’ogni tristizia ti disgrava* ‘of every sadness unburden thyself’ → “unload thee of all sorrow”: the prepositional phrase moves after the imperative; ‘every sadness’ becomes the collective “all sorrow”, and ‘unburden’ is rendered as “unload”.

> 145 e fa ragion ch’ io ti sia sempre allato,  
> 146 se più avvien che fortuna t’ accoglia,  
> 147 ove sia gente in simigliante piato:  
> 148 chè voler ciò udire è bassa voglia.”  

; and count that I am always at thy side, should it again fall out that Fortune brings thee where people are in similar contests : for the wish to hear it is a vulgar wish.”

- *e fa ragion ch’ io ti sia sempre allato* ‘and make reckoning that I be always beside thee’ → “and count that I am always at thy side”: the verbal phrase *fa ragion* becomes the single verb “count”. The subjunctive becomes indicative “am”, and *ti ... allato* becomes the phrase “at thy side”, placed entirely after “always”.
- *se più avvien che fortuna t’accoglia* ‘if it happens again that Fortune receive thee’ → “should it again fall out that Fortune brings thee”: the conditional conjunction is replaced by inverted “should”, while ‘happens’ becomes “fall out”. ‘Receive’ becomes the more active “brings”, the subjunctive becomes indicative, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *ove sia gente in simigliante piato* ‘where there be people in a like dispute’ → “where people are in similar contests”: the existential construction becomes a clause with “people” as its subject. The singular subjunctive becomes plural indicative “are”; the collective *gente* receives plural agreement, and singular *piato* becomes plural “contests”.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the causal subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *voler ciò udire* ‘to wish this to hear’ → “the wish to hear it”: the infinitive *voler* becomes a noun with an article. The demonstrative ‘this’ becomes “it” and follows “hear” instead of preceding it.
- *bassa* ‘low’ → “vulgar”: the translation gives the adjective’s evaluative sense rather than its literal spatial meaning.

## Summary — Close Echoes and Prose Reshaping

Carlyle’s translation stays close to the Italian in its sequence of expressions, repeated conjunctions, comparisons, and much of its imagery. Phrases such as “pitiless talons”, “the mirror of Narcissus”, and “the rigid Justice” preserve distinctive wording rather than replacing it with explanation. Its archaic English also accommodates inversions and compressed expressions that echo the original. Nevertheless, objects and complements frequently move after their verbs, while adjectives move before their nouns: *l’anima antica* becomes “the ancient spirit”. Longer phrases are sometimes rearranged to bring related elements together or make the prose easier to follow.

Changes of construction often go beyond this reordering. Relative clauses become adjectives, as *che tutto ardiva* becomes “all-daring”; adjectives become phrases, as *forsennata* becomes “out of her senses”. Conversely, *voler ciò udire* becomes the noun phrase “the wish to hear it”. Italian reflexive and causative expressions are frequently recast, sometimes changing the grammatical agent: *per febbre acuta gittan tanto leppo*, ‘through acute fever they emit so much stench’, becomes “burning fever makes them reek so strongly”. Simple pasts sometimes become past perfects, and simple presents become progressives. Subjunctives receive several treatments, including English modal verbs, while conditional inversion sometimes replaces an explicit ‘if’.

Word choice ranges from close correspondence to interpretation and intensification. *Altezza*, ‘height’, becomes “pride”; *mente*, ‘mind’, becomes “soul”; and *mondiglia*, ‘dross’, becomes the less disparaging “alloy”. Elsewhere the English makes violence more explicit, as *conciando* becomes “mangling”. Supplied words can clarify an implicit reference—“image” in “the Baptist's image”, or “spirits” after “ill-born”—but can also add a detail, notably “hungry” in the comparison with the swine. Omissions commonly affect emphasis, repetition, or direction: *ben* before *tre carati*, *giuso* after *discendon*, and the second locative *là* before *dov’io falsai* receive no separate equivalents.

Alongside these individually noted departures are routine grammatical adjustments. English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and normally omits the Italian definite article before possessives. Articles also frequently become possessives, especially with body parts and personal relationships: *la moglie* becomes “his wife”, and *le labbra* becomes “his lips”. Such changes make the prose recognizably English without greatly altering its sense. Overall, the translation combines unusually close verbal echoes with recurrent grammatical reshaping and occasional explanatory or interpretative additions.
