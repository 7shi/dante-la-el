# Inferno 12

## Lines 1–18 — The Descent and the Minotaur

> 1 Era lo loco, ove a scender la riva  
> 2 venimmo, alpestro, e per quel ch’ ivi er’ anco,  
> 3 tal, ch’ ogni vista ne sarebbe schiva.  

The place to which we came, in order to descend the bank, was alpine, and such, from what was there besides, that every eye would shun it.

- *Era lo loco* ‘was the place’ → “The place”: the subject comes first; “was” is postponed until after the relative clause.
- *ove* ‘where’ → “to which”: the relative adverb becomes a preposition and relative pronoun expressing destination.
- *a scender la riva venimmo* ‘to descend the bank we came’ → “we came, in order to descend the bank”: the main verb precedes the infinitive phrase, and “in order” makes its purpose explicit.
- *alpestro* ‘rugged’ → “alpine”: the translation uses an adjective suggesting mountainous terrain rather than directly describing its ruggedness.
- *per quel ch’ ivi er’ anco, tal* ‘because of that which there was besides, such’ → “such, from what was there besides”: “such” precedes the causal phrase; *per* becomes “from,” *quel ch’* is compressed into “what,” and “there” follows the verb.
- *ogni vista* ‘every sight’ → “every eye”: the faculty of seeing is replaced by its organ.
- *ne sarebbe schiva* ‘would be averse to it’ → “would shun it”: an adjective with a complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object.

> 4 Qual è quella ruina, che nel fianco  
> 5 di qua da Trento l’ Adice percosse  
> 6 o per tremuoto o per sostegno manco,—  

As is the ruin, which struck the Adige in its flank, on this side Trent, caused by earthquake or by defective prop,—

- *quella* ‘that’ → “the”: the demonstrative becomes a definite article.
- *che nel fianco … l’ Adice percosse* ‘which in the flank … the Adige struck’ → “which struck the Adige in its flank”: the verb precedes its object, the phrase describing the location of the blow follows it, and the possessive “its” replaces the article.
- *di qua da Trento* ‘on this side of Trent’ → “on this side Trent”: ‘of’ is omitted, and the geographical phrase is moved after the completed clause about the blow.
- *o per tremuoto o per sostegno manco* ‘either through earthquake or through support lacking’ → “caused by earthquake or by defective prop”: “caused” is supplied, the first *o* is omitted, and the causal prepositions become “by.” “Prop” makes *sostegno* more concrete, while *manco* becomes the preceding adjective “defective.”

> 7 chè da cima del monte, onde si mosse,  
> 8 al piano è sì la roccia discoscesa  
> 9 ch’ alcuna via darebbe a chi su fosse:  

for from the summit of the mountain, whence it moved, to the plain, the rock is shattered so, that it might give some passage to one that were above

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *da cima* ‘from summit’ → “from the summit”: English supplies the definite article.
- *si mosse* ‘moved itself’ → “it moved”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *è sì la roccia discoscesa* ‘is so the rock precipitous’ → “the rock is shattered so”: the subject precedes the verb and “so” follows the predicate. “Shattered” emphasizes brokenness rather than steepness.
- *alcuna via darebbe* ‘some way it would give’ → “it might give some passage”: the object follows the verb; “passage” replaces ‘way,’ and the conditional is expressed with “might” rather than ‘would.’
- *a chi su fosse* ‘to whoever above were’ → “to one that were above”: the indefinite relative becomes an antecedent followed by a relative clause, and the verb precedes “above.”

> 10 cotal di quel burrato era la scesa;  

: such of that rocky steep was the descent;

- *burrato* ‘precipice’ → “rocky steep”: a single noun becomes an adjective and a substantival description of steep terrain.

> 11 e in su la punta della rotta lacca  
> 12 l’ infamia di Creti era distesa,  
> 13 che fu concetta nella falsa vacca;  

and on the top of the broken cleft lay spread the infamy of Crete, which was conceived in the false cow;

- *punta* ‘point’ → “top”: the translation identifies the uppermost position rather than retaining the image of a point.
- *lacca* ‘hollow’ → “cleft”: the translation chooses a term suggesting a split or fissure.
- *l’ infamia di Creti era distesa* ‘the infamy of Crete was stretched out’ → “lay spread the infamy of Crete”: the predicate precedes the subject, and “lay” replaces “was,” making the posture explicit.

> 14 e quando vide noi sè stesso morse,  
> 15 sì come quei, cui l’ ira dentro fiacca.  

and when he saw us he gnawed himself, like one whom anger inwardly consumes.

- *sè stesso morse* ‘himself he bit’ → “he gnawed himself”: the object follows the verb, and “gnawed” suggests sustained or repeated biting.
- *sì come quei* ‘just as that one’ → “like one”: “like” replaces the comparative expression, without separately rendering *sì*, and “one” loses the demonstrative force of *quei*.
- *cui l’ ira dentro fiacca* ‘whom the anger within weakens’ → “whom anger inwardly consumes”: the article before the abstract noun is omitted, “inwardly” renders the internal location adverbially, and “consumes” replaces the image of weakening or breaking down.

> 16 Lo savio mio inver lui gridò : “Forse  
> 17 tu credi che qui sia il duca d’ Atene,  
> 18 che su nel mondo la morte ti porse?  

My Sage cried towards him : "Perhaps thou thinkest the Duke of Athens may be here, who, in the world above, gave thee thy death ?

- *Lo savio mio* ‘the wise one mine’ → “My Sage”: the substantival adjective becomes the noun “Sage,” and the possessive precedes it.
- *inver lui gridò* ‘towards him cried’ → “cried towards him”: the verb precedes the directional phrase.
- *che qui sia il duca d’ Atene* ‘that here be the duke of Athens’ → “the Duke of Athens may be here”: the introductory conjunction is omitted, the subject precedes the verb, and “here” follows it. The Italian subjunctive is rendered with the modal “may,” making possibility explicit.
- *che su nel mondo* ‘who above in the world’ → “who, in the world above,”: “above” is moved after “the world.”
- *la morte ti porse* ‘the death to thee proffered’ → “gave thee thy death”: the verb comes first, followed by the recipient and direct object; “gave” replaces ‘proffered,’ and the definite article becomes the possessive “thy.”

## Lines 19–51 — The Descent to the River of Blood

> 19 Partiti, bestia, chè questi non viene  
> 20 ammaestrato dalla tua sorella,  
> 21 ma vassi per veder le vostre pene.”  

Get thee gone, Monster! for this one comes not, instructed by thy sister; but passes on to see your punishments.”

- *Partiti* ‘Depart’ → “Get thee gone”: the imperative of a pronominal verb becomes an English idiomatic command.
- *bestia* ‘beast’ → “Monster”: a different noun is chosen.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *non viene* ‘not comes’ → “comes not”: the negative follows the verb.
- *vassi* ‘goes his way’ → “passes on”: the pronominal verb becomes a verb with a directional adverb.
- *pene* ‘pains’ → “punishments”: the translation makes the punitive sense explicit.

> 22 Qual è quel toro che si slaccia in quella  
> 23 che ha ricevuto già ’l colpo mortale,  
> 24 che gir non sa, ma qua e là saltella:  

As a bull, that breaks loose, in the moment when he has received the fatal stroke, and cannot go, but plunges hither and thither:

- *Qual è quel toro* ‘As is that bull’ → “As a bull”: the verb is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes an indefinite article.
- *si slaccia* ‘unbinds itself’ → “breaks loose”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive English expression.
- *in quella che* ‘at that [moment] when’ → “in the moment when”: the elliptical expression is expanded with “moment”, and the demonstrative becomes a definite article.
- *ha ricevuto già* ‘has already received’ → “has received”: *già* is omitted.
- *’l colpo mortale* ‘the stroke mortal’ → “the fatal stroke”: the adjective precedes the noun, and ‘mortal’ is rendered as “fatal”.
- *che gir non sa* ‘which does not know how to go’ → “and cannot go”: the relative clause becomes a coordinated clause; ‘knowing how’ becomes ability, and the fronted infinitive follows the modal verb.
- *qua e là saltella* ‘here and there skips’ → “plunges hither and thither”: “plunges” gives a more forceful movement; the adverbs become directional and follow the verb.

> 25 vid’ io lo Minotauro far cotale.  

so I saw the Minotaur do.

- *vid’ io lo Minotauro far cotale* ‘saw I the Minotaur do thus’ → “so I saw the Minotaur do.”: subject–verb order is reversed, and the expression of manner moves from the end to the beginning.

> 26 E quegli accorto gridò: “Corri al varco;  
> 27 mentre ch’ è in furia, è buon che tu ti cale.”  

And my wary Guide cried: “Run to the passage; whilst he is in fury, it is good that thou descend.”

- *quegli accorto* ‘that discerning one’ → “my wary Guide”: the demonstrative pronoun is replaced by the supplied noun “Guide” and possessive “my”; the adjective becomes an attributive modifier of that noun.
- *ti cale* ‘lower thyself’ → “descend”: the reflexive construction becomes a simple intransitive verb.

> 28 Così prendemmo via giù per lo scarco  
> 29 di quelle pietre, che spesso moviensi  
> 30 sotto i miei piedi per lo nuovo carco.  

Thus we took our way downwards on the ruin of those stones, which often moved beneath my feet, from the unusual weight.

- *prendemmo via* ‘took way’ → “we took our way”: the possessive “our” is supplied to complete the English expression.
- *per lo scarco* ‘through the heap’ → “on the ruin”: passage through or over a heap becomes movement on its surface; “ruin” replaces the noun for the accumulated debris.
- *che spesso moviensi* ‘which often moved themselves’ → “which often moved”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes an ordinary English intransitive verb.
- *per lo nuovo carco* ‘because of the new load’ → “from the unusual weight”: the causal preposition becomes “from”; *nuovo* is interpreted as unfamiliar or unusual, and ‘load’ becomes “weight”.

> 31 Io gìa pensando; e quei disse: “Tu pensi  
> 32 forse a questa rovina, ch’ è guardata  
> 33 da quell’ ira bestial ch’ io ora spensi.  

I went musing, and he said: “Perhaps thou art thinking of this fallen mass, guarded by that bestial rage, which I quelled just now.

- *pensando* ‘thinking’ → “musing”: a more specifically reflective activity is expressed.
- *quei* ‘that one’ → “he”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *Tu pensi forse* ‘Thou thinkest perhaps’ → “Perhaps thou art thinking”: the adverb moves to the beginning, and the simple present becomes progressive.
- *a questa rovina* ‘to this ruin’ → “of this fallen mass”: the preposition is adapted to English ‘thinking of’, and the noun is expanded into a phrase describing the fallen material.
- *ch’ è guardata* ‘which is guarded’ → “guarded”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial modifier.
- *quell’ ira bestial* ‘that rage bestial’ → “that bestial rage”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *ch’ io ora spensi* ‘which I now extinguished’ → “which I quelled just now”: the metaphor of extinguishing becomes “quelled”, and *ora* is interpreted as the immediately preceding moment.

> 34 Or vo’ che sappi, che l’ altra fiata  
> 35 ch’ i’ discesi quaggiù nel basso inferno,  
> 36 questa roccia non era ancor cascata.  

I would have thee know, that, when I went the other time, down here to the deep Hell, this rock had not yet fallen.

- *Or vo’ che sappi* ‘Now I want that thou know’ → “I would have thee know”: *Or* is omitted; direct volition becomes the idiom “would have”, and the finite subordinate clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction.
- *l’ altra fiata ch’ i’ discesi* ‘the other time that I descended’ → “when I went the other time”: the temporal noun phrase and its relative clause are recast as a clause beginning with “when”; the time phrase follows the verb, and ‘descended’ becomes the less specific “went”.
- *quaggiù nel basso inferno* ‘down here in the low Hell’ → “down here to the deep Hell”: “to” expresses destination rather than location, and ‘low’ becomes “deep”.
- *non era ancor cascata* ‘was not yet fallen’ → “had not yet fallen”: the Italian pluperfect with ‘be’ becomes the English pluperfect with “had”; the negative follows the auxiliary.

> 37 Ma certo poco pria, se ben discerno,  
> 38 che venisse Colui che la gran preda  
> 39 levò a Dite del cerchio superno,  

But certainly, if I distinguish rightly, short while before He came, who took from Dis the great prey of the upmost circle,

- *poco pria, se ben discerno, che venisse Colui* ‘a little before, if I discern well, that that one came’ → “if I distinguish rightly, short while before He came”: the conditional qualification moves before the time expression; *poco* is expanded into “short while”; the temporal construction with *che* becomes English ‘before’ with a finite clause; the imperfect subjunctive becomes indicative, and the demonstrative becomes “He”.
- *che la gran preda levò a Dite* ‘who the great prey took away from Dis’ → “who took from Dis the great prey”: the verb and the phrase “from Dis” move before the direct object; the removal expressed by *levò* is conveyed by “took” without a separate equivalent of ‘away’.
- *del cerchio superno* ‘from the circle uppermost’ → “of the upmost circle”: the translation reads the phrase as qualifying “prey”, rather than expressing the source of removal; the adjective moves before the noun.

> 40 da tutte parti l’ alta valle feda  
> 41 tremò sì, ch’ io pensai che l’ universo  
> 42 sentisse amor, per lo quale è chi creda  
> 43 più volte il mondo in caos converso;  

on all sides the deep loathsome valley trembled so, that I thought the universe felt love, whereby, as some believe, the world has oft-times been converted into chaos;

- *da tutte parti* ‘from all sides’ → “on all sides”: a preposition of source becomes one of location.
- *valle feda* ‘valley foul’ → “loathsome valley”: the adjective precedes the noun, and ‘foul’ becomes “loathsome”.
- *che l’ universo sentisse amor* ‘that the universe felt love’ → “the universe felt love”: the subordinating conjunction is omitted, and the imperfect subjunctive becomes an English indicative past.
- *per lo quale è chi creda* ‘through which there is someone who believes’ → “whereby, as some believe,”: the prepositional relative phrase becomes “whereby”; the existential construction becomes a parenthetical clause with “as”, and indefinite singular reference becomes plural “some”. The subjunctive becomes indicative.
- *più volte il mondo in caos converso* ‘many times the world into chaos converted’ → “the world has oft-times been converted into chaos”: the elliptical participial construction becomes a finite present-perfect passive with “has” and “been”; the frequency expression moves after the auxiliary, and the phrase expressing the resulting state follows the participle.

> 44 ed in quel punto questa vecchia roccia  
> 45 qui ed altrove tal fece riverso.  

and in that moment, here, and elsewhere, this ancient rock made such downfall.

- *in quel punto* ‘at that point’ → “in that moment”: the temporal sense is made explicit with “moment”.
- *questa vecchia roccia qui ed altrove* ‘this old rock here and elsewhere’ → “here, and elsewhere, this ancient rock”: the phrase of location moves from after the subject to before it; “ancient” gives a stronger expression of age.
- *tal fece riverso* ‘such made a collapse’ → “made such downfall”: *tal*, separated from its noun by the verb, is placed immediately before “downfall”; ‘collapse’ is rendered as “downfall”.

> 46 Ma ficca gli occhi a valle: chè s’ approccia  
> 47 la riviera del sangue, in la qual bolle  
> 48 qual che per violenza in altrui noccia.”  

But fix thy eyes upon the valley: for the river of blood draws nigh, in which boils every one who by violence injures others.”

- *ficca gli occhi a valle* ‘plant the eyes downwards’ → “fix thy eyes upon the valley”: the forceful *ficca* becomes “fix”, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the directional expression *a valle* is rendered as a prepositional phrase naming the object of sight.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *s’ approccia la riviera del sangue* ‘draws near the river of the blood’ → “the river of blood draws nigh”: the subject moves before the verb, the pronominal verb becomes a non-pronominal English expression, and the article before ‘blood’ is omitted.
- *qual che* ‘whoever’ → “every one who”: the indefinite relative is expanded into a universal expression followed by a relative pronoun.
- *in altrui noccia* ‘to others does harm’ → “injures others”: the verb and its prepositional complement become a transitive verb with a direct object; the object follows the verb, and the subjunctive becomes indicative.

> 49 O cieca cupidigia, e ria e folle,  
> 50 che sì ci sproni nella vita corta,  
> 51 e nell’ eterna poi sì mal c’ immolle!  

O blind cupidity [both wicked and foolish], which so incites us in the short life, and then, in the eternal, steeps us so bitterly!

- *e ria e folle* ‘and wicked and foolish’ → “[both wicked and foolish]”: the repeated conjunction becomes the English correlative construction ‘both … and’.
- *che sì ci sproni* ‘which so us spurs’ → “which so incites us”: the metaphorical ‘spurs’ becomes “incites”, and the object pronoun follows the verb. Italian second-person address becomes third-person agreement with “which”.
- *nella vita corta* ‘in the life short’ → “in the short life”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *e nell’ eterna poi* ‘and in the eternal then’ → “and then, in the eternal,”: the adverb moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *sì mal c’ immolle* ‘so badly us soaks’ → “steeps us so bitterly!”: ‘badly’ becomes “bitterly”, and ‘soaks’ becomes “steeps”; the object pronoun follows the verb, and the adverbial phrase moves to the end. The second-person verb becomes third-person English agreement.

## Lines 52–84 — The Centaurs Challenge the Travellers

> 52 Io vidi un’ ampia fossa in arco torta,  
> 53 come quella che tutto il piano abbraccia,  
> 54 secondo ch’ avea detto la mia scorta;  

I saw a wide fosse bent arcwise, as embracing all the plain, according to what my Guide had told me;

- *in arco torta* ‘into an arc bent’ → “bent arcwise”: the participle comes first, and the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *come quella che tutto il piano abbraccia* ‘like that which all the plain embraces’ → “as embracing all the plain”: the demonstrative and relative clause become a participial construction; the object follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *secondo ch’* ‘according as’ → “according to what”: the conjunction becomes a preposition governing a relative clause.
- *avea detto la mia scorta* ‘had said my escort’ → “my Guide had told me”: the subject moves before the verb, *scorta* becomes “Guide,” and “told me” supplies a recipient not expressed in the Italian.

> 55 e tra il piè della ripa ed essa, in traccia  
> 56 correan Centauri armati di saette,  
> 57 come solean nel mondo andare a caccia.  

and between it and the foot of the bank were Centaurs, running one behind the other, armed with arrows, as they were wont on earth to go in hunting.

- *tra il piè della ripa ed essa* ‘between the foot of the bank and it’ → “between it and the foot of the bank”: the two terms are reversed.
- *in traccia* ‘in a trail’ → “one behind the other”: the phrase is expanded to express successive movement in a line.
- *correan Centauri* ‘ran Centaurs’ → “were Centaurs, running”: the finite verb of movement becomes a participle attached to an existential construction.
- *nel mondo* ‘in the world’ → “on earth”: both the noun and the preposition change.
- *andare a caccia* ‘to go to the hunt’ → “to go in hunting”: the Italian noun phrase becomes an English construction with “in” and a gerund.

> 58 Vedendoci calar ciascun ristette,  
> 59 e della schiera tre si dipartiro  
> 60 con archi ed asticciuole prima elette.  

Perceiving us descend, they all stood still; and from the band three came forth with bows and javelins chosen first.

- *ciascun ristette* ‘each stopped’ → “they all stood still”: a distributive singular becomes a collective plural, and stopping is expressed as standing still.
- *si dipartiro* ‘departed’ → “came forth”: departure is rendered as emergence towards the speakers.
- *asticciuole* ‘little shafts’ → “javelins”: the diminutive is not expressed, and the shafts are interpreted as javelins rather than arrows.
- *prima elette* ‘first chosen’ → “chosen first”: the adverb follows rather than precedes the participle.

> 61 E l’ un gridò da lungi: “A qual martiro  
> 62 venite voi, che scendete la costa?  
> 63 Ditel costinci, se non, l’ arco tiro.”  

And one of them cried from far: “To what torment come ye, ye that descend the coast? Tell from thence; if not, I draw the bow.”

- *l’ un* ‘the one’ → “one of them”: the definite article disappears, and the partitive “of them” is supplied.
- *che* ‘who’ → “ye that”: a repeated personal pronoun is supplied before the relative.
- *costa* ‘slope’ → “coast”: the translation uses “coast” in its older sense of a hillside.
- *Ditel costinci* ‘Say it from there where you are’ → “Tell from thence”: the object pronoun *-l* is omitted, ‘Say’ becomes “Tell,” and the indication that the place is near the person addressed is not separately expressed.
- *l’ arco tiro* ‘the bow I draw’ → “I draw the bow”: the object moves after the verb.

> 64 Lo mio maestro disse: “La risposta  
> 65 farem noi a Chiron costà di presso;  
> 66 mal fu la voglia tua sempre sì tosta.”  

My Master said: “Our answer we will make to Chiron, there near at hand; unhappily thy will was always thus rash.”

- *La risposta farem noi* ‘The answer shall make we’ → “Our answer we will make”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the subject moves before the verb while the object remains first.
- *costà di presso* ‘there close by you’ → “there near at hand”: the indication of proximity to the person addressed is rendered by the more general expression “near at hand.”
- *mal* ‘ill’ → “unhappily”: the adverb is rendered as an explicit evaluation of the circumstance.
- *fu la voglia tua* ‘was the desire yours’ → “thy will was”: the subject moves before the verb, the possessive before its noun, and *voglia* is rendered as “will.”
- *sì tosta* ‘so hasty’ → “thus rash”: degree becomes manner, and hastiness is expressed as rashness.

> 67 Poi mi tentò e disse: “Quegli è Nesso,  
> 68 che morì per la bella Deianira,  
> 69 e fe’ di sè la vendetta egli stesso;  

Then he touched me and said: “That is Nessus, who died for the fair Dejanira, and of himself took vengeance for himself;

- *mi tentò* ‘me he touched’ → “he touched me”: the object pronoun follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *e fe’ di sè la vendetta egli stesso* ‘and made for himself the vengeance he himself’ → “and of himself took vengeance for himself”: *fare vendetta* becomes “took vengeance”; the emphatic subject *egli stesso* becomes the adverbial “of himself,” placed before the verb; and *di sè* moves to the end as “for himself.”

> 70 e quel di mezzo, che al petto si mira,  
> 71 è il gran Chirone, il qual nudrì Achille;  

he in the middle, who is looking down upon his breast, is the great Chiron, he who nursed Achilles;

- *e quel di mezzo* ‘and that one in the middle’ → “he in the middle”: the conjunction is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *che al petto si mira* ‘who at the breast looks at himself’ → “who is looking down upon his breast”: the reflexive construction becomes one with a possessive; the prepositional phrase follows the verb; the present becomes progressive; and “down” is supplied.
- *il qual* ‘who’ → “he who”: a personal pronoun is added before the relative.
- *nudrì* ‘nourished’ → “nursed”: the verb is rendered by a term emphasizing care and upbringing.

> 72 quell’ altro è Folo, che fu sì pien d’ ira.  

that other is Pholus, who was so full of rage.

- Literal.

> 73 D’ intorno al fosso vanno a mille a mille,  
> 74 saettando quale anima si svelle  
> 75 del sangue più che sua colpa sortille.”  

Around the fosse they go by thousands, piercing with their arrows whatever spirit wrenches itself out of the blood farther than its guilt has allotted for it.”

- *a mille a mille* ‘a thousand at a time’ → “by thousands”: the repeated distributive numeral becomes a plural expression.
- *saettando* ‘shooting arrows at’ → “piercing with their arrows”: the verb is expanded into a verb and instrumental phrase, “their” is supplied, and piercing replaces the action of shooting.
- *anima* ‘soul’ → “spirit”: a different designation is used.
- *più che* ‘more than’ → “farther than”: the general comparison is made explicitly spatial.
- *sortille* ‘allotted to it’ → “has allotted for it”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, and the enclitic dative is rendered with “for” rather than ‘to.’

> 76 Noi ci appressammo a quelle fiere snelle;  

We drew near those rapid beasts;

- *ci appressammo a* ‘we brought ourselves near to’ → “drew near”: the pronominal construction becomes an intransitive English idiom, with no separate reflexive pronoun or equivalent of *a*.
- *fiere snelle* ‘beasts nimble’ → “rapid beasts”: the adjective moves before the noun, and nimbleness is rendered as rapidity.

> 77 Chiron prese uno strale, e con la cocca  
> 78 fece la barba indietro alle mascelle.  

Chiron took an arrow, and with the notch put back his beard upon his jaws.

- *fece la barba indietro alle mascelle* ‘made the beard back to the jaws’ → “put back his beard upon his jaws”: *fece … indietro* becomes “put back,” with the adverb moved before the object; both definite articles become possessives; and movement towards the jaws is rendered as placement upon them.

> 79 Quando s’ ebbe scoperta la gran bocca,  
> 80 disse ai compagni: “Siete voi accorti,  
> 81 che quel di retro muove ciò ch’ ei tocca?  

When he had uncovered his great mouth, he said to his companions: “Have ye perceived that the one behind moves what he touches?

- *s’ ebbe scoperta la gran bocca* ‘had uncovered for himself the great mouth’ → “he had uncovered his great mouth”: the reflexive pronoun and definite article are represented by the possessive “his.”
- *ai compagni* ‘to the companions’ → “to his companions”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Siete voi accorti* ‘Are ye aware’ → “Have ye perceived”: the construction with *essere* and *accorti* is rendered as an English present perfect expressing perception.
- *quel di retro* ‘that one behind’ → “the one behind”: the demonstrative becomes a definite article with “one.”
- *ciò ch’* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun become a fused relative.

> 82 Così non soglion fare i piè de’ morti.”  

The feet of the dead are not wont to do so.”

- *Così non soglion fare i piè de’ morti* ‘So are not wont to do the feet of the dead’ → “The feet of the dead are not wont to do so”: the subject moves from the end to the beginning, and *Così* moves from the beginning to the end.

> 83 E il mio buon duca, che già gli era al petto  
> 84 dove le duo nature son consorti,  

And my good Guide, who was already at the breast of him, where the two natures are consorted,

- *duca* ‘leader’ → “Guide”: leadership is rendered as guidance.
- *che già gli era al petto* ‘who already to him was at the breast’ → “who was already at the breast of him”: the dative *gli* becomes the postnominal possessive phrase “of him,” and “already” follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *son consorti* ‘are companions’ → “are consorted”: the Italian expression of partnership becomes an English participial construction expressing conjunction.

## Lines 85–117 — A Guide Along the Boiling Blood

> 85 rispose: “Ben è vivo, e sì soletto  
> 86 mostrarli mi convien la valle buia;  

replied: “Indeed he is alive, and solitary thus have I to shew him the dark valley;

- *sì soletto* ‘thus all alone’ → “solitary thus”: the order is reversed, and the emphasis of the diminutive *soletto* is not separately expressed.
- *mostrarli mi convien* ‘to show him is incumbent on me’ → “have I to shew him”: the impersonal construction expressing obligation becomes a personal construction with “have”; the object pronoun follows the infinitive.
- *la valle buia* ‘the valley dark’ → “the dark valley”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 87 necessità 'l c' induce, e non diletto.  

necessity brings him to it, and not sport.

- *'l c' induce* ‘him there leads’ → “brings him to it”: the object follows the verb, and the locative *c’* becomes “to it”; leading is expressed as bringing.
- *diletto* ‘pleasure’ → “sport”: pleasure is rendered as recreation or amusement.

> 88 Tal si partì da cantare alleluia,  
> 89 che mi commise quest' uficio nuovo;  

From singing Alleluiah, came She who gave me this new office;

- *Tal si partì da cantare alleluia* ‘Such a one departed from singing alleluia’ → “From singing Alleluiah, came She”: the phrase beginning with *da* moves to the front; departure becomes arrival, and the indefinite *Tal* becomes the explicitly feminine “She”.
- *mi commise* ‘to me entrusted’ → “gave me”: entrusting becomes giving, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *quest' uficio nuovo* ‘this office new’ → “this new office”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 90 non è ladron, nè io anima fuia.  

he is no robber, nor I a thievish spirit.

- *non è ladron* ‘is not a robber’ → “he is no robber”: verbal negation becomes the negative determiner “no”.
- *anima fuia* ‘soul thievish’ → “a thievish spirit”: “spirit” replaces ‘soul’, an indefinite article is supplied, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 91 Ma per quella virtù, per cui io muovo  
> 92 li passi miei per sì selvaggia strada,  
> 93 danne un de' tuoi, a cui noi siamo a pruovo,  

But by that virtue through which I move my steps on such a wild way, give us some one of thine whom we may follow,

- *li passi miei* ‘the steps my’ → “my steps”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *per sì selvaggia strada* ‘through so wild a way’ → “on such a wild way”: passage “through” becomes movement “on”, and *sì* is expressed through the construction “such a”.
- *un de' tuoi* ‘one of yours’ → “some one of thine”: “some” is supplied to emphasize the indefinite selection.
- *a cui noi siamo a pruovo* ‘to whom we may be close behind’ → “whom we may follow”: being close behind becomes the active verb “follow”; the prepositional relative becomes its direct object.

> 94 che ne dimostri là ove si guada,  
> 95 e che porti costui in su la groppa:  
> 96 chè non è spirto che per l' aer vada.”  

that he may shew us where the ford is, and carry over him upon his back, for he is not a spirit to go through the air.”

- *che ne dimostri* ‘who may show us’ → “that he may shew us”: the relative construction becomes an explicit purpose clause, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *là ove si guada* ‘there where one fords’ → “where the ford is”: *là* is not separately expressed, and the impersonal verb becomes a noun with “is”.
- *e che porti costui* ‘and who may carry this man’ → “and carry over him”: the repeated relative construction is omitted, with “he may” understood from the preceding clause; “over” is supplied, and the demonstrative *costui* becomes the personal pronoun “him”.
- *in su la groppa* ‘upon the rump’ → “upon his back”: the less specific “back” replaces ‘rump’, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *spirto* ‘spirit’ → “a spirit”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *che per l' aer vada* ‘who through the air may go’ → “to go through the air”: the relative clause with a subjunctive verb becomes an infinitive phrase, and the phrase describing movement follows the verb.

> 97 Chiron si volse in sulla destra poppa,  

Chiron bent round on his right breast,

- *si volse* ‘turned himself’ → “bent round”: reflexive turning becomes bending, with “round” expressing the change of direction.
- *in sulla destra poppa* ‘on the right breast’ → “on his right breast”: a possessive replaces the definite article.

> 98 e disse a Nesso: “Torna, e sì li guida,  
> 99 e fa cansar, s' altra schiera v' intoppa.”  

and said to Nessus: “Turn, and guide them then; and if another troop encounter you, keep it off.”

- *Torna* ‘Turn back’ → “Turn”: the sense of returning is not separately expressed.
- *e sì li guida* ‘and thus them guide’ → “and guide them then”: *sì* becomes “then”; both the object pronoun and the adverb follow the verb.
- *fa cansar* ‘make draw aside’ → “keep it off”: the causative construction becomes a command to hold something away, and “it” supplies an explicit object.
- *s' altra schiera v' intoppa* ‘if another troop you encounters’ → “if another troop encounter you”: the conditional clause precedes rather than follows the command; the object pronoun follows the verb, and the Italian indicative becomes an English subjunctive.

> 100 Noi ci movemmo con la scorta fida  
> 101 lungo la proda del bollor vermiglio,  
> 102 ove i bolliti facean alte strida.  

We moved onwards with our trusty guide, along the border of the purple boiling, wherein the boiled were making loud shrieks.

- *Noi ci movemmo* ‘We moved ourselves’ → “We moved onwards”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, and “onwards” is supplied.
- *con la scorta fida* ‘with the escort trusty’ → “with our trusty guide”: “guide” replaces ‘escort’, a possessive replaces the definite article, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *del bollor vermiglio* ‘of the boiling vermilion’ → “of the purple boiling”: the colour changes from vermilion to purple, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *ove* ‘where’ → “wherein”: the relation of being within is made explicit.
- *facean alte strida* ‘made high shrieks’ → “were making loud shrieks”: the imperfect becomes an English past progressive, and *alte* is interpreted as loudness rather than height.

> 103 Io vidi gente sotto infino al ciglio;  

I saw people down in it even to the eyebrows;

- *sotto* ‘below’ → “down in it”: the translation supplies an explicit reference to what contains the people.
- *infino al ciglio* ‘as far as the eyebrow’ → “even to the eyebrows”: the emphatic limit is expressed by “even to”, and the singular becomes plural.

> 104 e il gran Centauro disse: “Ei son tiranni  
> 105 che dier nel sangue e nell' aver di piglio.  

and the great Centaur said: “These are tyrants who took to blood and plunder.

- *Ei* ‘They’ → “These”: a personal pronoun becomes a demonstrative.
- *dier nel sangue e nell' aver di piglio* ‘laid hold on blood and on possessions’ → “took to blood and plunder”: the idiom of seizing becomes “took to”; *aver*, possessions or wealth, becomes the activity “plunder”. The repeated preposition is expressed only once, and the definite articles are omitted.

> 106 Quivi si piangon li spietati danni;  

Here they lament their merciless offences;

- *Quivi* ‘There’ → “Here”: the spatial reference changes from more distant to immediate.
- *si piangon li spietati danni* ‘the merciless harms are lamented’ → “they lament their merciless offences”: the passive construction becomes active, with the lamenting persons as subject; a possessive replaces the definite article, and the harms done become “offences”.

> 107 quivi è Alessandro e Dionisio fero  
> 108 che fe' Cicilia aver dolorosi anni;  

here is Alexander; and fierce Dionysius, who made Sicily have years of woe;

- *quivi* ‘there’ → “here”: the spatial reference changes from more distant to immediate.
- *Dionisio fero* ‘Dionysius fierce’ → “fierce Dionysius”: the adjective precedes the name.
- *dolorosi anni* ‘sorrowful years’ → “years of woe”: the adjective becomes a noun in a following prepositional phrase.

> 109 e quella fronte ch' ha il pel così nero  
> 110 è Azzolino; e quell' altro ch' è biondo  
> 111 è Opizzo da Esti, il qual per vero  
> 112 fu spento dal figliastro su nel mondo.”  

and that brow which has the hair so black is Azzolino; and that other, who is blonde, is Obizzo of Este, who in verity was quenched by his step-son up in the world.”

- *da Esti* ‘from Este’ → “of Este”: the preposition of origin becomes one expressing affiliation.
- *il qual* ‘the which’ → “who”: the article-bearing relative expression becomes a simple personal relative pronoun.
- *per vero* ‘for truth’ → “in verity”: the adverbial expression is recast with “in”.
- *dal figliastro* ‘by the stepson’ → “by his step-son”: a possessive replaces the definite article.

> 113 Allor mi volsi al poeta, e quei disse:  
> 114 “Questi ti sia or primo, ed io secondo.”  

Then I turned me to the Poet, and he said: “Let him be chief guide to thee now, and me second.”

- *quei* ‘that one’ → “he”: a demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *Questi ti sia or primo* ‘This one to thee be now first’ → “Let him be chief guide to thee now”: the demonstrative becomes “him”; the jussive subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”; ‘first’ is expanded to “chief guide”. The pronoun *ti* becomes a following prepositional phrase, and “now” moves to the end.
- *ed io secondo* ‘and I second’ → “and me second”: the nominative becomes an object form, continuing the construction with “Let”.

> 115 Poco più oltre il Centauro s' affisse  
> 116 sopra una gente, che infino alla gola  
> 117 parea che di quel bulicame uscisse.  

A little farther on, the Centaur paused beside a people which, as far as the throat, seemed to issue from that boiling stream.

- *Poco più oltre* ‘Little farther beyond’ → “A little farther on”: “A” is supplied, and *oltre* is expressed by the directional “on”.
- *s' affisse* ‘fixed himself’ → “paused”: the reflexive expression becomes a simple intransitive verb denoting a temporary stop.
- *sopra una gente* ‘over a people’ → “beside a people”: the spatial relation is interpreted as proximity rather than position above.
- *che infino alla gola parea che di quel bulicame uscisse* ‘which, as far as the throat, it seemed that it emerged from that boiling pool’ → “which, as far as the throat, seemed to issue from that boiling stream”: the impersonal *parea che* construction, with a finite subjunctive, becomes a personal construction with “seemed” and an infinitive. *Bulicame*, a boiling pool or spring, becomes “boiling stream”.

## Lines 118–139 — The Shallows and the Ford

> 118 Mostrocci un' ombra dall' un canto sola,  

He shewed us a spirit by itself apart,

- *un’ ombra* ‘a shade’ → “a spirit”: the figurative designation is replaced by its referent.
- *dall’ un canto sola* ‘on one side alone’ → “by itself apart”: the spatial phrase and the adjective are recast as two adverbial expressions, with isolation expressed first.

> 119 dicendo: “Colui fesse in grembo a Dio  
> 120 lo cor che in sul Tamigi ancor si cola.”  

saying: “That one, in God’s bosom, pierced the heart which still is venerated on the Thames.”

- *fesse* ‘cleft’ → “pierced”: splitting is rendered as penetration.
- *in grembo a Dio* ‘in the lap of God’ → “in God’s bosom”: “bosom” replaces ‘lap’, the possessive construction changes, and the phrase moves before the verb.
- *in sul Tamigi ancor si cola* ‘on the Thames still is honoured’ → “still is venerated on the Thames”: the location moves after the verb. “Venerated” takes *cola* as a form of *colere*, ‘to honour or worship’, rather than *colare*, ‘to drip or flow’.

> 121 Poi vidi gente, che di fuor del rio  
> 122 tenea la testa ed ancor tutto il casso;  
> 123 e di costoro assai riconobb' io.  

Then some I saw, who kept the head and likewise all the chest out of the river; and of these I recognised many.

- *vidi gente* ‘I saw people’ → “some I saw”: the collective noun becomes an indefinite pronoun, and the object moves before the subject and verb.
- *che di fuor del rio tenea la testa ed ancor tutto il casso* ‘who outside the river held the head and also the whole chest’ → “who kept the head and likewise all the chest out of the river”: the location moves to the end; ‘held’ becomes “kept”, ‘also’ becomes “likewise”, and ‘the whole chest’ becomes “all the chest”. The relative clause now refers to plural “some” rather than singular *gente*.
- *e di costoro assai riconobb’ io* ‘and of these many recognised I’ → “and of these I recognised many”: the subject moves before the verb and the object follows it.

> 124 Così a più a più si facea basso  
> 125 quel sangue sì che cocea pur li piedi;  
> 126 e quivi fu del fosso il nostro passo.  

Thus more and more that blood grew shallow, until it [cooked] the feet only; and here was our passage through the fosse.

- *si facea basso quel sangue* ‘became low that blood’ → “that blood grew shallow”: the subject moves before the verb; the reflexive construction becomes “grew”, and ‘low’ is rendered specifically as “shallow”.
- *sì che cocea pur li piedi* ‘so that it cooked only the feet’ → “until it [cooked] the feet only”: a clause of consequence becomes one marking a temporal endpoint, and “only” moves after the noun phrase it qualifies.
- *quivi* ‘there’ → “here”: the spatial demonstrative changes.
- *del fosso* ‘of the fosse’ → “through the fosse”: the relation is made explicitly one of passage through something, and the phrase moves after “our passage”.

> 127 “Sì come tu da questa parte vedi  
> 128 lo bulicame che sempre si scema,”  
> 129 disse il Centauro, “voglio che tu credi  

“As thou seest the boiling stream, on this side, continually diminish,” said the Centaur, “so I would have thee to believe

- *Sì come* ‘Just as’ → “As”: the emphatic *Sì* is not separately represented.
- *da questa parte vedi lo bulicame* ‘on this side thou seest the boiling pool’ → “thou seest the boiling stream, on this side”: the spatial phrase moves from before the verb to after its object. *Bulicame* is expanded into an adjective and noun, with “stream” replacing ‘pool’.
- *che sempre si scema* ‘which continually grows less’ → “continually diminish”: the relative clause becomes an infinitive complement of “seest”, and the pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive verb.
- *voglio che tu credi* ‘I wish that thou believe’ → “so I would have thee to believe”: the present expression of will becomes the more qualified “would have”; the finite subordinate clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction. “So” is supplied to make the correspondence with the preceding comparison explicit.

> 130 che da quest' altra a più a più giù prema  
> 131 lo fondo suo, infin ch' ei si raggiunge  
> 132 ove la tirannia convien che gema.  

that, on this other, it lowers its bottom more and more, till it comes again to where tyranny is doomed to mourn.

- *a più a più giù prema lo fondo suo* ‘more and more downward may sink its bottom’ → “it lowers its bottom more and more”: the adverbial phrase moves from before the verb to after its object. The translation takes *prema* transitively, with the stream as subject and its bottom as object, rather than taking *lo fondo suo* as the subject that sinks. The downward direction is incorporated into “lowers”, the subjunctive becomes indicative, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *infin ch’ ei si raggiunge ove* ‘until it rejoins itself where’ → “till it comes again to where”: reflexive joining is paraphrased as coming again to a place.
- *la tirannia convien che gema* ‘tyranny—it is necessary that it groan’ → “tyranny is doomed to mourn”: the impersonal expression of necessity becomes a personal passive construction; “doomed” gives the necessity a stronger sense of imposed fate, and “mourn” replaces ‘groan’. The article before the abstract noun is omitted.

> 133 La divina giustizia di qua punge  
> 134 quell' Attila che fu flagello in terra,  

Divine Justice here torments that Attila, who was a scourge on earth;

- *La divina giustizia* ‘the divine justice’ → “Divine Justice”: the definite article is omitted.
- *di qua* ‘on this side’ → “here”: the directional phrase becomes a simple adverb of place.
- *punge* ‘pricks’ → “torments”: a specific physical action becomes a general expression of suffering.
- *flagello* ‘scourge’ → “a scourge”: English supplies an indefinite article before the predicative noun.

> 135 e Pirro e Sesto; ed in eterno munge  
> 136 le lagrime, che col bollor disserra  
> 137 a Rinier da Corneto, a Rinier Pazzo,  
> 138 che fecero alle strade tanta guerra.”  

and Pyrrhus and Sextus; and to eternity milks tears, which by the boiling it unlocks, from Rinier of Corneto, from Rinier Pazzo, who on the highways made so much war.”

- *in eterno* ‘for ever’ → “to eternity”: the adverbial expression is recast as a prepositional phrase with the noun “eternity”.
- *le lagrime* ‘the tears’ → “tears”: the definite article is omitted.
- *col bollor* ‘with the boiling’ → “by the boiling”: the preposition makes the boiling explicitly the means by which the action is accomplished.
- *a Rinier da Corneto, a Rinier Pazzo* ‘to Rinier from Corneto, to Rinier Pazzo’ → “from Rinier of Corneto, from Rinier Pazzo”: the Italian datives identify the persons affected; English instead identifies them as the source from which the tears are drawn. Within the first name, geographical *da* becomes “of”.
- *alle strade* ‘to the roads’ → “on the highways”: the relation is rendered as location, “highways” replaces the broader ‘roads’, and the phrase moves before the verb.

> 139 Poi si rivolse, e ripassossi il guazzo.  

Then he turned back, and repassed the ford.

- *si rivolse* ‘turned himself back’ → “he turned back”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive.
- *ripassossi* ‘recrossed for himself’ → “repassed”: the additional pronominal *si* is left unexpressed.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Syntax

The translation remains close to the Italian in its sequence of statements, comparisons, and descriptive details, but repeatedly adjusts their internal order. Subjects and verbs commonly precede objects that Dante places first, while adjectives and possessives move before their nouns. Thus *l’ arco tiro* becomes “I draw the bow,” and *la valle buia* becomes “the dark valley.” Some marked arrangements survive, however, as in “Our answer we will make” and “such of that rocky steep was the descent.” More generally, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending, and omits the Italian definite article before possessives: *Lo mio maestro* becomes “My Master.”

Changes of construction often make the prose more idiomatic or explicit. Pronominal verbs become ordinary intransitive expressions, as *si slaccia* becomes “breaks loose.” Finite clauses may be shortened: *ch’ è guardata* becomes “guarded,” while *che per l’ aer vada* becomes “to go through the air.” Elsewhere the translation expands or reorganizes a construction: *si piangon li spietati danni* becomes the active “they lament their merciless offences,” and impersonal expressions of obligation become personal ones. Italian subjunctives are variously represented by English indicatives, modals, or infinitives; tense and aspect also shift, as *Tu pensi forse* becomes “Perhaps thou art thinking.”

The vocabulary ranges from close equivalents to interpretation and paraphrase. *Alpestro*, ‘rugged’, becomes “alpine”; *discoscesa* becomes “shattered,” emphasizing brokenness; and *vermiglio* becomes “purple.” Some choices generalize a concrete action or image: *punge*, ‘pricks’, becomes “torments,” and *sproni*, ‘spurs’, becomes “incites.” Others preserve strikingly literal language, including “the infamy of Crete,” “the false cow,” and “milks tears.” The translation therefore does not consistently smooth away Dante’s unusual expressions, even when its surrounding syntax is rearranged.

Small omissions and additions recur throughout. *Già* is left unexpressed in the bull comparison, and introductory or emphatic words such as *Or* and *sì* sometimes disappear. Conversely, “in order” makes purpose explicit, “moment” fills out *in quella che*, and “my wary Guide” supplies a role and possessive for *quegli accorto*. Articles frequently become possessives with body parts or personal relationships, while demonstratives often become personal pronouns. Taken together, these departures produce an English prose that closely follows the Italian’s wording and rhetorical movement while clarifying relations, expanding compressed phrases, and accommodating English grammar.
