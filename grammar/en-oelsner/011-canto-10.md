# Inferno 10

## Lines 1–21 — The Open Sepulchres

> 1 Ora sen va per un secreto calle,  
> 2 tra il muro della terra e li martiri,  
> 3 lo mio maestro, ed io dopo le spalle.  

Now by a [secret] path, between the city-wall and the torments, my Master goes on, and I behind him.

- *sen va … lo mio maestro* ‘goes away … my master’ → “my Master goes on”: the pronominal expression for departure or onward movement becomes “goes on”; the subject precedes the verb, and both follow the descriptions of the route.
- *per un secreto calle* ‘through a secret path’ → “by a [secret] path”: the route is expressed with “by” rather than ‘through’.
- *muro della terra* ‘wall of the city’ → “city-wall”: the noun and its dependent phrase become a compound noun.
- *dopo le spalle* ‘behind the shoulders’ → “behind him”: the bodily reference is replaced by a pronoun identifying the person followed.

> 4 “O virtù somma, che per gli empi giri  
> 5 mi volvi,” cominciai, “come a te piace;  
> 6 parlami, e soddisfammi a’ miei desiri.  

“O Virtue supreme! who through the impious circles thus wheelest me, as it pleases thee,” I began; “speak to me, and satisfy my wishes.

- *mi volvi* ‘me thou turnest’ → “thus wheelest me”: “wheelest” gives a more specifically circular sense to the turning; “thus” is supplied, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *cominciai, come a te piace* ‘I began, as to thee it pleases’ → “as it pleases thee,” I began”: the reporting clause is moved from before the phrase expressing the guide’s pleasure to after it; the fronted indirect-object phrase becomes an unmarked object after the verb.
- *soddisfammi a’ miei desiri* ‘satisfy me as to my desires’ → “satisfy my wishes”: the personal object is omitted, and the desires themselves become the direct object.

> 7 La gente, che per li sepolcri giace,  
> 8 potrebbesi veder? già son levati  
> 9 tutti i coperchi, e nessun guardia face.”  

Might those people, who lie within the sepulchres, be seen? the covers all are raised, and none keeps guard.”

- *La gente, che … giace* ‘the people, who … lies’ → “those people, who lie”: the definite article becomes the demonstrative “those”; the Italian collective noun and singular verb become an English plural noun and verb.
- *per li sepolcri* ‘throughout the sepulchres’ → “within the sepulchres”: the distributive expression becomes one of enclosure, and the phrase follows rather than precedes the verb of lying.
- *potrebbesi veder* ‘could be seen?’ → “Might” … “be seen?”: the conditional of possibility becomes “Might”; the *si* construction is expressed by an English passive, with the modal placed before the subject.
- *già son levati tutti i coperchi* ‘already are raised all the lids’ → “the covers all are raised”: ‘already’ is omitted; the subject moves before the verb, while “all” follows rather than precedes the noun.
- *guardia face* ‘guard makes’ → “keeps guard”: the Italian idiom becomes the English idiom with “keeps”, and the object follows the verb.

> 10 Ed egli a me: “Tutti saran serrati,  
> 11 quando di Josaffàt qui torneranno  
> 12 coi corpi, che lassù hanno lasciati.  

And he to me: “All shall be closed up, when, from Jehosaphat, they return here with the bodies which they have left above.

- *di Josaffàt qui torneranno* ‘from Jehosaphat here they will return’ → “from Jehosaphat, they return here”: the future becomes the present customary in an English temporal clause referring to the future; “here” moves after the verb.
- *che lassù hanno lasciati* ‘which up there they have left’ → “which they have left above”: ‘up there’ becomes the less explicitly deictic “above”, and the location moves after the verb.

> 13 Suo cimitero da questa parte hanno  
> 14 con Epicuro tutti i suoi seguaci,  
> 15 che l’ anima col corpo morta fanno.  

In this part are entombed with Epicurus all his followers, who make the soul die with the body.

- *Suo cimitero … hanno* ‘their cemetery … they have’ → “are entombed”: possession of a cemetery becomes a passive statement of burial; the noun becomes a verb, and the possessive is no longer expressed.
- *da questa parte* ‘on this side’ → “In this part”: the expression of location is recast with “In” and placed at the beginning of the sentence rather than between the object and verb.
- *che l’ anima col corpo morta fanno* ‘who the soul with the body dead make’ → “who make the soul die with the body”: the predicative adjective ‘dead’ becomes the infinitive “die”, giving the construction a causative form; the main verb moves before its object, and the phrase concerning the body comes last.

> 16 Però alla dimanda che mi faci  
> 17 quinci entro soddisfatto sarai tosto,  
> 18 e al disio ancor, che tu mi taci.”  

Therefore to the question, which thou askest me, thou shalt soon have satisfaction here within; and also to the wish which thou holdest from me.”

- *che mi faci* ‘which to me thou makest’ → “which thou askest me”: making a question becomes asking it, and the personal object follows the verb.
- *quinci entro soddisfatto sarai tosto* ‘here within satisfied thou wilt be soon’ → “thou shalt soon have satisfaction here within”: being satisfied becomes having satisfaction, replacing the participle with a noun; “soon” precedes the main verbal phrase, and the expression of place moves to the end.
- *al disio ancor* ‘to the desire also’ → “also to the wish”: “also” moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *che tu mi taci* ‘which thou to me keepest silent’ → “which thou holdest from me”: remaining silent about something is expressed as withholding it; the Italian dative becomes a following phrase with “from”.

> 19 Ed io: “Buon Duca, non tegno nascosto  
> 20 a te mio cor, se non per dicer poco;  
> 21 e tu m’ hai non pur mo a ciò disposto.”  

And I : “Kind Guide, I do not keep my heart concealed from thee, except for brevity of speech, to which thou hast ere now disposed me.”

- *Buon Duca* ‘good leader’ → “Kind Guide”: general goodness is rendered as kindness, and leadership as guidance.
- *non tegno nascosto a te mio cor* ‘I do not keep hidden to thee my heart’ → “I do not keep my heart concealed from thee”: the object moves before its predicative complement, and ‘to thee’ becomes “from thee”.
- *se non per dicer poco* ‘if not in order to say little’ → “except for brevity of speech”: the limiting expression becomes “except”, while the purpose phrase containing an infinitive becomes a noun phrase.
- *e tu m’ hai non pur mo a ciò disposto* ‘and thou me hast not only now to this disposed’ → “to which thou hast ere now disposed me”: the coordinated clause becomes a relative clause, with ‘to this’ changed into the fronted “to which”; ‘not only now’ becomes “ere now”, making the implication of earlier occasions explicit; the object pronoun moves from before the auxiliary to after the participle.

## Lines 22–54 — Farinata Questions Dante About His Ancestors

> 22 “O Tosco, che per la città del foco  
> 23 vivo ten vai così parlando onesto,  
> 24 piacciati di ristare in questo loco.  

“O Tuscan! who through the city of fire goest alive, speaking thus decorously; may it please thee to stop in this place.

- *vivo ten vai* ‘alive thou goest on thy way’ → “goest alive”: the adjective follows the verb, and the pronominal expression *te ne vai* is reduced to “goest”.
- *così parlando onesto* ‘thus speaking decorous’ → “speaking thus decorously”: “thus” follows the participle, and the adjective used adverbially becomes an English adverb.
- *piacciati di ristare* ‘may it please thee to stay’ → “may it please thee to stop”: the subjunctive is expressed with “may”, and remaining in place is rendered as stopping.

> 25 La tua loquela ti fa manifesto  
> 26 di quella nobil patria natio,  
> 27 alla qual forse fui troppo molesto.”  

Thy speech clearly shews thee a native of that noble country, which perhaps I vexed too much.”

- *ti fa manifesto* ‘makes thee manifest’ → “clearly shews thee”: the construction with ‘make’ and a predicative adjective becomes a verb modified by an adverb.
- *di quella nobil patria natio* ‘of that noble country native’ → “a native of that noble country”: the adjective becomes a noun with an indefinite article, and its prepositional complement follows it.
- *alla qual forse fui troppo molesto* ‘to which perhaps I was too troublesome’ → “which perhaps I vexed too much”: being troublesome becomes the action of vexing; the indirect complement becomes a direct object, and “too much” modifies the verb rather than an adjective.

> 28 Subitamente questo suono uscio  
> 29 d’ una dell’ arche: però m’ accostai,  
> 30 temendo, un poco più al duca mio.  

Suddenly this sound issued from one of the chests : whereat in fear I drew a little closer to my Guide.

- *d’ una dell’ arche* ‘from one of the tombs’ → “from one of the chests”: “chests” gives the underlying sense of *arche* rather than its contextual sense of tombs.
- *però* ‘therefore’ → “whereat”: an explicitly consequential connective becomes a relative connective presenting the preceding event as the occasion for the response.
- *m’ accostai … un poco più* ‘I brought myself a little nearer’ → “I drew a little closer”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive one.
- *temendo* ‘fearing’ → “in fear”: the participle becomes a prepositional phrase and is moved before the main verb.
- *al duca mio* ‘to the leader mine’ → “to my Guide”: “Guide” renders *duca*, and the possessive moves before the noun.

> 31 Ed ei mi disse: “Volgiti; che fai?  
> 32 vedi là Farinata, che s’ è dritto;  
> 33 dalla cintola in su tutto il vedrai.”  

And he said to me : “ Turn thee round ; what art thou doing? lo there Farinata ! who has raised himself erect ; from the girdle upwards thou shalt see him all.”

- *Volgiti* ‘turn thyself’ → “Turn thee round”: “round” makes the direction of turning explicit.
- *che fai?* ‘what dost thou do?’ → “what art thou doing?”: the Italian present becomes an English present progressive.
- *vedi là Farinata* ‘see there Farinata’ → “lo there Farinata”: the imperative of seeing becomes the presentative “lo”.
- *che s’ è dritto* ‘who has straightened himself’ → “who has raised himself erect”: the verb is expanded into a verb and a predicative adjective.
- *tutto il vedrai* ‘all him thou shalt see’ → “thou shalt see him all”: the object pronoun follows the verb, and “all” follows the pronoun rather than preceding it.

> 34 Io avea già il mio viso nel suo fitto;  
> 35 ed ei s’ ergea col petto e colla fronte,  
> 36 come avesse lo inferno in gran dispitto;  

Already I had fixed my look on his ; and he rose upright with breast and countenance, as if he entertained great scorn of Hell

- *Io avea già il mio viso nel suo fitto* ‘I had already my gaze in his fixed’ → “Already I had fixed my look on his”: “Already” moves to the beginning, the participle moves before its object, and “on” replaces ‘in’.
- *s’ ergea* ‘raised himself’ → “rose upright”: the reflexive verb becomes an intransitive verb with an added adjective expressing the erect posture.
- *col petto e colla fronte* ‘with the breast and with the forehead’ → “with breast and countenance”: the repeated preposition and the bodily definite articles disappear; “countenance” broadens ‘forehead’ to the face or its expression.
- *come avesse lo inferno in gran dispitto* ‘as if he had Hell in great disdain’ → “as if he entertained great scorn of Hell”: the idiom of having something ‘in disdain’ becomes entertaining scorn ‘of’ it, and Hell moves from the verb’s object to the complement of “scorn”.

> 37 e le animose man del duca e pronte  
> 38 mi pinser tra le sepolture a lui,  
> 39 dicendo: “Le parole tue sien conte.”  

; and the bold and ready hands of my Guide pushed me amongst the sepultures to him, saying : “ Let thy words be numbered.”

- *le animose man … e pronte* ‘the bold hands … and ready’ → “the bold and ready hands”: the two adjectives are brought together before the noun.
- *del duca* ‘of the leader’ → “of my Guide”: “Guide” renders *duca*, and “my” is supplied.
- *mi pinser* ‘me pushed’ → “pushed me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Le parole tue sien conte* ‘the words thine be well-chosen’ → “Let thy words be numbered”: the jussive subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”, and the possessive moves before the noun. “Numbered” takes *conte* in the sense ‘counted’, rather than ‘well-chosen’ or ‘considered’.

> 40 Com’ io al piè della sua tomba fui,  
> 41 guardommi un poco, e poi quasi sdegnoso  
> 42 mi dimandò: “Chi fur li maggior tui?”  

[When] I was at the foot of his tomb, he looked at me a little ; and then, almost contemptuously, he asked me : “ Who were thy ancestors?”

- *Com’* ‘as’ → “[When]”: the temporal conjunction is rendered explicitly as “When”.
- *quasi sdegnoso* ‘almost disdainful’ → “almost contemptuously”: an adjective describing the speaker becomes an adverb modifying his asking.
- *mi dimandò* ‘me he asked’ → “he asked me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Chi fur li maggior tui?* ‘Who were the greater ones thine?’ → “Who were thy ancestors?”: the substantivized comparative is rendered by the noun expressing its idiomatic meaning, and the possessive moves before that noun.

> 43 Io, ch’ era d’ ubbedir disideroso,  
> 44 non gliel celai, ma tutto gliel’ apersi:  
> 45 ond’ ei levò le ciglia un poco in soso;  

I, being desirous to obey, concealed it not ; but opened the whole to him : whereupon he raised his brows a little

- *Io, ch’ era d’ ubbedir disideroso* ‘I, who was of obeying desirous’ → “I, being desirous to obey”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase, and the infinitival complement follows the adjective rather than preceding it.
- *non gliel celai* ‘not to him it I concealed’ → “concealed it not”: the indirect-object pronoun ‘to him’ is omitted, while the direct object and negative move after the verb.
- *ma tutto gliel’ apersi* ‘but all to him it I opened’ → “but opened the whole to him”: “the whole” replaces the combination of ‘all’ and the direct-object pronoun; the direct and indirect objects follow the verb.
- *ond’* ‘whence’ → “whereupon”: the connective is rendered as a response or succession rather than with its literal spatial form.
- *levò le ciglia un poco in soso* ‘raised the eyebrows a little upwards’ → “raised his brows a little”: “his” replaces the definite article, and *in soso* has no separate equivalent.

> 46 poi disse: “Fieramente furo avversi  
> 47 a me ed a’ miei primi, ed a mia parte,  
> 48 sì che per due fiate gli dispersi.”  

; then he said : “ Fiercely adverse were they to me, and to my progenitors, and to my party ; so that twice I scattered them.”

- *Fieramente furo avversi* ‘Fiercely they were adverse’ → “Fiercely adverse were they”: the predicative adjective moves before the verb.
- *a’ miei primi* ‘to my first ones’ → “to my progenitors”: the substantivized adjective is rendered by a noun specifying ancestry.
- *per due fiate* ‘for two times’ → “twice”: the prepositional phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *gli dispersi* ‘them I scattered’ → “I scattered them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 49 “S’ ei fur cacciati, ei tornar d’ ogni parte,”  
> 50 risposi io lui, “l’ una e l’ altra fiata;  
> 51 ma i vostri non appreser ben quell’ arte.”  

“ If they were driven forth, they returned from every quarter, both times,” I answered him ; “ but yours have not rightly learnt that art.”

- *risposi io lui* ‘answered I to him’ → “I answered him”: the subject moves before the verb, and the indirect complement becomes an English object without a preposition. The reporting clause is also moved after the rendering of *l’ una e l’ altra fiata*.
- *l’ una e l’ altra fiata* ‘the one and the other time’ → “both times”: the coordinated singular expressions become a plural phrase.
- *ma i vostri non appreser ben quell’ arte* ‘but yours did not learn well that art’ → “but yours have not rightly learnt that art”: the simple past becomes a present perfect; ‘well’ is rendered as “rightly” and moves before the participle.

> 52 Allor surse alla vista scoperchiata  
> 53 un’ ombra lungo questa infino al mento;  
> 54 credo che s’ era in ginocchie levata.  

Then, beside him, there rose a shadow, visible to the chin ; it had raised itself, I think, upon its knees.

- *surse* ‘rose’ → “there rose”: presentational “there” is supplied.
- *alla vista scoperchiata* ‘at the uncovered opening’ → “visible”: the locative expression becomes an adjective describing the shadow’s visibility; ‘uncovered’ has no separate equivalent.
- *lungo questa* ‘alongside this one’ → “beside him”: the feminine demonstrative becomes a masculine personal pronoun, and the phrase moves before the verb.
- *credo che s’ era in ginocchie levata* ‘I believe that it had on knees raised itself’ → “it had raised itself, I think, upon its knees”: the subordinate clause becomes the main assertion, with “I think” inserted parenthetically; the participle moves before the phrase describing posture, and “its” is supplied before “knees”.

## Lines 55–87 — A Father’s Grief and Exile Foretold

> 55 D' intorno mi guardò, come talento  
> 56 avesse di veder s' altri era meco;  

It looked around me, as if it had a wish to see whether some one were with me;

- *D' intorno mi guardò* ‘around me he looked’ → “It looked around me”: the verb precedes the phrase expressing where he looked, instead of following it.
- *come talento avesse* ‘as desire he had’ → “as if it had a wish”: “if” makes the hypothetical force of the Italian subjunctive explicit; the object follows the verb, and “a” is supplied.
- *s' altri era* ‘if another was’ → “whether some one were”: “whether” introduces the indirect question; the indefinite *altri* becomes “some one,” and indicative *era* becomes subjunctive “were”.

> 57 ma poi che il sospicar fu tutto spento,  
> 58 piangendo disse : “Se per questo cieco  
> 59 carcere vai per altezza d' ingegno,  
> 60 mio figlio ov' è, e perchè non è teco?”  

but when all its expectation was quenched, it said, weeping: “If through this blind prison thou goest by height of genius, where is my son and why is he not with thee?”

- *poi che* ‘after that’ → “when”: the expression of temporal succession becomes a simpler temporal conjunction.
- *il sospicar fu tutto spento* ‘the suspecting was wholly extinguished’ → “all its expectation was quenched”: the substantivized infinitive is interpreted as “expectation,” rather than suspicion or conjecture; “its” is supplied, and *tutto* becomes a modifier of the noun rather than of its extinguishing.
- *piangendo disse* ‘weeping he said’ → “it said, weeping”: the participial expression follows the verb instead of preceding it.
- *mio figlio ov' è* ‘my son where is’ → “where is my son”: the subject moves after the interrogative and verb.

> 61 Ed io a lui : “Da me stesso non vegno:  
> 62 colui, che attende là, per qui mi mena,  
> 63 forse cui Guido vostro ebbe a disdegno.”  

And I to him: “Of myself I come not: he, that waits yonder, leads me through this place; whom perhaps thy Guido held in disdain.”

- *Da me stesso non vegno* ‘From myself I do not come’ → “Of myself I come not”: “Of myself” expresses independent agency; the negative follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *colui* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *per qui mi mena* ‘through here me he leads’ → “leads me through this place”: ‘here’ is expanded into “this place,” and both the object pronoun and the expression of the route follow the verb.
- *forse cui* ‘perhaps whom’ → “whom perhaps”: the relative pronoun moves before the adverb.
- *Guido vostro* ‘Guido your’ → “thy Guido”: the possessive precedes the name; the formally plural, respectful possessive becomes singular “thy”.
- *ebbe a disdegno* ‘had in disdain’ → “held in disdain”: “held” replaces ‘had’ in the English idiom.

> 64 Le sue parole, e il modo della pena  
> 65 m' avevan di costui già letto il nome:  
> 66 però fu la risposta così piena.  

Already his words and the manner of his punishment had read his name to me: hence my answer was so full.

- *Le sue parole, e il modo della pena m' avevan di costui già letto il nome* ‘His words and the manner of the punishment had to me of this man already read the name’ → “Already his words and the manner of his punishment had read his name to me”: “Already” moves to the beginning of the sentence; “his” replaces the definite article before “punishment”; *di costui* becomes the possessive “his,” losing its demonstrative force; and the recipient “to me” follows the direct object instead of preceding the verb.
- *però fu la risposta così piena* ‘therefore was the answer so full’ → “hence my answer was so full”: the subject precedes the verb, and “my” replaces the definite article.

> 67 Di subito drizzato gridò : “Come  
> 68 dicesti : egli ebbe? non viv' egli ancora?  
> 69 non fiere gli occhi suoi lo dolce lome?”  

Rising instantly erect, he cried: “How saidst thou: he had? lives he not still? does not the sweet light strike his eyes?”

- *Di subito drizzato* ‘Suddenly straightened up’ → “Rising instantly erect”: the past participle becomes an active present participle with a complement, “erect”; the expression of suddenness follows “Rising” rather than preceding it.
- *non viv' egli ancora* ‘not lives he still’ → “lives he not still”: the negative moves from before the verb to after the subject.
- *non fiere gli occhi suoi lo dolce lome* ‘not strikes the eyes his the sweet light’ → “does not the sweet light strike his eyes”: the subject moves from after the object to before the main verb; the question uses auxiliary “does”; and the possessive precedes “eyes” instead of following it.

> 70 Quando s' accorse d' alcuna dimora  
> 71 ch' io faceva dinanzi alla risposta,  
> 72 supin ricadde, e più non parve fuora.  

When he perceived that I made some delay in answering, supine he fell again, and shewed himself no more.

- *Quando s' accorse d' alcuna dimora ch' io faceva dinanzi alla risposta* ‘When he became aware of some delay that I made before the answer’ → “When he perceived that I made some delay in answering”: becoming aware of a delay, qualified by a relative clause, becomes perceiving that a statement is true; ‘before the answer’ becomes “in answering,” replacing a noun with a gerund and changing the temporal relation.
- *e più non parve fuora* ‘and no more appeared outside’ → “and shewed himself no more”: the intransitive ‘appeared’ becomes the transitive, reflexive expression “shewed himself”; *fuora* is omitted, and “no more” follows the verb.

> 73 Ma quell' altro magnanimo, a cui posta  
> 74 restato m' era, non mutò aspetto,  
> 75 nè mosse collo, nè piegò sua costa.  

But that other, magnanimous, at whose desire I had stopped, changed not his aspect, nor moved his neck, nor bent his side.

- *a cui posta restato m' era* ‘at whose bidding I had stopped’ → “at whose desire I had stopped”: the expression of someone’s bidding or request becomes “desire”; the pronominal *restarsi* becomes plain “stopped,” and the auxiliary precedes the participle rather than following it.
- *non mutò aspetto* ‘did not change appearance’ → “changed not his aspect”: the negative follows the verb, and “his” is supplied before the noun.
- *nè mosse collo* ‘nor moved neck’ → “nor moved his neck”: the possessive “his” is supplied.

> 76 “E se,” continuando al primo detto,  
> 77 “egli han quell' arte,” disse, “male appresa,  
> 78 ciò mi tormenta più che questo letto.  

“And if,” continuing his former words, he said, “they have learnt that art badly, it more torments me than this bed.

- *continuando al primo detto* ‘continuing at the first saying’ → “continuing his former words”: the prepositional construction becomes a direct object; ‘first’ becomes “former,” singular *detto* becomes plural “words,” and “his” replaces the definite article.
- *egli han quell' arte … male appresa* ‘they have that art badly learned’ → “they have learnt that art badly”: the participle moves before the object, and the adverb follows the object instead of preceding the participle.
- *disse* ‘said’ → “he said”: the reporting clause moves before the words it introduces, instead of interrupting them between *arte* and *male appresa*.
- *ciò mi tormenta più* ‘that me torments more’ → “it more torments me”: the demonstrative becomes “it”; the object pronoun follows the verb, while “more” moves before it.

> 79 Ma non cinquanta volte fia raccesa  
> 80 la faccia della donna, che qui regge,  
> 81 che tu saprai quanto quell' arte pesa.  

But the face of the Queen, who reigns here, shall not be fifty times rekindled ere thou shalt know the hardness of that art.

- *non cinquanta volte fia raccesa la faccia della donna, che qui regge* ‘not fifty times shall be rekindled the face of the lady, who here reigns’ → “the face of the Queen, who reigns here, shall not be fifty times rekindled”: the negative follows the first auxiliary, and “fifty times” moves between the passive auxiliary and participle; the subject, together with its accompanying modifiers, moves before the verb instead of following it; “Queen” interprets the title more specifically than the Italian noun does; and the adverb “here” follows the verb.
- *che* ‘that’ → “ere”: the conjunction, following the preceding negative expression of elapsed time, is rendered explicitly as ‘before’.
- *quanto quell' arte pesa* ‘how much that art weighs’ → “the hardness of that art”: an indirect question becomes a noun phrase; the image of weight becomes an explicit statement of difficulty.

> 82 E se tu mai nel dolce mondo regge,  
> 83 dimmi, perchè quel popolo è sì empio  
> 84 incontro a' miei in ciascuna sua legge?”  

And so mayest thou once return to the sweet world, tell me why that people is so fierce against my kindred in all its laws?”

- *E se tu mai nel dolce mondo regge* ‘And if thou ever into the sweet world return’ → “And so mayest thou once return to the sweet world”: the conditional form is rendered as an explicit wish with “so mayest”; ‘ever’ becomes “once,” and the destination follows the verb instead of preceding it.
- *empio* ‘impious’ → “fierce”: the translation expresses hostility rather than impiety or lack of pity.
- *incontro a' miei* ‘against mine’ → “against my kindred”: the substantivized possessive is expanded into an explicit designation of relatives.
- *in ciascuna sua legge* ‘in each law of its own’ → “in all its laws”: the singular distributive expression becomes a plural collective one.

> 85 Ond' io a lui : “Lo strazio e il grande scempio,  
> 86 che fece l' Arbia colorata in rosso,  
> 87 tale orazion fa far nel nostro tempio.”  

Whereat I to him: “The havoc, and the great slaughter, which dyed the Arbia red, causes such orations in our temple.”

- *Ond'* ‘Whence’ → “Whereat”: the connective is rendered as a response to the preceding speech rather than literally as an expression of origin or consequence.
- *Lo strazio* ‘The mangling’ → “The havoc”: the more concrete expression of violent injury becomes a broader term for destruction.
- *che fece l' Arbia colorata in rosso* ‘which made the Arbia coloured in red’ → “which dyed the Arbia red”: *fece … colorata* is condensed into the single verb “dyed,” and the prepositional colour phrase becomes the complement “red”.
- *tale orazion* ‘such an oration’ → “such orations”: the singular becomes plural, and the object follows the verb instead of preceding it.
- *fa far* ‘makes make’ → “causes”: the causative verb and dependent infinitive are condensed into one verb, leaving the making or delivering of the orations implicit.

## Lines 88–120 — Distant Knowledge and Present Blindness

> 88 Poi ch’ ebbe sospirando il capo scosso,  
> 89 “A ciò non fui io sol,” disse, “nè certo  
> 90 senza cagion sarei con gli altri mosso;  

And sighing, he shook his head; then said: "In that I was not single; nor without cause, assuredly, should I have stirred with the others;

- *Poi ch’ ebbe sospirando il capo scosso* ‘After he had, sighing, shaken his head’ → “And sighing, he shook his head; then”: the temporal subordinate clause becomes a main clause followed by “then”; the anterior past becomes a simple past, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the participle moves before the subject.
- *A ciò* ‘To that’ → “In that”: the preposition changes.
- *non fui io sol … disse* ‘was not I alone … he said’ → “said:” and “I was not single”: the reporting verb moves from within the speech to its beginning; the subject moves before the verb, and “single” renders *sol*.
- *nè certo senza cagion sarei con gli altri mosso* ‘nor certainly without cause would I with the others have moved’ → “nor without cause, assuredly, should I have stirred with the others”: *certo* moves after the phrase about cause; “stirred” renders *mosso*, and the phrase about the others moves after the completed verb.

> 91 ma fu’ io sol colà, dove sofferto  
> 92 fu per ciascuno di torre via Fiorenza,  
> 93 colui che la difesi a viso aperto.”  

but I was single there, where all consented to extirpate Florence, I alone with open face defended her."

- *fu’ io sol … colui che la difesi* ‘I alone was … the one who defended her’ → “I was single” and “I alone” and “defended her”: the interrupted construction identifying the speaker as ‘the one who’ is recast as two assertions; “I alone” resumes and repeats the earlier assertion, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *sofferto fu per ciascuno* ‘it was allowed by each’ → “all consented”: the passive becomes active, permission becomes consent, and distributive ‘each’ becomes collective “all”.
- *di torre via* ‘to take away’ → “to extirpate”: the verb and adverb become a single, stronger verb.
- *a viso aperto* ‘with face open’ → “with open face”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 94 “Deh se riposi mai vostra semenza,”  
> 95 pregai io lui, “solvetemi quel nodo,  
> 96 che qui ha inviluppata mia sentenza.  

"Ah! so may thy seed sometime have rest," I prayed him, "solve the knot which has here involved my judgment.

- *se riposi mai* ‘if ever … rest’ → “so may” and “sometime have rest”: the conditional wording of the wish becomes an explicit optative with “may”; ‘ever’ becomes “sometime”, and the verb ‘rest’ becomes the phrase “have rest”.
- *vostra semenza* ‘your seed’ → “thy seed”: the respectful plural possessive becomes singular.
- *pregai io lui* ‘prayed I him’ → “I prayed him”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *solvetemi quel nodo* ‘untie for me that knot’ → “solve the knot”: the indirect object ‘for me’ is omitted, the demonstrative becomes a definite article, and “solve” replaces the physical verb ‘untie’.
- *che qui ha inviluppata mia sentenza* ‘which here has entangled my judgment’ → “which has here involved my judgment”: the adverb moves after the auxiliary, and “involved” renders ‘entangled’.

> 97 E’ par che voi veggiate, se ben odo,  
> 98 dinanzi quel, che il tempo seco adduce,  
> 99 e nel presente tenete altro modo.”  

It seems that you see beforehand what time brings with it, if I rightly hear; and have a different manner with the present."

- *se ben odo* ‘if well I hear’ → “if I rightly hear”: “rightly” interprets *ben* as accurate understanding; the subject precedes the adverb, and the whole clause moves after the statement about what time brings.
- *dinanzi quel, che* ‘beforehand that which’ → “beforehand what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun become the fused relative “what”.
- *il tempo seco adduce* ‘the time with itself brings’ → “time brings with it”: the article is omitted, the accompanying phrase moves after the verb, and the reflexive pronoun becomes “it”.
- *e nel presente tenete altro modo* ‘and in the present you keep another manner’ → “and have a different manner with the present”: ‘keep’ becomes “have”, ‘another’ becomes “a different”, and the initial phrase ‘in the present’ becomes the final “with the present”.

> 100 “Noi veggiam come quei, che ha mala luce,  
> 101 le cose,” disse, “che ne son lontano;  
> 102 cotanto ancor ne splende il sommo Duce:  

"Like one who has imperfect vision, we see the things," he said, "which are remote from us; so much light the Supreme Ruler still gives to us;

- *come quei, che ha mala luce* ‘like him who has bad light’ → “Like one who has imperfect vision”: the comparison moves before ‘we see’; the demonstrative becomes indefinite “one”, and “imperfect vision” interprets *mala luce* as defective eyesight.
- *che ne son lontano* ‘which from us are far’ → “which are remote from us”: the phrase ‘from us’ moves to the end, and the adverb *lontano* is rendered by the adjective “remote”.
- *cotanto ancor ne splende il sommo Duce* ‘so much still upon us shines the highest Leader’ → “so much light the Supreme Ruler still gives to us”: the intransitive ‘shines’ becomes the transitive “gives”, with “light” supplied as its object; the subject moves before the verb, and ‘the highest Leader’ becomes “the Supreme Ruler”.

> 103 quando s’ appressano, o son, tutto è vano  
> 104 nostro intelletto; e, s’ altri nol ci apporta,  
> 105 nulla sapem di vostro stato umano.  

when they draw nigh, or are, our intellect is altogether void; and except what others bring us, we know nothing of your human state.

- *s’ appressano* ‘approach’ → “they draw nigh”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes an English verb-and-adverb phrase without a reflexive pronoun.
- *tutto è vano nostro intelletto* ‘all is vain our intellect’ → “our intellect is altogether void”: the subject moves before the verb; *tutto*, agreeing with *intelletto*, becomes the adverb “altogether”, while *vano* becomes “void”.
- *e, s’ altri nol ci apporta* ‘and if another does not bring it to us’ → “and except what others bring us”: a negative conditional clause becomes an exception expressed through “what”; indefinite singular *altri* becomes plural “others”, and the object pronoun is absorbed into the relative construction.
- *nulla sapem di vostro stato umano* ‘nothing we know of your human state’ → “we know nothing of your human state”: the negative object moves after the verb.

> 106 Però comprender puoi che tutta morta  
> 107 fia nostra conoscenza da quel punto,  
> 108 che del futuro fia chiusa la porta.”  

Therefore thou mayest understand that all our knowledge shall be dead, from that moment when the portal of the Future shall be closed."

- *comprender puoi* ‘understand thou canst’ → “thou mayest understand”: the modal moves before the infinitive, and ‘canst’ becomes “mayest”.
- *che tutta morta fia nostra conoscenza* ‘that all dead shall be our knowledge’ → “that all our knowledge shall be dead”: the subject moves before the verb, and *tutta* is attached explicitly to “our knowledge” rather than retained beside ‘dead’.
- *da quel punto* ‘from that point’ → “from that moment”: “moment” makes the temporal sense explicit.
- *che del futuro fia chiusa la porta* ‘when of the future shall be closed the door’ → “when the portal of the Future shall be closed”: the subject moves before the verb, its dependent phrase moves after the noun, and ‘door’ becomes “portal”.

> 109 Allor, come di mia colpa compunto,  
> 110 dissi: “Or direte dunque a quel caduto,  
> 111 che il suo nato è co’ vivi ancor congiunto.  

Then, as compunctious for my fault, I said: "Now will you therefore tell that fallen one, that his child is still joined to the living.

- *come di mia colpa compunto* ‘as by my fault pricked’ → “as compunctious for my fault”: the participle becomes an adjective expressing remorse, and the preceding phrase giving its cause becomes a following phrase with “for”.
- *Or direte dunque a quel caduto* ‘Now you will therefore say to that fallen one’ → “Now will you therefore tell that fallen one”: the subject and auxiliary are inverted to express a request, and ‘say to’ becomes “tell” with a direct object.
- *il suo nato* ‘his born one’ → “his child”: the substantivized participle becomes an ordinary noun.
- *è co’ vivi ancor congiunto* ‘is with the living still joined’ → “is still joined to the living”: the phrase about the living moves after the participle, and ‘with’ becomes “to”.

> 112 E s’ io fui dianzi alla risposta muto,  
> 113 fat’ ei saper che il fei, perchè pensava  
> 114 già nell’ error che m’ avete soluto.”  

And if I was mute before, at the response, let him know, it was because my thoughts already were in that error which you have resolved for me."

- *fui dianzi alla risposta muto* ‘was before at the response mute’ → “was mute before, at the response”: the predicative adjective moves immediately after the verb.
- *fat’ ei saper* ‘make him know’ → “let him know”: the causative construction with ‘make’ becomes the idiomatic English expression with “let”.
- *che il fei, perchè pensava già nell’ error* ‘that I did it because I was already thinking within the error’ → “it was because my thoughts already were in that error”: the introductory ‘that’ is omitted; ‘I did it’ becomes “it was”; the activity of thinking becomes the noun “thoughts” with “were”; and the definite article before ‘error’ becomes a demonstrative.
- *che m’ avete soluto* ‘which for me you have untied’ → “which you have resolved for me”: the indirect object moves to the end, and “resolved” renders the metaphorical ‘untied’.

> 115 E già il Maestro mio mi richiamava:  
> 116 per ch’ io pregai lo spirito più avaccio,  
> 117 che mi dicesse, chi con lui si stava.  

And now my Master was recalling me: wherefore I, in more haste, besought the spirit to tell me who was with him.

- *E già* ‘And already’ → “And now”: “now” replaces ‘already’.
- *il Maestro mio mi richiamava* ‘the Master my me was calling back’ → “my Master was recalling me”: the possessive moves before the noun and the object pronoun after the verb; “recalling” renders ‘calling back’.
- *per ch’* ‘for which’ → “wherefore”: the prepositional relative expression becomes a single connective adverb.
- *io pregai lo spirito più avaccio* ‘I besought the spirit more quickly’ → “I, in more haste, besought the spirit”: the comparative adverb becomes a prepositional phrase and moves before the verb.
- *che mi dicesse* ‘that he should tell me’ → “to tell me”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an infinitive construction.
- *chi con lui si stava* ‘who with him was staying’ → “who was with him”: the pronominal verb of remaining becomes simple “was”, and the accompanying phrase moves after it.

> 118 Dissemi: “Qui con più di mille giaccio;  
> 119 qua entro è lo secondo Federico,  
> 120 e il Cardinale, e degli altri mi taccio.”  

He said to me: "With more than a thousand lie I here; the second Frederick is here within, and the Cardinal; and of the rest I speak not."

- *Qui con più di mille giaccio* ‘Here with more than a thousand I lie’ → “With more than a thousand lie I here”: “here” moves to the end, and the English subject follows the verb.
- *qua entro è lo secondo Federico* ‘here within is the second Frederick’ → “the second Frederick is here within”: the subject moves before the verb and the place expression after it.
- *e degli altri mi taccio* ‘and of the others I keep myself silent’ → “and of the rest I speak not”: ‘the others’ becomes “the rest”, and the affirmative pronominal expression for remaining silent becomes a negative statement about speaking.

## Lines 121–136 — A Warning and the Onward Path

> 121 Indi s’ascose; ed io in ver l’ antico  
> 122 poeta volsi i passi, ripensando  
> 123 a quel parlar che mi parea nemico.  

Therewith he hid himself; and I towards the ancient Poet turned my steps, revolving that saying which seemed hostile to me.

- *Indi* ‘then’ → “Therewith”: the temporal connective becomes one linking the withdrawal more closely with what precedes.
- *s’ascose* ‘himself he hid’ → “he hid himself”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.
- *volsi i passi* ‘turned the steps’ → “turned my steps”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *ripensando a quel parlar* ‘thinking again about that speaking’ → “revolving that saying”: reconsideration becomes metaphorical turning over in the mind; the prepositional complement becomes a direct object, and the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun.
- *che mi parea nemico* ‘which to me seemed hostile’ → “which seemed hostile to me”: the personal complement moves after the verb and adjective.

> 124 Egli si mosse; e poi così andando  
> 125 mi disse: “Perchè sei tu sì smarrito?”  
> 126 Ed io li soddisfeci al suo dimando.  

He moved on; and then, as we were going, he said to me: “Why art thou so bewildered?” And I satisfied him in his question.

- *Egli si mosse* ‘He moved’ → “He moved on”: “on” is supplied, suggesting continued movement.
- *così andando* ‘thus going’ → “as we were going”: the participial phrase becomes a temporal clause with a plural subject and a progressive verb; *così* is not separately rendered.
- *mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “he said to me”: the personal complement moves after the verb.
- *li soddisfeci* ‘to him I gave satisfaction’ → “satisfied him”: the Italian indirect object becomes an English direct object.
- *al suo dimando* ‘to his question’ → “in his question”: the preposition changes.

> 127 “La mente tua conservi quel che udito  
> 128 hai contra te,” mi comandò quel saggio,  
> 129 “ed ora attendi qui”; e drizzò il dito.  

“Let thy memory retain what thou hast heard against thee,” that Sage exhorted me; “and now mark here”; and he raised his finger.

- *La mente tua conservi* ‘May the mind thine preserve’ → “Let thy memory retain”: the exhortatory subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”; *mente* is narrowed to “memory”, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *quel che* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun are combined into a single relative word.
- *udito hai* ‘heard thou hast’ → “thou hast heard”: the auxiliary precedes the participle rather than following it.
- *mi comandò quel saggio* ‘to me commanded that wise one’ → “that Sage exhorted me”: the subject moves before the verb and the personal complement after it; commanding becomes exhorting, and the substantivized adjective becomes a noun.
- *attendi* ‘pay attention’ → “mark”: the instruction is expressed through a different verbal idiom.
- *drizzò il dito* ‘straightened the finger’ → “he raised his finger”: straightening is rendered as raising, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 130 “Quando sarai dinanzi al dolce raggio  
> 131 di quella, il cui bell’ occhio tutto vede,  
> 132 da lei saprai di tua vita il viaggio.”  

“When thou shalt stand before the sweet ray of that Lady, whose bright eye seeth all, from her shalt thou know the journey of thy life.”

- *sarai* ‘thou shalt be’ → “thou shalt stand”: the verb of being becomes a verb specifying posture or presence.
- *di quella* ‘of that one’ → “of that Lady”: “Lady” is supplied, turning the feminine demonstrative pronoun into a determiner accompanying a noun.
- *bell’ occhio* ‘beautiful eye’ → “bright eye”: beauty is rendered as brightness.
- *tutto vede* ‘all sees’ → “seeth all”: the object moves after the verb.
- *di tua vita il viaggio* ‘of thy life the journey’ → “the journey of thy life”: the possessive phrase moves after the noun it qualifies.

> 133 Appresso volse a man sinistra il piede;  
> 134 lasciammo il muro, e gimmo in ver lo mezzo  
> 135 per un sentier, che ad una valle fiede,  
> 136 che in fin lassù facea spiacer suo lezzo.  

Then to the sinister hand he turned his feet; we left the wall, and went towards the middle, by a path that strikes into a valley, which even up there annoyed us with its fetor.

- *volse a man sinistra il piede* ‘turned to the left hand the foot’ → “to the sinister hand he turned his feet”: the directional phrase moves before the verb; the singular foot becomes plural feet, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *ad una valle fiede* ‘towards a valley strikes’ → “strikes into a valley”: the verb moves before its directional complement, and “into” makes entry explicit.
- *che in fin lassù facea spiacer suo lezzo* ‘which right up there made its stench displease’ → “which even up there annoyed us with its fetor”: the causative construction becomes a direct verb of annoyance; the stench, the subject of the infinitive, becomes a complement introduced by “with”. “us” is supplied as the affected object, and the emphasis on the smell’s reaching so far upward is expressed by “even”.

## Summary — Close Echoes and Recast Syntax

The translation remains close to the Italian in its sequence of statements, much of its vocabulary, and many of its images, but repeatedly rearranges words into more familiar English constructions. Objects and complements generally move after their verbs, possessives before their nouns, and subjects before their predicates. Thus *di tua vita il viaggio* becomes “the journey of thy life”. Reporting clauses and parenthetical remarks also shift position. Yet the English does not consistently smooth away Italian or archaic patterns: “Fiercely adverse were they” and “With more than a thousand lie I here” retain conspicuous inversions.

Changes of construction often go beyond rearrangement. *Suo cimitero … hanno*, ‘they have their cemetery’, becomes the passive “are entombed”; *soddisfatto sarai*, ‘thou shalt be satisfied’, becomes “thou shalt soon have satisfaction”. Conversely, *quanto quell’ arte pesa*, ‘how much that art weighs’, is condensed into “the hardness of that art”, replacing both a clause and its image of weight with a noun phrase. Relative clauses sometimes become participial phrases, finite clauses become infinitives, and subordinate assertions become independent ones. Wishes and exhortations are made explicit through “may” or “Let”, while reflexive and pronominal verbs frequently receive simpler English equivalents.

The vocabulary ranges from close verbal echoes to interpretative substitutions. “chests” for *arche* follows the word’s underlying sense rather than its contextual meaning of tombs; “numbered” for *conte* selects a different sense from ‘well-chosen’ or ‘considered’. Elsewhere the translation specifies or redirects the Italian: *donna*, ‘lady’, becomes “Queen”; *bell’ occhio*, ‘beautiful eye’, becomes “bright eye”; and *empio*, ‘impious’, becomes “fierce”. Alongside these departures, expressions such as “blind prison”, “this bed”, and “with open face” closely preserve the Italian wording and imagery, even where the resulting English is unusual.

Omissions and additions are usually local. *Già* has no equivalent in the statement that the covers are raised, and *in soso*, ‘upwards’, disappears after “raised his brows”. The translation supplies “Lady” for *quella*, ‘that one’, and “light” when recasting *ne splende* as “gives to us”; *dopo le spalle*, ‘behind the shoulders’, becomes the less bodily “behind him”. More generally, beyond the changes individually recorded in the comparison, English subject pronouns regularly make explicit subjects expressed only by Italian verb endings, while articles before possessives are normally omitted. These routine grammatical adjustments coexist with a marked fidelity to the Italian’s phrasing: the prose often clarifies or reorganizes rather than freely paraphrases.
