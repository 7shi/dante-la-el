# Purgatorio 15

## Lines 1–15 — The Westward Walk and the Dazzling Light

> 1 Quanto tra l' ultimar dell' ora terza  
> 2 e il principio del dì par della spera  
> 3 che sempre a guisa di fanciullo scherza,  

As much as between the end of the third hour and the beginning of the day appears of the sphere which ever sports after the fashion of a child,

- *l’ ultimar* ‘the ending’ → “the end”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun denoting the endpoint.
- *dell’ ora terza* ‘of the hour third’ → “of the third hour”: the ordinal precedes the noun.
- *sempre a guisa di fanciullo scherza* ‘ever in the manner of child sports’ → “ever sports after the fashion of a child”: the verb moves ahead of the phrase of manner, and English supplies an article before the singular “child”.

> 4 tanto pareva già in ver la sera  
> 5 essere al sol del suo corso rimaso:  

so much appeared now to be left of the sun’s course towards evening;

- *già* ‘already’ → “now”: the adverb marks the present point rather than saying ‘already’.
- *in ver la sera* ‘towards the evening’ → “towards evening”: the phrase moves from before the infinitive to the end of the clause, and the article drops out.
- *essere al sol del suo corso rimaso* ‘to be to the sun of its course remaining’ → “to be left of the sun’s course”: the participle moves next to the infinitive; the dative ‘to the sun’ and the possessive ‘its’ are recast as the single English possessive “the sun’s”.

> 6 vespero là, e qui mezza notte era.  

it was vespers there, and here midnight.

- *vespero là, e qui mezza notte era* ‘vesper there, and here midnight was’ → “it was vespers there, and here midnight”: the singular becomes the English plural form “vespers”; the copula, deferred until after both time expressions in Italian, is placed before the first, with impersonal “it”.

> 7 E i raggi ne ferian per mezzo il naso,  
> 8 perchè per noi girato era sì il monte,  
> 9 che già dritti andavamo in ver l' occaso,  

And the rays were smiting on the middle of our noses, for the mount was so far circled by us, that we now were going straight to the west,

- *ne ferian per mezzo il naso* ‘us they smote right across the nose’ → “were smiting on the middle of our noses”: the imperfect is rendered by the progressive; the persons struck are expressed by the possessive “our”, the distributive singular becomes plural, and “the middle of” makes the point of impact a noun phrase.
- *perchè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating conjunction replaces the subordinating one.
- *per noi girato era sì il monte* ‘by us circled was so the mount’ → “the mount was so far circled by us”: the subject moves to the front and the agent to the end; the auxiliary precedes the participle, and “far” makes the extent expressed by *sì* explicit.
- *già dritti andavamo* ‘already straight we went’ → “we now were going straight”: ‘already’ becomes “now”, the imperfect becomes progressive, and the plural adjective describing the walkers becomes an adverb following the verb.
- *in ver l’ occaso* ‘towards the setting’ → “to the west”: the direction is named rather than expressed by the setting of the sun.

> 10 quand' io senti' a me gravar la fronte  
> 11 allo splendore assai più che di prima,  

when I felt my brow weighed down by the splendour far more than before,

- *io senti’ a me gravar la fronte* ‘I felt the brow weigh upon me’ → “I felt my brow weighed down”: the dative becomes a possessive, the brow moves before the expression of weight, and the infinitive gives way to a passive participle with “down”.
- *allo splendore* ‘at the splendour’ → “by the splendour”: English marks the splendour as the cause with “by” after the passive participle.
- *assai più che di prima* ‘much more than formerly’ → “far more than before”: “far” serves as the intensifier, and the single adverb “before” renders *di prima*.

> 12 e stupor m' eran le cose non conte:  

and amazement to me were the unknown things;

- *le cose non conte* ‘the things not known’ → “the unknown things”: the negation is incorporated into the adjective, which moves before the noun.

> 13 ond' io levai le mani in ver la cima  
> 14 delle mie ciglia, e fecimi il solecchio,  

wherefore I raised my hands towards the top of my eyes, and made me the shade

- *le mani* ‘the hands’ → “my hands”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *ciglia* ‘eyebrows’ → “eyes”: the translation names the eyes rather than the brows.
- *il solecchio* ‘the sunshade’ → “the shade”: the English noun does not specify shade from the sun.

> 15 che del soperchio visibile lima.  

which dulls the excess of light.

- *del soperchio visibile lima* ‘of the excessive visible [object] files away’ → “dulls the excess of light”: the verb moves before its object, and the image of filing away becomes one of dulling; the partitive construction becomes a direct object, with “excess” as its head and “light” specifying what is visible.

## Lines 16–48 — Reflected Light, the Ascent, and a Question

> 16 Come quando dall' acqua o dallo specchio  
> 17 salta lo raggio all' opposita parte,  

As when a ray of light leaps from the water or from the mirror to the opposite direction,

- *dall' acqua o dallo specchio salta lo raggio* ‘from the water or from the mirror leaps the ray’ → “a ray of light leaps from the water or from the mirror”: the subject comes before the verb, and the phrase of origin follows both; the article becomes indefinite, and “of light” is supplied.
- *all' opposita parte* ‘to the opposite side’ → “to the opposite direction”: a direction replaces a side.

> 18 salendo su per lo modo parecchio  
> 19 a quel che scende, e tanto si diparte  
> 20 dal cader della pietra in egual tratta,  

ascending at an angle similar to that at which it descends, and departs as far from the line of the falling stone in an equal space,

- *salendo su* ‘ascending up’ → “ascending”: the adverb is absorbed into the verb.
- *per lo modo parecchio a quel che scende* ‘in the manner like to that in which it descends’ → “at an angle similar to that at which it descends”: Okey specifies the respect in which the two manners are alike; the prepositions change to suit “angle”.
- *tanto si diparte* ‘as far it departs’ → “departs as far”: the expression of degree follows the verb; the pronominal verb becomes a simple intransitive.
- *dal cader della pietra* ‘from the falling of the stone’ → “from the line of the falling stone”: “line” is supplied, and the substantival infinitive becomes a participle modifying “stone”.
- *in egual tratta* ‘in equal stretch’ → “in an equal space”: an article is supplied and the stretch is expressed as a space.

> 21 sì come mostra esperienza ed arte:  

even as experiment and science shows,

- *mostra esperienza ed arte* ‘shows experience and art’ → “experiment and science shows”: the compound subject moves before the verb, which retains its singular form; Okey takes the two nouns in the senses of testing by observation and systematic knowledge.

> 22 così mi parve da luce rifratta  
> 23 ivi dinanzi a me esser percosso,  
> 24 per che a fuggir la mia vista fu ratta.  

so I seemed to be smitten by reflected light in front of me, wherefore mine eyes were swift to flee.

- *mi parve … esser percosso* ‘it seemed to me … to be struck’ → “I seemed to be smitten”: the person to whom it seemed becomes the grammatical subject, and the passive infinitive follows “seemed” directly.
- *da luce rifratta* ‘by light bent back’ → “by reflected light”: the participle comes before the noun; Okey uses the optical sense ‘reflected’, and places the phrase after the passive infinitive.
- *ivi dinanzi a me* ‘there in front of me’ → “in front of me”: the separate ‘there’ drops out, and the phrase moves after the infinitive.
- *a fuggir la mia vista fu ratta* ‘to flee my sight was swift’ → “mine eyes were swift to flee”: the infinitive moves to the end, and the singular faculty of sight becomes the plural organs, with a corresponding change in the verb.

> 25 “Che è quel, dolce padre, a che non posso  
> 26 schermar lo viso tanto che mi vaglia,”  
> 27 diss' io, “e pare in ver noi esser mosso?”  

“What is that, sweet Father, from which I cannot screen my sight so that it may avail me,” said I, “and seems to be moving towards us?”

- *a che* ‘against which’ → “from which”: the preposition changes with the English idiom for screening.
- *lo viso* ‘the sight’ → “my sight”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *tanto che mi vaglia* ‘so much that it avail me’ → “so that it may avail me”: the explicit degree drops out, the subjunctive is expressed by “may”, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *in ver noi esser mosso* ‘towards us to be moved’ → “to be moving towards us”: a passive infinitive becomes an active progressive, and the directional phrase follows it.

> 28 “Non ti maravigliar, se ancor t' abbaglia  
> 29 la famiglia del cielo,” a me rispose;  

“Marvel thou not if the heavenly household yet dazes thee,” he answered me,

- *Non ti maravigliar* ‘do not marvel’ → “Marvel thou not”: the Italian negative command formed with the infinitive of a pronominal verb becomes a finite imperative with an expressed subject and postposed negative.
- *ancor t' abbaglia la famiglia del cielo* ‘still dazzles thee the household of heaven’ → “the heavenly household yet dazes thee”: the subject moves before the adverb and verb, the object after the verb; the genitive becomes a preceding adjective, and the specifically visual verb becomes “dazes”.
- *a me rispose* ‘to me he replied’ → “he answered me”: the prepositional complement before the verb becomes a direct object after it.

> 30 “messo è, che viene ad invitar ch' uom saglia.  

“’tis a messenger that cometh to invite us to ascend.

- *messo è* ‘a messenger it is’ → “’tis a messenger”: the predicate noun follows the copula.
- *ad invitar ch' uom saglia* ‘to invite that one ascend’ → “to invite us to ascend”: the indefinite subject of a subjunctive clause becomes a definite first-person plural object of “invite”, followed by an infinitive.

> 31 Tosto sarà che a veder queste cose  
> 32 non ti fia grave, ma fiati diletto,  
> 33 quanto natura a sentir ti dispose.”  

Soon will it be that to behold these things shall not be grievous to thee, but shall be a joy to thee, as great as nature hath fitted thee to feel.”

- *a veder queste cose* ‘to see these things’ → “to behold these things”: “behold” renders the simpler verb of seeing.
- *non ti fia grave* ‘will not to thee be heavy’ → “shall not be grievous to thee”: the figurative adjective is rendered by “grievous”, and the dative moves after it.
- *ma fiati diletto* ‘but will be to thee delight’ → “but shall be a joy to thee”: the enclitic dative becomes a separate phrase after the noun, and an article is supplied.
- *quanto natura a sentir ti dispose* ‘as much as nature to feel thee disposed’ → “as great as nature hath fitted thee to feel”: the comparison is expressed in terms of greatness; the simple past becomes a present perfect, and the object and infinitive follow “fitted”.

> 34 Poi giunti fummo all' angel benedetto,  

When we had reached the blessed angel,

- *Poi giunti fummo* ‘after we were arrived’ → “When we had reached”: the temporal connective becomes “When”; the compound form with ‘be’ and a preceding participle becomes an English past perfect with a transitive verb.
- *all' angel benedetto* ‘at the angel blessed’ → “the blessed angel”: the destination becomes the direct object of “reached”, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 35 con lieta voce disse: “Entrate quinci  
> 36 ad un scaleo vie men che gli altri eretto.”  

with gladsome voice, he said: “Enter here to a stairway far less steep than the others.”

- *quinci* ‘by this way’ → “here”: a route is expressed as a place.
- *vie men che gli altri eretto* ‘far less than the others upright’ → “far less steep than the others”: “steep” gives the sense of the adjective as applied to the stairway, and it moves before the phrase of comparison.

> 37 Noi montavam, già partiti da linci,  

We were mounting, already departed thence,

- *montavam* ‘were ascending’ → “were mounting”: the imperfect is expressed by the English past progressive.
- *da linci* ‘from there’ → “thence”: preposition and adverb are combined in one adverb.

> 38 e “*Beati misericordes*” fue  
> 39 cantato retro, e “Godi tu che vinci.”  

and “*Beati misericordes*” was sung behind, and “Rejoice thou that overcomest.”

- Literal.

> 40 Lo mio maestro ed io soli ambo e due  
> 41 suso andavamo, ed io pensava, andando,  
> 42 prode acquistar nelle parole sue;  

My Master and I, alone we two, were mounting up, and I thought while journeying to gain profit from his words;

- *soli ambo e due* ‘alone both and two’ → “alone we two”: the emphatic expression for ‘both’ is reduced to “two”, with “we” supplied in apposition.
- *suso andavamo* ‘up we were going’ → “were mounting up”: “mounting” specifies upward motion, and the adverb follows the verb.
- *andando* ‘going’ → “while journeying”: a temporal conjunction is supplied and the verb is more specific.
- *prode acquistar* ‘profit to acquire’ → “to gain profit”: the object follows the infinitive.
- *nelle parole sue* ‘in the words of his’ → “from his words”: the words are construed as the source of the profit, and the possessive precedes the noun.

> 43 e dirizza' mi a lui sì domandando:  

and I directed me to him thus asking:

- Literal.

> 44 “Che volle dir lo spirto di Romagna,  
> 45 e ‘divieto’ e ‘consorto’ menzionando?”  

“What meant the spirit from Romagna by mentioning ‘exclusion’ and ‘partnership’?”

- *volle dir* ‘wished to say’ → “meant”: the idiom is rendered by a single verb.
- *di Romagna* ‘of Romagna’ → “from Romagna”: origin is made explicit.
- *e ‘divieto’ e ‘consorto’ menzionando* ‘both prohibition and partner mentioning’ → “by mentioning ‘exclusion’ and ‘partnership’”: “by” specifies the relation of the gerund to the question, and the gerund precedes its objects. The first of the paired conjunctions drops out; Okey takes the prohibition as an exclusion and renders the person sharing as the abstract relation of sharing.

> 46 Per ch' egli a me: “Di sua maggior magagna  
> 47 conosce il danno; e però non s' ammiri,  
> 48 se ne riprende perchè men sen piagna.  

Whereupon he to me: “He knoweth the hurt of his greatest defect, and therefore let none marvel if he reprove it, that it be less mourned for.

- *Per ch' egli a me* ‘for which he to me’ → “Whereupon he to me”: a single connective replaces the preposition and relative.
- *Di sua maggior magagna conosce il danno* ‘of his greatest fault he knows the harm’ → “He knoweth the hurt of his greatest defect”: the fronted genitive follows the noun it qualifies, and “defect” renders the fault or blemish.
- *non s' ammiri* ‘let one not marvel’ → “let none marvel”: the impersonal negative subjunctive becomes a command with a negative pronoun as its subject.
- *se ne riprende* ‘if he rebukes on that account’ → “if he reprove it”: the matter for which reproof is given becomes the direct object of reproof.
- *perchè men sen piagna* ‘so that one weep less for it’ → “that it be less mourned for”: the impersonal active subjunctive becomes a passive, with the cause of mourning as its subject.

## Lines 49–81 — The Good That Grows by Sharing

> 49 Perchè s' appuntan li vostri disiri  
> 50 dove per compagnia parte si scema,  
> 51 invidia move il mantaco ai sospiri.  

Forasmuch as your desires are centred where the portion is lessened by partnership, envy moves the bellows to your sighs.

- *Perchè* ‘because’ → “Forasmuch as”: the single conjunction becomes a phrase.
- *s' appuntan li vostri disiri* ‘are pointed your desires’ → “your desires are centred”: the subject moves before the verb, and the image of desires directed at a point becomes one of desires centred there.
- *per compagnia parte si scema* ‘through sharing a portion diminishes’ → “the portion is lessened by partnership”: the portion becomes definite, the reflexive verb becomes passive, and the phrase expressing the cause moves to the end.
- *ai sospiri* ‘to the sighs’ → “to your sighs”: a possessive replaces the article.

> 52 Ma se l' amor della spera suprema  
> 53 torcesse in suso il desiderio vostro,  
> 54 non vi sarebbe al petto quella tema:  

But if the love of the highest sphere wrested your desire upwards, that fear would not be at your heart;

- *spera suprema* ‘sphere highest’ → “highest sphere”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *torcesse in suso il desiderio vostro* ‘should turn upwards the desire of yours’ → “wrested your desire upwards”: the Italian imperfect subjunctive is rendered by an English hypothetical past; “wrested” gives the turning a sense of force, the possessive precedes the noun, and “upwards” follows the object.
- *non vi sarebbe al petto quella tema* ‘there would not be for you in the breast that fear’ → “that fear would not be at your heart”: the postponed subject comes first, the dative of the persons affected becomes a possessive, and the breast becomes the heart.

> 55 chè per quanti si dice più lì nostro,  
> 56 tanto possiede più di ben ciascuno,  
> 57 e più di caritate arde in quel chiostro.”  

for by so many more there are who say ‘ours,’ so much the more of good doth each possess, and the more of love burneth in that cloister.”

- *per quanti si dice più lì nostro* ‘by how many more “ours” is said there’ → “by so many more there are who say ‘ours,’”: the passive construction becomes an existential one with an active relative clause. The locative ‘there’ has no separate equivalent: “there” in “there are” introduces the people who speak.
- *tanto possiede più di ben ciascuno* ‘so much possesses more of good each’ → “so much the more of good doth each possess”: the two parts of the comparative are brought together, “the” and the auxiliary “doth” are supplied, and the subject comes before the main verb rather than after its object.
- *più di caritate* ‘more of charity’ → “the more of love”: the comparative gains “the”, and the more specific noun becomes “love”.

> 58 “Io son d' esser contento più digiuno,”  
> 59 diss' io, “che se mi fossi pria taciuto,  
> 60 e più di dubbio nella mente aduno.  

“I am more fasting from being satisfied,” said I, “than if I had kept silent at first, and more perplexity I amass in my mind.

- *d' esser contento più digiuno* ‘of being satisfied more fasting’ → “more fasting from being satisfied”: the adjective and its comparative come before their complement, which is introduced by “from”.
- *mi fossi pria taciuto* ‘had earlier kept myself silent’ → “I had kept silent at first”: the pronominal construction becomes intransitive, and the adverb, rendered as a phrase, moves to the end.
- *più di dubbio* ‘more of doubt’ → “more perplexity”: the partitive preposition disappears, and doubt is rendered as perplexity.
- *nella mente aduno* ‘in the mind I gather’ → “I amass in my mind”: the verb moves before the phrase of place, “amass” stresses accumulation, and a possessive replaces the article.

> 61 Com' esser puote che un ben distributo  
> 62 i più posseditor faccia più ricchi  
> 63 di sè, che se da pochi è posseduto?”  

How can it be that a good when shared, shall make the greater number of possessors richer in it, than if it is possessed by a few?”

- *esser puote* ‘be can’ → “can it be”: the modal precedes the infinitive, with a formal subject between them.
- *distributo* ‘distributed’ → “when shared”: the participle is made explicitly circumstantial by “when”, and distribution is rendered as sharing.
- *i più posseditor faccia* ‘the more numerous possessors it should make’ → “shall make the greater number of possessors”: the verb moves before its object; the present subjunctive is rendered with “shall”, and the comparison is expressed by a noun of number rather than by a modifier of the possessors.
- *di sè* ‘of itself’ → “in it”: the preposition changes, and a personal pronoun replaces the reflexive.
- *da pochi è posseduto* ‘by few it is possessed’ → “it is possessed by a few”: the agent follows the passive verb, and “a” is supplied.

> 64 Ed egli a me: “Però che tu rificchi  
> 65 la mente pure alle cose terrene,  
> 66 di vera luce tenebre dispicchi.  

And he to me: “Because thou dost again fix thy mind merely on things of earth, thou drawest darkness from true light.

- *rificchi* ‘fix anew’ → “dost again fix”: the prefix is rendered by a separate adverb, and the auxiliary “dost” is supplied.
- *la mente* ‘the mind’ → “thy mind”: a possessive replaces the article.
- *pure alle cose terrene* ‘only on the earthly things’ → “merely on things of earth”: the article drops out, and the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *di vera luce tenebre dispicchi* ‘from true light darkness thou pluckest off’ → “thou drawest darkness from true light”: the verb comes first, its object next, and the source last; the image of detaching something is rendered by “drawest”.

> 67 Quello infinito ed ineffabil bene  
> 68 che è lassù, così corre ad amore,  
> 69 come a lucido corpo raggio viene.  

That infinite and ineffable Good, that is on high, speedeth so to love as a ray of light comes to a bright body.

- *lassù* ‘up there’ → “on high”: the indication of place becomes a prepositional phrase without a demonstrative.
- *così corre* ‘so runs’ → “speedeth so”: the adverb follows the verb, and running becomes swift movement.
- *a lucido corpo raggio viene* ‘to bright body ray comes’ → “a ray of light comes to a bright body”: the subject and verb precede the destination, both nouns gain articles, and “of light” makes the nature of the ray explicit.

> 70 Tanto si dà, quanto trova d' ardore,  
> 71 sì che quantunque carità si estende,  
> 72 cresce sopr' essa l' eterno valore;  

As much of ardour as it finds, so much of itself doth it give, so that how far soever love extends, eternal goodness giveth increase upon it;

- *Tanto si dà, quanto trova d' ardore* ‘so much it gives itself, as much as it finds of ardour’ → “As much of ardour as it finds, so much of itself doth it give”: the measure found comes before the gift rather than after it; “of ardour” moves next to the expression of quantity, the direct reflexive object becomes partitive “of itself”, and “doth” carries the inversion.
- *quantunque carità si estende* ‘however much charity extends itself’ → “how far soever love extends”: degree is expressed as distance, charity becomes love, and the English verb is intransitive rather than reflexive.
- *cresce sopr' essa l' eterno valore* ‘grows upon it the eternal worth’ → “eternal goodness giveth increase upon it”: the subject comes first and loses its article; worth becomes goodness, and an intransitive verb of growth becomes a verb of giving with “increase” as its object.

> 73 e quanta gente più lassù s' intende,  
> 74 più v' è da bene amare, e più vi s' ama,  
> 75 e come specchio l' uno all' altro rende.  

and the more people on high who comprehend each other, the more there are to love well, and the more love is there, and like a mirror one giveth back to the other.

- *quanta gente più* ‘however much more folk’ → “the more people”: the collective singular becomes plural, and the comparative is brought together before the noun.
- *lassù s' intende* ‘up there understands itself’ → “on high who comprehend each other”: the adverb becomes a phrase, the finite predicate becomes a relative clause, and the pronominal verb is read reciprocally rather than as the collective's understanding or directing its attention.
- *più v' è da bene amare* ‘more there is to love well’ → “the more there are to love well”: the comparative gains “the”, and the singular existential becomes plural, taking the objects of love to be people.
- *più vi s' ama* ‘more one loves there’ → “the more love is there”: impersonal loving becomes the presence of love, with a noun in place of the verb.
- *l' uno all' altro rende* ‘the one to the other gives back’ → “one giveth back to the other”: the first article drops out, and the recipient follows the verb.

> 76 E se la mia ragion non ti disfama,  
> 77 vedrai Beatrice, ed ella pienamente  
> 78 ti torrà questa e ciascun' altra brama.  

And if my discourse stays not thy hunger, thou shalt see Beatrice, and she will free thee wholly from this and every other longing.

- *la mia ragion* ‘my reasoning’ → “my discourse”: the argument is rendered as the speech that conveys it.
- *non ti disfama* ‘does not rid thee of hunger’ → “stays not thy hunger”: the person acted upon becomes the possessor of the hunger, which is made the direct object.
- *pienamente ti torrà questa e ciascun' altra brama* ‘fully will take from thee this and each other longing’ → “will free thee wholly from this and every other longing”: the person from whom something is taken becomes the direct object of “free”, the longings become the complement of “from”, and the adverb moves after “thee”.

> 79 Procaccia pur che tosto sieno spente,  
> 80 come son già le due, le cinque piaghe,  
> 81 che si richiudon per esser dolente.”  

Strive only that soon, even as the other two are, the five wounds may be rased out, which are healed by our sorrowing.”

- *sieno spente … le cinque piaghe* ‘may be extinguished … the five wounds’ → “the five wounds may be rased out”: the subject moves before the passive, and extinction becomes erasure.
- *come son già le due* ‘as are already the two’ → “even as the other two are”: the comparison moves before the whole clause about the five wounds; “even” reinforces it, “other” distinguishes the two, ‘already’ is not separately rendered, and the subject precedes its verb.
- *si richiudon* ‘close up again’ → “are healed”: the pronominal verb becomes passive, and the closing of wounds is expressed by its result, without a separate equivalent for ‘again’.
- *per esser dolente* ‘through being sorrowful’ → “by our sorrowing”: being in a state described by an adjective becomes a possessive with a gerund; “our” gives the sorrow an explicit first-person plural reference absent from the infinitive.

## Lines 82–114 — Three Visions of Meekness

> 82 Com' io voleva dicer: “Tu m' appaghe,”  

As I was about to say: “Thou dost satisfy me,”

- *voleva dicer* ‘wished to say’ → “was about to say”: an intention is rendered as an action on the point of taking place.
- *Tu m' appaghe* ‘thou me satisfiest’ → “Thou dost satisfy me”: the verb is expanded with an auxiliary, and the object follows it.

> 83 vidimi giunto in su l' altro girone,  
> 84 sì che tacer mi fer le luci vaghe.  

I saw me arrived on the next circuit, so that my eager eyes made me silent.

- *vidimi* ‘I saw myself’ → “I saw me”: the reflexive is rendered by the simple object pronoun.
- *l' altro girone* ‘the other circuit’ → “the next circuit”: “next” makes the succession explicit.
- *tacer mi fer* ‘fall silent they made me’ → “made me silent”: the causative verb comes first, and an adjective replaces the infinitive.
- *le luci vaghe* ‘the eyes eager’ → “my eager eyes”: the possessive replaces the article, the adjective precedes the noun, and the subject moves from the end of the clause to before the verb.

> 85 Ivi mi parve in una visione  
> 86 estatica di subito esser tratto  

There meseemed to be suddenly caught up in a dream of ecstacy,

- *mi parve* ‘to me it seemed’ → “meseemed”: the impersonal verb and its experiencer are expressed in a single English form.
- *in una visione estatica* ‘in an ecstatic vision’ → “in a dream of ecstacy”: “dream” replaces ‘vision’, a phrase with a noun replaces the adjective, and the whole phrase moves to the end.
- *di subito esser tratto* ‘suddenly to be drawn’ → “to be suddenly caught up”: the adverb moves inside the passive infinitive, and “caught up” replaces ‘drawn’.

> 87 e vedere in un tempio più persone;  

and to see many persons in a temple,

- *in un tempio più persone* ‘in a temple several persons’ → “many persons in a temple”: the object precedes the place phrase, and “many” gives *più* a stronger numerical sense.

> 88 ed una donna in su l' entrar con atto  
> 89 dolce di madre dicer: “Figliuol mio,  
> 90 perchè hai tu così verso noi fatto?  

and a woman about to enter, with the tender attitude of a mother, saying: “My son, why hast thou thus dealt with us?

- *in su l' entrar* ‘at the point of entering’ → “about to enter”: the preposition and nominal infinitive become an English infinitive construction.
- *con atto dolce di madre* ‘with gesture sweet of mother’ → “with the tender attitude of a mother”: “attitude” renders the manner conveyed by the gesture; “tender” precedes its noun, and both English nouns take articles.
- *dicer* ‘to say’ → “saying”: the infinitive dependent on the verb of seeing becomes a present participle.
- *Figliuol mio* ‘son mine’ → “My son”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *hai tu così verso noi fatto* ‘hast thou thus towards us done’ → “hast thou thus dealt with us”: “dealt with” replaces ‘done towards’, bringing the participle before the prepositional phrase.

> 91 Ecco, dolenti, lo tuo padre ed io  
> 92 ti cercavamo”; e come qui si tacque,  
> 93 ciò che pareva prima disparìo.  

Behold thy father and I sought thee sorrowing”; and as here she was silent, that which first appeared, disappeared.

- *dolenti, lo tuo padre ed io ti cercavamo* ‘sorrowing, thy father and I thee were seeking’ → “thy father and I sought thee sorrowing”: the description of the seekers moves from before the subject to after the object, the object follows the verb, and a simple past renders the Italian imperfect without explicitly marking duration.
- *si tacque* ‘fell silent’ → “was silent”: the English gives the state rather than the transition into it.
- *pareva prima* ‘was appearing before’ → “first appeared”: the adverb precedes the verb, “first” renders ‘before’, and the simple past replaces the imperfect.

> 94 Indi m' apparve un' altra con quelle acque  
> 95 giù per le gote, che il dolor distilla  
> 96 quando di gran dispetto in altrui nacque;  

Then appeared to me another woman, with those waters adown her cheeks which grief distils when it rises in one by reason of great anger,

- *m' apparve* ‘to me appeared’ → “appeared to me”: the experiencer follows the verb.
- *un' altra* ‘another’ → “another woman”: the noun implicit in the feminine form is supplied.
- *giù per le gote* ‘down along the cheeks’ → “adown her cheeks”: one preposition renders the directional phrase, and a possessive replaces the article.
- *di gran dispetto in altrui nacque* ‘from great indignation in someone was born’ → “it rises in one by reason of great anger”: the verb moves to the front, its past tense becomes a general present, and ‘was born’ becomes “rises”; the cause moves to the end and is made explicit by “by reason of”.

> 97 e dir: “Se tu se' sire della villa,  
> 98 del cui nome ne' Dei fu tanta lite,  
> 99 ed onde ogni scienza disfavilla,  

and saying: “If thou art lord of the city for whose name was so great strife among the gods, and whence all knowledge sparkles,

- *dir* ‘to say’ → “saying”: the infinitive becomes a participle.
- *del cui nome* ‘over whose name’ → “for whose name”: the English presents the name as what was contended for.
- *ne' Dei fu tanta lite* ‘among the gods was so great a strife’ → “was so great strife among the gods”: the prepositional phrase moves to the end, and “strife” is used as a mass noun.

> 100 vendica te di quelle braccia ardite  
> 101 che abbracciar nostra figlia, o Pisistrato.”  

avenge thee of those daring arms which embraced our daughter, O Pisistratus.”

- *braccia ardite* ‘arms daring’ → “daring arms”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 102 E il signor mi parea benigno e mite  
> 103 risponder lei con viso temperato:  

And the lord seemed to me kindly and gently to answer her with placid mien:

- *mi parea* ‘to me seemed’ → “seemed to me”: the experiencer follows the verb.
- *benigno e mite* ‘kind and gentle’ → “kindly and gently”: adjectives describing the lord become adverbs describing how he answers.
- *risponder lei* ‘to reply to her’ → “to answer her”: the person addressed becomes the direct object of “answer”.
- *con viso temperato* ‘with countenance composed’ → “with placid mien”: “placid” expresses the composure and precedes the noun.

> 104 “Che farem noi a chi mal ne disira,  
> 105 se quei, che ci ama, è per noi condannato?”  

“What shall we do to him who desires ill to us, if he who loveth us is condemned by us?”

- *farem noi* ‘shall-do we’ → “shall we do”: the future ending becomes an auxiliary, with the subject between auxiliary and verb.
- *a chi mal ne disira* ‘to whoever ill to us desires’ → “to him who desires ill to us”: the single relative is expanded to “him who”, and the verb precedes its objects.
- *ci ama* ‘us loves’ → “loveth us”: the object follows the verb.
- *è per noi condannato* ‘is by us condemned’ → “is condemned by us”: the agent follows the participle.

> 106 Poi vidi genti accese in foco d' ira,  
> 107 con pietre un giovinetto ancider, forte  
> 108 gridando a sè pur: “Martira, martira!”  

Then saw I people, kindled with the fire of anger, slaying a youth with stones, and ever crying out loudly to each other: “Kill, kill!”

- *in foco d' ira* ‘in fire of anger’ → “with the fire of anger”: “with” replaces ‘in’, and an article is supplied.
- *con pietre un giovinetto ancider* ‘with stones a youth to slay’ → “slaying a youth with stones”: the infinitive after the verb of seeing becomes a participle; the verb comes first and the instrument last.
- *forte gridando a sè pur* ‘loudly crying to themselves continually’ → “and ever crying out loudly to each other”: a conjunction is supplied, “ever” moves to the front, “loudly” follows the expanded “crying out”, and the reflexive is interpreted reciprocally.
- *Martira, martira* ‘Torment, torment’ → “Kill, kill”: the imperative is rendered by the fatal outcome rather than the infliction of suffering.

> 109 e lui vedea chinarsi, per la morte  
> 110 che l' aggravava già, in ver la terra,  
> 111 ma degli occhi facea sempre al ciel porte,  

and him saw I sinking towards the ground, because of death, which already was weighing him down, but of his eyes ever made he gates unto heaven,

- *chinarsi, per la morte che l' aggravava già, in ver la terra* ‘to bend himself down, because of the death which him was weighing down already, towards the ground’ → “sinking towards the ground, because of death, which already was weighing him down”: a participle replaces the reflexive infinitive, and the movement is expressed as a sinking rather than a bending. The direction is brought forward to follow “sinking” rather than being separated from it by the clause about death. English uses the abstract noun without the article; in the relative clause the adverb moves to the front and the object follows “weighing”.
- *degli occhi* ‘of the eyes’ → “of his eyes”: a possessive replaces the article.
- *facea sempre* ‘made always’ → “ever made he”: the adverb precedes the verb, with the subject following in English.
- *al ciel porte* ‘unto heaven gates’ → “gates unto heaven”: the noun precedes the phrase giving the destination.

> 112 orando all' alto Sire in tanta guerra,  
> 113 che perdonasse a' suoi persecutori,  
> 114 con quell' aspetto che pietà disserra.  

praying to the high Lord in such torture, with that look which unlocks pity, that he would forgive his persecutors.

- *in tanta guerra* ‘in so great a war’ → “in such torture”: the degree is expressed by “such”, and the image of warfare becomes one of suffering.
- *che perdonasse a' suoi persecutori* ‘that he might grant pardon to his persecutors’ → “that he would forgive his persecutors”: “would” renders the imperfect subjunctive of the petition, and the persons forgiven become direct objects.
- *con quell' aspetto che pietà disserra* ‘with that look which pity unlocks’ → “with that look which unlocks pity”: the object follows the verb, and the whole phrase is brought forward to precede the petition.

## Lines 115–145 — Waking from the Visions and Entering the Smoke

> 115 Quando l' anima mia tornò di fuori  
> 116 alle cose, che son fuor di lei vere,  
> 117 io riconobbi i miei non falsi errori.  

When my soul returned outwardly to the things which are true outside it, I recognised my not false errors.

- *l’anima mia* ‘the soul of mine’ → “my soul”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *di fuori* ‘to the outside’ → “outwardly”: an adverb replaces the phrase of direction.
- *son fuor di lei vere* ‘are outside her true’ → “are true outside it”: the predicative adjective comes directly after the verb, and the grammatically feminine pronoun becomes neuter.

> 118 Lo duca mio, che mi potea vedere  
> 119 far sì com' uom che dal sonno si slega,  
> 120 disse: “Che hai, che non ti puoi tenere,  

My Leader, who could see me acting like a man who frees himself from sleep, said: “What aileth thee that thou canst not control thyself,

- *Lo duca mio* ‘the leader of mine’ → “My Leader”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *mi potea vedere far* ‘me could see do’ → “could see me acting”: the object pronoun follows the verb of perception, and the dependent infinitive becomes a participle.
- *sì com’uom* ‘so as a man’ → “like a man”: the two-part expression of comparison is reduced to one preposition.
- *dal sonno si slega* ‘from the sleep himself unbinds’ → “frees himself from sleep”: the verb precedes the reflexive, the prepositional phrase comes last, and the article drops out.
- *Che hai* ‘what hast thou’ → “What aileth thee”: a question about what the addressee has becomes one about what afflicts him.
- *non ti puoi tenere* ‘not thyself canst hold’ → “thou canst not control thyself”: the negative follows the modal, the reflexive follows the infinitive, and ‘hold’ is rendered in the sense of self-control.

> 121 ma se' venuto più che mezza lega,  
> 122 velando gli occhi e con le gambe avvolte,  
> 123 a guisa di cui vino o sonno piega?”  

but art come more than half a league, veiling thine eyes, and with staggering legs, after the manner of him whom wine or sleep overcomes?”

- *mezza lega* ‘half league’ → “half a league”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *gli occhi* ‘the eyes’ → “thine eyes”: a possessive replaces the article with the body part.
- *le gambe avvolte* ‘the legs entangled’ → “staggering legs”: the article disappears, and an active participle before the noun describes the gait rather than the disposition of the legs.
- *a guisa di cui* ‘in the manner of whom’ → “after the manner of him whom”: the prepositional idiom changes, and English gives the relative an explicit antecedent.
- *piega* ‘bends’ → “overcomes”: the effect is expressed without the physical image of bending.

> 124 “O dolce padre mio, se tu m' ascolte,  
> 125 io ti dirò,” diss' io, “ciò che mi apparve  
> 126 quando le gambe mi furon sì tolte.”  

“O sweet Father mine, if thou listen to me, I will tell thee,” said I, “what appeared to me when my legs were thus taken from me.”

- *m’ascolte* ‘me thou hear’ → “thou listen to me”: the direct object becomes the object of a preposition following the verb.
- *ti dirò* ‘to thee shall tell’ → “will tell thee”: the inflected future becomes an auxiliary and infinitive, and the indirect object follows the verb.
- *ciò che mi apparve* ‘that which to me appeared’ → “what appeared to me”: a fused relative replaces demonstrative and relative, and the indirect object follows the verb.
- *le gambe mi furon sì tolte* ‘the legs from me were so taken’ → “my legs were thus taken from me”: the article becomes a possessive, and the pronoun of deprivation becomes a prepositional phrase after the participle.

> 127 Ed ei: “Se tu avessi cento larve  
> 128 sopra la faccia, non mi sarien chiuse  
> 129 le tue cogitazion, quantunque parve.  

And he: “If thou hadst a hundred masks upon thy face, thy thoughts, however slight, would not be hidden from me.

- *cento larve* ‘hundred masks’ → “a hundred masks”: English supplies an indefinite article before the numeral.
- *sopra la faccia* ‘upon the face’ → “upon thy face”: a possessive replaces the article.
- *non mi sarien chiuse le tue cogitazion* ‘not to me would be closed thy thoughts’ → “thy thoughts” … “would not be hidden from me”: the subject moves ahead of the conditional, the negative follows the auxiliary, and being closed *to* someone becomes being hidden *from* someone.
- *quantunque parve* ‘however small’ → “however slight”: the adjective is rendered in the sense of importance or degree, and the qualification moves with the noun to a position before the predicate.

> 130 Ciò che vedesti fu, perchè non scuse  
> 131 d' aprir lo core all' acque della pace  
> 132 che dall' eterno fonte son diffuse.  

What thou sawest was in order that thou have no excuse from opening thy heart to the waters of peace, which are poured from the eternal fount.

- *Ciò che* ‘that which’ → “What”: one relative word serves also as antecedent.
- *perchè non scuse d’aprir* ‘so that thou excuse not thyself from opening’ → “in order that thou have no excuse from opening”: the purpose construction is retained, but the negated verb becomes a verb of possession with a negated noun.
- *lo core* ‘the heart’ → “thy heart”: a possessive replaces the article.
- *dall’eterno fonte son diffuse* ‘from the eternal fount are spread abroad’ → “are poured from the eternal fount”: the source phrase follows the passive verb, and the participle is made specific to a liquid.

> 133 Non domandai, ‘Che hai,’ per quel che face  
> 134 chi guarda pur con l' occhio che non vede,  
> 135 quando disanimato il corpo giace;  

I asked not: ‘What aileth thee,’ for that reason which he asks who looks but with the eye that seeth not when senseless the body lies,

- *Non domandai* ‘not I asked’ → “I asked not”: the negative follows the verb.
- *Che hai* ‘what hast thou’ → “What aileth thee”: the possessor becomes the object of a verb of affliction.
- *per quel che face chi* ‘on account of that which he does who’ → “for that reason which he asks who”: the demonstrative is expanded into an explicit reason, and the general verb ‘does’ is interpreted as repeating the act of asking.
- *non vede* ‘not sees’ → “seeth not”: the negative follows the verb.
- *disanimato* ‘deprived of animation’ → “senseless”: the participle is rendered as an adjective denoting lack of consciousness.

> 136 ma domandai per darti forza al piede:  
> 137 così frugar conviensi i pigri, lenti  
> 138 ad usar lor vigilia quando riede.”  

but I asked to give strength to thy feet; so must the slothful be goaded who are slow to use their waking hour when it returns.”

- *darti forza al piede* ‘give thee strength to the foot’ → “give strength to thy feet”: the personal indirect object is represented by a possessive, and the singular body part becomes plural.
- *frugar conviensi i pigri* ‘to prod is needful the slothful’ → “must the slothful be goaded”: the impersonal construction becomes a modal passive, with the object of the infinitive as subject.
- *lenti* ‘slow’ → “who are slow”: a finite relative clause replaces the adjective in apposition.
- *ad usar lor vigilia* ‘to use their wakefulness’ → “to use their waking hour”: the abstract noun becomes an adjective and a noun of time.
- *quando riede* ‘when it comes back’ → “when it returns”: the implied subject is singular as in Italian, but now refers to the hour rather than to wakefulness.

> 139 Noi andavam per lo vespero, attenti  
> 140 oltre, quanto potean gli occhi allungarsi,  
> 141 contra i raggi serotini e lucenti;  

We were journeying on through the evening, straining our eyes forward, as far as we could, against the evening and shining rays;

- *andavam* ‘we went’ → “were journeying on”: the progressive brings out the continuance of the imperfect, and the general verb of motion is expanded.
- *per lo vespero* ‘through the eventide’ → “through the evening”: the time of day is expressed in ordinary English diction.
- *attenti oltre* ‘intent ahead’ → “straining our eyes forward”: a participial action replaces the adjective of attention, and the eyes, named in the next phrase in Italian, are made its object here.
- *quanto potean gli occhi allungarsi* ‘as far as the eyes could stretch themselves’ → “as far as we could”: the travellers replace the eyes as subject, and the action after the modal is left to be understood from the preceding phrase.
- *i raggi serotini e lucenti* ‘the rays evening and shining’ → “the evening and shining rays”: both modifiers precede the noun, and the first is expressed by a noun used attributively.

> 142 ed ecco a poco a poco un fummo farsi  
> 143 verso di noi, come la notte oscuro,  
> 144 nè da quello era loco da cansarsi.  

and lo, little by little, a smoke, dark as night, rolling towards us, nor any room was there to escape from it.

- *un fummo farsi verso di noi* ‘a smoke make its way toward us’ → “a smoke” … “rolling towards us”: the reflexive infinitive after the word of presentation becomes a participle specifying the manner of approach.
- *come la notte oscuro* ‘as the night dark’ → “dark as night”: the adjective precedes the comparison, the article drops out, and the whole description moves immediately after the noun.
- *nè da quello era loco da cansarsi* ‘nor from that was there place to withdraw oneself’ → “nor any room was there to escape from it”: the source phrase moves to the end and loses its demonstrative force; ‘place’ becomes “any room,” and the reflexive infinitive becomes an intransitive one.

> 145 Questo ne tolse gli occhi e l' aer puro.  

This reft us of sight and the pure air.

- *ne tolse gli occhi e l’aer puro* ‘from us took the eyes and the air pure’ → “reft us of sight and the pure air”: the persons deprived become the direct object and the things lost take “of”; the eyes are rendered by their faculty, without an article, and the adjective precedes “air.”

## Summary — Order, Idiom, and Emphasis

Okey often keeps Dante’s words but changes the order in which they reach the reader. A delayed subject comes forward in *s’appuntan li vostri disiri*, “your desires are centred”; a fronted object follows its verb in *prode acquistar*, “to gain profit”. Modifiers move closer to what they qualify: in “far less steep than the others”, for instance, the adjective no longer waits until after the comparison. Two routine adjustments generally go unmarked in the comparison: English supplies subject pronouns where the Italian verb ending identifies the subject, and drops the article before a possessive, as in *le tue cogitazion*, “thy thoughts”.

Changes of construction often shift the emphasis without changing the reference. In *non ti disfama*, ‘does not rid thee of hunger’, the person is the object; in “stays not thy hunger”, the hunger is. Similarly, the impersonal *più vi s’ama*, ‘more one loves there’, becomes “the more love is there”: an act is expressed as the presence of love. Okey frequently renders an imperfect by a progressive and an infinitive after a verb of seeing by a participle, as with *ancider*, “slaying”. With body parts, an Italian article often gives way to an English possessive: *le mani* becomes “my hands”.

Some additions make a relation more precise. In the optical comparison, *modo* becomes “angle”, and “the line of the falling stone” supplies “line” to name what the distance is measured from. Elsewhere a detail drops out: *ivi* has no separate equivalent before “in front of me”, and *salendo su* needs only “ascending”. Word choice can also alter an image: *lima*, ‘files away’, becomes “dulls”, while *gli occhi*, ‘the eyes’, becomes “sight” at the close. Yet Okey does not consistently smooth away unfamiliar phrasing. He keeps the paradox of *non falsi errori* in “not false errors” and follows both the image and the unusual order of *invidia move il mantaco ai sospiri* in “envy moves the bellows to your sighs”.
