# Purgatorio 25

## Lines 1–6 — An Ascent That Brooks No Delay

> 1 Ora era onde il salir non volea storpio,  

’Twas an hour when the ascent brooked no impediment,

- *Ora era* ‘an hour it was’ → “’Twas an hour”: the subject and verb come first, and English supplies the indefinite article.
- *onde* ‘on account of which’ → “when”: the causal relative becomes a temporal one.
- *il salir* ‘the going up’ → “the ascent”: a noun replaces the Italian infinitive used as a noun.
- *non volea storpio* ‘did not want hindrance’ → “brooked no impediment”: unwillingness is expressed as refusal to tolerate, and the negative shifts from the verb to the noun phrase.

> 2 chè il sole avea lo cerchio di merigge  
> 3 lasciato al Tauro e la notte allo Scorpio.  

for the sun to the Bull, and night to the Scorpion, had left the meridian circle.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating conjunction replaces the subordinating one.
- *il sole avea lo cerchio di merigge lasciato al Tauro e la notte allo Scorpio* ‘the sun had the circle of midday left to the Bull and the night to the Scorpion’ → “the sun to the Bull, and night to the Scorpion, had left the meridian circle”: each recipient now immediately follows its subject, and the auxiliary and participle are brought together after both pairs. The object moves from between the auxiliary and participle to the end; within it, the phrase with ‘of’ becomes a modifier before the noun. The article before ‘night’ is dropped.

> 4 Per che, come fa l' uom che non s' affigge,  

Wherefore as does a man who halts not,

- *Per che* ‘for which reason’ → “Wherefore”: a single connective replaces the prepositional phrase.
- *l’ uom* ‘the man’ → “a man”: the generic definite article becomes indefinite.
- *non s’ affigge* ‘does not fix himself in place’ → “halts not”: an intransitive verb replaces the reflexive construction, and the negative follows the verb.

> 5 ma vassi alla via sua, checchè gli appaia,  

but goes on his way whatever may appear to him,

- *vassi alla via sua* ‘betakes himself to his way’ → “goes on his way”: the pronominal verb becomes plain “goes”, the preposition changes from ‘to’ to “on”, and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *checchè gli appaia* ‘whatever to him appear’ → “whatever may appear to him”: “may” conveys the force of the subjunctive, and the indirect object follows the verb.

> 6 se di bisogno stimolo il trafigge:  

if the spur of necessity prick him,

- *di bisogno stimolo* ‘of need a spur’ → “the spur of necessity”: the modifying phrase follows the noun rather than preceding it, ‘need’ becomes “necessity”, and English gives the noun a definite article where Italian has none.
- *il trafigge* ‘him pierces’ → “prick him”: the object follows the verb, ‘pierce’ becomes “prick”, and the Italian present indicative becomes an English subjunctive after “if”.

## Lines 7–39 — The Question of the Shades’ Leanness

> 7 così entrammo noi per la callaia,  
> 8 uno innanzi altro, prendendo la scala  
> 9 che per artezza i salitor dispaia.  

so we entered by the gap, one in front of the other, mounting the stairway, which by its straitness parts the climbers.

- *entrammo noi* ‘entered we’ → “we entered”: the expressed subject moves before the verb.
- *per la callaia* ‘through the narrow passage’ → “by the gap”: the passage is called a “gap”, and “by” marks the way taken.
- *uno innanzi altro* ‘one before another’ → “one in front of the other”: the preposition is expanded and the second person is made definite.
- *prendendo la scala* ‘taking the stairway’ → “mounting the stairway”: the translation specifies the upward motion.
- *per artezza* ‘by narrowness’ → “by its straitness”: the possessive is supplied.
- *i salitor dispaia* ‘the climbers unpairs’ → “parts the climbers”: the verb moves before its object; the verb of separation loses the explicit sense of a pair.

> 10 E quale il cicognin che leva l' ala  
> 11 per voglia di volare, e non s' attenta  
> 12 d' abbandonar lo nido, e giù la cala:  

And like the little stork that lifts its wing through desire to fly, and, venturing not to abandon the nest, drops it down,

- *quale* ‘as’ → “like”: the comparison opens with a preposition rather than the first term of the Italian *quale … tal* construction.
- *il cicognin* ‘the storkling’ → “the little stork”: a separate adjective renders the diminutive suffix.
- *l' ala* ‘the wing’ → “its wing”: the possessive replaces the article.
- *non s' attenta* ‘does not venture’ → “venturing not”: a finite reflexive verb becomes a participle without a reflexive pronoun, and the negative follows it.
- *e giù la cala* ‘and down it lowers’ → “drops it down”: the conjunction is omitted, and the verb now precedes both pronoun and adverb.

> 13 tal era io con voglia accesa e spenta  
> 14 di domandar, venendo infino all' atto  
> 15 che fa colui ch' a dicer s' argomenta.  

even so was I with desire to ask kindled and quenched, going so far as the movement which he makes who is preparing to speak.

- *tal* ‘such’ → “even so”: the answering term of the comparison becomes an emphatic adverbial phrase.
- *con voglia accesa e spenta di domandar* ‘with desire kindled and quenched to ask’ → “with desire to ask kindled and quenched”: the infinitive is brought forward next to “desire”.
- *venendo infino all' atto* ‘coming as far as the gesture’ → “going so far as the movement”: “going” replaces ‘coming’, “so” is supplied, and the gesture is termed a “movement”.
- *che fa colui* ‘which makes he’ → “which he makes”: the subject moves before the verb, and a personal pronoun renders the demonstrative.
- *ch' a dicer s' argomenta* ‘who to speak prepares himself’ → “who is preparing to speak”: the reflexive present becomes a progressive, with the infinitive after it.

> 16 Non lasciò, per l' andar che fosse ratto,  
> 17 lo dolce padre mio, ma disse: “Scocca  
> 18 l' arco del dir che insino al ferro hai tratto.”  

My sweet Father did not cease, even though the pace was swift, but said: “Discharge the bow of thy speech which thou hast drawn to the iron.”

- *Non lasciò* ‘did not leave off’ → “did not cease”: the translation uses an intransitive verb and places it after its subject.
- *per l' andar che fosse ratto* ‘for all that the going might be swift’ → “even though the pace was swift”: a concessive conjunction replaces the construction with *per*; the substantivized infinitive becomes “the pace”, and the subjunctive gives way to an indicative.
- *lo dolce padre mio* ‘the sweet father of mine’ → “My sweet Father”: the whole subject is brought forward from after the concessive phrase, and the possessive precedes the adjective and noun.
- *del dir* ‘of speaking’ → “of thy speech”: a noun replaces the substantivized infinitive, and a possessive is supplied.
- *che insino al ferro hai tratto* ‘which to the iron thou hast drawn’ → “which thou hast drawn to the iron”: the phrase marking the limit of the draw moves after the verb.

> 19 Allor sicuramente aprii la bocca,  
> 20 e cominciai: “Come si può far magro  
> 21 là dove l' uopo di nutrir non tocca?”  

Then securely I opened my mouth, and began: “How can one grow lean there where the need of food is not felt?”

- *la bocca* ‘the mouth’ → “my mouth”: the possessive replaces the article.
- *si può far magro* ‘can one make oneself lean’ → “can one grow lean”: the impersonal construction takes “one” as subject, and a verb of becoming replaces the reflexive expression.
- *l' uopo di nutrir* ‘the need to nourish’ → “the need of food”: the infinitive denoting an act becomes a noun denoting what is consumed.
- *non tocca* ‘does not touch’ → “is not felt”: the active verb becomes a passive expression of the effect on the person concerned.

> 22 “Se t' ammentassi come Meleagro  
> 23 si consumò al consumar d' un stizzo,  
> 24 non fora,” disse, “questo a te sì agro;  

“If thou wouldst call to mind how Meleager was consumed at the consuming of a firebrand,” said he, “this would not be so difficult to thee;

- *t' ammentassi* ‘thou wert to remember’ → “thou wouldst call to mind”: the reflexive imperfect subjunctive is rendered with a modal and a verbal phrase.
- *si consumò* ‘wasted away’ → “was consumed”: the reflexive form becomes passive.
- *al consumar d' un stizzo* ‘at the burning away of a brand’ → “at the consuming of a firebrand”: the English keeps the verbal noun but uses “consuming” in the sense of being consumed.
- *non fora, disse, questo* ‘would not be, he said, this’ → “said he, “this would not be”: the reporting clause is moved ahead of the conclusion, its subject follows the verb, and “this” precedes its predicate.
- *a te sì agro* ‘to thee so sour’ → “so difficult to thee”: the figurative adjective is interpreted, and the phrase “to thee” follows it.

> 25 e se pensassi come al vostro guizzo  
> 26 guizza dentro allo specchio vostra image,  
> 27 ciò che par duro ti parrebbe vizzo.  

and if thou wouldst think how, to your every movement your image flits about in the mirror, that which seems hard would seem easy to thee.

- *pensassi* ‘thou wert to think’ → “thou wouldst think”: a modal renders the imperfect subjunctive.
- *al vostro guizzo* ‘at your flicker’ → “to your every movement”: the quick motion is generalized, and “every” is supplied.
- *guizza dentro allo specchio vostra image* ‘flickers within the mirror your image’ → “your image flits about in the mirror”: the subject moves from last to first; “flits about” varies the wording rather than repeating the preceding noun as a verb.
- *ti parrebbe vizzo* ‘to thee would seem soft’ → “would seem easy to thee”: the physical adjective is interpreted as the opposite of difficulty, and the person to whom it seems so is named last.

> 28 Ma perchè dentro a tuo voler t' adage,  
> 29 ecco qui Stazio, ed io lui chiamo e prego,  
> 30 che sia or sanator delle tue piage.”  

But in order that thou mayst find rest in thy desire, lo here Statius, and him I call and pray, that he now be the healer of thy wounds.”

- *dentro a tuo voler t' adage* ‘within thy wish thou mayst set thyself at ease’ → “thou mayst find rest in thy desire”: the reflexive verb becomes a verb with a noun object, and the phrase beginning with ‘within’ moves to the end.
- *ed io lui chiamo e prego* ‘and I him call and pray’ → “and him I call and pray”: the object pronoun moves before the expressed subject.
- *che sia or sanator* ‘that he be now healer’ → “that he now be the healer”: the adverb moves before the verb, and a definite article is supplied.

> 31 “Se la veduta eterna gli dislego,”  
> 32 rispose Stazio, “là dove tu sie,  
> 33 discolpi me non potert' io far nego.”  

“If,” answered Statius, “I unfold to him in thy presence the eternal things he has seen, let my excuse be that I may not deny thee.”

- *Se la veduta eterna gli dislego, rispose Stazio, là dove tu sie* ‘if the eternal sight to him I untie, answered Statius, there where thou art’ → “If,” answered Statius, “I unfold to him in thy presence the eternal things he has seen”: the reporting clause moves from after the verb to immediately after “If”. “I unfold to him” replaces the metaphor of untying and puts the recipient after the verb. The locative clause becomes the prepositional phrase “in thy presence”. The direct object moves from before the verb to the end: the singular noun of seeing is interpreted as the objects seen and becomes a plural noun with a relative clause and an expressed seer.
- *discolpi me* ‘may it excuse me’ → “let my excuse be”: the verb with a personal object becomes a noun with a possessive in a construction with “let”.
- *non potert' io far nego* ‘my not being able to make refusal to thee’ → “that I may not deny thee”: the infinitival subject becomes a finite clause, and the phrase for making a refusal becomes a transitive verb.

> 34 Poi cominciò: “Se le parole mie,  
> 35 figlio, la mente tua guarda e riceve,  
> 36 lume ti fieno al come che tu die.  

Then he began: “Son, if thy mind heed and receive my words, they shall be a light unto thee on the how which thou utterest.

- *Se le parole mie, figlio,* ‘if the words of mine, son’ → “Son, if”: the vocative moves ahead of the condition; “my words” is postponed until after both verbs, with the possessive now before the noun.
- *la mente tua guarda e riceve* ‘the mind of thine heeds and receives’ → “thy mind heed and receive”: the possessive precedes the noun, and the uninflected English subjunctives replace Italian indicatives.
- *lume ti fieno* ‘a light to thee they shall be’ → “they shall be a light unto thee”: the copula precedes its complement and the phrase naming the recipient.
- *al come* ‘as to the how’ → “on the how”: “on” marks the question as the subject on which light is cast.

> 37 Sangue perfetto, che mai non si beve  
> 38 dall' assetate vene, e si rimane  
> 39 quasi alimento che di mensa leve,  

Perfect blood, which never is drunk by the thirsty veins, and is left behind, as ’twere food which thou removest from the table,

- *Sangue perfetto* ‘blood perfect’ → “Perfect blood”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *mai non si beve* ‘never does one drink’ → “never is drunk”: the English takes *si* passively and renders Italian negative concord with the single negative “never”.
- *si rimane* ‘remains’ → “is left behind”: the intransitive expression becomes passive, with “behind” making the sense of remainder explicit.
- *quasi alimento* ‘like food’ → “as ’twere food”: the comparison is expanded into a clause.
- *che di mensa leve* ‘which from table thou mightst remove’ → “which thou removest from the table”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase, the indicative replaces the subjunctive, and the table takes a definite article.

## Lines 40–72 — The Formation of the Human Soul

> 40 prende nel core a tutte membra umane  
> 41 virtute informativa, come quello  
> 42 ch' a farsi quelle per le vene vane.  

acquires in the heart a virtue potent to inform all human members, like that blood which flows through the veins to become those.

- *prende* ‘takes’ → “acquires”: the verb is rendered in the sense of coming to possess.
- *a tutte membra umane virtute informativa* ‘for all members human a virtue formative’ → “a virtue potent to inform all human members”: the noun phrase comes first; its adjective is expanded into an adjective and infinitive, with the members as the direct object of the infinitive. “human” precedes its noun.
- *quello* ‘that’ → “that blood”: the noun to which the demonstrative refers is supplied.
- *a farsi quelle per le vene vane* ‘to make itself those through the veins goes’ → “flows through the veins to become those”: the verb of motion comes first and is made specific to blood; the phrase of purpose comes last, with a verb of becoming for the Italian reflexive.

> 43 Ancor digesto, scende ov' è più bello  
> 44 tacer che dire; e quindi poscia geme  
> 45 sopr' altrui sangue in natural vasello.  

Refined yet again, it descends there whereof to be silent is more seemly than to speak, and thence afterwards distils upon other’s blood, in natural vessel.

- *Ancor digesto* ‘further digested’ → “Refined yet again”: the participle is interpreted as refinement and placed before the adverb.
- *ov' è più bello tacer che dire* ‘where it is more beautiful to keep silent than to speak’ → “there whereof to be silent is more seemly than to speak”: the relative of place is expanded into a destination and a relative meaning ‘of which’; the first infinitive becomes the subject and precedes the predicate. Beauty is rendered as seemliness.

> 46 Ivi s' accoglie l' uno e l' altro insieme,  
> 47 l' un disposto a patire e l' altro a fare,  
> 48 per lo perfetto loco onde si preme;  

There the one is mingled with the other; one designed to be passive, the other to be active, by reason of the perfect place whence it springs;

- *s' accoglie l' uno e l' altro insieme* ‘the one and the other come together’ → “the one is mingled with the other”: the postposed pair becomes a subject before a passive verb and a complement with “with”; the adverb of union is absorbed into that construction.
- *l' un disposto a patire e l' altro a fare* ‘the one disposed to undergo and the other to act’ → “one designed to be passive, the other to be active”: the first article and the conjunction drop out; disposition becomes design, and the two verbal infinitives become forms of ‘be’ with contrasting adjectives.
- *per* ‘on account of’ → “by reason of”: a prepositional phrase replaces the single preposition.
- *si preme* ‘it is pressed out’ → “it springs”: an intransitive verb of emergence replaces the passive expression.

> 49 e, giunto lui, comincia ad operare,  
> 50 coagulando prima, e poi avviva  
> 51 ciò che per sua materia fe' constare.  

and, joined thereto, it begins to operate, first coagulating, and then giving life to that which it had solidified for its own material.

- *giunto lui* ‘joined to it’ → “joined thereto”: the relation to the pronoun is expressed by a compound adverb.
- *coagulando prima* ‘coagulating first’ → “first coagulating”: the adverb precedes the participle.
- *e poi avviva* ‘and then it quickens’ → “and then giving life to”: the finite verb becomes a participial phrase parallel to “coagulating”; its object is introduced by “to”.
- *per sua materia fe' constare* ‘for its material it caused to set’ → “it had solidified for its own material”: the causative verb and infinitive become one transitive verb, the simple past becomes a past perfect, and the material phrase comes last, with “own” emphasizing the possessive.

> 52 Anima fatta la virtute attiva,  
> 53 qual d' una pianta, in tanto differente,  
> 54 che quest' è in via e quella è già a riva,  

The active virtue having become a soul, like that of a plant, in so far different that the former is on the way, and the latter is already at the goal,

- *Anima fatta la virtute attiva* ‘a soul become, the virtue active’ → “The active virtue having become a soul”: the subject comes first, with its adjective before the noun; the bare participle becomes a perfect participial construction, followed by its complement.
- *qual d' una pianta* ‘like [the soul] of a plant’ → “like that of a plant”: “that” fills the Italian ellipsis.
- *quest' … quella* ‘this one … that one’ → “the former” … “the latter”: relative order of mention replaces the demonstrative contrast.
- *è già a riva* ‘is already at shore’ → “is already at the goal”: the image of the shore is rendered as an endpoint.

> 55 tanto opra poi che già si move e sente,  
> 56 come fungo marino; ed indi imprende  
> 57 ad organar le posse ond' è semente.  

then effects so much that now it moves and feels, like a sea-fungus; and then sets about developing organs for the powers whereof it is the germ.

- *tanto opra poi* ‘so much it effects then’ → “then effects so much”: the time adverb moves to the front and the expression of degree follows the verb.
- *già si move* ‘already it moves itself’ → “now it moves”: the time adverb shifts from ‘already’ to ‘now’, and the English verb is intransitive rather than reflexive.
- *come fungo marino* ‘like marine fungus’ → “like a sea-fungus”: an article is supplied and the following adjective becomes the first element of a compound noun.
- *indi* ‘thence’ → “then”: the adverb is taken temporally.
- *imprende ad organar le posse* ‘undertakes to organize the powers’ → “sets about developing organs for the powers”: the infinitive becomes a participle after “sets about”; organs are named explicitly as what is developed, and the powers become the complement of “for” rather than the direct object.
- *semente* ‘seed’ → “the germ”: the noun is rendered by a related term and given a definite article.

> 58 Or si spiega, figliuolo, or si distende  
> 59 la virtù ch' è dal cor del generante,  
> 60 ove natura a tutte membra intende;  

Now, son, expands, now distends, the virtue which proceeds from the heart of the begetter, where nature intends all human members;

- *si spiega, figliuolo* ‘unfolds itself, little son’ → “son, expands”: the vocative moves before the verb and loses the diminutive; an intransitive verb replaces the reflexive.
- *si distende* ‘stretches itself out’ → “distends”: the reflexive is rendered by a single verb without a pronoun.
- *è dal cor* ‘is from the heart’ → “proceeds from the heart”: the verb of being becomes a verb of motion from a source.
- *del generante* ‘of the one begetting’ → “of the begetter”: the substantivized participle becomes an agent noun.
- *a tutte membra intende* ‘directs itself toward all members’ → “intends all human members”: the verb precedes a direct object rather than following a prepositional complement; “human” is supplied.

> 61 ma come d' animal divegna fante,  
> 62 non vedi tu ancor; quest' è tal punto  
> 63 che più savio di te fe' già errante:  

but how from an animal it becomes a human being thou seest not yet; this is that point which made one wiser than thou to err;

- *d' animal* ‘from animal’ → “from an animal”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *fante* ‘a speaking being’ → “a human being”: the noun is rendered by what it designates rather than by its reference to speech.
- *non vedi tu ancor* ‘not seest thou yet’ → “thou seest not yet”: the emphatic object pronoun implicit in the preceding clause is not repeated in English; the expressed subject precedes the verb and the negative follows it.
- *tal punto* ‘such a point’ → “that point”: a demonstrative replaces the expression of kind.
- *più savio di te fe' già errante* ‘one wiser than thee it once made erring’ → “made one wiser than thou to err”: the verb precedes its object, the comparison takes “than thou” rather than a preposition with an oblique pronoun, and an infinitive replaces the participial complement; the time adverb is omitted.

> 64 sì che, per sua dottrina, fe' disgiunto  
> 65 dall' anima il possibile intelletto,  
> 66 perchè da lui non vide organo assunto.  

so that by his teaching he made the intellectual faculty separate from the soul, because he saw no organ occupied by it.

- *fe' disgiunto dall' anima il possibile intelletto* ‘made disjoined from the soul the possible intellect’ → “he made the intellectual faculty separate from the soul”: the object precedes its complement. The technical expression is interpreted as a faculty: the adjective of possibility is no longer separately rendered, and the noun denoting intellect becomes an adjective qualifying “faculty”.
- *da lui non vide organo assunto* ‘by it he did not see an organ taken up’ → “he saw no organ occupied by it”: the negative shifts from the verb to the noun, the initial agent phrase moves after the participle, and taking up an organ is rendered as occupying it.

> 67 Apri alla verità che viene il petto,  

Open thy breast to the truth which is coming,

- *Apri alla verità che viene il petto* ‘open to the truth that comes the breast’ → “Open thy breast to the truth which is coming”: the direct object moves next to the verb, the article becomes a possessive, and the simple present becomes progressive.

> 68 e sappi che, sì tosto come al feto  
> 69 l' articular del cerebro è perfetto,  

and know that so soon as the organisation of the brain is perfect in the embryo,

- *al feto* ‘in the fetus’ → “in the embryo”: the phrase is moved from before the subject to after the predicate, and a different developmental term is used.
- *l' articular* ‘the articulating’ → “the organisation”: the substantivized infinitive becomes an abstract noun, with articulation interpreted as organisation.

> 70 lo Motor primo a lui si volge, lieto  
> 71 sopra tanta arte di natura, e spira  
> 72 spirito nuovo di virtù repleto,  

the First Mover turns him to it, rejoicing over such handiwork of nature, and breathes into it a new spirit with virtue filled,

- *lo Motor primo* ‘the Mover first’ → “the First Mover”: the adjective precedes its noun.
- *a lui si volge* ‘to it turns himself’ → “turns him to it”: the directional complement moves after the verb, and the reflexive is expressed by the older English use of “him” for ‘himself’.
- *lieto* ‘glad’ → “rejoicing”: the adjective becomes a participle.
- *tanta arte* ‘so great an art’ → “such handiwork”: degree is expressed by “such”, and art by the work produced.
- *spira* ‘breathes’ → “breathes into it”: the destination is made explicit.
- *spirito nuovo* ‘a spirit new’ → “a new spirit”: English supplies the article and puts the adjective first.
- *di virtù repleto* ‘of virtue full’ → “with virtue filled”: fullness is expressed by a participle with “with” rather than by an adjective with ‘of’.

## Lines 73–105 — The Soul and Its New Form

> 73 che ciò che trova attivo quivi tira  
> 74 in sua sustanzia, e fassi un' alma sola,  
> 75 che vive e sente, e sè in sè rigira.  

which draws into its substance that which it finds active there, and becomes one single soul, that lives, and feels, and turns round upon itself.

- *che ciò che trova attivo quivi tira in sua sustanzia* ‘which that which it finds active there draws into its substance’ → “which draws into its substance that which it finds active there”: the verb and the phrase of direction both move ahead of the long direct object.
- *fassi un' alma sola* ‘makes itself a soul single’ → “becomes one single soul”: the reflexive becomes a verb of becoming; the adjective moves before the noun, and the article is rendered as the numeral “one”.
- *e sè in sè rigira* ‘and itself in itself it turns round’ → “and turns round upon itself”: the verb moves forward; English uses “turns round” without a direct object and renders the second reflexive in a phrase with “upon” rather than ‘in’.

> 76 E perchè meno ammiri la parola,  
> 77 guarda il calor del sol che si fa vino,  
> 78 giunto all' umor che dalla vite cola.  

And that thou mayst marvel less at my words, look at the sun’s heat, that is made wine when combined with the juice which flows from the vine.

- *perchè meno ammiri* ‘in order that less thou marvel’ → “that thou mayst marvel less”: “mayst” expresses the purpose subjunctive, and the adverb follows the verb.
- *la parola* ‘the word’ → “at my words”: English supplies the preposition after “marvel”, substitutes a possessive for the article, and uses the plural.
- *guarda il calor del sol* ‘behold the heat of the sun’ → “look at the sun’s heat”: the transitive imperative becomes a verb with a preposition, and the possessive precedes the thing possessed.
- *si fa vino* ‘makes itself wine’ → “is made wine”: the reflexive construction of becoming is rendered as a passive.
- *giunto all' umor* ‘joined to the moisture’ → “when combined with the juice”: the bare participle acquires a temporal conjunction; “combined with” replaces ‘joined to’, and “juice” specifies the liquid.
- *che dalla vite cola* ‘which from the vine flows’ → “which flows from the vine”: the verb precedes the phrase of source.

> 79 E quando Lachesis non ha più lino,  
> 80 solvesi dalla carne, ed in virtute  
> 81 ne seco porta e l' umano e il divino:  

And when Lachesis has no more thread, it frees itself from the flesh, and bears away in potency both the human and the divine;

- *non ha più lino* ‘has flax no longer’ → “has no more thread”: the negation is attached to the quantity possessed, and the material is named by what is made of it.
- *solvesi* ‘loosens itself’ → “it frees itself”: the English verb expresses release rather than loosening.
- *in virtute ne seco porta* ‘in potency thence with itself it carries’ → “bears away in potency”: the verb comes first; “away” conveys the movement from there, but ‘with itself’ has no separate equivalent.
- *e l' umano e il divino* ‘and the human and the divine’ → “both the human and the divine”: the first of the paired conjunctions becomes “both”.

> 82 l' altre potenze, tutte quante mute;  
> 83 memoria, intelligenza e volontade,  
> 84 in atto molto più che prima acute.  

the other powers, the whole of them mute; memory, intelligence and will, keener far in action than they were before.

- *tutte quante mute* ‘all of them mute’ → “the whole of them mute”: the emphatic plural expression of totality becomes a collective singular with “of them”.
- *in atto molto più che prima acute* ‘in act much more than before keen’ → “keener far in action than they were before”: the final adjective moves to the front and takes the comparative ending; “far” expresses the degree, “in action” follows rather than precedes the comparison, and the abbreviated ‘than before’ becomes a finite clause.

> 85 Senz' arrestarsi, per sè stessa cade  
> 86 mirabilmente all' una delle rive;  
> 87 quivi conosce prima le sue strade.  

Staying not, it falls of itself in wondrous wise to one of the shores; there it first learns its ways.

- *Senz' arrestarsi* ‘without stopping oneself’ → “Staying not”: a preposition with a reflexive infinitive becomes a negated participle without a reflexive object.
- *per sè stessa cade* ‘by its own self it falls’ → “it falls of itself”: the verb moves before the reflexive phrase, and ‘by’ becomes “of”.
- *mirabilmente* ‘wondrously’ → “in wondrous wise”: a single adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *all' una delle rive* ‘to the one of the shores’ → “to one of the shores”: English drops the article before ‘one’.
- *conosce prima* ‘knows first’ → “first learns”: the adverb moves before the verb, and “learns” expresses coming to know.

> 88 Tosto che loco lì la circonscrive,  
> 89 la virtù formativa raggia intorno,  
> 90 così e quanto nelle membra vive;  

Soon as it is circumscribed in place there, the formative virtue radiates around, in form and quantity as in the living members;

- *loco lì la circonscrive* ‘place there circumscribes it’ → “it is circumscribed in place there”: the object becomes the subject of a passive, ‘place’ becomes a phrase with “in” rather than the agent, and “there” moves to the end.
- *la virtù formativa* ‘the virtue formative’ → “the formative virtue”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *così e quanto* ‘thus and as much’ → “in form and quantity as”: Okey interprets the two terms of comparison as specifying form and extent, supplying nouns for both and placing “as” after the pair.
- *nelle membra vive* ‘in the members living’ → “in the living members”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 91 e come l' aer, quand' è ben piorno,  
> 92 per l' altrui raggio che in sè si riflette  
> 93 di diversi color diventa adorno,  

and as the air, when it is full saturate, becomes decked with divers colours through another’s rays which are reflected in it,

- *ben piorno* ‘well laden with moisture’ → “full saturate”: the adjective becomes “saturate”, intensified by “full” for ‘well’.
- *per l' altrui raggio che in sè si riflette* ‘through the ray of another which in it reflects itself’ → “through another’s rays which are reflected in it”: the possessive comes first, the singular ray becomes plural, and the reflexive verb becomes passive with “in it” following. The entire phrase of cause moves after the statement of the effect.
- *di diversi color diventa adorno* ‘with diverse colours becomes adorned’ → “becomes decked with divers colours”: the verb and its complement move ahead of the colour phrase, and ‘adorned’ becomes “decked”.

> 94 così l' aer vicin quivi si mette  
> 95 in quella forma che in lui suggella  
> 96 virtualmente l' alma che ristette;  

so the neighbouring air sets itself into that form which the soul that is there fixed impresses upon it by means of its virtue;

- *l' aer vicin quivi si mette in quella forma* ‘the air neighbouring there sets itself in that form’ → “the neighbouring air sets itself into that form”: the adjective moves before the noun, the first ‘there’ is not separately rendered, and “into” makes the change of form explicit.
- *che in lui suggella virtualmente l' alma che ristette* ‘which in it seals by its power the soul that came to rest’ → “which the soul that is there fixed impresses upon it by means of its virtue”: the subject moves before the verb; the simple past of coming to rest becomes a present state with an explicit “there”. The image of sealing becomes “impresses upon”, with the pronoun after the verb, and the adverb of effective power is expanded into a phrase of means.

> 97 e simigliante poi alla fiammella  
> 98 che segue il fuoco là 'vunque si muta,  
> 99 segue allo spirto sua forma novella.  

and then, like the flame which follows the fire wheresoever it moves, the spirit is followed by its new form.

- *e simigliante poi alla fiammella* ‘and similar then to the little flame’ → “and then, like the flame”: “then” moves ahead of the comparison, “like” replaces the adjective with its preposition, and the diminutive is lost.
- *là 'vunque si muta* ‘there wherever it shifts itself’ → “wheresoever it moves”: the locative expressions are combined, and the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb of motion.
- *segue allo spirto sua forma novella* ‘follows upon the spirit its form new’ → “the spirit is followed by its new form”: the active clause with its subject last becomes a passive with the spirit as subject; the adjective precedes “form”.

> 100 Però che quindi ha poscia sua paruta,  
> 101 è chiamat' ombra; e quindi organa poi  
> 102 ciascun sentire infino alla veduta.  

Inasmuch as therefrom it afterwards has its semblance, it is called a shade; and therefrom it forms the organs of every sense even to sight.

- *è chiamat' ombra* ‘is called shade’ → “it is called a shade”: English supplies an indefinite article for the predicate noun.
- *organa poi* ‘it then furnishes with organs’ → “it forms the organs of”: what the Italian expresses in the verb becomes a verb with an explicit noun, and this second ‘then’ is not rendered.
- *ciascun sentire* ‘each sensing’ → “every sense”: the substantival infinitive denoting the activity is rendered by a noun for the faculty.
- *infino alla veduta* ‘as far as the sight’ → “even to sight”: “even” emphasizes the endpoint, and the abstract noun loses its article.

> 103 Quindi parliamo, e quindi ridiam noi,  
> 104 quindi facciam le lagrime e i sospiri  
> 105 che per lo monte aver sentiti puoi.  

By this we speak, and by this we laugh, by this we make the tears and the sighs which thou mayst have heard about the mount.

- *Quindi parliamo* ‘thence we speak’ → “By this we speak”: the adverb of source becomes a demonstrative phrase of means.
- *e quindi ridiam noi* ‘and thence laugh we’ → “and by this we laugh”: the same instrumental rendering recurs, and the explicit subject moves before the verb.
- *quindi facciam* ‘thence we make’ → “by this we make”: the adverb is again rendered as a phrase of means.
- *per lo monte aver sentiti puoi* ‘about the mount have heard thou canst’ → “thou mayst have heard about the mount”: the modal moves before the perfect infinitive and expresses possibility; the phrase of place moves to the end, and the English participle does not mark agreement with the preceding objects.

## Lines 106–139 — Shades and Singing in the Flames

> 106 Secondo che ci affliggono i disiri  
> 107 e gli altri affetti, l' ombra si figura;  
> 108 e questa è la cagion di che tu ammiri.”  

The shade takes its form according as the desires and the other affections prick us, and this is the cause of that whereof thou marvellest.”

- *Secondo che ci affliggono i disiri e gli altri affetti* ‘according as us afflict the desires and the other affections’ → “according as the desires and the other affections prick us”: the clause moves after the main clause; within it the subjects move before the verb and the object after it. ‘Afflict’ becomes “prick”.
- *l' ombra si figura* ‘the shade shapes itself’ → “The shade takes its form”: the reflexive verb becomes a verb with a possessive and a noun.
- *di che tu ammiri* ‘of what thou marvellest at’ → “of that whereof thou marvellest”: English makes the antecedent “that” explicit and expresses the relation to the verb by “whereof”.

> 109 E già venuto all' ultima tortura  
> 110 s' era per noi, e volto alla man destra,  
> 111 ed eravamo attenti ad altra cura.  

And now had we come to the last turning, and had wheeled round to the right hand, and were intent on other care.

- *già* ‘already’ → “now”: the time adverb marks the point reached rather than priority.
- *venuto all' ultima tortura s' era per noi* ‘come to the last turning had it been on our part’ → “had we come to the last turning”: the impersonal construction becomes personal, with “we” as subject; the auxiliary moves from after the destination to before the participle.
- *e volto* ‘and turned’ → “and had wheeled round”: the auxiliary, shared in Italian, is repeated; the single participle becomes “wheeled round”.
- *alla man destra* ‘to the hand right’ → “to the right hand”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 112 Quivi la ripa fiamma in fuor balestra,  
> 113 e la cornice spira fiato in suso,  
> 114 che la riflette, e via da lei sequestra:  

There the bank flashes forth flames, and the cornice breathes a blast upward, which bends them back, and keeps them away from it;

- *fiamma in fuor balestra* ‘flame outward shoots’ → “flashes forth flames”: the verb moves before its object, ‘shoots’ becomes “flashes”, and the singular becomes plural.
- *fiato* ‘breath’ → “a blast”: the unarticled noun becomes a count noun with an indefinite article.
- *la riflette* ‘it turns back’ → “bends them back”: the object follows the verb and becomes plural to agree with “flames”; the turning back is expressed as bending.
- *via da lei sequestra* ‘away from her separates’ → “keeps them away from it”: separation is expressed as continued exclusion; the verb comes first, its understood object is repeated, and the feminine pronoun becomes neuter.

> 115 onde ir ne convenìa dal lato schiuso  
> 116 ad uno ad uno; ed io temeva il foco  
> 117 quinci, e quindi temea cadere in giuso.  

wherefore it behoved us to go on the side which was free one by one; and on this side I feared the fire, and on that I feared to fall downward.

- *ir ne convenìa* ‘to go us it behoved’ → “it behoved us to go”: the impersonal verb with its formal subject comes first, followed by the pronoun and the infinitive.
- *dal lato schiuso* ‘on the open side’ → “on the side which was free”: the adjective becomes a relative clause, with “free” for ‘open’.
- *io temeva il foco quinci* ‘I feared the fire on this side’ → “on this side I feared the fire”: the phrase of place moves from after the object to before the subject.
- *quindi* ‘on that side’ → “on that”: “side” is understood from the preceding phrase.

> 118 Lo duca mio dicea: “Per questo loco  
> 119 si vuol tenere agli occhi stretto il freno,  
> 120 però ch' errar potrebbesi per poco.”  

My Leader said: “Along this place the rein must be kept tight on the eyes, because lightly a false step might be taken.”

- *Lo duca mio* ‘the leader mine’ → “My Leader”: the postposed possessive precedes the noun.
- *dicea* ‘was saying’ → “said”: the imperfect is given by the simple past.
- *Per questo loco* ‘through this place’ → “Along this place”: the preposition expresses movement along rather than through.
- *si vuol tenere agli occhi stretto il freno* ‘one must hold on the eyes tight the rein’ → “the rein must be kept tight on the eyes”: the impersonal construction becomes passive, with the object as subject; “kept” replaces ‘hold’, and the phrase about the eyes follows “tight”.
- *errar potrebbesi per poco* ‘one might err for very little’ → “lightly a false step might be taken”: ‘err’ becomes the taking of a step, and the impersonal potential becomes a modal passive; the final phrase becomes an initial adverb.

> 121 “*Summae Deus clementiae*” nel seno  
> 122 al grande ardore allora udii cantando,  
> 123 che di volger mi fe' caler non meno;  

“*Summae Deus clementiae*” I then heard sung in the heart of the great burning, which made me no less eager to turn aside;

- *nel seno al grande ardore* ‘in the bosom of the great heat’ → “in the heart of the great burning”: “heart” replaces the bodily image ‘bosom’, “burning” replaces ‘heat’, and the phrase moves after the verb of hearing.
- *allora udii cantando* ‘then I heard singing’ → “I then heard sung”: the active gerund becomes a passive participle describing the hymn.
- *che di volger mi fe' caler non meno* ‘which for turning made me care no less’ → “which made me no less eager to turn aside”: the infinitive ‘care’ becomes the adjective “eager”; the degree phrase precedes the adjective, and the act of turning comes last, with “aside” supplied.

> 124 e vidi spirti per la fiamma andando:  
> 125 per ch' io guardava loro ed a' miei passi,  
> 126 compartendo la vista a quando a quando.  

and I saw spirits going through the flames; wherefore I looked at them and at my steps, with divided gaze from time to time.

- *per la fiamma andando* ‘through the flame going’ → “going through the flames”: the participle comes first and the singular becomes plural.
- *guardava loro ed a' miei passi* ‘was watching them and looking to my steps’ → “looked at them and at my steps”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and English uses “at” for both objects where only the second has a preposition in Italian.
- *compartendo la vista* ‘dividing the sight’ → “with divided gaze”: the active gerund becomes a prepositional phrase with a participial adjective; ‘the sight’ becomes unarticled “gaze”.
- *a quando a quando* ‘now and again’ → “from time to time”: a different repeated expression gives the interval.

> 127 Appresso il fine ch' a quell' inno fassi,  
> 128 gridavano alto: “*Virum non cognosco*”;  
> 129 indi ricominciavan l' inno bassi.  

After the end which is made to that hymn, they cried aloud: “*Virum non cognosco*”; then softly began the hymn again.

- *ch' a quell' inno fassi* ‘which to that hymn is made’ → “which is made to that hymn”: the passive sense of the Italian construction with *si* is expressed by an auxiliary and participle, and the phrase about the hymn follows the verb.
- *gridavano alto* ‘they would cry high’ → “they cried aloud”: the repeated action of the imperfect is given by the simple past; “aloud” renders the adverbial adjective.
- *ricominciavan l' inno bassi* ‘they would begin the hymn again low’ → “softly began the hymn again”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the sense of the verbal prefix is expressed separately after the object. The final plural adjective describing the singers becomes an adverb before the verb.

> 130 Finitolo, anco gridavano: “Al bosco  
> 131 si tenne Diana, ed Elice caccionne  
> 132 che di Venere avea sentito il tosco.”  

It being finished, they further cried: “Diana kept in the wood, and chased Helice forth who had felt the poison of Venus.”

- *Finitolo* ‘having finished it’ → “It being finished”: the participle with an attached object pronoun becomes a passive absolute construction.
- *anco gridavano* ‘again they would cry’ → “they further cried”: “further” marks addition, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *Al bosco si tenne Diana* ‘to the wood kept herself Diana’ → “Diana kept in the wood”: the subject comes first; the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb, and the initial prepositional phrase becomes a phrase of location after it.
- *Elice caccionne* ‘Helice she drove thence’ → “chased Helice forth”: the object moves after the verb; “forth” gives the outward movement without the explicit reference back to the place in *-ne*.
- *di Venere avea sentito il tosco* ‘of Venus had felt the poison’ → “had felt the poison of Venus”: the possessive phrase moves after its noun.

> 133 Indi al cantar tornavano; indi donne  
> 134 gridavano e mariti che fur casti,  
> 135 come virtute e matrimonio imponne.  

Then turned they to their chanting; then cried they women and husbands who were chaste, as virtue and marriage require of us.

- *al cantar tornavano* ‘to the singing they would return’ → “turned they to their chanting”: the verb comes first and the imperfect becomes a simple past; ‘return’ becomes “turned”, and the substantivized infinitive with an article becomes a verbal noun with a possessive.
- *donne gridavano e mariti* ‘women they would cry and husbands’ → “cried they women and husbands”: the verb moves before both objects, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *imponne* ‘lay upon us’ → “require of us”: the construction changes from imposing something upon someone to requiring it of someone; the attached pronoun becomes a separate prepositional complement.

> 136 E questo modo credo che lor basti  
> 137 per tutto il tempo che il foco gli abbrucia:  

And this fashion I think suffices them for all the time the fire burns them:

- *questo modo credo che lor basti* ‘this manner I believe that for them it may suffice’ → “this fashion I think suffices them”: “fashion” renders ‘manner’ and “think” renders ‘believe’; the explicit subordinate construction becomes a clause with parenthetical “I think”, and the subjunctive becomes indicative, with the pronoun after the verb.
- *che* ‘that’ → “all the time the fire burns them”: English leaves the temporal clause unmarked after “time”.

> 138 con tal cura convien, con cotai pasti  
> 139 che la piaga dassezzo si ricucia.  

with such treatment, and with such diet, must the last wound be healed.

- *con tal cura convien, con cotai pasti* ‘with such care it is necessary, with such meals’ → “with such treatment, and with such diet, must”: “treatment” renders ‘care’, and the plural ‘meals’ becomes the collective singular “diet”. The two phrases are joined by an added conjunction, and the intervening impersonal verb becomes a modal following both.
- *che la piaga dassezzo si ricucia* ‘that the wound at last be sewn up’ → “the last wound be healed”: the subordinate clause becomes the subject and passive infinitive of the modal construction. The adverb ‘at last’ is taken adjectivally with “wound”, and the image of stitching up is replaced by its result.

## Summary — Reordering the Phrase, Keeping the Pattern

Okey often brings the subject forward and places the object after the verb: *entrammo noi* ‘entered we’ becomes “we entered”, and *i salitor dispaia* ‘the climbers unpairs’ becomes “parts the climbers”. Adjectives, too, commonly move before their nouns, as in *la virtù formativa*, “the formative virtue”. But he does not regularize every inversion: “even so was I” keeps the order of *tal era io*, and “Then turned they” puts the verb before the subject.

Changes of construction often alter which word carries the sense. The substantivized infinitive *il salir* becomes the noun “the ascent”; the reflexive *si figura* ‘shapes itself’ becomes “takes its form”; and the gerund and its object in *compartendo la vista* ‘dividing the sight’ become “with divided gaze”. At times Okey gives the force of an image rather than its wording: *agro* ‘sour’ becomes “difficult”, and *a riva* ‘at shore’ becomes “at the goal”.

Some additions make a reference explicit: *quello* becomes “that blood”, and *per artezza* ‘by narrowness’ becomes “by its straitness”. Elsewhere a distinction drops out: “the flame” does not mark the diminutive in *fiammella*, and the second *poi* in *organa poi* has no separate equivalent. Two routine adjustments generally go unremarked in the comparison: English supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending, and drops the article before a possessive, as when *la mente tua* becomes “thy mind”.

Okey stays particularly close where repetition gives a passage its shape. He renders *quindi* three times by “by this”, preserving the repeated opening even as he expresses means rather than source. He also retains the linked images of bow and iron in *arco* and *ferro*, and even the unusual noun-like use of *come* in “the how”.
