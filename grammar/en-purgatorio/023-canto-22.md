# Purgatorio 22

## Lines 1–12 — The Ascent and the Kindling of Love

> 1 Già era l' angel retro a noi rimaso,  

Already was the angel left behind us,

- *era l' angel retro a noi rimaso* ‘had the angel behind us remained’ → “was the angel left behind us”: the Italian pluperfect of ‘remain’ becomes a passive construction, and the participle moves ahead of the phrase of place.

> 2 l' angel che n' avea volti al sesto giro,  

the angel that had turned us to the sixth circle,

- *n' avea volti* ‘us had turned’ → “had turned us”: the object pronoun follows the verb instead of preceding the auxiliary.
- *giro* ‘circuit’ → “circle”: the English names the shape rather than the course around it.

> 3 avendomi dal viso un colpo raso;  

having erased a scar from my face,

- *avendomi dal viso un colpo raso* ‘having for me from the face a stroke scraped away’ → “having erased a scar from my face”: the participle moves up beside the auxiliary, and its object now precedes the phrase of place. The Italian dative marking the person affected becomes the possessive “my”; the act of scraping away becomes erasure, and the stroke is rendered as the mark it left.

> 4 e quei ch' hanno a giustizia lor disiro,  
> 5 detto n' avea beati, e le sue voci  
> 6 con *sitiunt*, senz' altro, ciò forniro.  

and had said to us that those who have their desire to righteousness were blessed, and his words accomplished that with *sitiunt*, and naught else.

- *quei ch' hanno a giustizia lor disiro* ‘those who have toward justice their desire’ → “those who have their desire to righteousness”: the phrase giving the direction of desire follows the object instead of preceding it, and ‘justice’ is rendered as “righteousness”.
- *detto n' avea beati* ‘called to us had he blessed’ → “had said to us that” … “were blessed”: the Italian places those called blessed before the verb, puts the participle before the auxiliary, and uses ‘blessed’ as an object complement. English puts the verb of saying first, moves the recipient after it, and supplies “that” and “were” to make a reported clause.
- *voci* ‘voices’ → “words”: the utterance is rendered in terms of what was said.
- *con sitiunt, senz' altro* ‘with sitiunt, without aught else’ → “with *sitiunt*, and naught else”: the phrase moves to the end, and a conjunction replaces the preposition of exclusion.
- *ciò forniro* ‘that they accomplished’ → “accomplished that”: the demonstrative object follows the verb.

> 7 Ed io, più lieve che per l' altre foci,  
> 8 m' andava sì che senza alcun labore  
> 9 seguiva in su gli spiriti veloci;  

And I, lighter than by the other passages, went on so that without any toil I was following the fleet spirits upward,

- *più lieve che per l' altre foci* ‘lighter than through the other openings’ → “lighter than by the other passages”: the openings are rendered as routes, with “by” in place of ‘through’.
- *m' andava* ‘I was going my way’ → “went on”: the pronominal expression of motion becomes a verb with “on”, and the imperfect is rendered by a simple past.
- *seguiva in su gli spiriti veloci* ‘I followed upward the spirits swift’ → “I was following the fleet spirits upward”: the English progressive brings out the ongoing action of the imperfect; the directional phrase moves after the object, and the adjective moves before its noun.

> 10 quando Virgilio cominciò: “Amore,  
> 11 acceso di virtù, sempre altro accese,  
> 12 pur che la fiamma sua paresse fuore:  

when Virgil began: “Love, kindled by virtue, hath ever kindled other love, if but its flame were shown forth:

- *sempre altro accese* ‘always another kindled’ → “hath ever kindled other love”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, the adverb moves between auxiliary and participle, and the object follows the verb, with “love” supplied as the noun understood with *altro*.
- *pur che* ‘provided that’ → “if but”: the condition is expressed by “if” qualified by the restrictive “but”.
- *la fiamma sua* ‘the flame of its own’ → “its flame”: the postposed possessive moves before the noun.
- *paresse fuore* ‘appeared outwardly’ → “were shown forth”: an intransitive imperfect subjunctive becomes a passive subjunctive expression.

## Lines 13–45 — Statius Corrects Virgil’s Mistake

> 13 onde, dall' ora che tra noi discese  
> 14 nel limbo dello inferno Juvenale,  
> 15 che la tua affezion mi fe' palese,  

wherefore from that hour when Juvenal, who made thy affection manifest to me, descended among us in the limbo of Hell

- *dall' ora che* ‘from the hour that’ → “from that hour when”: the article becomes a demonstrative, and the relative is made explicitly temporal.
- *tra noi discese nel limbo dello inferno Juvenale* ‘among us descended into the limbo of Hell Juvenal’ → “Juvenal” … “descended among us in the limbo of Hell”: the subject moves from last to first, the verb comes before “among us”, and the destination is expressed with “in” rather than ‘into’.
- *che la tua affezion mi fe' palese* ‘who thy affection to me made manifest’ → “who made thy affection manifest to me”: the relative clause now precedes the main verb; within it, the verb precedes its object, and the indirect object comes last.

> 16 mia benvoglienza inverso te fu quale  
> 17 più strinse mai di non vista persona,  

my goodwill towards thee hath been such as never yet did bind to an unseen person,

- *fu* ‘was’ → “hath been”: the simple past becomes a present perfect.
- *quale più strinse mai* ‘such as ever bound most closely’ → “such as never yet did bind”: the comparison of degree is recast with a negative time expression; there is no separate equivalent for *più*.
- *di non vista persona* ‘of not-seen person’ → “to an unseen person”: English uses “to” with “bind”, supplies an indefinite article, and combines the negative and participle in one adjective.

> 18 sì ch' or mi parran corte queste scale.  

so that now these stairs will seem short to me.

- *mi parran corte queste scale* ‘to me will seem short these stairs’ → “these stairs will seem short to me”: the subject moves to the front and the experiencer to the end.

> 19 Ma dimmi, e come amico mi perdona  
> 20 se troppa sicurtà m' allarga il freno,  
> 21 e come amico omai meco ragiona:  

But tell me, and as a friend forgive me if too great confidence slacken my rein, and talk with me now as with a friend:

- *come amico mi perdona* ‘as friend me forgive’ → “as a friend forgive me”: English supplies the indefinite article and puts the object after the imperative.
- *m' allarga il freno* ‘to me loosens the rein’ → “slacken my rein”: the dative becomes a possessive on the object; the Italian indicative is rendered by the uninflected English subjunctive after “if”.
- *come amico omai meco ragiona* ‘as friend now with me converse’ → “talk with me now as with a friend”: the imperative moves from last to first, “with me” precedes “now”, and the comparison moves to the end. The added “with” makes the friend the person spoken to.

> 22 Come potè trovar dentro al tuo seno  
> 23 loco avarizia, tra cotanto senno  
> 24 di quanto, per tua cura, fosti pieno?”  

How could avarice find place in thy breast, amid so much wisdom as by thy diligence thou wast filled with?”

- *potè trovar dentro al tuo seno loco avarizia* ‘could find within thy breast place avarice’ → “could avarice find place in thy breast”: the subject moves between the auxiliary and infinitive, “place” immediately follows “find”, and the phrase expressing location comes last, with “in” for ‘within’.
- *di quanto, per tua cura, fosti pieno* ‘of as much as, through thy care, thou wast full’ → “as by thy diligence thou wast filled with”: the relative of quantity is reduced to “as”; the adjective becomes a participle, with its preposition deferred to the end. The word “diligence” specifies the kind of care.

> 25 Queste parole Stazio mover fenno  
> 26 un poco a riso pria; poscia rispose:  

These words first moved Statius a little to laughter; then he answered:

- *Stazio mover fenno un poco a riso pria* ‘Statius move they caused a little to laughter first’ → “first moved Statius a little to laughter”: a causative with an infinitive becomes a single transitive verb, followed by its object, and the adverb moves from the end of the clause to before the verb.

> 27 “Ogni tuo dir d' amor m' è caro cenno.  

“Every word of thine is a precious token of love to me.

- *Ogni tuo dir* ‘every saying of thine’ → “Every word of thine”: the nominalized infinitive becomes “word”, with the possessive moved after the noun.
- *d' amor m' è caro cenno* ‘of love to me is dear token’ → “is a precious token of love to me”: the verb comes first, an indefinite article is supplied, and both “of love” and “to me” move after the noun. ‘Dear’ is rendered in the sense of ‘precious’.

> 28 Veramente più volte appaion cose,  
> 29 che danno a dubitar falsa matera,  
> 30 per le vere ragion che sono ascose.  

Truly many times things appear that give false matter for doubting, because of the true reasons which are hidden.

- *appaion cose* ‘appear things’ → “things appear”: the subject precedes the verb.
- *danno a dubitar falsa matera* ‘give for doubting false matter’ → “give false matter for doubting”: the object comes immediately after the verb, and the Italian preposition and infinitive become “for” with a gerund.

> 31 La tua domanda tuo creder m' avvera  
> 32 esser ch' io fossi avaro in l' altra vita,  
> 33 forse per quella cerchia dov' io era.  

Thy question proves to me thy belief to be, that I was avaricious in the other life, perchance because of that circle where I was.

- *tuo creder m' avvera esser* ‘thy believing to me makes certain to be’ → “proves to me thy belief to be”: the governing verb and indirect object come before the infinitive construction, and the nominalized *creder* becomes the noun “belief”.
- *ch' io fossi avaro* ‘that I were avaricious’ → “that I was avaricious”: the Italian subjunctive in the reported belief becomes an English indicative.

> 34 Or sappi ch' avarizia fu partita  
> 35 troppo da me, e questa dismisura  
> 36 migliaia di lunari hanno punita;  

Now know that avarice was too far parted from me, and this excess thousands of moons have punished;

- *fu partita troppo da me* ‘was parted too much from me’ → “was too far parted from me”: the degree is expressed as distance, and its modifier moves before the participle.
- *migliaia di lunari* ‘thousands of lunations’ → “thousands of moons”: the lunar periods are called “moons”.

> 37 e, se non fosse ch' io drizzai mia cura,  
> 38 quand' io intesi là dove tu esclame,  
> 39 crucciato quasi all' umana natura:  

and were it not that I set straight my inclination, when I gave heed to the lines where thou exclaimest, angered as ’twere against human nature:

- *se non fosse* ‘if it were not’ → “were it not”: inversion replaces the conditional conjunction.
- *mia cura* ‘my care’ → “my inclination”: the object is rendered as a bent of the will rather than as care or concern.
- *intesi là dove* ‘attended there where’ → “gave heed to the lines where”: a verbal phrase replaces the single verb, and “the lines” supplies a textual referent for the adverb of place.
- *quasi all' umana natura* ‘as if at human nature’ → “as ’twere against human nature”: “as ’twere” expands the qualifying adverb, and “against” makes the direction of the anger more explicit.

> 40 ‘Perchè non reggi tu, o sacra fame  
> 41 dell' oro, l' appetito de' mortali?’  

‘Wherefore dost thou not regulate the lust of mortals, O hallowed hunger of gold?’—

- *non reggi tu* ‘dost not govern thou’ → “dost thou not regulate”: the negative question uses auxiliary “dost”, with the subject before “not”; ‘govern’ is rendered as “regulate”.
- *o sacra fame dell' oro* ‘O sacred hunger of gold’ → “O hallowed hunger of gold”: the vocative moves after the object of the question, “hallowed” renders ‘sacred’, and English uses no article with “gold”.
- *l' appetito de' mortali* ‘the appetite of the mortals’ → “the lust of mortals”: “lust” is stronger than ‘appetite’, and the generic plural loses its article.

> 42 voltando sentirei le giostre grame.  

at the rolling I should feel the grievous jousts.

- *voltando* ‘rolling’ → “at the rolling”: a verbal form denoting an accompanying action becomes a prepositional phrase with a definite article.
- *le giostre grame* ‘the jousts grievous’ → “the grievous jousts”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 43 Allor m' accorsi che troppo aprir l' ali  
> 44 potean le mani a spendere, e pente' mi  
> 45 così di quel come degli altri mali.  

Then I perceived that our hands could open their wings too wide in spending, and I repented of that as well as of other sins.

- *m' accorsi* ‘I became aware’ → “I perceived”: a pronominal verb becomes a simple transitive verb.
- *troppo aprir l' ali potean le mani a spendere* ‘too much open the wings could the hands in spending’ → “our hands could open their wings too wide in spending”: the subject comes first, the modal precedes the infinitive, and the two definite articles become possessives, including a supplied first-person plural. The degree phrase follows “wings” and specifies width; the final infinitive becomes a gerund after “in”.
- *e pente' mi* ‘and repented myself’ → “and I repented”: English uses the verb without a reflexive pronoun.
- *così di quel come degli altri mali* ‘as much of that as of the other evils’ → “of that as well as of other sins”: the two-part comparison becomes “as well as” between the complements; “sins” narrows ‘evils’, and the definite article is dropped.

## Lines 46–78 — Prodigality and the Light of Faith

> 46 Quanti risurgeran coi crini scemi,  
> 47 per ignoranza, che di questa pecca  
> 48 toglie il penter vivendo, e negli estremi!  

How many will rise again with shorn locks, through ignorance, which taketh away repentance of this sin during life and at the last hour!

- *coi crini scemi* ‘with the locks shorn’ → “with shorn locks”: the article is dropped and the adjective comes before the noun.
- *che di questa pecca toglie il penter* ‘which of this fault takes away the repenting’ → “which taketh away repentance of this sin”: the verb comes before its object, the phrase specifying the fault comes last, and the substantivized infinitive becomes an abstract noun without an article.
- *vivendo* ‘while living’ → “during life”: the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase with a noun.
- *negli estremi* ‘in the last moments’ → “at the last hour”: the preposition changes and the substantivized plural adjective is rendered with a singular noun.

> 49 E sappi che la colpa, che rimbecca  
> 50 per dritta opposizione alcun peccato,  
> 51 con esso insieme qui suo verde secca.  

And know that the offence which repels any sin by its direct opposite, here, together with it, dries up its luxuriance.

- *rimbecca* ‘strikes back at’ → “repels”: the verb is rendered as driving something back rather than striking back at it.
- *per dritta opposizione alcun peccato* ‘by direct opposition any sin’ → “any sin by its direct opposite”: the object comes before the phrase of means; the relation of opposition becomes an opposite, with a possessive supplied.
- *con esso insieme qui* ‘with it together here’ → “here, together with it”: the adverb of place comes first, and the other adverb precedes the prepositional phrase.
- *suo verde secca* ‘its verdure dries up’ → “dries up its luxuriance”: the object follows the verb, and the figurative greenness is rendered as luxuriant growth.

> 52 Però, s' io son tra quella gente stato  
> 53 che piange l' avarizia, per purgarmi,  
> 54 per lo contrario suo m' è incontrato.”  

Therefore if I, to purge me, have been among that people who bewail avarice, this hath befallen me because of its contrary.”

- *son tra quella gente stato* ‘have among that people been’ → “have been among that people”: the auxiliary and participle are brought together before the prepositional phrase.
- *che piange l' avarizia* ‘which bewails avarice’ → “who bewail avarice”: the relative is personal and the verb plural, construing the collective as people rather than as a singular body; the abstract noun loses its article.
- *per purgarmi* ‘to purge myself’ → “to purge me”: the purpose phrase moves forward from after the relative clause, and the simple object pronoun does duty for the reflexive.
- *per lo contrario suo m' è incontrato* ‘on account of the contrary of it has it happened to me’ → “this hath befallen me because of its contrary”: the cause comes last, with the possessive before the noun; English supplies a demonstrative subject and makes the person affected the direct object.

> 55 “Or quando tu cantasti le crude armi  
> 56 della doppia tristizia di Jocasta,”  
> 57 disse il cantor de' bucolici carmi,  

“Now when thou didst sing of the savage strife of Jocasta’s twofold sorrow,” said the singer of the Bucolic lays,

- *cantasti le crude armi* ‘sang the cruel arms’ → “didst sing of the savage strife”: the simple past is expressed with an auxiliary, the direct object becomes the subject matter introduced by a preposition, and the plural arms are rendered as the strife itself.
- *della doppia tristizia di Jocasta* ‘of the double sorrow of Jocasta’ → “of Jocasta’s twofold sorrow”: the final name becomes a possessive before the noun, displacing the article.

> 58 “per quello che Cliò teco lì tasta,  
> 59 non par che ti facesse ancor fedele  
> 60 la fè, senza la qual ben far non basta.  

“by that which Clio touches with thee there, it seems not that faith had yet made thee faithful, without which good works are not enough.

- *teco lì tasta* ‘with thee there touches’ → “touches with thee there”: the verb precedes both modifiers.
- *non par* ‘not seems’ → “it seems not”: the negative follows the verb, and English supplies an impersonal subject.
- *che ti facesse ancor fedele la fè* ‘that thee made yet faithful the faith’ → “that faith had yet made thee faithful”: the subject comes first and loses its article; the imperfect subjunctive is rendered by a past perfect, with the personal object after the verb.
- *ben far non basta* ‘to do well does not suffice’ → “good works are not enough”: the infinitive and its adverb become a plural noun and its adjective, and the singular verb of sufficiency becomes a plural copula with a complement.

> 61 Se così è, qual sole o quai candele  
> 62 ti stenebraron sì, che tu drizzasti  
> 63 poscia di retro al pescator le vele?”  

If this be so, what sun or what candles dispelled the darkness for thee, so that thou didst thereafter set thy sails to follow the Fisherman?”

- *Se così è* ‘if so it is’ → “If this be so”: a demonstrative subject is supplied, the complement follows the verb, and the indicative becomes a subjunctive.
- *ti stenebraron sì, che* ‘so freed thee from darkness that’ → “dispelled the darkness for thee, so that”: darkness becomes the direct object and the person a beneficiary; the adverb of degree is drawn into the result conjunction.
- *tu drizzasti poscia di retro al pescator le vele* ‘thou didst direct afterward behind the fisherman the sails’ → “thou didst thereafter set thy sails to follow the Fisherman”: the time adverb moves before the main verb, the sails come immediately after it with a possessive instead of the article, and the directional phrase becomes an infinitive of purpose.

> 64 Ed egli a lui: “Tu prima m' inviasti  
> 65 verso Parnaso a ber nelle sue grotte,  
> 66 e poi appresso Dio m' alluminasti.  

And he to him: “Thou first didst send me towards Parnassus to drink in its caves, and then didst light me on to God.

- *m' inviasti* ‘me sentest’ → “didst send me”: an auxiliary expresses the past, and the object follows the main verb.
- *nelle sue grotte* ‘in its grottoes’ → “in its caves”: the more general noun is used.
- *appresso Dio m' alluminasti* ‘toward God me illuminedst’ → “didst light me on to God”: illumination becomes lighting someone on their way, with the object after the verb and the destination last.

> 67 Facesti come quei che va di notte,  
> 68 che porta il lume retro e sè non giova,  
> 69 ma dopo sè fa le persone dotte,  

Thou didst like one who goes by night, and carries the light behind him, and profits not himself, but maketh persons wise that follow him,

- *che porta* ‘who carries’ → “and carries”: coordination replaces the repeated relative.
- *retro* ‘behind’ → “behind him”: English makes the point of reference explicit.
- *sè non giova* ‘himself does not benefit’ → “profits not himself”: the verb precedes the negative and the reflexive.
- *dopo sè fa le persone dotte* ‘after himself makes the persons learned’ → “maketh persons wise that follow him”: the opening phrase becomes a final relative clause with a verb of following; the article disappears and learning is rendered as wisdom.

> 70 quando dicesti: ‘Secol si rinnova;  
> 71 torna giustizia e primo tempo umano,  
> 72 e progenie discende dal ciel nuova.’  

when thou saidst: ‘The world is renewed, justice returns and the first age of man, and a new progeny descends from heaven.’

- *Secol si rinnova* ‘an age renews itself’ → “The world is renewed”: the noun is taken in the sense of the world and given a definite article; the reflexive form becomes a passive.
- *torna giustizia* ‘returns justice’ → “justice returns”: the first subject comes before the verb rather than after it.
- *primo tempo umano* ‘first human time’ → “the first age of man”: an article is supplied, time is rendered as an age, and the adjective becomes a phrase with a noun.
- *progenie discende dal ciel nuova* ‘progeny descends from heaven new’ → “a new progeny descends from heaven”: the delayed adjective is brought before its noun and an indefinite article is supplied.

> 73 Per te poeta fui, per te cristiano;  

Through thee I was a poet, through thee a Christian,

- *poeta fui* ‘poet I was’ → “I was a poet”: the predicate follows the verb and takes an indefinite article.
- *cristiano* ‘Christian’ → “a Christian”: the predicate is expressed as a noun with an indefinite article.

> 74 ma perchè veggi me' ciò ch' io disegno,  
> 75 a colorare stenderò la mano.  

but that thou mayst see better what I outline I will put forth my hand to fill in colour.

- *perchè veggi me'* ‘in order that thou see better’ → “that thou mayst see better”: the purpose clause expresses the Italian subjunctive with a modal auxiliary.
- *ciò ch' io disegno* ‘that which I draw’ → “what I outline”: a fused relative replaces the demonstrative and relative, and drawing is specified as outlining.
- *a colorare stenderò la mano* ‘to colour I shall stretch out the hand’ → “I will put forth my hand to fill in colour”: the purpose infinitive comes last and is expanded into a phrase; the verb of extension is rendered idiomatically and the article becomes a possessive.

> 76 Già era il mondo tutto quanto pregno  
> 77 della vera credenza, seminata  
> 78 per li messaggi dell' eterno regno;  

Already the whole world was big with the true belief, sown by the apostles of the everlasting kingdom;

- *era il mondo tutto quanto pregno* ‘was the world all of it pregnant’ → “the whole world was big”: the subject comes before the verb, its emphatic postposed modifiers become a single prenominal adjective, and pregnancy is expressed by the idiomatic adjective “big”.
- *della vera credenza* ‘of the true belief’ → “with the true belief”: English uses a different preposition with its predicate adjective.
- *per li messaggi* ‘by the messengers’ → “by the apostles”: the translation identifies the messengers rather than using the general term.

## Lines 79–111 — Statius’s Conversion and the Poets in Limbo

> 79 e la parola tua sopra toccata  
> 80 sì consonava ai nuovi predicanti,  
> 81 ond' io a visitarli presi usata.  

and thy words, touched on above, harmonised so with the new preachers, that the habit took me of visiting them.

- *la parola tua* ‘thy word’ → “thy words”: the singular becomes plural, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *sopra toccata* ‘above touched upon’ → “touched on above”: the adverb moves after the participle.
- *sì consonava* ‘so harmonised’ → “harmonised so”: the degree adverb moves after the verb.
- *ai nuovi predicanti* ‘to the new preachers’ → “with the new preachers”: English uses “with” after “harmonised”.
- *ond’ io a visitarli presi usata* ‘whence I took to visiting them as a habit’ → “that the habit took me of visiting them”: “that” completes the English construction with “so”; the habit becomes the subject of “took” and the speaker its object. The infinitive preceding the Italian verb becomes an *of*-phrase with a gerund at the end.

> 82 Vennermi poi parendo tanto santi,  
> 83 che, quando Domizian li perseguette,  
> 84 senza mio lagrimar non fur lor pianti.  

They then became so holy in my sight, that when Domitian persecuted them, their wailings were not without tears of mine.

- *Vennermi poi parendo tanto santi* ‘they came then to seem to me so holy’ → “They then became so holy in my sight”: the progressive construction for coming to seem something becomes “became”; the pronoun of the person to whom they seemed so is expanded into the final “in my sight”.
- *li perseguette* ‘them persecuted’ → “persecuted them”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *senza mio lagrimar non fur lor pianti* ‘without my weeping were not their lamentations’ → “their wailings were not without tears of mine”: the subject moves from last to first and the *without*-phrase from first to last; the substantival infinitive denoting the act of weeping becomes the plural noun “tears”, with its possessive expressed by “of mine”.

> 85 E mentre che di là per me si stette,  
> 86 io li sovvenni, e lor dritti costumi  
> 87 fer dispregiare a me tutte altre sette;  

And while by me yon world was trod, I succoured them, and their righteous lives made me despise all other sects;

- *di là per me si stette* ‘over there on my part there was a staying’ → “by me yon world was trod”: the adverb of place becomes the subject “yon world”; the impersonal construction for staying there becomes a passive verb of motion, with “by me” placed first.
- *li sovvenni* ‘them I succoured’ → “I succoured them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *lor dritti costumi* ‘their upright ways’ → “their righteous lives”: the noun for habits or conduct is rendered by “lives”.
- *fer dispregiare a me* ‘made despise, on my part’ → “made me despise”: the person who does the despising, expressed by a prepositional phrase after the infinitive, becomes the object of “made” before the infinitive.

> 88 e pria ch' io conducessi i Greci ai fiumi  
> 89 di Tebe, poetando, ebb' io battesmo;  
> 90 ma per paura chiuso cristian fu' mi  
> 91 lungamente mostrando paganesmo;  

and ere in my poem I had brought the Greeks to Thebes’ rivers, I received baptism, but through fear I was a secret Christian, long time pretending paganism;

- *pria ch’ io conducessi* ‘before I should lead’ → “ere in my poem I had brought”: the Italian imperfect subjunctive after the temporal conjunction becomes an English past perfect, and ‘lead’ becomes “brought”.
- *ai fiumi di Tebe* ‘to the rivers of Thebes’ → “to Thebes’ rivers”: the following *di*-phrase becomes a preceding possessive.
- *poetando* ‘composing poetry’ → “in my poem”: the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase with a possessive, moved forward to follow “ere”.
- *ebb’ io battesmo* ‘had I baptism’ → “I received baptism”: the subject moves before the verb, and “received” specifies the sense of ‘had’.
- *chiuso cristian fu’ mi* ‘a hidden Christian was I’ → “I was a secret Christian”: the predicate moves after the verb and takes an indefinite article; the pronominal *mi* has no separate equivalent.
- *lungamente* ‘for long’ → “long time”: an adverb becomes a phrase with a noun.
- *mostrando paganesmo* ‘showing paganism’ → “pretending paganism”: “pretending” makes explicit that the outward show was a pretence.

> 92 e questa tepidezza il quarto cerchio  
> 93 cerchiar mi fe' più ch' al quarto centesmo.  

and this lukewarmness made me speed round the fourth circle more than four times a hundred years.

- *il quarto cerchio cerchiar mi fe’* ‘the fourth circle to circle made me’ → “made me speed round the fourth circle”: the causative verb moves first, its object comes before the infinitive, and the object of the infinitive moves last. “speed round” adds a sense of haste to the verb of circling.
- *più ch’ al quarto centesmo* ‘beyond the fourth hundredth’ → “more than four times a hundred years”: the ordinal expression becomes a multiplication of cardinal numbers, and the understood unit “years” is supplied.

> 94 Tu dunque, che levato hai il coperchio  
> 95 che m' ascondeva quanto bene io dico,  
> 96 mentre che del salire avem soperchio,  

Thou therefore, who hast lifted the covering which hid from me the great good I tell of, while we have time to spare on the ascent,

- *il coperchio* ‘the lid’ → “the covering”: the specific noun becomes a more general one.
- *m’ ascondeva* ‘from me hid’ → “hid from me”: the pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase following the verb.
- *quanto bene io dico* ‘how great a good I speak of’ → “the great good I tell of”: the expression of degree becomes a definite noun phrase modified by a relative clause.
- *del salire avem soperchio* ‘of the ascending we have a surplus’ → “we have time to spare on the ascent”: “time” specifies what there is a surplus of; the initial phrase with a substantival infinitive becomes a final phrase with a noun and a preposition of circumstance.

> 97 dimmi dov' è Terenzio nostro antico,  
> 98 Cecilio, Plauto e Varro, se lo sai;  
> 99 dimmi se son dannati, ed in qual vico.”  

tell me, where is our ancient Terence, Cæcilius, Plautus, and Varro if thou knowest; tell me if they are damned, and in what ward.”

- *Terenzio nostro antico* ‘Terence our ancient’ → “our ancient Terence”: the possessive and adjective both move before the name.
- *se lo sai* ‘if thou knowest it’ → “if thou knowest”: the object pronoun is left unexpressed.
- *in qual vico* ‘in which street’ → “in what ward”: the noun for a street is taken in the sense of a district or division.

> 100 “Costoro, e Persio, ed io, e altri assai,”  
> 101 rispose il duca mio, “siam con quel Greco  
> 102 che le Muse lattar più ch' altro mai,  
> 103 nel primo cinghio del carcere cieco.  

“They, and Persius, and I, and many others,” my Leader answered, “are with that Greek to whom the Muses gave suck more than to any other, in the first circle of the dark prison.

- *Costoro* ‘these men’ → “They”: a demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *altri assai* ‘others in great number’ → “many others”: the expression of quantity precedes the word it qualifies.
- *rispose il duca mio* ‘answered my leader’ → “my Leader answered”: the subject moves before the verb and the possessive before the noun.
- *che le Muse lattar* ‘whom the Muses suckled’ → “to whom the Muses gave suck”: the transitive verb becomes a phrase with “gave”, and its direct object becomes the recipient introduced by “to”.
- *più ch’ altro mai* ‘more than any other ever’ → “more than to any other”: “to” follows the changed construction, and *mai* has no separate equivalent.
- *del carcere cieco* ‘of the prison blind’ → “of the dark prison”: “dark” renders the figurative adjective, which moves before the noun.

> 104 Spesse fiate ragioniam del monte,  
> 105 che sempre ha le nutrici nostre seco.  

Ofttimes we talk of the mount which hath our foster-mothers ever with it.

- *Spesse fiate* ‘many times’ → “Ofttimes”: adjective and plural noun become a single adverb.
- *ragioniam del monte* ‘we discourse of the mountain’ → “we talk of the mount”: “talk” is a less formal verb.
- *sempre ha le nutrici nostre seco* ‘always has our nurses with itself’ → “hath our foster-mothers ever with it”: the adverb moves from before the verb to after its object; the possessive moves before the noun, and “foster-mothers” renders the more specific idea of women who nurse. The reflexive becomes an ordinary pronoun.

> 106 Euripide v' è nosco ed Antifonte,  
> 107 Simonide, Agatone ed altri piùe  
> 108 Greci, che già di lauro ornar la fronte.  

Euripides is there with us, and Antiphon, Simonides, Agathon, and many other Greeks, who once decked their brows with laurel.

- *v’ è nosco* ‘there is with us’ → “is there with us”: the locative moves after the verb.
- *altri piùe Greci* ‘other Greeks besides, in greater number’ → “many other Greeks”: the expression of additional number becomes the preceding quantifier “many”.
- *già di lauro ornar la fronte* ‘once with laurel adorned the brow’ → “once decked their brows with laurel”: the phrase naming the adornment moves to the end; the distributive singular with the definite article becomes a plural with a possessive.

> 109 Quivi si veggion delle genti tue  
> 110 Antigone, Deifile ed Argia,  
> 111 ed Ismene sì trista come fue.  

There are seen of thy people Antigone, Deiphyle and Argia, and Ismene so sad as she was.

- *si veggion* ‘one sees’ → “are seen”: the Italian *si*-construction is rendered by an English passive.
- *delle genti tue* ‘of the peoples of thine’ → “of thy people”: the plural becomes a collective singular, and the possessive moves before the noun.

## Lines 112–144 — The Poets Approach the Tree

> 112 Vedesi quella che mostrò Langia;  

There is seen she who showed Langia;

- *Vedesi* ‘is seen’ → “There is seen”: the passive construction with *si* is retained, but a locative adverb is supplied.
- *quella* ‘that one’ → “she”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.

> 113 evvi la figlia di Tiresia, e Teti,  
> 114 e con le suore sue Deidamia.”  

there is Tiresias’ daughter, and Thetis, and Deidamia with her sisters.”

- *evvi* ‘is there’ → “there is”: the locative attached to the verb comes first in English.
- *la figlia di Tiresia* ‘the daughter of Tiresias’ → “Tiresias’ daughter”: a possessive before the noun replaces the phrase with *di*, and the article drops out.
- *con le suore sue Deidamia* ‘with the sisters of hers Deidamia’ → “Deidamia with her sisters”: the name moves before the accompanying phrase, and the possessive before its noun.

> 115 Tacevansi ambo e due già li poeti,  
> 116 di nuovo attenti a riguardare intorno,  
> 117 liberi dal salire e dai pareti;  

Now were both poets silent, intent anew on gazing around, freed from the ascent and from the walls;

- *Tacevansi ambo e due già li poeti* ‘kept silent both of the two already the poets’ → “Now were both poets silent”: the temporal adverb moves to the front and becomes “Now”; the pronominal verb becomes a copula and adjective, and the emphatic double expression for ‘both’ becomes a single determiner replacing the article.
- *di nuovo attenti a riguardare* ‘anew intent to gaze’ → “intent anew on gazing”: the adverb moves after the adjective, and a preposition with a gerund replaces the infinitive.
- *liberi dal salire* ‘free from climbing’ → “freed from the ascent”: a participle replaces the adjective, and a noun replaces the substantivized infinitive.

> 118 e già le quattro ancelle eran del giorno  
> 119 rimase addietro, e la quinta era al temo,  
> 120 drizzando pure in su l' ardente corno;  

and already four handmaids of the day were left behind, and the fifth was at the chariot pole, directing yet upward its flaming horn,

- *le quattro ancelle* ‘the four handmaids’ → “four handmaids”: the definite article is omitted.
- *eran del giorno rimase addietro* ‘had of the day remained behind’ → “of the day were left behind”: the modifier of the handmaids moves before the verb instead of interrupting it; the pluperfect of ‘remain’ becomes a passive expression of the resulting state.
- *al temo* ‘at the pole’ → “at the chariot pole”: the kind of pole is specified.
- *drizzando pure in su* ‘aiming still upwards’ → “directing yet upward”: the verb of pointing is rendered by the more general “directing”, with “yet” for continuance.
- *l' ardente corno* ‘the burning horn’ → “its flaming horn”: a possessive replaces the article, and ‘burning’ becomes “flaming”.

> 121 quando il mio duca: “Io credo ch'allo estremo  
> 122 le destre spalle volger ci convegna,  
> 123 girando il monte come far solemo.”  

when my Leader: “I think it behoves us to turn our right shoulders to the edge and circle the mount as we are wont to do.”

- *credo ch'allo estremo le destre spalle volger ci convegna* ‘believe that to the edge the right shoulders to turn us it behoves’ → “think it behoves us to turn our right shoulders to the edge”: “think” introduces a clause without an expressed conjunction; the subjunctive has no marked English equivalent. The impersonal verb and its pronoun precede the infinitive, the object and the phrase of direction follow it, and a possessive replaces the article before the body part.
- *girando il monte* ‘circling the mount’ → “and circle the mount”: the gerund becomes a second infinitive coordinated with “turn”.
- *far solemo* ‘to do we are wont’ → “we are wont to do”: the governing verb comes before its infinitive.

> 124 Così l' usanza fu lì nostra insegna,  
> 125 e prendemmo la via con men sospetto  
> 126 per l' assentir di quell' anima degna.  

Thus custom there was our guide, and we took up our way with less doubt because of the assent of that worthy spirit.

- *l' usanza* ‘the custom’ → “custom”: the abstract noun loses its article.
- *fu lì* ‘was there’ → “there was”: the locative moves before the verb.
- *nostra insegna* ‘our standard’ → “our guide”: the function of the standard replaces its image.
- *prendemmo la via* ‘we took the way’ → “we took up our way”: “up” is added to the verb, and a possessive replaces the article.
- *sospetto* ‘apprehension’ → “doubt”: the English stresses uncertainty.
- *per l' assentir* ‘through the assenting’ → “because of the assent”: the causal relation is made explicit, and an ordinary noun replaces the substantivized infinitive.
- *anima degna* ‘soul worthy’ → “worthy spirit”: “spirit” replaces ‘soul’, and the adjective precedes it.

> 127 Elli givan dinanzi, ed io soletto  
> 128 diretro, ed ascoltava i lor sermoni  
> 129 ch' a poetar mi davano intelletto.  

They journeyed on in front, and I, solitary, behind; and I hearkened to their discourse which gave me understanding in poesy.

- *givan* ‘were going’ → “journeyed on”: the simple past renders the imperfect, with a more specific verb and an added particle of continuance.
- *soletto* ‘all alone’ → “solitary”: a plain adjective renders the expressive diminutive.
- *ascoltava* ‘was listening to’ → “I hearkened to”: a simple past replaces the imperfect, with a more literary verb.
- *i lor sermoni* ‘their discourses’ → “their discourse”: the plural becomes a collective singular.
- *ch' a poetar mi davano intelletto* ‘which for making poetry were giving me understanding’ → “which gave me understanding in poesy”: the simple past replaces the imperfect; the phrase with the infinitive moves to the end and becomes a phrase naming a field of understanding rather than an activity.

> 130 Ma tosto ruppe le dolci ragioni  
> 131 un arbor che trovammo in mezza strada,  
> 132 con pomi ad odorar soavi e buoni.  

But soon the sweet converse was broken by a tree which we found in the midst of the way, with fruit wholesome and pleasant to smell.

- *ruppe le dolci ragioni un arbor* ‘broke the sweet discourses a tree’ → “the sweet converse was broken by a tree”: the object becomes the subject of a passive, the postposed subject becomes its agent, and a collective singular replaces the plural.
- *in mezza strada* ‘in mid-road’ → “in the midst of the way”: the adjective becomes a noun with an *of*-phrase.
- *pomi ad odorar soavi e buoni* ‘fruits to smell sweet and good’ → “fruit wholesome and pleasant to smell”: the plural becomes a mass noun; ‘good’ is interpreted as “wholesome” and moved first, while the infinitive moves after “pleasant”, the rendering of ‘sweet’.

> 133 E come abete in alto si digrada  
> 134 di ramo in ramo, così quello in giuso,  
> 135 cred' io perchè persona su non vada.  

And even as a pine tree grows gradually less from bough to bough upwards, so did that downwards; I think so that none may go up.

- *come abete* ‘as a fir’ → “even as a pine tree”: “even” strengthens the comparison, the bare noun acquires an article, and a different tree name is used.
- *in alto si digrada di ramo in ramo* ‘upwards diminishes by degrees from bough to bough’ → “grows gradually less from bough to bough upwards”: the single pronominal verb is expanded into a verb, adverb, and comparative, and the direction moves to the end.
- *così quello in giuso* ‘so that one downwards’ → “so did that downwards”: “did” fills the Italian verbal ellipsis, in the past rather than the present of the comparison.
- *cred' io* ‘think I’ → “I think”: the expressed subject moves before the verb.
- *perchè persona su non vada* ‘in order that anyone up not go’ → “so that none may go up”: the negation is transferred to the subject, “may” renders the subjunctive of purpose, and the direction follows the verb.

> 136 Dal lato, onde il cammin nostro era chiuso,  
> 137 cadea dell' alta roccia un liquor chiaro,  
> 138 e si spandeva per le foglie suso.  

On the side where our path was blocked, a clear spring fell from the high rock and spread itself above over the leaves.

- *Dal lato, onde* ‘from the side whence’ → “On the side where”: both expressions of source become expressions of position.
- *il cammin nostro* ‘the path of ours’ → “our path”: the postposed possessive becomes a determiner before the noun.
- *chiuso* ‘closed’ → “blocked”: the participle is rendered in the sense of obstructed.
- *cadea dell' alta roccia un liquor chiaro* ‘was falling from the high rock a liquid clear’ → “a clear spring fell from the high rock”: the postposed subject moves before the verb, its adjective precedes the noun, and “spring” names a source or stream rather than simply a liquid; the simple past renders the imperfect.
- *si spandeva per le foglie suso* ‘was spreading itself through the leaves above’ → “spread itself above over the leaves”: the simple past renders the imperfect, the final adverb moves forward, and “over” replaces ‘through’.

> 139 Li due poeti all' arbor s' appressaro;  
> 140 ed una voce per entro le fronde  
> 141 gridò: “Di questo cibo avrete caro.”  

The two poets drew near the tree; and a voice from within the foliage cried: “Of this food ye shall have scarcity.”

- *all' arbor s' appressaro* ‘to the tree drew themselves near’ → “drew near the tree”: the pronominal verb becomes an English idiom without a reflexive, and the tree follows it without a preposition.
- *per entro le fronde* ‘through the midst of the leafy branches’ → “from within the foliage”: the English marks the source of the voice, and a collective singular replaces the plural.
- *avrete caro* ‘you will have dearth’ → “ye shall have scarcity”: the English takes *caro* in its substantival sense of dearth rather than its adjectival sense of ‘dear’.

> 142 Poi disse: “Più pensava Maria onde  
> 143 fosser le nozze orrevoli ed intere,  
> 144 ch' alla sua bocca, ch' or per voi risponde.  

Then it said: “Mary thought more how the wedding-feast might be honourable and complete, than of her own mouth, which now answers for you.

- *Più pensava Maria* ‘more was thinking Mary’ → “Mary thought more”: the subject moves first and the adverb last; the simple past renders the imperfect.
- *onde fosser le nozze orrevoli ed intere* ‘by what means might be the nuptials honourable and complete’ → “how the wedding-feast might be honourable and complete”: “how” expresses means; the plural becomes a singular naming the feast, and the subject precedes the verb.
- *ch' alla sua bocca* ‘than to her mouth’ → “than of her own mouth”: English thinks *of* rather than *to*, and “own” adds emphasis.

## Lines 145–154 — Examples of Temperance

> 145 E le Romane antiche per lor bere  
> 146 contente furon d' acqua, e Daniello  
> 147 dispregiò cibo ed acquistò sapere.  

And the Roman women of old were content with water for their drink, and Daniel despised food and gained wisdom.

- *le Romane antiche* ‘the ancient Roman women’ → “the Roman women of old”: the feminine noun is rendered by “Roman women”, and the adjective by the phrase “of old”.
- *per lor bere contente furon d' acqua* ‘for their drinking content were with water’ → “were content with water for their drink”: the verb comes before “content”, and the phrase of purpose moves to the end; the infinitive used as a noun becomes “drink”.
- *sapere* ‘knowledge’ → “wisdom”: the English suggests more than knowledge alone.

> 148 Lo secol primo quant' oro fu bello;  
> 149 fe' saporose con fame le ghiande,  
> 150 e nettare con sete ogni ruscello.  

The first age was fair as gold; it made acorns savoury with hunger, and every stream nectar with thirst.

- *Lo secol primo* ‘the age first’ → “The first age”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *quant' oro fu bello* ‘as gold was fair’ → “was fair as gold”: the comparison follows the verb and its adjective instead of preceding them.
- *fe' saporose con fame le ghiande* ‘made savoury with hunger the acorns’ → “it made acorns savoury with hunger”: the object moves before its adjective and loses the generic definite article.
- *e nettare con sete ogni ruscello* ‘and nectar with thirst every stream’ → “and every stream nectar with thirst”: the object again comes first, before the noun that says what the stream was made.

> 151 Mele e locuste furon le vivande  
> 152 che nutriro il Batista nel diserto;  
> 153 per ch' egli è glorioso, e tanto grande  
> 154 quanto per l' Evangelio v' è aperto.”  

Honey and locusts were the meat which nourished the Baptist in the wilderness; therefore he is glorious, and so great as in the Gospel is revealed to you.

- *le vivande* ‘the foods’ → “the meat”: a plural noun becomes a singular mass noun, with “meat” in the sense of food.
- *nel diserto* ‘in the desert’ → “in the wilderness”: the English uses a different term for the uninhabited place.
- *per ch'* ‘for which reason’ → “therefore”: a connective phrase becomes an adverb.
- *per l' Evangelio* ‘through the Gospel’ → “in the Gospel”: the English indicates where the revelation is found rather than the means by which it is made.
- *v' è aperto* ‘to you is made plain’ → “is revealed to you”: the pronominal complement moves after the verb, and making plain is expressed as revealing.

## Summary — English Order and Italian Phrasing

Okey often keeps Dante’s words but changes the order in which they reach the reader. A subject delayed until after the verb may come first in English; an object placed before an infinitive may follow it. In *mi parran corte queste scale*, ‘to me will seem short these stairs’, the two ends of the phrase exchange places: “these stairs will seem short to me”. He regularly moves an adjective before its noun or brings a participle back beside its auxiliary. Two routine changes generally pass unmarked in the comparison: English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits the article before a possessive, as in *il cammin nostro*, “our path”.

A change of order sometimes goes with a change of construction. The tree is the delayed subject of *ruppe*, ‘broke’; Okey keeps it late by using a passive, “the sweet converse was broken by a tree”. The substantivized infinitive *il penter*, ‘the act of repenting’, becomes “repentance”, while the gerund *poetando*, ‘composing poetry’, becomes “in my poem”. An Italian imperfect may become a simple past, as in *ascoltava*, “I hearkened to”, or a progressive, as in *seguiva*, “I was following”.

Okey sometimes names what the Italian leaves to be inferred: *là dove*, ‘there where’, becomes “the lines where”, and *al temo*, ‘at the pole’, becomes “at the chariot pole”. Elsewhere a word drops out: *lo*, ‘it’, has no separate equivalent in “if thou knowest”. The choice of noun can also shift the emphasis. For *insegna*, ‘standard’, Okey gives “guide”, naming what the standard does rather than the object itself. Yet he keeps the image of the “light behind him” and the repeated *per te*, “through thee”. At times he even retains a distinctly Italian turn of order, leaving *questa dismisura*, “this excess”, before both the subject and the verb rather than smoothing the phrase into ordinary English prose.
