# Purgatorio 14

## Lines 1–15 — Two Spirits Question Dante

> 1 “Chi è costui che il nostro monte cerchia,  
> 2 prima che morte gli abbia dato il volo,  
> 3 ed apre gli occhi a sua voglia e coperchia?”  

“Who is this that circles our mount ere death have given him flight, and opens and shuts his eyes at his will?”

- *il nostro monte cerchia* ‘our mount circles’ → “circles our mount”: the verb comes before its object.
- *gli abbia dato il volo* ‘to him have given the flight’ → “have given him flight”: the recipient follows the verb, and the article before “flight” is dropped.
- *apre gli occhi a sua voglia e coperchia* ‘opens the eyes at his pleasure and covers them’ → “opens and shuts his eyes at his will”: “shuts” gives the sense of the more pictorial *coperchia*; the two verbs are brought together before their shared object, “his” replaces the article, and the phrase expressing choice comes last.

> 4 “Non so chi sia; ma so ch' ei non è solo;  

“I know not who he may be, but I know that he is not alone;

- *Non so* ‘not know’ → “I know not”: the negative follows the verb.
- *chi sia* ‘who he be’ → “who he may be”: “may” conveys the subjunctive of the indirect question.
- *non è solo* ‘not is alone’ → “is not alone”: the negative follows the verb.

> 5 domandal tu che più gli t' avvicini,  
> 6 e dolcemente, sì che parli, acco' lo.”  

do thou question him who art nearer to him, and gently greet him that he may speak.”

- *domandal tu* ‘question him thou’ → “do thou question him”: the imperative takes auxiliary “do”, with the emphatic subject between auxiliary and main verb.
- *che più gli t' avvicini* ‘who nearer to him drawest thyself’ → “who art nearer to him”: a reflexive verb of approach becomes a statement of relative position, with “to him” following “nearer”.
- *dolcemente, sì che parli, acco' lo* ‘gently, so that he speak, welcome him’ → “gently greet him that he may speak”: “greet” renders the imperative of reception; the purpose clause follows the imperative instead of separating it from its adverb, and “may” expresses the subjunctive.

> 7 Così due spirti, l' uno all' altro chini,  
> 8 ragionavan di me ivi a man dritta,  

Thus two spirits, one leaning against the other, were discoursing of me there on the right hand;

- *l' uno all' altro chini* ‘the one toward the other inclined’ → “one leaning against the other”: the first article drops out, the plural adjective becomes a participle attached to “one”, and “against” replaces the preposition of direction.
- *ragionavan* ‘discoursed’ → “were discoursing”: the English progressive brings out the continuing action of the imperfect.
- *a man dritta* ‘at right hand’ → “on the right hand”: English changes the preposition, supplies the article, and puts the adjective before the noun.

> 9 poi fer li visi, per dirmi, supini;  

then held up their faces to speak to me;

- *fer li visi, per dirmi, supini* ‘made the faces, to say to me, upturned’ → “held up their faces to speak to me”: the verb and its distant predicative adjective become “held up”; the possessive replaces the article, and the purpose phrase, with “speak” for ‘say’, now comes last.

> 10 e disse l' uno: “O anima, che fitta  
> 11 nel corpo ancora, in ver lo ciel ten vai,  
> 12 per carità ne consola, e ne ditta  

and one said: “O soul, that fixed yet in thy body dost journey towards heaven, for charity console us, and tell us

- *disse l' uno* ‘said the one’ → “one said”: the subject precedes the verb and loses its article.
- *nel corpo ancora* ‘in the body still’ → “yet in thy body”: the adverb moves forward and the article becomes a possessive.
- *in ver lo ciel ten vai* ‘towards the heaven thou goest thy way’ → “dost journey towards heaven”: the directional phrase follows the verb and loses its article; the pronominal verb of going becomes “dost journey”.
- *ne consola* ‘us console’ → “console us”: the object follows the imperative.
- *ne ditta* ‘to us tell’ → “tell us”: the recipient follows the imperative.

> 13 onde vieni, e chi sei; chè tu ne fai  
> 14 tanto maravigliar della tua grazia,  
> 15 quanto vuol cosa che non fu più mai.”  

whence thou comest, and who thou art; for thou dost make us marvel so greatly at thy grace, as needs must a thing that never was.”

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *tu ne fai tanto maravigliar* ‘thou us makest so much marvel’ → “thou dost make us marvel so greatly”: the causative takes auxiliary “dost”, the object follows “make”, and the expression of degree follows the infinitive.
- *della tua grazia* ‘of thy grace’ → “at thy grace”: English changes the preposition after “marvel”.
- *quanto vuol cosa* ‘as much as a thing requires’ → “as needs must a thing”: the degree comparison is continued by “as”, and the verb of requirement becomes an expression of necessity with the action of causing wonder left understood.
- *che non fu più mai* ‘that was never before’ → “that never was”: “never” precedes the verb, and the sense of ‘before’ in *più mai* is not separately expressed.

## Lines 16–48 — The Arno and Its Wretched Vale

> 16 Ed io: “Per mezza Toscana si spazia  
> 17 un fiumicel che nasce in Falterona,  
> 18 e cento miglia di corso nol sazia.  

And I: “Through the midst of Tuscany there spreads a stream which rises in Falterona and a course of a hundred miles satiates it not.

- *Per mezza Toscana* ‘through half Tuscany’ → “Through the midst of Tuscany”: *mezza* is taken as denoting the middle rather than half the extent; the adjective becomes a noun with an *of*-phrase.
- *si spazia* ‘ranges’ → “there spreads”: the pronominal verb becomes an intransitive verb introduced by “there”, keeping the subject after the verb.
- *un fiumicel* ‘a little river’ → “a stream”: the diminutive is conveyed by the choice of noun rather than a separate modifier.
- *nasce* ‘is born’ → “rises”: the verb of birth becomes the English verb for a river’s beginning.
- *cento miglia di corso* ‘a hundred miles of course’ → “a course of a hundred miles”: the measure and the thing measured exchange places, and “a” is supplied before “course”.
- *nol sazia* ‘not it satiates’ → “satiates it not”: the object pronoun and the negative both move after the verb, in reverse order.

> 19 Di sopr' esso rech' io questa persona;  

From its banks I bring this body;

- *Di sopr' esso* ‘from upon it’ → “From its banks”: the spatial expression becomes a noun, “banks”, and the pronoun referring to the river becomes possessive.
- *rech' io* ‘bring I’ → “I bring”: the expressed subject moves before the verb.
- *questa persona* ‘this person’ → “this body”: the noun is rendered in its bodily sense.

> 20 dirvi ch' io sia, saria parlare indarno,  
> 21 chè il nome mio ancor molto non suona.”  

to tell you who I may be were to speak in vain, for my name as yet sounds not for much.”

- *ch' io sia* ‘who I be’ → “who I may be”: the Italian subjunctive is rendered with a modal auxiliary.
- *saria* ‘would be’ → “were”: the conditional becomes an English irrealis form without an auxiliary.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *ancor molto non suona* ‘as yet much does not sound’ → “as yet sounds not for much”: the verb precedes the negative, and the adverb of degree becomes the complement of “for” at the end.

> 22 “Se ben lo intendimento tuo accarno  
> 23 con lo intelletto,” allora mi rispose  
> 24 quei che prima dicea, “tu parli d' Arno.”  

“If I penetrate truly thy meaning with my understanding,” then answered me he who first spake, “thou art talking of the Arno.”

- *ben lo intendimento tuo accarno* ‘well the meaning thine I pierce’ → “I penetrate truly thy meaning”: the figurative verb moves before its object, *ben* becomes “truly” after the verb, and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *con lo intelletto* ‘with the intellect’ → “with my understanding”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the faculty is called “understanding”.
- *mi rispose* ‘to me answered’ → “answered me”: the indirect object moves after the verb and takes no preposition.
- *dicea* ‘was speaking’ → “spake”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *tu parli d' Arno* ‘thou speakest of Arno’ → “thou art talking of the Arno”: the simple present becomes progressive, and an article is supplied before the river’s name.

> 25 E l' altro disse a lui: “Perchè nascose  
> 26 questi il vocabol di quella riviera,  
> 27 pur com' uom fa dell' orribili cose?”  

And the other said to him: “Why did he conceal the name of that river, even as one does of horrible things?”

- *nascose questi* ‘concealed this one’ → “did he conceal”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, placed between the interrogative auxiliary and the main verb.
- *il vocabol* ‘the word’ → “the name”: the more general noun is rendered as a designation.
- *uom* ‘a man’ → “one”: the generic noun becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *dell' orribili cose* ‘of the horrible things’ → “of horrible things”: the definite article is dropped for a generic plural.

> 28 E l' ombra, che di ciò domandata era,  
> 29 si sdebitò così: “Non so, ma degno  
> 30 ben è che il nome di tal valle pera:  

And the shade who was asked this question, acquitted him thus: “I know not, but verily ’tis meet that the name of such a vale perish;

- *di ciò domandata era* ‘about this questioned was’ → “was asked this question”: the auxiliary precedes the participle, and the prepositional expression becomes a direct object with “question” supplied.
- *si sdebitò così* ‘discharged himself thus’ → “acquitted him thus”: *sdebitò* is rendered by another verb of release from obligation, and the preverbal reflexive becomes a postverbal “him”, used reflexively.
- *Non so* ‘not I know’ → “I know not”: the negative follows the verb.
- *degno ben è* ‘worthy indeed is’ → “verily ’tis meet”: the emphatic adverb moves first, “’tis” supplies an impersonal subject, and the predicate adjective follows the verb.
- *di tal valle* ‘of such vale’ → “of such a vale”: English inserts the indefinite article after “such”.

> 31 chè dal principio suo, dov' è sì pregno  
> 32 l' alpestro monte, ond' è tronco Peloro,  
> 33 che in pochi lochi passa oltra quel segno,  

for from its beginning (where the rugged mountain-chain, whence Pelorus is cut off, is so fruitful that in few places it exceeds that mark)

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating conjunction replaces the subordinating one.
- *dal principio suo* ‘from the beginning its’ → “from its beginning”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *è sì pregno l' alpestro monte* ‘is so pregnant the rugged mountain’ → “the rugged mountain-chain, whence Pelorus is cut off, is so fruitful”: the subject, together with its relative clause, moves before the predicate; “mountain-chain” gives *monte* a collective sense, and “fruitful” renders the image of pregnancy by its implication.
- *ond' è tronco Peloro* ‘whence is severed Pelorus’ → “whence Pelorus is cut off”: the subject moves before the passive verb, and a phrasal verb renders *tronco*.
- *passa oltra quel segno* ‘passes beyond that mark’ → “it exceeds that mark”: a single transitive verb replaces the verb of motion and the expression of passing beyond.

> 34 infin là 've si rende per ristoro  
> 35 di quel che il ciel della marina asciuga,  
> 36 ond' hanno i fiumi ciò che va con loro,  

as far as there where it yields itself to restore that which the sky soaks up from the sea, whence rivers have that which flows with them,

- *si rende* ‘itself yields’ → “it yields itself”: the reflexive moves after the verb.
- *per ristoro di quel che* ‘for replenishment of that which’ → “to restore that which”: the noun of purpose becomes an infinitive, with a direct object instead of an *of*-phrase.
- *della marina asciuga* ‘from the sea dries up’ → “soaks up from the sea”: the verb moves before the source phrase, and removal by drying becomes absorption.
- *hanno i fiumi* ‘have the rivers’ → “rivers have”: the subject moves before the verb, and the generic plural loses its article.
- *va* ‘goes’ → “flows”: the general verb of motion becomes one specific to water.

> 37 virtù così per nimica si fuga  
> 38 da tutti, come biscia, o per sventura  
> 39 del loco o per mal uso che li fruga:  

virtue is driven forth as an enemy by all, even as a snake, either because of the ill-favoured place or of evil habit which incites them;

- *così per nimica si fuga da tutti, come biscia* ‘so as an enemy is put to flight by all, as a snake’ → “is driven forth as an enemy by all, even as a snake”: the pronominal passive becomes an English passive before the enemy phrase; “driven forth” replaces the image of putting to flight, and the force of anticipatory *così* is carried into “even as”.
- *o per sventura del loco* ‘either through the misfortune of the place’ → “either because of the ill-favoured place”: misfortune, the governing noun, becomes a quality attributed directly to the place.
- *o per mal uso* ‘or through bad custom’ → “or of evil habit”: the second alternative shares the preceding “because”, and *uso* is taken as habit.
- *li fruga* ‘them goads’ → “incites them”: the object follows the verb, and the image of prodding becomes incitement.

> 40 ond' hanno sì mutata lor natura  
> 41 gli abitator della misera valle,  
> 42 che par che Circe gli avesse in pastura.  

wherefore the dwellers in the wretched vale have so changed their nature that it seems as if Circe had them in her pasturing.

- *hanno sì mutata lor natura gli abitator della misera valle* ‘have so changed their nature the dwellers of the wretched vale’ → “the dwellers in the wretched vale have so changed their nature”: the subject moves from after the object to before the auxiliary; “in” replaces the genitive of place, and the English participle has no agreement with the object.
- *par che* ‘it seems that’ → “it seems as if”: the subordinate clause is introduced explicitly as a comparison.
- *gli avesse in pastura* ‘them she had at pasture’ → “had them in her pasturing”: the object follows the verb, the Italian imperfect subjunctive has no distinct English inflection here, and “her pasturing” supplies a possessive and presents pasturing as an activity.

> 43 Tra brutti porci, più degni di galle  
> 44 che d' altro cibo fatto in uman uso,  
> 45 dirizza prima il suo povero calle.  

Among filthy hogs, more worthy of acorns than of other food made for use of man, it first directs its feeble course.

- *brutti* ‘ugly’ → “filthy”: foulness rather than ugliness is made explicit.
- *fatto in uman uso* ‘made for human use’ → “made for use of man”: the adjective becomes a following *of*-phrase.
- *dirizza prima* ‘directs first’ → “it first directs”: the adverb moves before the verb.
- *il suo povero calle* ‘its poor path’ → “its feeble course”: *povero* is taken in the sense of weakness, and the path becomes a river’s course.

> 46 Botoli trova poi, venendo giuso,  
> 47 ringhiosi più che non chiede lor possa,  
> 48 ed a lor, disdegnosa, torce il muso.  

Then, coming downward it finds curs snarling more than their power warrants, and from them scornfully turns aside its snout.

- *Botoli trova poi, venendo giuso* ‘curs it finds then, coming downward’ → “Then, coming downward it finds curs”: the time adverb and participial phrase move to the front, and the object moves after the verb.
- *ringhiosi* ‘snarling by disposition’ → “snarling”: the adjective becomes a participle describing an action.
- *che non chiede lor possa* ‘than asks their power’ → “than their power warrants”: the subject moves before the verb, “warrants” replaces ‘asks’ or ‘calls for’, and the non-negating *non* of the Italian comparison has no English counterpart.
- *a lor* ‘toward them’ → “from them”: the English specifies the direction away from them rather than their relation to the gesture.
- *disdegnosa* ‘disdainful’ → “scornfully”: the adjective describing the subject becomes an adverb modifying the act.
- *torce il muso* ‘turns the snout’ → “turns aside its snout”: “aside” makes the deflection explicit, and the definite article becomes possessive.

## Lines 49–81 — The Prophecy and Guido’s Name

> 49 Vassi cadendo, e, quanto ella più ingrossa,  
> 50 tanto più trova di can farsi lupi  
> 51 la maledetta e sventurata fossa.  

On it goes in its descent, and, the greater its increase, the more it finds the dogs growing to wolves, this accurst and ill-fated ditch.

- *Vassi cadendo* ‘it goes its way falling’ → “On it goes in its descent”: “On” gives the sense of continued motion in the pronominal verb; the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase with a possessive.
- *quanto ella più ingrossa* ‘by how much the more it swells’ → “the greater its increase”: the finite verb becomes a noun with a possessive, and the degree of swelling becomes the size of the increase.
- *tanto più* ‘by so much the more’ → “the more”: the second half of the comparison needs no separate word for *tanto* in English.
- *di can farsi lupi* ‘from dogs to make themselves wolves’ → “the dogs growing to wolves”: the starting point of the change becomes the definite subject of a participle; the reflexive infinitive becomes a verb of growth, with “to” marking the result.
- *la maledetta e sventurata fossa* ‘the accurst and ill-fated ditch’ → “this accurst and ill-fated ditch”: the article becomes a demonstrative.

> 52 Discesa poi per più pelaghi cupi,  
> 53 trova le volpi, sì piene di froda  
> 54 che non temono ingegno che le occupi.  

Having then descended through many deep gorges, it finds the foxes, so full of fraud that they fear no wit that may trap them.

- *Discesa poi* ‘descended then’ → “Having then descended”: the past participle standing alone becomes a perfect participial construction, with the adverb between auxiliary and participle.
- *più pelaghi cupi* ‘several deep pools’ → “many deep gorges”: “many” gives the plural a stronger quantitative sense; hollows in the terrain replace stretches of deep water, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *non temono ingegno* ‘they do not fear ingenuity’ → “they fear no wit”: the negative goes with the object rather than the verb.
- *che le occupi* ‘that them should catch’ → “that may trap them”: the subjunctive becomes a modal construction, catching is particularized as trapping, and the object follows the verb.

> 55 Nè lascerò di dir, perch' altri m' oda;  

Nor will I cease speaking, for all that another may hear me;

- *lascerò di dir* ‘shall I leave off saying’ → “will I cease speaking”: the infinitive governed by *di* becomes an English gerund, and saying becomes speaking.
- *perch' altri m' oda* ‘because another should hear me’ → “for all that another may hear me”: Okey takes the conjunction with the subjunctive concessively rather than causally; “may” carries the possibility, and the object follows the verb.

> 56 e buon sarà a costui, se ancor s' ammenta  
> 57 di ciò, che vero spirto mi disnoda.  

and it will be well for him if he mind him again of what true prophecy unfolds to me.

- *buon sarà a costui* ‘good will it be for this man’ → “it will be well for him”: English puts the anticipatory subject first, uses “well” for the predicate adjective, and drops the demonstrative force of *costui*.
- *se ancor s' ammenta* ‘if again he reminds himself’ → “if he mind him again”: the adverb moves to the end, plain “him” has reflexive force, and the Italian indicative becomes an English subjunctive.
- *di ciò, che* ‘of that which’ → “of what”: antecedent and relative are fused.
- *vero spirto mi disnoda* ‘true spirit unties for me’ → “true prophecy unfolds to me”: the inspiration is named by what it reveals; unfolding replaces untying, and the indirect object moves after the verb.

> 58 Io veggio tuo nipote, che diventa  
> 59 cacciator di quei lupi, in su la riva  
> 60 del fiero fiume, e tutti gli sgomenta.  

I see thy grandson, who is becoming a hunter of those wolves on the bank of the fierce river, and strikes them all with terror.

- *che diventa cacciator* ‘who becomes hunter’ → “who is becoming a hunter”: the simple present becomes progressive, and English supplies an article before the predicate noun.
- *in su la riva* ‘up upon the bank’ → “on the bank”: one preposition does the work of the compound expression.
- *e tutti gli sgomenta* ‘and all of them he terrifies’ → “and strikes them all with terror”: the single verb becomes a phrase with a noun, and the object and its quantifier follow “strikes” in reverse order.

> 61 Vende la carne loro, essendo viva;  

He sells their flesh while yet alive;

- *la carne loro* ‘the flesh of them’ → “their flesh”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *essendo viva* ‘being alive’ → “while yet alive”: the gerund becomes a temporal clause with the verb understood; “yet” makes continued life explicit.

> 62 poscia gli ancide come antica belva:  

then slaughters them like worn-out cattle:

- *gli ancide* ‘them he kills’ → “slaughters them”: the object follows the verb, and “slaughters” is more specific than ‘kills’.
- *come antica belva* ‘like an old beast’ → “like worn-out cattle”: age is interpreted as exhaustion, and a singular beast becomes collective, domesticated “cattle”.

> 63 molti di vita, e sè di pregio priva.  

many he deprives of life and himself of honour.

- *molti di vita, e sè di pregio priva* ‘many of life, and himself of esteem, he deprives’ → “many he deprives of life and himself of honour”: the verb governing both pairs moves into the first pair; “honour” renders worth or esteem.

> 64 Sanguinoso esce della trista selva;  

He cometh forth bloody from the sad wood;

- *Sanguinoso esce* ‘bloody he comes out’ → “He cometh forth bloody”: the predicate adjective moves from its emphatic initial position to follow the verb.
- *della* ‘out of the’ → “from the”: English marks the point of departure with “from”.

> 65 lasciala tal, che di qui a mill' anni  
> 66 nello stato primaio non si rinselva.”  

he leaves it such, that hence a thousand years it re-woods not itself to its primal state.”

- *lasciala* ‘leaves-it’ → “he leaves it”: the attached object becomes a separate word.
- *di qui a mill' anni* ‘from here to a thousand years’ → “hence a thousand years”: “hence” turns the starting point into a temporal adverb; the preposition marking the far limit disappears.
- *nello stato primaio* ‘in the state primal’ → “to its primal state”: the phrase moves after the verb, “to” marks the state as a result, the article becomes a possessive, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *non si rinselva* ‘not itself it re-woods’ → “it re-woods not itself”: both negative and reflexive move after the verb.

> 67 Come all' annunzio de' dogliosi danni  
> 68 si turba il viso di colui che ascolta,  
> 69 da qualche parte il periglio lo assanni:  

As at the announcement of grievous ills the face of him who listens is troubled, from whatever side the peril may assault him,

- *de' dogliosi danni* ‘of the grievous harms’ → “of grievous ills”: the article drops out, and “ills” renders injuries or losses.
- *si turba il viso* ‘becomes troubled the face’ → “the face of him who listens is troubled”: the whole noun subject precedes the predicate; a pronominal verb of change becomes “is troubled”.
- *da qualche parte* ‘from some side’ → “from whatever side”: an indefinite becomes a free-choice expression in conjunction with the following subjunctive.
- *lo assanni* ‘should fasten its teeth in him’ → “may assault him”: the modal renders the subjunctive, the object follows the verb, and the image of fangs becomes a general verb of attack.

> 70 così vid' io l' altr' anima, che volta  
> 71 stava ad udir, turbarsi e farsi trista,  
> 72 poi ch' ebbe la parola a sè raccolta.  

so saw I the other soul, that had turned round to hear, grow troubled and sad, after it had gathered these words to itself.

- *che volta stava ad udir* ‘that stood turned to hear’ → “that had turned round to hear”: a continuing posture is expressed as a completed turn in the past perfect; “round” specifies the motion.
- *turbarsi e farsi trista* ‘to become troubled and to make itself sad’ → “grow troubled and sad”: the two pronominal infinitives become one bare infinitive after “saw”, with two predicate adjectives.
- *poi ch' ebbe la parola a sè raccolta* ‘after it had the utterance to itself gathered’ → “after it had gathered these words to itself”: the participle moves up beside its auxiliary, and the singular utterance with a definite article becomes plural “words” with a demonstrative.

> 73 Lo dir dell' una, e dell' altra la vista  
> 74 mi fe' voglioso di saper lor nomi,  

The speech of the one and the other’s countenance made me long to know their names,

- *Lo dir* ‘the saying’ → “The speech”: a substantivized infinitive becomes an ordinary noun.
- *dell' altra la vista* ‘of the other the appearance’ → “the other’s countenance”: a possessive replaces the prepositional phrase, and appearance is narrowed to the face.
- *mi fe' voglioso di saper* ‘me made desirous of knowing’ → “made me long to know”: the object follows “made”; the adjective becomes a bare infinitive, which in turn takes a *to*-infinitive.

> 75 e domanda ne fei con preghi mista:  

and question I made of them mingled with prayers:

- *ne fei* ‘of it I made’ → “I made of them”: Okey takes the pronoun as referring to those questioned rather than the matter asked about; its equivalent follows the verb.
- *con preghi mista* ‘with prayers mingled’ → “mingled with prayers”: the participle moves before its complement.

> 76 per che lo spirto, che di pria parlòmi,  
> 77 ricominciò: “Tu vuoi ch' io mi deduca  
> 78 nel fare a te ciò, che tu far non vuo' mi;  

wherefore the spirit that first spake to me, began again: “Thou wouldst that I condescend in doing that for thee which thou wilt not do for me;

- *per che* ‘for which’ → “wherefore”: the preposition and relative become a connective adverb.
- *di pria* ‘before’ → “first”: the translation gives order of speaking rather than simply priority in time.
- *vuoi* ‘thou wishest’ → “Thou wouldst”: the present verb of wishing becomes a modal expressing desire.
- *ch' io mi deduca* ‘that I bring myself down’ → “that I condescend”: a single nonreflexive verb gives the sense of the Italian reflexive.
- *nel fare a te ciò* ‘in the doing for thee of that’ → “in doing that for thee”: the articulated infinitive becomes a gerund without an article, and the direct object moves before the beneficiary.
- *che tu far non vuo' mi* ‘which thou to do dost not wish for me’ → “which thou wilt not do for me”: the infinitive moves after the negated verb of volition, and the postponed pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase.

> 79 ma da che Dio in te vuol che traluca  
> 80 tanta sua grazia, non ti sarò scarso:  

but since God wills that so much of his grace shine forth in thee, I will not be chary with thee;

- *in te vuol che traluca tanta sua grazia* ‘in thee wills that there shine through so much grace of his’ → “wills that so much of his grace shine forth in thee”: the locative moves to the end, the subject of the subjunctive precedes its verb, and the quantity is expressed partitively; “forth” replaces the sense of shining through.
- *non ti sarò scarso* ‘I shall not to thee be sparing’ → “I will not be chary with thee”: the dative becomes a phrase with “with” after the adjective; “chary” expresses reluctance to give.

> 81 però sappi ch' io son Guido del Duca.  

therefore know that I am Guido del Duca.

- Literal.

## Lines 82–114 — Envy Confessed and Romagna Lamented

> 82 Fu il sangue mio d' invidia sì riarso,  
> 83 che, se veduto avessi uom farsi lieto,  
> 84 visto m' avresti di livore sparso.  

My blood was so inflamed with envy, that if I had seen a man make him glad, thou wouldst have seen me suffused with lividness.

- *Fu il sangue mio d' invidia sì riarso* ‘was the blood of mine by envy so scorched’ → “My blood was so inflamed with envy”: the subject comes before the verb, the possessive before the noun, and the phrase naming the cause after the participle; scorching becomes inflammation.
- *veduto avessi* ‘seen I had’ → “I had seen”: the auxiliary comes before the participle; the Italian pluperfect subjunctive is rendered by the English past perfect in the conditional clause.
- *uom farsi lieto* ‘a man make himself glad’ → “a man make him glad”: the simple pronoun does the work of the Italian reflexive.
- *visto m' avresti* ‘seen me thou wouldst have’ → “thou wouldst have seen me”: the auxiliaries precede the participle and the object follows it.
- *di livore sparso* ‘with lividness overspread’ → “suffused with lividness”: the participle is recast and placed before its complement.

> 85 Di mia semente cotal paglia mieto.  

Of my sowing such straw I reap.

- *Di mia semente* ‘of my seed’ → “Of my sowing”: the noun for what is sown becomes a noun for the act.

> 86 O gente umana, perchè poni il core  
> 87 là 'v' è mestier di consorto divieto?  

O human folk, why set the heart there where exclusion of partnership is necessary?

- *gente umana* ‘folk human’ → “human folk”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *poni il core* ‘dost thou set the heart’ → “set the heart”: the finite second-person singular becomes a bare verb in a question without an expressed subject.
- *è mestier di consorto divieto* ‘there is need of a sharer’s exclusion’ → “exclusion of partnership is necessary”: the thing needed becomes the subject of an adjectival predicate; the person who would share becomes the abstract “partnership”.

> 88 Questi è Rinier, quest' è il pregio e l' onore  
> 89 della casa da Calboli, ove nullo  
> 90 fatto s' è erede poi del suo valore.  

This is Rinier; this is the glory and the honour of the House of Calboli, where none since hath made himself heir of his worth.

- *il pregio* ‘the distinction’ → “the glory”: distinction is expressed as renown.
- *della casa da Calboli* ‘of the house from Calboli’ → “of the House of Calboli”: the preposition of origin becomes the English preposition used in naming a house.
- *fatto s' è erede poi del suo valore* ‘made himself has heir since of his worth’ → “since hath made himself heir of his worth”: the temporal adverb moves forward, leaving “of his worth” directly after “heir”; the auxiliary precedes the participle, and the reflexive follows it; the Italian reflexive perfect with *essere* takes “hath” in English.

> 91 E non pur lo suo sangue è fatto brullo,  
> 92 tra il Po e il monte e la marina e il Reno,  
> 93 del ben richiesto al vero ed al trastullo:  

And not only his blood between the Po and the mountains, and the seashore and the Reno, is stripped of the good required of truth and chivalry,

- *è fatto brullo* ‘is made bare’ → “is stripped”: participle and adjective become a single participle; the predicate moves after the geographical phrase, which in Italian separates it from its complement.
- *il monte* ‘the mountain’ → “the mountains”: the collective singular becomes plural.
- *al vero ed al trastullo* ‘for the true and for recreation’ → “of truth and chivalry”: the complements of requirement take “of” rather than ‘for’; the substantivized adjective becomes an abstract noun, and recreation is interpreted as chivalry.

> 94 chè dentro a questi termini è ripieno  
> 95 di venenosi sterpi, sì che tardi  
> 96 per coltivare omai verrebber meno.  

for inside these boundaries is choked with poisonous growths, so that tardily now would they be rooted out by cultivation.

- *è ripieno di venenosi sterpi* ‘is full of poisonous shrubs’ → “is choked with poisonous growths”: fullness becomes choking, and the specific shrubs become growths in general.
- *per coltivare* ‘by cultivating’ → “by cultivation”: the infinitive becomes a noun, and the phrase moves to the end.
- *omai verrebber meno* ‘by now they would dwindle away’ → “now would they be rooted out”: the sense of ‘by now’ is reduced to “now”; an intransitive expression for disappearing becomes a passive expression for removal.

> 97 Ov' è il buon Lizio ed Arrigo Mainardi,  
> 98 Pier Traversaro e Guido di Carpigna?  

Where is the good Lizio, and Arrigo Mainardi, Pier Traversaro and Guido di Carpigna?

- Literal.

> 99 O Romagnoli tornati in bastardi!  

O ye Romagnols turned to bastards!

- *O Romagnoli* ‘O Romagnols’ → “O ye Romagnols”: a pronoun of address is added.

> 100 Quando in Bologna un Fabbro si ralligna?  

When in Bologna shall a Fabbro take root again?

- *un Fabbro si ralligna* ‘a Fabbro takes root again’ → “shall a Fabbro take root again”: the present is interpreted as future; the interrogative auxiliary precedes the subject, and the Italian pronominal verb becomes a verb phrase without a reflexive.

> 101 quando in Faenza un Bernardin di Fosco,  
> 102 verga gentil di picciola gramigna?  

when in Faenza a Bernardin di Fosco, noble scion of a lowly plant?

- *verga gentil* ‘shoot noble’ → “noble scion”: the adjective moves before the noun, and “scion” gives the plant term an English equivalent also used of descent.
- *di picciola gramigna* ‘of little couch grass’ → “of a lowly plant”: smallness is interpreted as low station, and a particular grass becomes a plant in general with an indefinite article.

> 103 Non ti maravigliar, s' io piango, Tosco,  
> 104 quando rimembro con Guido da Prata  
> 105 Ugolin d' Azzo che vivette nosco,  

Marvel thou not, Tuscan, if I weep, when I remember with Guido da Prata, Ugolin d’ Azzo who lived among us,

- *Non ti maravigliar* ‘not thyself to marvel’ → “Marvel thou not”: the Italian negative singular command uses an infinitive with a reflexive pronoun; English uses an imperative without a reflexive and places the negative after the expressed subject.
- *s' io piango, Tosco* ‘if I weep, Tuscan’ → “Tuscan, if I weep”: the address moves before the clause about weeping.
- *nosco* ‘with us’ → “among us”: living with becomes living among.

> 106 Federico Tignoso e sua brigata,  
> 107 la casa Traversara e gli Anastagi  
> 108 (e l' una gente e l' altra è diretata),  

Federico Tignoso and his fellowship, the House of Traversaro, and the Anastagi (the one race and the other now without heirs),

- *sua brigata* ‘his company’ → “his fellowship”: the group of companions is rendered by a term emphasizing their association.
- *la casa Traversara* ‘the Traversara house’ → “the House of Traversaro”: the family designation modifying ‘house’ becomes an “of” phrase.
- *e l' una gente e l' altra è diretata* ‘both the one stock and the other is bereft of heirs’ → “the one race and the other now without heirs”: the first conjunction and the finite verb disappear; “now” is supplied, and an adjectival phrase replaces the participle.

> 109 le donne e i cavalier, gli affanni e gli agi  
> 110 che ne invogliava amore e cortesia,  
> 111 là dove i cor son fatti sì malvagi.  

the ladies and the knights, the toils and the sports of which love and courtesy enamoured us, there where hearts are grown so wicked.

- *gli affanni e gli agi* ‘the troubles and the comforts’ → “the toils and the sports”: distress is expressed as exertion, ease as recreation.
- *che ne invogliava amore e cortesia* ‘for which love and courtesy made us long’ → “of which love and courtesy enamoured us”: arousing desire becomes “enamoured”, with “of” marking its object; the compound subject moves before the verb and the personal object after it. The Italian imperfect and singular agreement have no distinct marks in the English past form.
- *i cor son fatti sì malvagi* ‘the hearts are become so wicked’ → “hearts are grown so wicked”: the definite article disappears in the English plural, and becoming is expressed as growing.

> 112 O Brettinoro, chè non fuggi via,  
> 113 poichè gita se n' è la tua famiglia,  
> 114 e molta gente per non esser ria?  

O Brettinoro, why dost thou not flee away, since thy household is gone forth, and much people in order not to be guilty?

- *chè non fuggi via* ‘why not fleest thou away’ → “why dost thou not flee away”: English forms the negative question with an auxiliary and leaves the main verb in the base form.
- *gita se n' è la tua famiglia* ‘gone away is thy family’ → “thy household is gone forth”: the subject moves before the auxiliary and participle; the Italian pronominal expression of departure becomes “gone forth”, and ‘family’ is taken as “household”.
- *per non esser ria* ‘so as not to be wicked’ → “in order not to be guilty”: the purpose infinitive is expanded with “in order”, and wickedness is rendered as guilt.

## Lines 115–147 — Failing Lineages and Voices of Warning

> 115 Ben fa Bagnacaval, che non rifiglia,  
> 116 e mal fa Castrocaro, e peggio Conio,  
> 117 che di figliar tai conti più s' impiglia;  

Well doth Bagnacaval that beareth no more sons, and ill doth Castrocaro, and Conio worse, that yet troubleth to beget such Counts;

- *non rifiglia* ‘does not bear children again’ → “beareth no more sons”: the negative and the sense of repetition in the verb become “no more”; “sons” makes the object explicit and more specific.
- *e peggio Conio* ‘and worse Conio’ → “and Conio worse”: the name comes before the adverb.
- *di figliar tai conti più s' impiglia* ‘in begetting such counts further entangles itself’ → “yet troubleth to beget such Counts”: “yet” gives the continuing sense of *più*; the reflexive image of entanglement becomes “troubleth”, and the infinitive phrase follows the verb instead of preceding it.

> 118 ben faranno i Pagan, dacchè il demonio  
> 119 lor sen girà; ma non però che puro  
> 120 giammai rimanga d' essi testimonio.  

the Pagani will do well when their Demon shall go away; but not indeed that unsullied witness may ever remain of them.

- *ben faranno i Pagan* ‘well will do the Pagani’ → “the Pagani will do well”: the subject moves to the front and the adverb to the end.
- *dacchè* ‘once’ → “when”: the conjunction is taken temporally, with less emphasis on the time from which something holds.
- *il demonio lor sen girà* ‘the demon of theirs will take himself off’ → “their Demon shall go away”: the possessive comes before the noun, and the pronominal expression of departure becomes a verb and adverb.
- *ma non però che* ‘but not for that reason that’ → “but not indeed that”: the adverb pointing back to what precedes becomes an emphatic adverb.
- *puro giammai rimanga d' essi testimonio* ‘pure may ever remain of them witness’ → “unsullied witness may ever remain of them”: “unsullied” qualifies “witness” directly instead of serving as the complement of “remain”; the delayed subject moves forward, and “may” renders the subjunctive.

> 121 O Ugolin de' Fantolin, sicuro  
> 122 è il nome tuo, da che più non s' aspetta  
> 123 chi far lo possa tralignando oscuro.  

O Ugolin de’ Fantolin thy name is safe, since no more expectation is there of one who may blacken it by degenerating.

- *sicuro è il nome tuo* ‘safe is the name of yours’ → “thy name is safe”: the possessive precedes the noun, and the subject precedes the verb and its complement.
- *più non s' aspetta chi* ‘no longer is someone awaited who’ → “no more expectation is there of one who”: the verbal construction becomes an existential one with the noun “expectation”; the person awaited is introduced by “of”.
- *far lo possa tralignando oscuro* ‘make it might, degenerating, dark’ → “may blacken it by degenerating”: the modal comes first; ‘make dark’ becomes one verb, followed by its object, and the gerund of means moves to the end with an explicit “by”.

> 124 Ma va via, Tosco, omai, ch' or mi diletta  
> 125 troppo di pianger più che di parlare,  
> 126 sì m' ha nostra ragion la mente stretta.”  

But now go thy way, Tuscan, for now it delights me far more to weep than to talk, so hath our discourse wrung my spirit.”

- *va via, Tosco, omai* ‘go away, Tuscan, now’ → “now go thy way, Tuscan”: the final adverb moves to the front, and “go thy way” replaces the verb and adverb of departure.
- *ch' or mi diletta troppo di pianger più che di parlare* ‘for now it pleases me exceedingly to weep more than to speak’ → “for now it delights me far more to weep than to talk”: the object pronoun follows the verb, and the separated expressions of degree are combined in “far more” before the first infinitive.
- *sì m' ha nostra ragion la mente stretta* ‘so has our discourse gripped the mind for me’ → “so hath our discourse wrung my spirit”: the personal pronoun becomes a possessive, “spirit” takes the place of ‘mind’, and the participle moves before its object, with ‘gripped’ rendered as “wrung”.

> 127 Noi sapevam che quell' anime care  
> 128 ci sentivano andar; però tacendo  
> 129 facevan noi del cammin confidare.  

We knew that those dear souls heard us going; therefore by their silence they made us confident of the way.

- *quell' anime care* ‘those souls dear’ → “those dear souls”: the adjective precedes its noun.
- *ci sentivano andar* ‘us they heard go’ → “heard us going”: the object follows the verb of perception, and a participle replaces the infinitive.
- *tacendo* ‘keeping silent’ → “by their silence”: a prepositional phrase with a possessive noun replaces the gerund and makes the relation of means explicit.
- *facevan noi del cammin confidare* ‘made us in the way trust’ → “they made us confident of the way”: an adjective replaces the infinitive, and its prepositional complement follows it.

> 130 Poi fummo fatti soli procedendo,  
> 131 folgore parve, quando l' aer fende,  
> 132 voce che giunse d' incontra, dicendo:  

After we were left alone journeying on, a voice, that seemed like lightning when it cleaves the air, smote against us, saying:

- *Poi fummo fatti soli procedendo* ‘then we were made alone proceeding’ → “After we were left alone journeying on”: the independent statement becomes a temporal clause; ‘made’ becomes “left”, and “journeying on” renders the gerund of onward movement.
- *folgore parve, quando l' aer fende, voce* ‘lightning it seemed, when the air it cleaves, a voice’ → “a voice, that seemed like lightning when it cleaves the air”: the delayed noun comes first; the assertion about its appearance becomes a relative clause with an explicit “like”, and “the air” follows its verb.
- *che giunse d' incontra* ‘which came from opposite’ → “smote against us”: the relative clause becomes the main predicate; arrival from the opposite direction becomes an impact, with “us” supplied as its object.

> 133 “Anciderammi qualunque m' apprende”;  
> 134 e fuggì, come tuon che si dilegua,  
> 135 se subito la nuvola scoscende.  

“Everyone that findeth me shall slay me”; and fled like a thunderclap which peals away if suddenly the cloud bursts.

- *Anciderammi qualunque m' apprende* ‘will slay me whoever lays hold of me’ → “Everyone that findeth me shall slay me”: the indefinite relative becomes “Everyone” with a relative clause and moves before the main verb; ‘lays hold of’ is taken as “findeth”, and the object pronoun of that verb follows it.
- *come tuon che si dilegua* ‘like thunder that fades away’ → “like a thunderclap which peals away”: the sound is made a single clap, and the verb of fading is rendered by a phrase that also expresses the sound.

> 136 Come da lei l' udir nostro ebbe tregua,  
> 137 ed ecco l' altra con sì gran fracasso,  
> 138 che somigliò tuonar che tosto segua:  

When from it our hearing had truce, lo the second, with such loud crash that ’twas like thunder that follows quickly:

- *l' udir nostro* ‘the hearing of ours’ → “our hearing”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *ed ecco l' altra* ‘and behold the other’ → “lo the second”: the conjunction drops out, and “second” makes the order explicit.
- *con sì gran fracasso* ‘with so great a crash’ → “with such loud crash”: “such” replaces the adverb of degree, and greatness is specified as loudness.
- *che somigliò tuonar che tosto segua* ‘that it resembled thundering that quickly follow’ → “that ’twas like thunder that follows quickly”: “’twas like” replaces the verb of resemblance, the substantival infinitive becomes a noun, and the subjunctive becomes indicative, with the adverb after the verb.

> 139 “Io sono Aglauro che divenni sasso”;  
> 140 ed allor per ristringermi al poeta,  
> 141 indietro feci e non innanzi il passo.  

“I am Aglauros who was turned to stone” and then to press me close to the Poet, I made a step back, and not forward.

- *divenni sasso* ‘became stone’ → “was turned to stone”: a passive construction replaces the intransitive verb of becoming.
- *per ristringermi al poeta* ‘in order to draw myself close to the poet’ → “to press me close to the Poet”: the purpose is conveyed by the bare “to” of the infinitive; “press me close” gives the reflexive movement with an object pronoun rather than ‘myself’.
- *indietro feci e non innanzi il passo* ‘backward I made and not forward the step’ → “I made a step back, and not forward”: the verb and its object come before the contrasted directions, and the definite article becomes indefinite.

> 142 Già era l' aura d' ogni parte queta,  
> 143 ed ei mi disse: “Quel fu il duro camo,  
> 144 che dovria l' uom tener dentro a sua meta.  

Now was the air quiet on every side, and he said to me: “That was the hard bit which ought to hold man within his bounds.

- *Già* ‘already’ → “Now”: the time reached is marked without the explicit sense of ‘already’.
- *d' ogni parte queta* ‘on every side quiet’ → “quiet on every side”: the adjective precedes the phrase of extent.
- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *che dovria l' uom tener* ‘which ought man to hold’ → “which ought to hold man”: the object moves after the infinitive.
- *dentro a sua meta* ‘within his limit’ → “within his bounds”: the singular becomes plural.

> 145 Ma voi prendete l' esca sì che l' amo  
> 146 dell' antico avversario a sè vi tira;  
> 147 e però poco val freno o richiamo.  

But ye take the bait, so that the old adversary’s hook draws you to him, and therefore little avails bridle or lure.

- *l' amo dell' antico avversario* ‘the hook of the old adversary’ → “the old adversary’s hook”: the following possessive phrase becomes a preceding genitive.
- *a sè vi tira* ‘to himself you draws’ → “draws you to him”: the verb comes first, followed by its object and then the phrase of direction; “him” refers to the adversary rather than reflexively to the grammatical subject, the hook.
- *richiamo* ‘recall’ → “lure”: the word is taken in the concrete sense of a means of calling something back, rather than as the call itself.

## Lines 148–151 — Heaven Calls, the Eye Looks Down

> 148 Chiamavi il cielo, e intorno vi si gira,  
> 149 mostrandovi le sue bellezze eterne,  

The heavens call to you, and circle around you, displaying unto you their eternal splendours,

- *Chiamavi il cielo* ‘calls you the heaven’ → “The heavens call to you”: the singular subject becomes plural and precedes the verb; the object pronoun attached to the verb becomes “to you”.
- *intorno vi si gira* ‘round you it turns’ → “circle around you”: the reflexive verb becomes the intransitive “circle”, now plural, and the phrase for its motion around you follows it.
- *mostrandovi* ‘showing you’ → “displaying unto you”: “displaying” replaces ‘showing’, and the attached indirect object becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *le sue bellezze eterne* ‘its beauties eternal’ → “their eternal splendours”: the possessive is plural to match “The heavens”; ‘beauties’ becomes “splendours”, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 150 e l' occhio vostro pure a terra mira;  

and your eye gazes only to earth;

- *l' occhio vostro* ‘the eye of yours’ → “your eye”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *pure a terra mira* ‘only to earth looks’ → “gazes only to earth”: “gazes” renders ‘looks’, and the verb moves ahead of the adverb and the phrase of direction.

> 151 onde vi batte chi tutto discerne.”  

wherefore he who discerns all things doth buffet you.”

- *vi batte* ‘you strikes’ → “doth buffet you”: the simple present becomes a periphrastic present with “doth”; “buffet” renders ‘strike’, and the object follows the verb.
- *chi tutto discerne* ‘whoever everything discerns’ → “he who discerns all things”: the subject moves from after the main verb to before it; the single relative pronoun is expanded to “he who”, and ‘everything’ becomes “all things” and follows “discerns”.

## Summary — Word Order and Verbal Choice

Okey’s most frequent departure is a change of order. A subject delayed in Italian often comes before its verb in English, as when *si turba il viso* (‘becomes troubled the face’) becomes “the face of him who listens is troubled”. Object pronouns usually move the other way: *ne consola* (‘us console’) becomes “console us”. Two routine adjustments generally go unmarked in the comparison: Okey supplies subject pronouns where the Italian verb ending already identifies the subject, and omits the Italian article before a possessive, as in *il nome mio*, “my name”.

A change of construction can bring out a distinction without translating each word separately. The imperfect *ragionavan* becomes the progressive “were discoursing”; the subjunctive *sia* in *chi sia* becomes “may be” in “who he may be”. Conversely, *turbarsi e farsi trista* (‘to become troubled and to grow sad’) needs only one English verb, “grow troubled and sad”. Words are also supplied or left unexpressed where the two languages mark a relation differently: *tacendo* (‘keeping silent’) becomes “by their silence”, making the means explicit, while the non-negating *non* of *che non chiede lor possa* has no counterpart in “than their power warrants”.

In his choice of words Okey sometimes specifies, sometimes generalizes. Of a river, *nasce* (‘is born’) becomes “rises” and *va* (‘goes’) becomes “flows”; by contrast, *assanni* (‘fasten its teeth in’) becomes the less pictorial “may assault”. Yet the questions naming Lizio and his companions follow the Italian closely, and Okey does not invariably smooth out its inversions or unusual verbs. In “such straw I reap” he keeps the object before the verb, as in *cotal paglia mieto*; in “re-woods not itself” he goes so far as to fashion an English verb on the model of *si rinselva*.
