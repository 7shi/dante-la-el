# Purgatorio 19

## Lines 1–9 — A Woman Appears in a Dream

> 1 Nell' ora che non può il calor diurno  
> 2 intiepidar più il freddo della luna,  
> 3 vinto da terra o talor da Saturno;  

In the hour when the day’s heat, overcome by Earth or at times by Saturn, can no more warm the cold of the moon;

- *che* ‘in which’ → “when”: a temporal adverb introduces the relative clause.
- *non può … intiepidar più* ‘cannot … make lukewarm any longer’ → “can no more warm”: the negative and *più* are rendered together as “no more”, before the infinitive; “warm” does not specify the degree of warmth.
- *il calor diurno* ‘the heat of the day’ → “the day’s heat”: the adjective becomes a possessive before the noun, and the subject moves from after the modal verb to before it.
- *vinto da terra o talor da Saturno* ‘overcome by earth or sometimes by Saturn’ → “overcome by Earth or at times by Saturn”: the participial phrase moves from the end of the clause to immediately after the noun it qualifies; the single adverb becomes “at times”.

> 4 quando i geomanti lor maggior fortuna  
> 5 veggiono in oriente, innanzi all' alba,  
> 6 surger per via che poco le sta bruna:  

when the geomancers see their Fortuna Major, rising in the East, before the dawn, by a way which short time remains dark to it,

- *lor maggior fortuna* ‘their greater fortune’ → “their Fortuna Major”: the Italian words are given as the Latin name, with the adjective after the noun; the object moves from before the verb of seeing to after it.
- *surger* ‘rise’ → “rising”: the infinitive dependent on the verb of seeing becomes a participle and moves ahead of the phrases of place and time.
- *per via* ‘by way’ → “by a way”: English supplies the indefinite article before the noun.
- *poco le sta bruna* ‘little [time] to it stays dark’ → “short time remains dark to it”: the expression of duration is expanded with “time”; “remains” renders ‘stays’, and the dative pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase after the adjective.

> 7 mi venne in sogno una femmina balba,  
> 8 negli occhi guercia e sopra i piè distorta,  
> 9 con le man monche, e di colore scialba.  

there came to me in a dream, a stuttering woman, with eyes asquint, and crooked on her feet, with maimed hands, and of sallow hue.

- *mi venne* ‘to me came’ → “there came to me”: English introduces “there” and places the recipient after the verb.
- *in sogno* ‘in dream’ → “in a dream”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *una femmina balba* ‘a woman stuttering’ → “a stuttering woman”: the modifier precedes the noun.
- *negli occhi guercia* ‘in the eyes squinting’ → “with eyes asquint”: the adjective describing the woman becomes a predicate of “eyes” in a phrase with “with”; the definite article drops out.
- *sopra i piè distorta* ‘upon the feet crooked’ → “crooked on her feet”: the adjective moves before the prepositional phrase, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *con le man monche* ‘with the hands maimed’ → “with maimed hands”: the adjective moves before the noun, and the definite article drops out.
- *di colore scialba* ‘in colour pale’ → “of sallow hue”: the adjective describing the woman now modifies “hue” and precedes it; “sallow” gives the pallor a more specific tint.

## Lines 10–42 — The Siren’s Song and Dante’s Awakening

> 10 Io la mirava; e, come il sol conforta  
> 11 le fredde membra che la notte aggrava,  

I gazed upon her; and, as the sun comforteth the cold limbs which night weighs down,

- *Io la mirava* ‘I her was gazing at’ → “I gazed upon her”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the object pronoun before the verb becomes the complement of a preposition after it.
- *la notte* ‘the night’ → “night”: the article is omitted.

> 12 così lo sguardo mio le facea scorta  
> 13 la lingua, e poscia tutta la drizzava  
> 14 in poco d' ora, e lo smarrito volto,  
> 15 come amor vuol, così le colorava.  

so my look made ready her tongue, and then set her full straight in short time, and her pallid face even as love wills did colour.

- *lo sguardo mio* ‘the look of mine’ → “my look”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *le facea scorta la lingua* ‘was making agile for her the tongue’ → “made ready her tongue”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, the adjective of nimbleness becomes one of readiness, and the indirect object becomes a possessive.
- *tutta la drizzava* ‘all of her it was straightening’ → “set her full straight”: the imperfect verb becomes a simple past with a predicative adjective; the object follows the verb, and the adjective agreeing with it becomes the intensifier “full”.
- *in poco d' ora* ‘in a little of an hour’ → “in short time”: the partitive expression becomes an adjective modifying “time”.
- *lo smarrito volto, come amor vuol, così le colorava* ‘the wan face, as love wills, so it was colouring for her’ → “her pallid face even as love wills did colour”: the indirect object becomes a possessive before “face”; the correlative *come … così* becomes “even as”, and the imperfect becomes a past with auxiliary “did”.

> 16 Poi ch' ell' avea il parlar così disciolto,  
> 17 cominciava a cantar sì che con pena  
> 18 da lei avrei mio intento rivolto.  

When she had her tongue thus loosed, she began to sing, so that with difficulty should I have turned my attention from her.

- *Poi ch'* ‘after that’ → “When”: the temporal conjunction does not explicitly mark succession in English.
- *il parlar* ‘the speaking’ → “her tongue”: the substantivized infinitive becomes the organ of speech, with a possessive in place of the article.
- *cominciava* ‘she was beginning’ → “she began”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *con pena* ‘with pain’ → “with difficulty”: the phrase is rendered in its idiomatic sense.
- *da lei avrei mio intento rivolto* ‘from her I should have my attention turned’ → “should I have turned my attention from her”: the prepositional phrase moves to the end, the participle comes before its object, and the English subject follows the first auxiliary.

> 19 “Io son,” cantava, “io son dolce Sirena,  
> 20 che i marinari in mezzo mar dismago:  
> 21 tanto son di piacere a sentir piena.  

“I am,” she sang, “I am the sweet Siren, who leads mariners astray in mid-sea, so full am I of pleasantness to hear.

- *cantava* ‘she was singing’ → “she sang”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *dolce Sirena* ‘sweet Siren’ → “the sweet Siren”: a definite article is supplied.
- *che i marinari in mezzo mar dismago* ‘who the mariners in mid-sea I lead astray’ → “who leads mariners astray in mid-sea”: the verb changes from first person to third and moves forward; its object loses the article, and the phrase of place comes last.
- *tanto son di piacere a sentir piena* ‘so much am I of pleasure to hear full’ → “so full am I of pleasantness to hear”: the adjective moves up beside its degree word, and “pleasantness” expresses a quality rather than the pleasure it gives.

> 22 Io volsi Ulisse del suo cammin vago  
> 23 col canto mio; e qual meco si ausa  
> 24 rado sen parte, sì tutto l' appago.”  

I turned Ulysses from his wandering way with my song, and whoso liveth with me rarely departs, so wholly do I satisfy him.”

- *del suo cammin* ‘from his way’ → “from his wandering way”: the adjective that follows *cammin* in Italian is placed before “way”; Okey takes *vago* with the way rather than with Ulysses.
- *col canto mio* ‘with the song of mine’ → “with my song”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *qual meco si ausa* ‘whoever with me accustoms himself’ → “whoso liveth with me”: the reflexive verb of habituation becomes an intransitive verb of dwelling, and its complement follows it.
- *rado sen parte* ‘rarely takes himself away from there’ → “rarely departs”: a single verb replaces the pronominal expression, without separately rendering the sense ‘from there’.
- *sì tutto l' appago* ‘so completely him I satisfy’ → “so wholly do I satisfy him”: English uses auxiliary inversion after the initial adverbial and places the object after the verb.

> 25 Ancor non era sua bocca richiusa,  
> 26 quando una donna apparve santa e presta  
> 27 lunghesso me per far colei confusa.  

Her mouth was not yet shut, when a lady appeared holy and alert alongside me, to put her to confusion.

- *Ancor non era sua bocca richiusa* ‘still not was her mouth closed again’ → “Her mouth was not yet shut”: the subject moves before the verb, the temporal adverb follows the negative, and the sense of closing again is not separately expressed.
- *donna* ‘woman’ → “lady”: the English noun is more honorific.
- *presta* ‘ready’ → “alert”: readiness is rendered as watchfulness.
- *per far colei confusa* ‘to make that woman confounded’ → “to put her to confusion”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the predicative participle becomes a prepositional phrase with a noun.

> 28 “O Virgilio, o Virgilio, chi è questa?”  
> 29 fieramente diceva; ed ei venia  
> 30 con gli occhi fitti pure in quella onesta.  

“O Virgil, Virgil, who is this?” angrily she said; and he came with eyes ever fixed on that honest one.

- *o Virgilio* ‘O Virgil’ → “Virgil”: the second vocative particle is omitted.
- *questa* ‘this woman’ → “this”: the demonstrative no longer marks the feminine.
- *fieramente diceva* ‘fiercely she was saying’ → “angrily she said”: the adverb specifies anger, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *venia* ‘he was coming’ → “he came”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *con gli occhi* ‘with the eyes’ → “with eyes”: the article is omitted.
- *fitti pure* ‘fixed solely’ → “ever fixed”: Okey expresses persistence rather than exclusiveness, and places the adverb before the participle.
- *in quella onesta* ‘upon that worthy woman’ → “on that honest one”: the feminine adjective used substantivally is rendered by an adjective with the supporting pronoun “one”.

> 31 L' altra prendeva, e dinanzi l' apria  
> 32 fendendo i drappi, e mostravami il ventre;  
> 33 quel mi svegliò col puzzo che n' uscia.  

He seized the other, and, rending her clothes, laid her open in front and showed me her belly; that awakened me with the stench which issued therefrom.

- *L' altra prendeva* ‘the other he was taking’ → “He seized the other”: the object moves after the verb, the imperfect becomes a simple past, and “seized” is more forceful than ‘took’.
- *e dinanzi l' apria fendendo i drappi* ‘and in front he was opening her, splitting the garments’ → “and, rending her clothes, laid her open in front”: the participial phrase moves forward and takes a possessive for the article; the imperfect becomes a simple past with a result adjective, and the pronoun and phrase of place move after the verb.
- *mostravami il ventre* ‘was showing me the belly’ → “showed me her belly”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *mi svegliò* ‘me awakened’ → “awakened me”: the object follows the verb.
- *n' uscia* ‘from it was issuing’ → “issued therefrom”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the expression of source follows the verb.

> 34 Io mossi gli occhi, e il buon Virgilio: “Almen tre  
> 35 voci t' ho messe,” dicea; “surgi e vieni,  
> 36 troviam l' aperta per la qual tu entre.”  

I turned my eyes, and the good Virgil said: “At least three calls have I uttered to thee; arise and come, find we the opening by which thou mayst enter.”

- *mossi gli occhi* ‘moved the eyes’ → “turned my eyes”: the verb specifies a change of direction, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *dicea* ‘he was saying’ → “said”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the reporting verb moves from within the speech to before it.
- *voci t' ho messe* ‘voices to thee I have sent forth’ → “calls have I uttered to thee”: “calls” specifies the use of the voice, “uttered” replaces the Italian verbal idiom, and the recipient moves after the verb; English inverts auxiliary and subject after the fronted object.
- *l' aperta* ‘the open space’ → “the opening”: a substantivized adjective becomes a noun.
- *per la qual tu entre* ‘through which thou enter’ → “by which thou mayst enter”: “by” expresses the means of entry, and “mayst” renders the subjunctive.

> 37 Su mi levai, e tutti eran già pieni  
> 38 dell' alto dì i giron del sacro monte,  
> 39 ed andavam col sol nuovo alle reni.  

Up I lifted me, and all the circles of the holy mount were now filled with the high day, and we journeyed with the new sun at our backs.

- *mi levai* ‘myself I lifted’ → “I lifted me”: the reflexive object moves after the verb.
- *tutti eran già pieni dell' alto dì i giron del sacro monte* ‘all were already full of the high day, the circles of the holy mount’ → “all the circles of the holy mount were now filled with the high day”: the postponed subject joins its quantifier before the verb, “now” replaces ‘already’, and the adjective with its complement becomes “filled with”.
- *andavam* ‘we were going’ → “we journeyed”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, with a verb specific to travel.
- *col sol nuovo* ‘with the sun new’ → “with the new sun”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *alle reni* ‘at the loins’ → “at our backs”: a less anatomically specific noun is used, and the article becomes a possessive.

> 40 Seguendo lui, portava la mia fronte  
> 41 come colui che l' ha di pensier carca,  
> 42 che fa di sè un mezzo arco di ponte,  

Following him, I was bearing my brow like one that hath it burdened with thought, who makes of himself half an arch of a bridge,

- *la mia fronte* ‘my forehead’ → “my brow”: Okey uses the shorter literary term.
- *come colui che* ‘as that one who’ → “like one that”: the explicit demonstrative force is dropped.
- *l' ha di pensier carca* ‘it hath with thought laden’ → “hath it burdened with thought”: the pronoun moves after the verb, and the participial adjective precedes its complement.
- *un mezzo arco di ponte* ‘a half arch of bridge’ → “half an arch of a bridge”: “half” quantifies “an arch” rather than modifying ‘arch’ after its article, and “bridge” gains an article.

## Lines 43–75 — The Ascent to the Fifth Circle

> 43 quand' io udi': “Venite, qui si varca,”  
> 44 parlare in modo soave e benigno,  
> 45 qual non si sente in questa mortal marca.  

when I heard: “Come, here is the pass,” spoken in a tone so gentle and kind as is not heard in this mortal confine.

- *qui si varca* ‘here one crosses’ → “here is the pass”: the impersonal verb of crossing becomes a noun naming the place to cross.
- *parlare* ‘speak’ → “spoken”: the active infinitive dependent on ‘heard’ becomes a passive participle referring to the words heard.
- *in modo soave e benigno* ‘in a manner sweet and kindly’ → “in a tone so gentle and kind”: manner of speaking is narrowed to tone of voice; the first adjective is rendered by “gentle”, and “so” is supplied in anticipation of the comparison.
- *qual non si sente* ‘such as one does not hear’ → “as is not heard”: the comparison is divided between the preceding “so” and “as”, and the impersonal construction becomes passive.
- *in questa mortal marca* ‘in this mortal borderland’ → “in this mortal confine”: the word for a bounded territory or march is rendered by a word for a limit or enclosure.

> 46 Con l' ali aperte che parean di cigno,  
> 47 volseci in su colui che sì parlonne,  
> 48 tra' due pareti del duro macigno.  

With outspread wings which swanlike seemed, he who thus spoke to us did turn us upward, between the two walls of the hard stone.

- *Con l' ali aperte* ‘with the wings open’ → “With outspread wings”: the article drops out, and the adjective becomes a participle placed before the noun.
- *che parean di cigno* ‘which seemed to be of a swan’ → “which swanlike seemed”: the phrase of possession becomes an adjective of resemblance, placed before the verb.
- *volseci in su colui che sì parlonne* ‘turned us upward he who thus spoke to us’ → “he who thus spoke to us did turn us upward”: the subject, including its relative clause, moves before the main verb; the simple past is expressed with “did” and an infinitive, and the attached pronouns become separate words.

> 49 Mosse le penne poi e ventilonne,  
> 50 *qui lugent* affermando esser beati,  
> 51 ch' avran di consolar l' anime donne.  

He stirred his pinions then, and fanned us, affirming *qui lugent* to be blessed, for they shall have their souls rich in consolation.

- *Mosse le penne* ‘moved the feathers’ → “He stirred his pinions”: “stirred” specifies the movement, “pinions” takes the feathers collectively, and a possessive replaces the article.
- *qui lugent affermando esser beati* ‘those who mourn affirming to be blessed’ → “affirming *qui lugent* to be blessed”: the Latin is retained, but the participle moves before the subject of the infinitive.
- *ch' avran di consolar l' anime donne* ‘for they shall have, of consolation, their souls mistresses’ → “for they shall have their souls rich in consolation”: the complement moves from before ‘their souls’ to the end; the noun meaning ‘mistresses’ becomes the adjective “rich”, and having command of consolation becomes being rich in it.

> 52 “Che hai, che pure in ver la terra guati?”  
> 53 la guida mia incominciò a dirmi,  

“What aileth thee, that thou gazest ever to the ground?” my Guide began to say to me;

- *Che hai* ‘what hast thou’ → “What aileth thee”: what the addressee has becomes what affects him; the subject becomes the object.
- *che pure in ver la terra guati* ‘that continually toward the ground thou gazest’ → “that thou gazest ever to the ground”: the verb moves before both the adverb and the phrase of direction, and ‘toward’ becomes “to”.
- *la guida mia* ‘the guide of mine’ → “my Guide”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 54 poco ambo e due dall' angel sormontati.  

both of us having mounted a little above the angel.

- *poco ambo e due dall' angel sormontati* ‘a little, both of us, above the angel mounted’ → “both of us having mounted a little above the angel”: the absolute phrase is recast with a perfect participle; the subject comes first, and both the measure of ascent and the reference to the angel follow the verb.

> 55 Ed io: “Con tanta suspizion fa irmi  
> 56 novella vision ch' a sè mi piega,  
> 57 sì ch' io non posso dal pensar partirmi.”  

And I: “In such dread I am made to go by a strange vision, which bends me to itself, so that I cannot keep me from thinking thereon.”

- *Con tanta suspizion* ‘with so much apprehension’ → “In such dread”: accompaniment becomes a state, the expression of degree changes, and “dread” is stronger than apprehension.
- *fa irmi novella vision* ‘makes me go a new vision’ → “I am made to go by a strange vision”: the causative becomes passive, with the vision expressed as agent instead of subject; ‘new’ is interpreted as unfamiliar or strange.
- *ch' a sè mi piega* ‘which to itself me bends’ → “which bends me to itself”: verb, object, and directional complement are reordered.
- *sì ch' io non posso dal pensar partirmi* ‘so that I cannot from the thinking withdraw myself’ → “so that I cannot keep me from thinking thereon”: the reflexive verb of departure becomes one of restraint and moves before its complement; the nominal infinitive becomes a gerund, with “thereon” supplied to specify its object.

> 58 “Vedesti,” disse, “quella antica strega,  
> 59 che sola sopra noi omai si piagne?  

“Sawest thou,” he said, “that ancient witch because of whom alone above us now they weep?

- *che sola* ‘who alone’ → “because of whom alone”: the relative is interpreted as giving the cause of the weeping, and the causal relation is made explicit.
- *si piagne* ‘one weeps’ → “they weep”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal plural.

> 60 vedesti come l' uom da lei si slega?  

Sawest thou how man frees him from her?

- *da lei si slega* ‘from her he unbinds himself’ → “frees him from her”: the verb of untying becomes the more general verb of freeing, the reflexive is expressed by “him”, and the phrase of separation moves to the end.

> 61 Bastiti, e batti a terra le calcagne,  

Let that suffice thee, and spurn the earth with thy heels,

- *Bastiti* ‘may it suffice thee’ → “Let that suffice thee”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”, and “that” expressly points back.
- *e batti a terra le calcagne* ‘and strike against the ground the heels’ → “and spurn the earth with thy heels”: the ground becomes the direct object of a verb meaning to kick or thrust away; the heels become the instrument, with a possessive in place of the article.

> 62 gli occhi rivolgi al logoro, che gira  
> 63 lo Rege eterno con le rote magne.”  

turn thine eyes to the lure which the eternal King spinneth round with the mighty spheres.”

- *gli occhi rivolgi* ‘the eyes turn’ → “turn thine eyes”: the object follows the imperative, and a possessive replaces the article.
- *che gira lo Rege eterno* ‘which turns the King eternal’ → “which the eternal King spinneth round”: the subject moves before the verb and its adjective before the noun; ‘turns’ is expanded into an explicitly circular motion.
- *con le rote magne* ‘with the wheels great’ → “with the mighty spheres”: the wheels are rendered as spheres, and the adjective is interpreted as one of power and moved before the noun.

> 64 Quale il falcon che prima ai piè si mira,  
> 65 indi si volge al grido, e si protende  
> 66 per lo disio del pasto che là il tira:  

Like the falcon, that first gazes at his feet, then turns at the call, and spreads his wings with desire of the repast which draws him there,

- *Quale il falcon* ‘such as the falcon’ → “Like the falcon”: the opening member of the comparison becomes a preposition.
- *ai piè si mira* ‘at his feet looks at himself’ → “gazes at his feet”: the reflexive construction is absorbed into the act of gazing at a part of oneself; the feet follow the verb and take a possessive.
- *si volge al grido* ‘turns himself at the cry’ → “turns at the call”: the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb, and the cry is interpreted as a call.
- *si protende* ‘stretches himself out’ → “spreads his wings”: the reflexive becomes a transitive construction specifying the wings.
- *per lo disio* ‘through the desire’ → “with desire”: the causal phrase becomes one of accompanying feeling, without the article.
- *che là il tira* ‘which there him pulls’ → “which draws him there”: the verb moves before the pronoun, and the adverb of place moves to the end.

> 67 tal mi fec' io, e tal, quanto si fende  
> 68 la roccia per dar via a chi va suso,  
> 69 n' andai infino ove il cerchiar si prende.  

such I became; and, far as the rock is cleft to give passage to him who mounts, such I went, up to where the circling is begun.

- *tal mi fec' io* ‘such made myself I’ → “such I became”: the reflexive verb becomes a verb of change of state, and the subject moves before it.
- *e tal, quanto si fende la roccia* ‘and such, as far as cleaves itself the rock’ → “and, far as the rock is cleft”: “such” is deferred until after the intervening clause; within that clause the rock moves before the verb, and the pronominal construction becomes passive.
- *per dar via* ‘to give way’ → “to give passage”: “passage” is used for ‘way’, avoiding the different English idiom ‘give way’.
- *a chi va suso* ‘to whoever goes up’ → “to him who mounts”: the indefinite relative gains an explicit antecedent, and verb and adverb become a single verb.
- *n' andai infino* ‘I went on from there as far as’ → “I went, up to”: the particle marking movement away or onward has no separate equivalent, and the limit of movement is expressed by “up to”.
- *il cerchiar si prende* ‘the circling is taken up’ → “the circling is begun”: taking up an activity is rendered directly as beginning it.

> 70 Com' io nel quinto giro fui dischiuso,  
> 71 vidi gente per esso che piangea,  
> 72 giacendo a terra tutta volta in giuso.  

When I was in the open, on the fifth circle, I saw people about it who wept, lying on the ground all turned downwards.

- *nel quinto giro fui dischiuso* ‘into the fifth circuit I was released’ → “I was in the open, on the fifth circle”: the passive event becomes a state in the open; the phrase naming the circuit moves after the verb and becomes a location with “on”.
- *vidi gente per esso che piangea* ‘I saw folk throughout it that was weeping’ → “I saw people about it who wept”: distribution over the circle becomes “about it”; the singular collective and its singular relative verb are construed as plural, and the imperfect is rendered by a simple past.
- *giacendo a terra* ‘lying on earth’ → “lying on the ground”: English uses a noun phrase with the article for the bare Italian expression of place.
- *tutta volta in giuso* ‘all of it turned downward’ → “all turned downwards”: the singular agreement with the collective noun disappears; “all” applies to the people in the plural.

> 73 “*Adhaesit pavimento anima mea*,”  
> 74 senti' dir lor con sì alti sospiri,  
> 75 che la parola appena s' intendea.  

“*Adhaesit pavimento anima mea*,” I heard them say with such deep sighs that hardly were the words understood.

- *senti' dir lor* ‘I heard say them’ → “I heard them say”: the pronoun denoting the speakers moves before the infinitive.
- *con sì alti sospiri* ‘with so high sighs’ → “with such deep sighs”: degree is expressed by “such” modifying the noun phrase, and the adjective of height or intensity becomes “deep”.
- *che la parola appena s' intendea* ‘that the utterance scarcely was understood’ → “that hardly were the words understood”: the singular utterance becomes plural words; the adverb moves to the front and the auxiliary before the subject, with an English passive for the Italian construction with *si*.

## Lines 76–108 — The Way Up and the Pope’s Late Conversion

> 76 “O eletti di Dio, li cui soffriri  
> 77 e giustizia e speranza fan men duri,  
> 78 drizzate noi verso gli alti saliri.”  

“O chosen of God, whose sufferings both justice and hope make less hard, direct us towards the high ascents.”

- *e giustizia e speranza* ‘and justice and hope’ → “both justice and hope”: the first of the paired conjunctions becomes “both”.
- *verso gli alti saliri* ‘towards the high climbings’ → “towards the high ascents”: the substantivized infinitive becomes an ordinary plural noun.

> 79 “Se voi venite dal giacer sicuri,  
> 80 e volete trovar la via più tosto,  
> 81 le vostre destre sien sempre di furi.”  

“If ye come secure from lying prostrate, and desire to find the way most quickly, let your right hands be ever to the outside.”

- *dal giacer sicuri* ‘from lying down secure’ → “secure from lying prostrate”: the adjective precedes its complement, and “prostrate” makes the posture explicit.
- *più tosto* ‘more quickly’ → “most quickly”: the comparative becomes a superlative.
- *le vostre destre sien* ‘your right hands be’ → “let your right hands be”: “let” expresses the injunction conveyed by the subjunctive.

> 82 Così pregò il poeta, e sì risposto  
> 83 poco dinanzi a noi ne fu; per ch' io  
> 84 nel parlare avvisai l' altro nascosto,  

Thus prayed the poet, and thus a little in front of us was answer made; wherefore I noted what else was concealed in the words,

- *sì risposto poco dinanzi a noi ne fu* ‘thus answered a little before us to us was’ → “thus a little in front of us was answer made”: the place phrase precedes the whole predicate instead of separating participle and auxiliary; the impersonal passive becomes a passive with the noun “answer”, and the dative *ne* (‘to us’) is not separately rendered.
- *per ch' io nel parlare avvisai l' altro nascosto* ‘for which I in the speaking perceived the other thing hidden’ → “wherefore I noted what else was concealed in the words”: “wherefore” combines preposition and relative; the substantivized adjective and its participle become a clause with a finite passive. The phrase denoting speech moves to the end, where it qualifies what was concealed rather than the act of noticing.

> 85 e volsi gli occhi allora al signor mio:  
> 86 ond' egli m' assentì con lieto cenno  
> 87 ciò che chiedea la vista del disio.  

and turned mine eyes then to my Lord; whereat he gave assent with glad sign to what the look of my desire was craving.

- *ond' egli m' assentì con lieto cenno* ‘whereupon he granted me with glad sign’ → “whereat he gave assent with glad sign”: the verb becomes a verb–noun phrase, and the recipient *m’* is left unexpressed.
- *ciò che chiedea la vista del disio* ‘that which asked the look of the desire’ → “to what the look of my desire was craving”: “to” completes “gave assent”; “what” combines antecedent and relative. The subject precedes the verb, the imperfect is rendered by a progressive, asking becomes craving, and “my” replaces the definite article.

> 88 Poi ch' io potei di me fare a mio senno,  
> 89 trassimi sopra quella creatura,  
> 90 le cui parole pria notar mi fenno,  

When I could do with me according to my own mind, I drew forward above that creature whose words before made me take note,

- *Poi ch'* ‘after that’ → “When”: the temporal conjunction loses its explicit sense of succession.
- *di me fare a mio senno* ‘of me do at my judgment’ → “do with me according to my own mind”: the verb precedes its complement, “with” replaces ‘of’, and the phrase of discretion is expanded with “according to” and the emphatic “own”.
- *trassimi* ‘I drew myself’ → “I drew forward”: a directional adverb replaces the reflexive pronoun.
- *notar mi fenno* ‘take note me made’ → “made me take note”: the causative verb precedes both pronoun and infinitive.

> 91 dicendo: “Spirto, in cui pianger matura  
> 92 quel senza il quale a Dio tornar non puossi,  
> 93 sosta un poco per me tua maggior cura.  

saying: “Spirit, in whom weeping matures that without which one cannot turn to God, stay a while for me thy greater care.

- *a Dio tornar non puossi* ‘to God return one cannot’ → “one cannot turn to God”: the impersonal construction becomes one with the subject “one”; the negative modal precedes the infinitive, its complement follows, and “turn” does not express the ‘back’ of *tornar*.
- *un poco* ‘a little’ → “a while”: the expression of amount becomes explicitly temporal.

> 94 Chi fosti e perchè volti avete i dossi  
> 95 al su, mi di', e se vuoi ch' io t' impetri  
> 96 cosa di là, ond' io vivendo mossi.”  

Who thou wast, and why ye have your backs turned upward, tell me, and if thou wouldst that I obtain aught for thee yonder, whence living I set forth.”

- *volti avete i dossi al su* ‘turned have ye the backs toward the up’ → “ye have your backs turned upward”: the participle follows verb and object, a possessive replaces the definite article, and an adverb replaces the prepositional phrase.
- *mi di'* ‘me tell’ → “tell me”: the pronoun follows the imperative.
- *se vuoi* ‘if thou willest’ → “if thou wouldst”: the present indicative of wishing becomes a modal expression of willingness.
- *ch' io t' impetri cosa* ‘that I for thee obtain by entreaty something’ → “that I obtain aught for thee”: “obtain” leaves the means implicit, “aught” renders the indefinite object under “if”, and the beneficiary follows the object.
- *di là* ‘from there’ → “yonder”: the adverb gives the place without a separate preposition of source.
- *ond' io vivendo mossi* ‘whence I living moved’ → “whence living I set forth”: the participle precedes the subject, and “set forth” specifies the movement as a departure.

> 97 Ed egli a me: “Perchè i nostri diretri  
> 98 rivolga il cielo a sè, saprai; ma prima,  
> 99 *scias quod ego fui successor Petri*.  

And he to me: “Wherefore heaven turneth our backs to itself shalt thou know; but first, *scias quod ego fui successor Petri*.

- *i nostri diretri rivolga il cielo a sè* ‘our backs should turn heaven to itself’ → “heaven turneth our backs to itself”: within the indirect question the Italian places the object first and the subject after the verb; English has subject–verb–object order and an indicative in place of the subjunctive.
- *saprai* ‘thou shalt know’ → “shalt thou know”: the English future has its auxiliary before the subject after the fronted object clause.

> 100 Intra Siestri e Chiaveri si adima  
> 101 una fiumana bella, e del suo nome  
> 102 lo titol del mio sangue fa sua cima.  

Between Sestri and Chiaveri flows down a fair river, and from its name the title of my race takes origin.

- *si adima* ‘lowers itself’ → “flows down”: the reflexive verb of descent becomes an intransitive verb specifying the motion of water, with a directional adverb.
- *una fiumana bella* ‘a river fair’ → “a fair river”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *del mio sangue* ‘of my blood’ → “of my race”: the translation gives the lineage meant by the figure of blood.
- *fa sua cima* ‘makes its summit’ → “takes origin”: the image of the title’s summit or head is interpreted as a statement of its source; neither the possessive nor the image is retained.

> 103 Un mese e poco più prova' io come  
> 104 pesa il gran manto a chi dal fango il guarda,  
> 105 che piuma sembran tutte l' altre some.  

One month, and little more, I learned how the great mantle weighs on him who keeps it from the mire, so that all other burdens seem feathers.

- *poco più* ‘a little more’ → “little more”: the English uses “little” without the indefinite article.
- *prova' io* ‘experienced I’ → “I learned”: the subject precedes the verb, and the result of experience replaces the experiencing.
- *pesa il gran manto* ‘weighs the great mantle’ → “the great mantle weighs”: the subject precedes the verb.
- *a chi dal fango il guarda* ‘to whoever from the mire guards it’ → “on him who keeps it from the mire”: “on” completes the English verb of weighing, the indefinite relative becomes pronoun plus relative, and the verb precedes both its object and the phrase of protection.
- *che piuma sembran tutte l' altre some* ‘that a feather seem all the other burdens’ → “so that all other burdens seem feathers”: “so” marks the result explicitly; the subject moves before the verb and loses its definite article, and the predicate noun follows the verb in the plural instead of the singular.

> 106 La mia conversione, omè! fu tarda;  
> 107 ma, come fatto fui Roman Pastore,  
> 108 così scopersi la vita bugiarda.  

My conversion, ah me! was late; but when I was made Pastor of Rome, so I discovered the life which is false.

- *come fatto fui Roman Pastore* ‘as made I was Roman Pastor’ → “when I was made Pastor of Rome”: “when” gives the temporal sense of the first member of the *come … così* construction; subject and auxiliary precede the participle, and the adjective becomes a following phrase with “of”.
- *la vita bugiarda* ‘the lying life’ → “the life which is false”: the adjective becomes a relative clause, with “false” in place of the personifying ‘lying’.

## Lines 109–141 — Avarice’s Penance and a Fellowservant’s Rebuke

> 109 Vidi che lì non si quetava il core,  
> 110 nè più salir poteasi in quella vita:  

I saw that there the heart was not at rest, nor could one mount higher in that life;

- *non si quetava il core* ‘did not quiet itself the heart’ → “the heart was not at rest”: the subject moves before the verb, and a phrase of state replaces the reflexive verb.
- *nè più salir poteasi* ‘nor higher to climb was it possible’ → “nor could one mount higher”: the impersonal construction becomes personal with indefinite “one”; the modal verb comes before the infinitive, and “higher” follows it.

> 111 per che di questa in me s' accese amore.  

wherefore love of this was kindled within me.

- *di questa* ‘of this [life]’ → “of this”: the phrase moves from before the verb to after “love”; English “this” does not mark the feminine agreement with the preceding noun.
- *in me s' accese amore* ‘in me caught fire love’ → “love of this was kindled within me”: the subject comes first and the phrase of place last; the Italian verb of becoming alight is rendered by a passive.

> 112 Fino a quel punto misera e partita  
> 113 da Dio anima fui, del tutto avara:  

Up to that moment, I was a soul wretched and parted from God, wholly avaricious;

- *misera e partita da Dio anima fui* ‘wretched and parted from God a soul I was’ → “I was a soul wretched and parted from God”: the copula moves to the front of the predicate, the noun comes before its modifiers, and English supplies the indefinite article.

> 114 or, come vedi, qui ne son punita.  

now, as thou seest, here am I punished for it.

- *qui ne son punita* ‘here for it I am punished’ → “here am I punished for it”: “for it” moves from before the verb to the end, and the expressed subject follows the auxiliary.

> 115 Quel ch' avarizia fa qui si dichiara  
> 116 in purgazion dell' anime converse,  
> 117 e nulla pena il monte ha più amara.  

What avarice works, here is declared in the purgation of the down-turned souls, and no more bitter penalty hath the mount.

- *in purgazion* ‘in purgation’ → “in the purgation”: the definite article is supplied.
- *dell' anime converse* ‘of the souls turned’ → “of the down-turned souls”: the participle moves before the noun; Okey takes the turning as bodily and supplies its direction, rather than rendering the participle as ‘converted’.
- *nulla pena il monte ha più amara* ‘no penalty the mount has more bitter’ → “no more bitter penalty hath the mount”: the comparative adjective moves next to its noun, before it, and the verb moves before the subject.

> 118 Sì come l' occhio nostro non s' aderse  
> 119 in alto, fisso alle cose terrene,  
> 120 così giustizia qui a terra il merse.  

Even as our eye, fixed on earthly things, did not lift itself on high, so here justice hath sunk it to earth.

- *non s' aderse in alto* ‘did not raise itself on high’ → “did not lift itself on high”: the verb and its complement come after the description of the eye rather than before it.
- *fisso alle cose terrene* ‘fixed to the things earthly’ → “fixed on earthly things”: English uses “on” with “fixed”, drops the definite article, and puts the adjective first.
- *giustizia qui a terra il merse* ‘justice here to earth it sank’ → “here justice hath sunk it to earth”: “here” precedes the subject; the object and the phrase of direction follow the verb. The Italian simple past becomes an English present perfect.

> 121 Come avarizia spense a ciascun bene  
> 122 lo nostro amore, onde operar perde' si,  

As avarice quenched our love for every good, wherefore our works were lost,

- *a ciascun bene lo nostro amore* ‘toward each good our love’ → “our love for every good”: the object now precedes the phrase qualifying it; “for” expresses the direction of love, and “every” replaces distributive ‘each’.
- *onde operar perde' si* ‘whence acting was lost’ → “wherefore our works were lost”: English makes the consequence explicit with “wherefore”, turns the substantival infinitive into a plural noun, adds a possessive, and renders the construction with *si* by a plural passive.

> 123 così giustizia qui stretti ne tiene,  
> 124 ne' piedi e nelle man legati e presi;  

so justice here doth hold us fast, bound and seised by feet and hands;

- *stretti ne tiene* ‘fast us holds’ → “doth hold us fast”: the finite verb is expanded with “doth”, and both the object and its complement move after “hold”.
- *ne' piedi e nelle man legati e presi* ‘in the feet and in the hands bound and taken’ → “bound and seised by feet and hands”: the participles come first; one “by” replaces the two prepositions, the articles disappear, and ‘taken’ is rendered by “seised”.

> 125 e quanto fia piacer del giusto Sire,  
> 126 tanto staremo immobili e distesi.”  

and so long as it shall be the pleasure of the just Lord, so long shall we lie here motionless and outstretched.”

- *quanto … tanto* ‘as much … so much’ → “so long as” … “so long”: the correlatives are made explicitly temporal.
- *fia piacer* ‘shall be pleasure’ → “it shall be the pleasure”: English supplies an impersonal subject and a definite article.
- *del giusto Sire* ‘of the just Sire’ → “of the just Lord”: “Lord” replaces the title ‘Sire’.
- *staremo* ‘we shall remain’ → “shall we lie here”: the subject follows the auxiliary, “lie” specifies posture, and “here” is supplied.

> 127 Io m' era inginocchiato, e volea dire;  

I had kneeled down, and was about to speak;

- *m' era inginocchiato* ‘had knelt’ → “had kneeled down”: English uses a non-reflexive verb with “down” for the Italian pronominal verb.
- *volea dire* ‘wished to speak’ → “was about to speak”: intention is rendered as imminence.

> 128 ma com' io cominciai, ed ei s' accorse,  
> 129 solo ascoltando, del mio riverire,  

but as I began, and he perceived my reverence merely by listening,

- *s' accorse … del mio riverire* ‘became aware … of my showing reverence’ → “perceived my reverence”: a transitive verb replaces the pronominal verb with its prepositional complement; the substantival infinitive becomes a noun.
- *solo ascoltando* ‘only listening’ → “merely by listening”: “by” explicitly marks the gerund as expressing means, and the phrase moves after the object.

> 130 “Qual cagion,” disse, “in giù così ti torse?”  

“What reason,” he said, “thus bent thee down?”

- *in giù così ti torse* ‘downward thus thee bent’ → “thus bent thee down”: the direction moves to the end, and the object follows the verb.

> 131 Ed io a lui: “Per vostra dignitate  
> 132 mia coscienza dritto mi rimorse.”  

And I to him: “Because of your dignity my conscience smote me for standing.”

- *Per vostra dignitate* ‘on account of your dignity’ → “Because of your dignity”: the English possessive conveys possession but does not distinguish the deferential form of address.
- *dritto mi rimorse* ‘upright me bit’ → “smote me for standing”: the adjective describing the speaker becomes a phrase giving the occasion for the reproach; striking replaces biting, and the object follows the verb.

> 133 “Drizza le gambe, levati su frate,”  
> 134 rispose; “non errar, conservo sono  
> 135 teco e con gli altri ad una potestate.  

“Make straight thy legs, uplift thee, brother,” he answered; “err not, a fellowservant am I with thee and with the others unto one Power.

- *Drizza le gambe* ‘straighten the legs’ → “Make straight thy legs”: a verb and adjective replace the single imperative, and the definite article before the body part becomes a possessive.
- *levati su* ‘raise thyself up’ → “uplift thee”: the separate directional adverb is absorbed into the verb.
- *non errar* ‘not to err’ → “err not”: the Italian negative command uses an infinitive with preceding negation; English uses an imperative with following negation.
- *conservo sono* ‘fellowservant I am’ → “a fellowservant am I”: English adds the indefinite article and places the expressed subject after the copula.

> 136 Se mai quel santo evangelico suono  
> 137 che dice ‘*Neque nubent*’ intendesti,  
> 138 ben puoi veder perch' io così ragiono.  

If ever thou didst understand that hallowed gospel sound which saith, ‘*Neque nubent*,’ well canst thou see why thus I speak.

- *quel santo evangelico suono che dice ‘Neque nubent’ intendesti* ‘that holy evangelical sound which says ‘Neque nubent’ thou understoodest’ → “thou didst understand that hallowed gospel sound which saith, ‘*Neque nubent*,’”: the governing verb moves before its object and is expanded with “didst”; “hallowed” renders ‘holy’, and the attributive noun “gospel” replaces an adjective.
- *ben puoi veder* ‘well thou canst see’ → “well canst thou see”: after the initial adverb, the expressed subject follows the modal.
- *così ragiono* ‘thus I discourse’ → “thus I speak”: the more general verb “speak” replaces ‘discourse’.

> 139 Vattene omai; non vo' che più t' arresti,  

Now get thee hence; I desire not that thou stay longer,

- *Vattene omai* ‘get thee hence now’ → “Now get thee hence”: the time adverb moves to the front.
- *più t' arresti* ‘longer thou stop thyself’ → “thou stay longer”: the pronominal verb becomes intransitive “stay”, and the adverb follows it.

> 140 chè la tua stanza mio pianger disagia,  
> 141 col qual maturo ciò che tu dicesti.  

for thy tarrying disturbs my weeping, whereby I mature that which thou didst say.

- *mio pianger disagia* ‘my weeping inconveniences’ → “disturbs my weeping”: the verb moves before its object, and “disturbs” renders the sense of causing hindrance.
- *col qual* ‘with which’ → “whereby”: a single relative adverb expresses the instrumental phrase.
- *tu dicesti* ‘thou saidst’ → “thou didst say”: the simple past becomes a construction with auxiliary “didst”.

## Lines 142–145 — His Niece Alagia

> 142 Nepote ho io di là ch' ha nome Alagia,  

A niece have I yonder, by name Alagia,

- *Nepote* ‘niece’ → “A niece”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *ch' ha nome Alagia* ‘who has the name Alagia’ → “by name Alagia”: a prepositional phrase replaces the relative clause.

> 143 buona da sè, pur che la nostra casa  
> 144 non faccia lei per esemplo malvagia;  

good in herself, if but our house make her not evil by ensample;

- *da sè* ‘of herself’ → “in herself”: the preposition changes.
- *pur che* ‘provided that’ → “if but”: the condition is expressed with “if”, followed by the restrictive “but”.
- *non faccia lei per esemplo malvagia* ‘not make her by example evil’ → “make her not evil by ensample”: the negative moves from before the subjunctive verb to after its object, and the predicative adjective comes before the phrase of means rather than after it.

> 145 e questa sola di là m' è rimasa.”  

and she alone is left me yonder.”

- *questa* ‘this one’ → “she”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *di là m' è rimasa* ‘yonder to me has remained’ → “is left me yonder”: the perfect of an intransitive verb becomes a construction expressing the resulting state, the dative pronoun follows the participle, and the adverbial moves from before the verb phrase to the end.

## Summary — Where Okey Follows the Italian Order and Where He Changes It

Okey often brings a postponed subject forward, as in *pesa il gran manto*, ‘weighs the great mantle’, rendered “the great mantle weighs”. More than a single word may move: *vinto da terra o talor da Saturno* comes at the end of its clause, but “overcome by Earth or at times by Saturn” follows immediately after “the day’s heat”, making clear what is overcome. Yet Okey also keeps some conspicuous inversions. In “A niece have I yonder”, the object, verb, subject, and adverb follow the order of *Nepote ho io di là*.

Changes of construction often go with changes of emphasis. Italian imperfects frequently become simple pasts, as when *cantava*, ‘she was singing’, becomes “she sang”. The impersonal *qui si varca*, ‘here one crosses’, becomes “here is the pass”: the action gives way to the place where it is performed. Okey sometimes makes a detail explicit: *si protende*, ‘stretches himself out’, becomes “spreads his wings”. Elsewhere he leaves a distinction unexpressed: “shut” renders *richiusa* without the sense of closing again. A change of word can also change the image, as when “rich in consolation” replaces the figure in *donne* of souls as ‘mistresses’ of consolation.

Two routine adjustments generally go unmarked in the comparison. English supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the person in the verb ending, and omits the Italian article before a possessive: *portava la mia fronte* becomes “I was bearing my brow”. In other respects Okey can follow the phrasing closely. He keeps the repetition of *Io son* in “I am,” she sang, “I am the sweet Siren”, preserves the paired movement of *sì come … così* in “Even as” and “so”, and leaves the Latin phrases in Latin.
