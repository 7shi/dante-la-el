# Purgatorio 4

## Lines 1–3 — The Soul’s Concentration

> 1 Quando per dilettanze ovver per doglie,  
> 2 che alcuna virtù nostra comprenda,  
> 3 l' anima bene ad essa si raccoglie,  

When through impression of pleasure, or of pain, which some one of our faculties receives, the soul is wholly centred on that faculty,

- *per dilettanze ovver per doglie* ‘through delights or through pains’ → “through impression of pleasure, or of pain”: “impression” is supplied; the two plural nouns become singular mass nouns dependent on it, and the second *per* is not repeated as “through”.
- *alcuna virtù nostra* ‘some faculty of ours’ → “some one of our faculties”: the single faculty is picked out by “one” from a plural set, and the possessive precedes “faculties”.
- *comprenda* ‘may apprehend’ → “receives”: apprehending is rendered as receiving, and the Italian present subjunctive becomes an English present indicative.
- *bene ad essa si raccoglie* ‘well upon it gathers itself’ → “is wholly centred on that faculty”: “wholly” renders the intensifying *bene*; the reflexive action becomes a state expressed by “is … centred”, and the phrase naming its focus moves after the participle, with “that faculty” in place of the pronoun.

## Lines 4–36 — The Absorbed Soul and the Steep Ascent

> 4 par che a nulla potenza più intenda;  

it seems that it gives heed to no other of its powers;

- *a nulla potenza più intenda* ‘to no power any longer it attends’ → “it gives heed to no other of its powers”: the verb comes first and becomes a phrase with a noun; the sense of *più* is conveyed by “other” rather than by an adverb of time. The singular *potenza* becomes plural in the partitive “of its powers,” with the possessive supplied.

> 5 e questo è contra quello error, che crede  
> 6 che un' anima sopr' altra in noi s' accenda.  

and this is contrary to that error, which believes that one soul above another is kindled within us.

- *contra* ‘against’ → “contrary to”: a preposition becomes an adjective with a preposition.
- *in noi s' accenda* ‘in us kindles itself’ → “is kindled within us”: the pronominal verb becomes passive, and the phrase of place follows it.

> 7 E però, quando s' ode cosa o vede  
> 8 che tenga forte a sè l' anima volta,  
> 9 vassene il tempo, e l' uom non se n' avvede:  

And therefore, when aught is heard or seen which holds the soul strongly bent to it, the time passes away and we perceive it not;

- *s' ode cosa o vede* ‘one hears a thing or sees [it]’ → “aught is heard or seen”: the indefinite construction becomes an English passive, with the thing heard or seen placed first and rendered by a pronoun.
- *che tenga forte a sè l' anima volta* ‘that may hold strongly toward itself the soul turned’ → “which holds the soul strongly bent to it”: the relative subjunctive becomes indicative; the object comes directly after the verb, “strongly” goes with “bent,” and the reflexive phrase becomes “to it” after the participle.
- *vassene il tempo* ‘goes its way the time’ → “the time passes away”: the subject comes first, and the pronominal verb of going becomes an English phrase for the passage of time.
- *l' uom non se n' avvede* ‘one does not become aware of it’ → “we perceive it not”: the generic singular becomes first-person plural, the pronominal construction becomes a transitive verb with a direct object, and the negative comes last.

> 10 ch' altra potenza è quella che l' ascolta,  
> 11 ed altra quella che ha l' anima intera;  
> 12 questa è quasi legata, e quella è sciolta.  

for one faculty is that which notes it, and another that which possesses the undivided soul; the former is as ’twere bound, the latter free.

- *altra potenza è quella* ‘a different power is that’ → “one faculty is that”: the first *altra* becomes “one” in anticipation of “another,” and *potenza* is rendered in its sense of a faculty.
- *l' ascolta* ‘listens to it’ → “notes it”: the verb of hearing becomes one of noticing, and the object follows it.
- *ha l' anima intera* ‘has the soul entire’ → “possesses the undivided soul”: ‘has’ is strengthened to “possesses,” and the postposed adjective becomes “undivided” before the noun.
- *questa* ‘this one’ → “the former”: where the demonstrative ordinarily points to the nearer, second-mentioned faculty, Okey expressly identifies the first.
- *quasi legata* ‘as if bound’ → “as ’twere bound”: the qualifying adverb is expanded into a clause.
- *e quella è sciolta* ‘and that one is loose’ → “the latter free”: the more distant demonstrative is assigned to the second-mentioned faculty, ‘loose’ becomes “free,” and both conjunction and copula drop out.

> 13 Di ciò ebb' io esperienza vera,  
> 14 udendo quello spirto ed ammirando:  

Of this I had true experience, while hearing that spirit and marvelling;

- *ebb' io esperienza vera* ‘had I experience true’ → “I had true experience”: the subject precedes the verb and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *udendo* ‘hearing’ → “while hearing”: the temporal relation of the gerund is made explicit.

> 15 chè ben cinquanta gradi salito era  
> 16 lo sole, ed io non m' era accorto, quando  

for full fifty degrees had climbed the sun, and I had not perceived it, when

- *ben cinquanta gradi* ‘quite fifty degrees’ → “full fifty degrees”: the emphatic adverb is rendered by “full” before the numeral.
- *salito era* ‘climbed was’ → “had climbed”: the English perfect uses *have* rather than *be*, and the auxiliary precedes the participle.
- *non m' era accorto* ‘had not become aware’ → “had not perceived it”: the pronominal verb becomes transitive, with its object made explicit.

> 17 venimmo dove quell' anime ad una  
> 18 gridaro a noi: “Qui è vostro domando.”  

we came to where those souls with one voice cried out to us: “Here is what you ask.”

- *dove* ‘where’ → “to where”: the direction implicit after the verb of coming is marked by a preposition.
- *ad una* ‘as one’ → “with one voice”: the adverbial phrase is expanded by naming the voice.
- *gridaro* ‘cried’ → “cried out”: the English verb adds a particle.
- *vostro domando* ‘your request’ → “what you ask”: the possessive becomes the subject of a relative clause, and the noun becomes its verb.

> 19 Maggiore aperta molte volte impruna,  
> 20 con una forcatella di sue spine,  
> 21 l' uom della villa, quando l' uva imbruna,  

A bigger opening many a time the peasant hedges up with a little forkful of his thorns, when the grape is darkening,

- *molte volte* ‘many times’ → “many a time”: the repeated occasions are expressed by a distributive singular.
- *impruna, con una forcatella di sue spine, l' uom della villa* ‘hedges with thorns, with a little fork of his thorns, the man of the village’ → “the peasant hedges up with a little forkful of his thorns”: the subject moves from after the instrumental phrase to before the verb and is compressed to one noun. The verb no longer itself specifies thorns; “up” marks closure, and the diminutive fork is rendered as the quantity it holds.
- *imbruna* ‘darkens’ → “is darkening”: the simple present becomes progressive.

> 22 che non era la calla, onde salìne  
> 23 lo duca mio ed io appresso, soli,  
> 24 come da noi la schiera si partìne.  

than was the gap by which my leader mounted, and I after him, we two alone, when the troop parted from us.

- *salìne lo duca mio* ‘mounted the leader of mine’ → “my leader mounted”: the postverbal subject comes first and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *appresso* ‘after’ → “after him”: the person followed is named by a pronoun rather than left implicit.
- *soli* ‘alone’ → “we two alone”: the adjective’s plural reference is made explicit by a pronoun and numeral.
- *come da noi la schiera si partìne* ‘as from us the troop departed’ → “when the troop parted from us”: the conjunction is expressly temporal, the phrase of separation moves to the end, and the pronominal verb becomes a simple intransitive.

> 25 Vassi in Sanleo, e discendesi in Noli;  

One can walk at Sanleo and get down to Noli;

- *Vassi in Sanleo* ‘one goes in Sanleo’ → “One can walk at Sanleo”: Okey supplies the modal sense of possibility, specifies walking, and renders the place phrase with “at.”
- *discendesi in Noli* ‘one descends into Noli’ → “get down to Noli”: the second impersonal verb shares the preceding English subject and modal; the verb becomes a phrase, with the destination marked by “to.”

> 26 montasi su Bismantova in cacume  
> 27 con esso i piè: ma qui convien ch' uom voli;  

one can mount Bismantova to its summit, with feet alone; but here a man must fly,

- *montasi su Bismantova* ‘one climbs up on Bismantova’ → “one can mount Bismantova”: the impersonal present again acquires a modal, and the place becomes the direct object of “mount.”
- *in cacume* ‘to the summit’ → “to its summit”: a possessive makes the relation to Bismantova explicit.
- *con esso i piè* ‘with the feet themselves’ → “with feet alone”: the reinforcing *esso* is interpreted as excluding other means; “alone” follows the noun, and the article is dropped.
- *convien ch' uom voli* ‘it is necessary that one fly’ → “a man must fly”: the impersonal verb and subordinate subjunctive become a single clause with a modal, and generic *uom* becomes “a man.”

> 28 dico con l' ali snelle e con le piume  
> 29 del gran disio, di retro a quel condotto,  
> 30 che speranza mi dava e facea lume.  

I mean with the swift wings and with the plumes of great desire, behind that Leader, who gave me hope, and was a light to me.

- *dico* ‘I say’ → “I mean”: the verb of saying is rendered as a clarification.
- *l' ali snelle* ‘the wings swift’ → “the swift wings”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *del gran disio* ‘of the great desire’ → “of great desire”: the article is dropped.
- *di retro a* ‘at the back of’ → “behind”: the phrase becomes a single preposition.
- *speranza mi dava* ‘hope to me gave’ → “gave me hope”: the verb moves before both objects, with the recipient before the thing given; the imperfect is rendered by a simple past.
- *facea lume* ‘made light’ → “was a light to me”: the imperfect of making becomes a simple past of being, light becomes a count noun, and the recipient is expressed again.

> 31 Noi salivam per entro il sasso rotto,  
> 32 e d' ogni lato ne stringea lo stremo,  
> 33 e piedi e man voleva il suol di sotto.  

We were climbing within the cleft rock, and on either side the surface pressed against us, and the ground beneath required both feet and hands.

- *salivam* ‘we climbed’ → “We were climbing”: the progressive brings out the ongoing force of the imperfect.
- *per entro* ‘through the inside of’ → “within”: one preposition conveys the interior position without separately expressing motion through it.
- *il sasso rotto* ‘the rock broken’ → “the cleft rock”: the participle moves before the noun and specifies a split rather than breakage generally.
- *d' ogni lato* ‘on every side’ → “on either side”: the distributive is restricted to the two sides.
- *ne stringea lo stremo* ‘us squeezed the edge’ → “the surface pressed against us”: the postposed subject comes first and is understood as a surface rather than an edge; the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the direct object becomes the object of “against.”
- *e piedi e man voleva il suol di sotto* ‘both feet and hands wanted the ground below’ → “the ground beneath required both feet and hands”: the subject and its modifier move before the verb, the paired objects follow it, and the imperfect of wanting is rendered by a simple past of requiring.

> 34 Poi che noi fummo in su l' orlo supremo  
> 35 dell' alta ripa, alla scoperta piaggia:  
> 36 “Maestro mio,” diss' io, “che via faremo?”  

After we were on the upper edge of the high cliff, out on the open hillside, “Master mine,” said I, “what way shall we take?”

- *in su l' orlo supremo* ‘up on the highest edge’ → “on the upper edge”: the compound expression of place is reduced to “on,” and the superlative following the noun becomes “upper” before it.
- *dell' alta ripa* ‘of the high bank’ → “of the high cliff”: the noun is rendered as a cliff rather than a bank.
- *alla scoperta piaggia* ‘on the uncovered slope’ → “out on the open hillside”: “out” is supplied, ‘uncovered’ becomes “open,” and the slope is specified as a hillside.
- *che via faremo* ‘what way shall we make’ → “what way shall we take”: the English idiom substitutes taking a way for making it.

## Lines 37–69 — The Ascent and the Sun on the Left

> 37 Ed egli a me: “Nessun tuo passo caggia;  
> 38 pur su al monte retro a me acquista,  
> 39 fin che n' appaia alcuna scorta saggia.”  

And he to me: “Let no step of thine descend, ever up the mount behind me win thy way, until some wise escort appear to us.”

- *Nessun tuo passo caggia* ‘no step of thine fall’ → “Let no step of thine descend”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”; falling becomes descending.
- *su al monte* ‘up on the mount’ → “up the mount”: the mount becomes the object of the preposition of direction.
- *acquista* ‘make gain’ → “win thy way”: the verb is given an object expressing progress.
- *fin che n' appaia alcuna scorta saggia* ‘until to us appear some escort wise’ → “until some wise escort appear to us”: the subject moves before the verb, the adjective before its noun, and the indirect object to the end.

> 40 Lo sommo er' alto che vincea la vista,  
> 41 e la costa superba più assai  
> 42 che da mezzo quadrante a centro lista.  

So high was the top that it surpassed my sight, and the slope steeper far than a line from mid-quadrant to centre.

- *Lo sommo er' alto che* ‘the top was high such that’ → “So high was the top that”: “So” makes the degree explicit, and the adjective is brought forward, with inversion of verb and subject.
- *vincea la vista* ‘overcame sight’ → “surpassed my sight”: the verb becomes one of exceeding a limit, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *superba più assai* ‘lofty more by much’ → “steeper far”: the adjective is interpreted as describing the incline, and the separate comparative becomes an inflection.
- *da mezzo quadrante a centro lista* ‘from mid-quadrant to centre a line’ → “a line from mid-quadrant to centre”: the noun moves before the phrases describing it.

> 43 Io era lasso, quando cominciai:  
> 44 “O dolce padre, volgiti e rimira  
> 45 com' io rimango sol, se non ristai.”  

Weary was I when I began: “O sweet father, turn thee and look how I remain alone, if thou stay not.”

- *Io era lasso* ‘I was weary’ → “Weary was I”: the predicate adjective is put first and the subject last.
- *rimira* ‘look again’ → “look”: the verb is rendered simply, without a separate indication of looking back or again.

> 46 “Figliuol mio,” disse, “infin quivi ti tira,”  

“My son,” said he, “so far as there drag thee,”

- *Figliuol mio* ‘son mine’ → “My son”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *infin quivi ti tira* ‘as far as there pull thyself’ → “so far as there drag thee”: the limit is expressed by “so far as”; the reflexive, which precedes the Italian verb, follows the more emphatic “drag”.

> 47 additandomi un balzo poco in sue,  
> 48 che da quel lato il poggio tutto gira.  

pointing out to me a terrace a little higher up, which on that side circles the whole mountain.

- *additandomi* ‘pointing with the finger to me’ → “pointing out to me”: the compound verb becomes a phrasal verb, and the enclitic becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *un balzo* ‘a ledge’ → “a terrace”: a more specific term for the shelf of ground.
- *poco in sue* ‘a little up’ → “a little higher up”: the comparison is made explicit.
- *il poggio tutto gira* ‘the hill entire it circles’ → “circles the whole mountain”: the verb moves before its object and the adjective before its noun; “mountain” renders ‘hill’.

> 49 Sì mi spronaron le parole sue  
> 50 ch' io mi sforzai, carpando appresso lui,  
> 51 tanto che il cinghio sotto i piè mi fue.  

So did his words spur me on, that I forced me, creeping after him, so far that the ledge was under my feet.

- *Sì mi spronaron le parole sue* ‘so me spurred the words of his’ → “So did his words spur me on”: the fronted “So” is followed by the auxiliary “did”; the subject precedes the main verb, the possessive precedes its noun, and the object follows “spur”, to which “on” is added.
- *io mi sforzai* ‘I exerted myself’ → “I forced me”: the reflexive verb of effort is rendered as an act of compulsion, with the object after the verb.
- *carpando* ‘clambering’ → “creeping”: the English conveys low, laborious movement rather than explicitly suggesting climbing.
- *tanto che* ‘so much that’ → “so far that”: degree is expressed as distance.
- *il cinghio* ‘the girdle’ → “the ledge”: the feature itself replaces the figurative term for its encircling shape.
- *sotto i piè mi fue* ‘under the feet to me it was’ → “was under my feet”: the verb comes first, and the dative of the person becomes a possessive.

> 52 A seder ci ponemmo ivi ambo e dui  
> 53 volti a levante, ond' eravam saliti:  
> 54 chè suole a riguardar giovare altrui.  

There we both did sit us down, turned towards the East, whence we had ascended; for to look back is wont to cheer men.

- *A seder ci ponemmo ivi ambo e dui* ‘to sit we set ourselves there, both and two’ → “There we both did sit us down”: the adverb is brought to the front, the doubled expression of number is reduced to “both” and placed with the subject, and the verb of placing with its infinitive becomes a form of “sit” with “down”.
- *eravam saliti* ‘we were ascended’ → “we had ascended”: the English pluperfect takes ‘have’ rather than ‘be’.
- *suole a riguardar giovare altrui* ‘is wont, looking back, to benefit others’ → “to look back is wont to cheer men”: the infinitive denoting the activity comes first as the English subject; the general benefit is specified as cheering, and the indefinite pronoun becomes a generic noun.

> 55 Gli occhi prima drizzai a' bassi liti;  
> 56 poscia gli alzai al sole, ed ammirava  
> 57 che da sinistra n' eravam feriti.  

First mine eyes I directed to the shores below; then did raise them to the sun, and marvelled that we were smitten by it on the left side.

- *Gli occhi prima drizzai* ‘the eyes first I directed’ → “First mine eyes I directed”: the adverb moves to the front, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *a' bassi liti* ‘to the low shores’ → “to the shores below”: the attributive adjective becomes a following adverb of place.
- *gli alzai* ‘them I raised’ → “did raise them”: the simple past becomes a form with “did”, and the pronoun moves after the verb.
- *ammirava* ‘I was marvelling’ → “marvelled”: the simple English past leaves the continuing force of the imperfect unmarked.
- *da sinistra n' eravam feriti* ‘from the left by it we were struck’ → “we were smitten by it on the left side”: the passive verb comes first, followed by the agent and then the indication of place; direction from the left becomes location on the left, with “side” supplied.

> 58 Ben s' avvide il poeta, che io stava  
> 59 stupido tutto al carro della luce,  
> 60 dove tra noi ed Aquilone intrava.  

Right well the Poet perceived that I was all astonished at the chariot of the light, where ’twas entering between us and the North.

- *Ben s' avvide il poeta* ‘well did the poet become aware’ → “Right well the Poet perceived”: “Right” intensifies the adverb, the subject moves before the verb, and the pronominal verb becomes a simple transitive verb.
- *io stava stupido tutto* ‘I remained astonished wholly’ → “I was all astonished”: the verb of remaining becomes a copula, and the intensifier moves before the adjective.
- *tra noi ed Aquilone intrava* ‘between us and the north wind it entered’ → “’twas entering between us and the North”: the verb comes before the prepositional phrase, the progressive conveys the imperfect, and the name of the wind is rendered as a direction.

> 61 Ond' egli a me: “Se Castore e Polluce  
> 62 fossero in compagnia di quello specchio,  
> 63 che su e giù del suo lume conduce,  

Whereupon he to me: “If Castor and Pollux were in company of that mirror, which purveys of his light upward and downward,

- *Ond' egli a me* ‘whence he to me’ → “Whereupon he to me”: the connective is rendered as marking the ensuing response.
- *su e giù del suo lume conduce* ‘up and down of its light conveys’ → “purveys of his light upward and downward”: the verb moves to the front and takes the sense of supplying; the directions move to the end, and the possessive is personalized.

> 64 tu vederesti il Zodiaco rubecchio  
> 65 ancora all' Orse più stretto rotare,  
> 66 se non uscisse fuor del cammin vecchio.  

thou wouldst see the glowing Zodiac revolve yet closer to the Bears, unless it strayed from its ancient path.

- *il Zodiaco rubecchio* ‘the Zodiac ruddy’ → “the glowing Zodiac”: the adjective moves before the noun, and a term of colour becomes one of radiance.
- *ancora all' Orse più stretto rotare* ‘yet to the Bears more closely to revolve’ → “revolve yet closer to the Bears”: the infinitive comes first, without ‘to’ after the verb of seeing, and the comparison precedes the phrase naming its point of reference.
- *se non uscisse fuor* ‘if it did not go out’ → “unless it strayed”: the negated condition becomes an exception with “unless”, and going out is interpreted as deviating.
- *del cammin vecchio* ‘of the path old’ → “from its ancient path”: “from” follows the verb of deviation, the article becomes a possessive, and the adjective is intensified and placed before the noun.

> 67 Come ciò sia, se il vuoi poter pensare,  
> 68 dentro raccolto, immagina Sion  
> 69 con questo monte in su la terra stare,  

If thou wouldst have power to conceive how that may be, rapt within thyself, imagine Zion and this mount to be placed on the earth

- *Come ciò sia, se il vuoi poter pensare* ‘how that may be, if thou wishest to be able to think it’ → “If thou wouldst have power to conceive how that may be”: the conditional clause comes first; the anticipatory ‘it’ of the Italian gives way to the following indirect question as the object of “conceive”, and the infinitive of ability becomes “have power”.
- *dentro raccolto* ‘within, gathered’ → “rapt within thyself”: the participle comes first and suggests absorption; “thyself” specifies the inward reference.
- *con questo monte* ‘with this mount’ → “and this mount”: accompaniment becomes coordination.
- *in su la terra stare* ‘upon the earth to stand’ → “to be placed on the earth”: the infinitive moves before the phrase of place, and standing becomes a passive expression of position.

## Lines 70–102 — The Equator and the Easing Ascent

> 70 sì che ambo e due hanno un solo orizzon  
> 71 e diversi emisperi; onde la strada,  
> 72 che mal non seppe carreggiar Feton,  

so that both have one sole horizon and different hemispheres; wherefore the way, which, to his hurt, Phaeton knew not how to drive,

- *ambo e due* ‘both and two’ → “both”: one word does the work of the emphatic pair.
- *mal* ‘ill’ → “to his hurt”: the adverb becomes a phrase specifying whose hurt is meant.
- *non seppe carreggiar Feton* ‘knew not how to drive Phaeton’ → “Phaeton knew not how to drive”: the subject moves from after the infinitive to before the finite verb.

> 73 vedrai come a costui convien che vada  
> 74 dall' un, quando a colui dall' altro fianco,  
> 75 se l' intelletto tuo ben chiaro bada.”  

thou shalt see must needs pass this on the one side when it passes Zion on the other, if thy mind right clearly apprehends.”

- *vedrai come* ‘thou shalt see how’ → “thou shalt see”: the connective is dropped; the previously named way serves directly as the subject of “must needs pass”.
- *a costui convien che vada* ‘with respect to this one it is necessary that it go’ → “must needs pass this”: the impersonal verb and subjunctive clause become a modal construction; the demonstrative becomes the direct object of “pass”.
- *dall' un … dall' altro fianco* ‘on the one … on the other flank’ → “on the one side” … “on the other”: the noun is expressed with the first alternative instead of the second, and “side” replaces ‘flank’.
- *quando a colui* ‘when with respect to that one’ → “when it passes Zion”: the verb left understood in Italian is repeated, and the demonstrative is replaced by the name of its referent.
- *l' intelletto tuo* ‘the intellect of thine’ → “thy mind”: the possessive moves before the noun, and “mind” replaces ‘intellect’.
- *ben chiaro bada* ‘very clearly attends’ → “right clearly apprehends”: paying heed is rendered as grasping or understanding.

> 76 “Certo, maestro mio,” diss' io, “unquanco  
> 77 non vidi chiaro sì com' io discerno,  
> 78 là dove mio ingegno parea manco,  

“Of a surety, Master mine,” said I, “never saw I so clearly as I discern, there where my wit seemed at fault,

- *Certo* ‘certainly’ → “Of a surety”: an adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *unquanco non vidi* ‘ever yet I saw not’ → “never saw I”: the negative and the adverb are combined in “never”, with no separate equivalent for ‘yet’; the initial negative brings the subject after the verb.
- *chiaro sì* ‘clearly so’ → “so clearly”: the degree word moves before the adverb.
- *là dove mio ingegno parea manco* ‘there where my wit seemed deficient’ → “there where my wit seemed at fault”: a predicative adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.

> 79 che il mezzo cerchio del moto superno  
> 80 che si chiama Equatore in alcun' arte,  
> 81 e che sempre riman tra il sole e il verno,  

that the median circle of the heavenly motion, which is called Equator in one of the sciences, and which ever remains ’twixt the sun and winter,

- *mezzo cerchio* ‘middle circle’ → “median circle”: “median” gives the positional adjective a more technical turn.
- *moto superno* ‘motion supernal’ → “heavenly motion”: the adjective is recast and moved before the noun.
- *si chiama* ‘calls itself’ → “is called”: the Italian *si* construction becomes an English passive.
- *in alcun' arte* ‘in a certain art’ → “in one of the sciences”: the singular indefinite phrase becomes a partitive with a plural noun, and ‘art’ is taken in the sense of a science.

> 82 per la ragion che di', quinci si parte  
> 83 verso settentrion, quanto gli Ebrei  
> 84 vedevan lui verso la calda parte.  

for the reason that thou tellest, departs here towards the North, as far as the Hebrews used to see it towards the hot climes.

- *quinci si parte* ‘from here it departs’ → “departs here”: the adverb moves after the verb and no longer expressly marks the point of departure; the pronominal verb is rendered by a simple intransitive.
- *quanto* ‘as much as’ → “as far as”: the comparison is made expressly one of distance.
- *vedevan lui* ‘saw it’ → “used to see it”: the habitual force of the imperfect is made explicit.
- *la calda parte* ‘the hot region’ → “the hot climes”: the singular noun becomes a plural and is given a specifically climatic sense.

> 85 Ma se a te piace, volentier saprei  
> 86 quanto avemo ad andar, chè il poggio sale  
> 87 più che salir non posson gli occhi miei.”  

But if it please thee, willingly would I know how far we have to go, for the hillside rises higher than mine eyes can reach.”

- *a te piace* ‘to thee it is pleasing’ → “it please thee”: the experiencer moves after the verb as its object, and the indicative becomes an English conditional subjunctive.
- *volentier saprei* ‘willingly I would know’ → “willingly would I know”: the conditional is expressed by an auxiliary placed before the subject.
- *quanto* ‘how much’ → “how far”: quantity is specified as distance.
- *il poggio* ‘the hill’ → “the hillside”: the part being ascended is named rather than the whole hill.
- *più che salir non posson gli occhi miei* ‘more than can climb my eyes’ → “higher than mine eyes can reach”: the comparison is specified as one of height; the eyes move before the verb, the possessive before the noun, and ‘climb’ becomes “reach”. The comparative *non* has no English negative equivalent.

> 88 Ed egli a me: “Questa montagna è tale,  
> 89 che sempre al cominciar di sotto è grave,  
> 90 e quanto uom più va su, e men fa male.  

And he to me: “This mountain is such, that ever at the beginning below ’tis toilsome, and the more a man ascends the less it wearies.

- *al cominciar* ‘at the commencing’ → “at the beginning”: the nominalized infinitive becomes an ordinary noun.
- *di sotto* ‘down below’ → “below”: the locution is reduced to one adverb.
- *è grave* ‘it is heavy’ → “’tis toilsome”: weight is rendered in terms of effort.
- *quanto uom più va su, e men fa male* ‘in proportion as a man more goes up, and less it hurts’ → “the more a man ascends the less it wearies”: paired English comparatives replace the proportional clause and its coordinating conjunction; ‘goes up’ becomes a single verb, and hurt is narrowed to weariness.

> 91 Però quand' ella ti parrà soave  
> 92 tanto, che il su andar ti fia leggiero,  
> 93 come a seconda giuso andar per nave,  

Therefore when it shall seem to thee so pleasant that the ascending becomes to thee easy, even as in a boat to descend with the stream,

- *ella ti parrà soave tanto* ‘it to thee shall seem pleasant so much’ → “it shall seem to thee so pleasant”: the personal complement moves after the verb, and the degree word moves from after the adjective to before it.
- *il su andar* ‘the upward going’ → “the ascending”: the direction and the action are combined in one verbal noun.
- *ti fia leggiero* ‘to thee shall be light’ → “becomes to thee easy”: future being becomes present becoming, the personal complement follows the verb, and ‘light’ is rendered as “easy”.
- *come a seconda giuso andar per nave* ‘as with the current downward to go by boat’ → “even as in a boat to descend with the stream”: “even” reinforces the comparison; the means of travel becomes a phrase of location and moves forward, ‘go downward’ becomes one verb, and the phrase for moving with the current comes last.

> 94 allor sarai al fin d' esto sentiero:  
> 95 quivi di riposar l' affanno aspetta.  
> 96 Più non rispondo, e questo so per vero.”  

then shalt thou be at the end of this path: there hope to rest thy weariness. No more I answer, and this I know for truth.”

- *sarai* ‘thou shalt be’ → “shalt thou be”: the auxiliary precedes the subject after “then”.
- *di riposar l' affanno aspetta* ‘to rest the weariness expect’ → “hope to rest thy weariness”: the governing imperative moves before the infinitive, expectation becomes hope, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *Più non rispondo* ‘further I do not answer’ → “No more I answer”: the Italian negative and adverb become a single initial negative phrase.
- *per vero* ‘for true’ → “for truth”: an adjective becomes an abstract noun.

> 97 E, com' egli ebbe sua parola detta,  
> 98 una voce di presso sonò: “Forse  
> 99 che di sedere in prima avrai distretta.”  

And when he had said his word, a voice hard by sounded: “Perchance ere that thou wilt have need to sit.”

- *ebbe sua parola detta* ‘had his word said’ → “had said his word”: the participle moves next to its auxiliary, before the object.
- *Forse che* ‘perhaps that’ → “Perchance”: the particle introducing the conjecture has no separate equivalent.
- *di sedere in prima avrai distretta* ‘of sitting beforehand thou wilt have necessity’ → “ere that thou wilt have need to sit”: the time phrase moves first and expressly points back with “that”; the complement of ‘necessity’ moves from before the verb to after “need” and takes the English infinitive construction.

> 100 Al suon di lei ciascun di noi si torse,  
> 101 e vedemmo a mancina un gran petrone,  
> 102 del qual nè io nè ei prima s' accorse.  

At sound of it each of us turned him round, and we saw on the left a great mass of stone, which neither I nor he perceived before.

- *Al suon di lei* ‘at the sound of her’ → “At sound of it”: the article is dropped, and the feminine pronoun referring to the voice becomes neuter.
- *si torse* ‘himself turned’ → “turned him round”: the reflexive is represented by a postverbal personal pronoun, with “round” making the direction explicit.
- *a mancina* ‘on the left hand’ → “on the left”: the reference to the hand is left implicit.
- *un gran petrone* ‘a great boulder’ → “a great mass of stone”: a single noun for a large rock is expanded into a noun and a phrase of material.
- *del qual nè io nè ei prima s' accorse* ‘of which neither I nor he previously became aware’ → “which neither I nor he perceived before”: the pronominal verb governing *di* becomes a transitive verb, so the relative needs no preposition; the time adverb moves after the verb.

## Lines 103–135 — Belacqua Waits Outside the Gate

> 103 Là ci traemmo; ed ivi eran persone  
> 104 che si stavano all' ombra dietro al sasso,  
> 105 com' uom per negligenza a star si pone.  

Thither drew we on; and there were persons, lounging in the shade behind the rock, even as a man settles him to rest for laziness.

- *Là ci traemmo* ‘thither we drew ourselves’ → “Thither drew we on”: the reflexive becomes a verb of motion with “on”, and the subject follows the verb.
- *ed ivi eran persone* ‘and there were persons there’ → “and there were persons”: the English has the existential “there” but no separate expression for the place, *ivi*.
- *che si stavano all’ ombra* ‘who were staying in the shade’ → “lounging in the shade”: a participle replaces the relative clause, and “lounging” specifies a manner of staying.
- *com’ uom* ‘as a man’ → “even as a man”: “even” is supplied.
- *per negligenza a star si pone* ‘through negligence to stay he sets himself’ → “settles him to rest for laziness”: the reflexive verb comes first, the infinitive is rendered as “to rest”, and the phrase giving the cause comes last, with “laziness” for negligence.

> 106 Ed un di lor, che mi sembrava lasso,  
> 107 sedeva ed abbracciava le ginocchia,  
> 108 tenendo il viso giù tra esse basso.  

And one of them, who seemed to me weary, was sitting and clasping his knees, holding his face low down between them.

- *sedeva ed abbracciava* ‘sat and embraced’ → “was sitting and clasping”: the two imperfects become progressives with a shared auxiliary; “clasping” renders the action of putting the arms around something.
- *le ginocchia* ‘the knees’ → “his knees”: the possessive replaces the definite article.
- *il viso giù tra esse basso* ‘the face down between them low’ → “his face low down between them”: the possessive again replaces the article, and “low” is brought forward to join “down”.

> 109 “O dolce signor mio,” diss' io, “adocchia  
> 110 colui che mostra sè più negligente  
> 111 che se pigrizia fosse sua sirocchia.”  

“O sweet my Lord,” said I, “set thine eye on that one who shows himself lazier than if Sloth were his very sister.”

- *signor mio* ‘lord my’ → “my Lord”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *adocchia* ‘eye’ → “set thine eye on”: the single verb is expanded into a phrase.
- *più negligente* ‘more negligent’ → “lazier”: an inflected comparative replaces the separate comparative adverb, and laziness is specified rather than negligence.
- *sua sirocchia* ‘his sister’ → “his very sister”: “very” is supplied for emphasis.

> 112 Allor si volse a noi, e pose mente,  
> 113 movendo il viso pur su per la coscia,  
> 114 e disse: “Or va su tu, che se' valente.”  

Then turned he to us and gave heed, moving his face only over his thigh, and said: “Now go thou up who art valiant.”

- *si volse* ‘he turned himself’ → “turned he”: the reflexive becomes intransitive, with the subject after the verb.
- *pose mente* ‘set his mind’ → “gave heed”: one idiom for paying attention replaces another.
- *il viso* ‘the face’ → “his face”: the possessive replaces the article.
- *su per la coscia* ‘up along the thigh’ → “over his thigh”: “over” replaces the directional phrase, and the possessive replaces the article.
- *va su tu* ‘go up thou’ → “go thou up”: the emphatic pronoun is placed between verb and adverb.

> 115 Conobbi allor chi era; e quell' angoscia,  
> 116 che m' avacciava un poco ancor la lena,  

Then knew I who he was; and that toil which still oppressed a little my breath,

- *Conobbi allor* ‘I recognized then’ → “Then knew I”: the adverb comes first, and recognition is expressed by “knew”, with the subject following.
- *quell’ angoscia* ‘that distress’ → “that toil”: Okey names the exertion rather than the distress it causes.
- *che m’ avacciava un poco ancor la lena* ‘which quickened my breath a little still’ → “which still oppressed a little my breath”: “oppressed” gives the effect on the breathing rather than its quickening; the dative *m’* becomes a possessive, and “still” moves before the verb.

> 117 non m' impedì l' andare a lui; e poscia  
> 118 che a lui fui giunto, alzò la testa appena,  

did not hinder my going to him; and after I had got to him, his head he scarce did lift,

- *non m’ impedì l’ andare a lui* ‘did not hinder me from going to him’ → “did not hinder my going to him”: the personal object becomes the possessive subject of “going”; the gerund replaces the substantival infinitive.
- *che a lui fui giunto* ‘that to him I had arrived’ → “I had got to him”: “after” alone introduces the clause without a separate equivalent for *che*; the destination follows the verb, and “got” renders arrival.
- *alzò la testa appena* ‘he raised the head scarcely’ → “his head he scarce did lift”: the object comes first with a possessive instead of the article; the adverb precedes the verb, which is expressed with auxiliary “did”.

> 119 dicendo: “Hai ben veduto come il sole  
> 120 dall' omero sinistro il carro mena?”  

saying: “Hast thou truly seen how the sun drives his chariot on thy left side?”

- *ben veduto* ‘well seen’ → “truly seen”: the adverb is taken as confirmation rather than as describing how well he saw.
- *dall’ omero sinistro* ‘from the shoulder left’ → “on thy left side”: “on” replaces ‘from’, “side” generalizes the shoulder, and a possessive replaces the article; the adjective precedes the noun, and the whole phrase moves to the end.
- *il carro mena* ‘the chariot he drives’ → “drives his chariot”: the verb precedes its object, and the possessive replaces the article.

> 121 Gli atti suoi pigri e le corte parole  
> 122 mosson le labbra mie un poco a riso;  

His lazy actions and the brief words moved my lips to smile a little;

- *Gli atti suoi pigri* ‘the actions his lazy’ → “His lazy actions”: both possessive and adjective precede the noun.
- *le labbra mie* ‘the lips my’ → “my lips”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *un poco a riso* ‘a little to laughter’ → “to smile a little”: the noun becomes a verbal infinitive, “smile” is milder than laughter, and the expression of degree comes last.

> 123 poi cominciai: “Belacqua, a me non duole  
> 124 di te omai; ma dimmi, perchè assiso  
> 125 quiritta sei? attendi tu iscorta,  
> 126 o pur lo modo usato t' hai ripriso?”  

then I began: “Belacqua, it grieves me not for thee now; but tell me, why art thou seated here? dost thou await escort, or hast thou but resumed thy wonted habit?”

- *a me non duole* ‘to me it does not cause pain’ → “it grieves me not”: the fronted dative becomes the object of “grieves”, and the negative follows it.
- *omai* ‘by now’ → “now”: the sense of a point reached by this time is reduced to the simple time adverb.
- *assiso quiritta sei* ‘seated here thou art’ → “art thou seated here”: the verb moves forward for the English question.
- *o pur lo modo usato t’ hai ripriso* ‘or simply the accustomed manner hast thou taken up again for thyself’ → “or hast thou but resumed thy wonted habit”: the verb precedes its object, with “but” between auxiliary and participle; “resumed” expresses taking up again, “habit” interprets manner, and the personal reference of the reflexive is conveyed by “thy”.

> 127 Ed ei: “Frate, l' andare in su che porta?  
> 128 chè non mi lascerebbe ire ai martiri  
> 129 l' uccel di Dio che siede in su la porta.  

And he: “Brother what avails it to ascend? For God’s winged angel that sits at the gate, would not let me pass to the torments.

- *l’ andare in su che porta?* ‘going upward, what does it bring?’ → “what avails it to ascend?”: the question word comes first, bringing a result becomes availing, and anticipatory “it” points to the postponed infinitive “to ascend”.
- *non mi lascerebbe ire* ‘would not let me go’ → “would not let me pass”: “pass” specifies going through; the whole predicate moves after its subject.
- *l’ uccel di Dio* ‘the bird of God’ → “God’s winged angel”: Okey names the being meant and retains the bird image as the adjective “winged”; the possessive comes first.
- *in su la porta* ‘upon the gate’ → “at the gate”: the English gives proximity rather than position upon.

> 130 Prima convien che tanto il ciel m' aggiri  
> 131 di fuor da essa, quanto fece in vita,  
> 132 perch' io indugiai al fine i buon sospiri;  

First must the heavens revolve around me outside it, so long as they did during my life, because I delayed my healing sighs to the end:

- *convien che* ‘it is necessary that’ → “must”: a modal replaces the impersonal verb and its subordinate subjunctive clause.
- *tanto* ‘for so long’ → “so long”: the first term of the comparison is postponed until after “outside it”.
- *il ciel m’ aggiri* ‘the sky circle me’ → “the heavens revolve around me”: the singular becomes plural, and a transitive verb becomes an intransitive verb with “around”.
- *di fuor da essa* ‘outside of it’ → “outside it”: the separate preposition after the expression of place is unnecessary in English.
- *quanto fece in vita* ‘as long as it did in life’ → “as they did during my life”: “as” completes the comparison with “so long”; the subject is plural to match “heavens”, and “during my life” makes both duration and possession explicit.
- *al fine i buon sospiri* ‘until the end the good sighs’ → “my healing sighs to the end”: the time phrase follows the object, the possessive replaces the article, and “healing” interprets the goodness of the sighs by their effect.

> 133 se orazione in prima non m' aita,  
> 134 che surga su di cor che in grazia viva:  
> 135 l' altra che val, che in ciel non è udita?”  

unless before, a prayer aids me, which may rise up from a heart that lives in grace: what profits another that in heaven is not heard?”

- *se orazione in prima non m’ aita* ‘if prayer beforehand does not aid me’ → “unless before, a prayer aids me”: “unless” absorbs the negative, “before” moves ahead of the subject, prayer takes an indefinite article, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *che surga su di cor* ‘which rise up from a heart’ → “which may rise up from a heart”: “may” expresses the Italian subjunctive.
- *che in grazia viva* ‘that in grace live’ → “that lives in grace”: the verb comes before the prepositional phrase, and the subjunctive becomes indicative.
- *l’ altra che val* ‘the other, what does it avail?’ → “what profits another”: the interrogative comes first, the verb moves before its subject, and the definite ‘the other’ becomes indefinite “another”.

## Lines 136–139 — Noon and the Foot of Night

> 136 E già il poeta innanzi mi saliva,  

And already the poet was mounting before me,

- *innanzi mi saliva* ‘before me he was ascending’ → “was mounting before me”: the imperfect is rendered by the past progressive, and the verb is moved before the phrase of place.

> 137 e dicea: “Vienne omai, vedi ch' è tocco  
> 138 meridian dal sole, e dalla riva  
> 139 copre la notte già col piè Morrocco.”  

and saying: “Come on now, thou seest the meridian is touched by the sun, and Night already with her foot covers from Ganges’ banks to Morocco.”

- *e dicea* ‘and he was saying’ → “and saying”: the finite imperfect becomes a participle sharing the auxiliary of the preceding verb.
- *Vienne omai* ‘come hence now’ → “Come on now”: the particle attached to the imperative is rendered by “on”, expressing an urging forward rather than departure from a place.
- *vedi ch’ è tocco meridian dal sole* ‘thou seest that is touched meridian by the sun’ → “thou seest the meridian is touched by the sun”: the conjunction is omitted, and the subject of the passive is given an article and placed before its verb.
- *e dalla riva copre la notte già col piè Morrocco* ‘and from the bank covers the night already with the foot Morocco’ → “and Night already with her foot covers from Ganges’ banks to Morocco”: the subject moves before the verb and loses its article; the adverb and the phrase of instrument also move before the verb, and the foot is identified as “her foot”. The phrase of origin moves after the verb: the singular bank becomes plural and is identified as belonging to the Ganges. Morocco, the direct object of the Italian verb, becomes the endpoint of a “from … to” construction.

## Summary — Close Phrasing and Idiomatic Shifts

Okey often keeps the Italian sequence even where it gives the English an unusual turn: he leaves the object first in “A bigger opening many a time the peasant hedges up” and preserves the verbless *Ed egli a me* as “And he to me”. Elsewhere he brings a delayed subject forward, as in *salìne lo duca mio*, “my leader mounted”, or moves a modifier before its noun, as in *l’ ali snelle*, “the swift wings”. Inversion is not always a sign of literalness: “Weary was I” reverses the straightforward order of *Io era lasso*.

Changes of construction often accompany these shifts. The impersonal *convien ch’ uom voli* becomes the personal “a man must fly”, while the noun phrase *vostro domando*, ‘your request’, becomes the clause “what you ask”. Okey sometimes brings out a distinction that English would otherwise leave unmarked: “We were climbing” conveys the ongoing action of *salivam*, and “used to see” makes the habitual sense of *vedevan* explicit. As a matter of ordinary English grammar, he also supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the person through the verb ending, and omits the article that Italian uses before a possessive, as in *lo modo usato*, rendered with the possessive in “thy wonted habit”.

Okey’s choice of words can make an image more specific or less so. In *l’ uccel di Dio*, ‘the bird of God’, “God’s winged angel” names the figure but keeps something of the image in “winged”; by contrast, *omero*, ‘shoulder’, becomes simply “side”. Additions range from a small clarification, as when *ad una*, ‘as one’, becomes “with one voice”, to the explicit identification of *dalla riva*, ‘from the bank’, as “from Ganges’ banks”. Other expressions are compressed: *ambo e due*, ‘both and two’, needs only “both”. Such departures sit beside close renderings like *il carro della luce*, “the chariot of the light”, where Okey retains not only the image but the relation and order of its words.
