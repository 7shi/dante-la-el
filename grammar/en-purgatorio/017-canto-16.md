# Purgatorio 16

## Lines 1–9 — The Veil of Smoke

> 1 Buio d' inferno e di notte privata  
> 2 d' ogni pianeta sotto pover cielo,  
> 3 quant' esser può di nuvol tenebrata,  

Gloom of Hell and of a night bereft of every planet under a meagre sky, darkened by cloud as much as it can be,

- *Buio* ‘darkness’ → “Gloom”: Okey chooses a less absolute term for the absence of light.
- *di notte privata* ‘of night deprived’ → “of a night bereft”: the indefinite article is supplied, and the participle is rendered by “bereft”.
- *sotto pover cielo* ‘under poor sky’ → “under a meagre sky”: an article is supplied, and “meagre” renders the figurative adjective.
- *quant' esser può di nuvol tenebrata* ‘as much as it can be by cloud darkened’ → “darkened by cloud as much as it can be”: the participle moves first, followed by the phrase naming the cause of the darkening; the expression of degree comes last, with the modal before the infinitive.

> 4 non fece al viso mio sì grosso velo,  
> 5 come quel fummo ch' ivi ci coperse,  
> 6 nè a sentir di così aspro pelo;  

made not to my sight so thick a veil, nor of a pile so harsh to the feel, as that smoke which there covered us;

- *al viso mio* ‘to the sight of mine’ → “to my sight”: *viso* is taken in the sense of sight, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *sì grosso velo* ‘so thick veil’ → “so thick a veil”: English inserts the indefinite article between adjective and noun.
- *come quel fummo ch' ivi ci coperse* ‘as that smoke which there us covered’ → “as that smoke which there covered us”: the comparison is postponed until after the second description of the veil, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *nè a sentir di così aspro pelo* ‘nor to feel of so rough a pile’ → “nor of a pile so harsh to the feel”: the infinitive becomes a noun phrase and moves to the end; the noun for the nap of the fabric takes an article and precedes its description.

> 7 chè l' occhio stare aperto non sofferse:  

for it suffered not the eye to stay open:

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *l' occhio stare aperto non sofferse* ‘the eye to stay open it did not suffer’ → “it suffered not the eye to stay open”: the governing verb moves before its object and infinitive, and the negative follows the verb.

> 8 onde la scorta mia saputa e fida  
> 9 mi s' accostò, e l' omero m' offerse.  

wherefore my wise and trusty Escort closed up to me, and offered me his shoulder.

- *la scorta mia saputa e fida* ‘the escort of mine, wise and faithful’ → “my wise and trusty Escort”: the possessive and both adjectives move before the noun; “trusty” gives *fida* the sense of dependable.
- *mi s' accostò* ‘to me he drew himself near’ → “closed up to me”: an intransitive turn replaces the reflexive verb, and the phrase for the person approached moves after it.
- *l' omero m' offerse* ‘the shoulder to me he offered’ → “offered me his shoulder”: the verb moves first, followed by the recipient and then the direct object; the possessive replaces the Italian article with the part of the body.

## Lines 10–42 — Voices in the Smoke

> 10 Sì come cieco va dietro a sua guida  
> 11 per non smarrirsi, e per non dar di cozzo  
> 12 in cosa che il molesti o forse ancida:  

Even as a blind man goeth behind his guide in order not to stray, and not to butt against aught that may do him hurt, or perchance kill him,

- *Sì come* ‘just as’ → “Even as”: “Even” gives the emphasis of the comparison.
- *cieco* ‘a blind one’ → “a blind man”: the adjective used as a noun is expanded with “man”.
- *va dietro a sua guida* ‘goeth behind to his guide’ → “goeth behind his guide”: English “behind” takes its object without a second preposition.
- *per non smarrirsi* ‘in order not to lose oneself’ → “in order not to stray”: an intransitive verb replaces the reflexive.
- *e per non dar di cozzo* ‘and in order not to give a knock’ → “and not to butt”: the purpose construction is not repeated in full, and a single verb replaces the phrase with *dar*.
- *in cosa* ‘into a thing’ → “against aught”: the preposition changes with the verb, and an indefinite pronoun replaces the noun.
- *che il molesti* ‘that him hurt’ → “that may do him hurt”: “may” expresses the subjunctive; the object follows “do”, and the Italian verb is rendered by a verb and noun.
- *o forse ancida* ‘or perhaps slay’ → “or perchance kill him”: the object, understood from the preceding verb in Italian, is repeated.

> 13 m' andava io per l' aere amaro e sozzo,  
> 14 ascoltando il mio duca che diceva  
> 15 pur: “Guarda che da me tu non sie mozzo.”  

so went I through the bitter and foul air, listening to my Leader who was saying ever: “Look that thou be not cut off from me.”

- *m' andava io* ‘I went my way’ → “so went I”: the pronominal turn of the verb is dropped, and “so” is supplied to complete the comparison.
- *l' aere amaro e sozzo* ‘the air bitter and foul’ → “the bitter and foul air”: both adjectives precede the noun.
- *ascoltando il mio duca* ‘hearkening to my guide’ → “listening to my Leader”: the English verb requires “to” where the Italian takes a direct object; “Leader” renders the guide as one who leads.
- *da me tu non sie mozzo* ‘from me thou be not severed’ → “thou be not cut off from me”: the prepositional phrase moves to the end, and “cut off” renders the participle.

> 16 Io sentia voci, e ciascuna pareva  
> 17 pregar, per pace e per misericordia,  
> 18 l' Agnel di Dio, che le peccata leva.  

I heard voices, and each one seemed to pray for peace and for mercy, to the Lamb of God that taketh away sins.

- *Io sentia* ‘I was hearing’ → “I heard”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by the simple past.
- *pareva* ‘was seeming’ → “seemed”: here too a simple past renders the imperfect.
- *pregar, per pace e per misericordia, l' Agnel di Dio* ‘to beseech, for peace and for mercy, the Lamb of God’ → “to pray for peace and for mercy, to the Lamb of God”: the person besought is the direct object in Italian but is introduced by “to” in English.
- *che le peccata leva* ‘that the sins taketh away’ → “that taketh away sins”: the object follows the verb and loses its definite article.

> 19 Pure “*Agnus Dei*” eran le loro esordia;  
> 20 una parola in tutti era ed un modo,  
> 21 sì che parea tra esse ogni concordia.  

Only “*Agnus Dei*” were their beginnings; one word was with them all, and one measure; so that full concord seemed to be among them.

- *una parola in tutti era* ‘one word in all was’ → “one word was with them all”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase, “with” replaces ‘in’, and “them” is supplied.
- *un modo* ‘one manner’ → “one measure”: the more specific noun interprets the manner as that of the voices in unison.
- *parea tra esse ogni concordia* ‘there appeared among them every concord’ → “full concord seemed to be among them”: ‘every’ becomes “full”; the postposed subject moves to the front, and the appearance of concord is expressed with “seemed to be”.

> 22 “Quei sono spirti, maestro, ch' i' odo?”  
> 23 diss' io. Ed egli a me: “Tu vero apprendi,  
> 24 e d' iracondia van solvendo il nodo.”  

“Are those spirits, Master, that I hear?” said I. And he to me: “Thou apprehendest truly, and they are untying the knot of anger.”

- *Quei sono* ‘those are’ → “Are those”: subject and verb are inverted for the English question.
- *vero apprendi* ‘truth thou apprehendest’ → “apprehendest truly”: what is apprehended is expressed instead by an adverb following the verb.
- *d' iracondia van solvendo il nodo* ‘of anger they go loosening the knot’ → “they are untying the knot of anger”: the progressive in “are” replaces ‘go’ with a gerund; the phrase qualifying the knot follows its noun instead of preceding the verb.

> 25 “Or tu chi se', che il nostro fummo fendi,  
> 26 e di noi parli pur come se tue  
> 27 partissi ancor lo tempo per calendi?”  

“Now who art thou that cleavest our smoke, and speakest of us even as if thou didst still measure time by calends?”

- *tu chi se'* ‘thou who art’ → “who art thou”: the interrogative comes first and the subject follows the verb.
- *il nostro fummo fendi* ‘our smoke cleavest’ → “cleavest our smoke”: the object follows the verb.
- *di noi parli* ‘of us speakest’ → “speakest of us”: the prepositional phrase follows the verb.
- *partissi ancor lo tempo* ‘shouldst divide still the time’ → “didst still measure time”: the hypothetical imperfect subjunctive becomes a past form with “didst”; “still” falls between auxiliary and verb, ‘divide’ becomes “measure”, and the article is dropped.

> 28 Così per una voce detto fue;  
> 29 onde il maestro mio disse: “Rispondi,  
> 30 e domanda se quinci si va sue.”  

Thus by a voice was said; wherefore my Master said: “Answer thou and ask if by this way we go upward.”

- *detto fue* ‘said was’ → “was said”: the passive auxiliary precedes the participle.
- *il maestro mio* ‘the master mine’ → “my Master”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *quinci* ‘hence’ → “by this way”: an adverb of place is expanded into a phrase denoting the route.
- *si va sue* ‘one goes upward’ → “we go upward”: the impersonal construction becomes first-person plural.

> 31 Ed io: “O creatura, che ti mondi,  
> 32 per tornar bella a colui che ti fece,  
> 33 maraviglia udirai se mi secondi.”  

And I: “O creature that art cleansing thee to return fair unto him who made thee, a marvel shalt thou hear if thou follow me.”

- *ti mondi* ‘thyself thou cleansest’ → “art cleansing thee”: a progressive renders the simple present, and the reflexive object follows the verb.
- *colui che ti fece* ‘him who thee made’ → “him who made thee”: the object follows the verb.
- *maraviglia udirai* ‘a marvel thou wilt hear’ → “a marvel shalt thou hear”: the subject follows the English future auxiliary.
- *mi secondi* ‘me thou second’ → “thou follow me”: “follow” interprets the verb, and the object moves after it.

> 34 “Io ti seguiterò quanto mi lece,”  
> 35 rispose; “e se veder fummo non lascia,  
> 36 l' udir ci terrà giunti in quella vece.”  

“I will follow thee so far as is permitted me,” it answered, “and if the smoke lets us not see, hearing shall keep us in touch in its stead.”

- *ti seguiterò* ‘thee I shall follow’ → “I will follow thee”: the object follows the verb.
- *quanto mi lece* ‘as much as is lawful to me’ → “so far as is permitted me”: extent is expressed by “so far as”, and a passive replaces the impersonal verb.
- *veder fummo non lascia* ‘see smoke does not let’ → “the smoke lets us not see”: the infinitive moves after the finite verb, the subject gains an article, and “us”, understood in Italian, is supplied.
- *l' udir* ‘the act of hearing’ → “hearing”: the substantivized infinitive with its article becomes an English verbal noun without an article.
- *ci terrà giunti* ‘us it will hold joined’ → “shall keep us in touch”: the object follows the verb, and an idiom replaces the participle.
- *in quella vece* ‘in that stead’ → “in its stead”: a possessive replaces the demonstrative.

> 37 Allora incominciai: “Con quella fascia  
> 38 che la morte dissolve men vo suso,  
> 39 e venni qui per la infernale ambascia;  

Then began I: “With those swathings which death dissolves I am journeying upward and here did come through the anguish of Hell;

- *quella fascia* ‘that swathing’ → “those swathings”: the noun and demonstrative become plural.
- *la morte* ‘the death’ → “death”: the article is dropped.
- *men vo suso* ‘I go my way upward’ → “I am journeying upward”: “journeying” conveys the pronominal phrase, and a progressive replaces the simple present.
- *venni qui* ‘I came here’ → “here did come”: the adverb is brought forward and the simple past is rendered with “did”.
- *la infernale ambascia* ‘the infernal anguish’ → “the anguish of Hell”: a following phrase with “of” replaces the adjective.

> 40 e, se Dio m' ha in sua grazia richiuso  
> 41 tanto, che vuol ch' io veggia la sua corte  
> 42 per modo tutto fuor del modern' uso,  

and if God hath received me so far into his grace that he wills that I may behold his court in a manner quite outside modern use,

- *m' ha in sua grazia richiuso tanto* ‘me hath within his grace enclosed so much’ → “hath received me so far into his grace”: reception into grace replaces enclosure within it; “me” follows the participle, and the expression of degree moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *ch' io veggia* ‘that I see’ → “that I may behold”: “may” marks the subjunctive, and “behold” renders ‘see’.
- *per modo* ‘by way’ → “in a manner”: the preposition changes and an article is supplied.
- *tutto fuor del modern' uso* ‘wholly outside of the modern usage’ → “quite outside modern use”: “quite” renders the adverbial *tutto*, and “outside” takes the noun directly, without the preposition and article of *del*.

## Lines 43–75 — The Cause of Evil and the Freedom of the Will

> 43 non mi celar chi fosti anzi la morte,  
> 44 ma dilmi, e dimmi s' io vo bene al varco;  

hide not from me who thou wast before death, but tell it me, and tell me if I am going aright for the pass;

- *non mi celar* ‘not from me hide’ → “hide not from me”: the negative infinitive used as a command becomes an imperative, with the negative and the pronoun after the verb.
- *anzi la morte* ‘before the death’ → “before death”: English uses the abstract noun without the article.
- *dilmi* ‘tell it to me’ → “tell it me”: the English makes both objects explicit; in the contracted Italian form the thing to be told is understood.
- *io vo bene al varco* ‘I go well to the pass’ → “I am going aright for the pass”: the simple present becomes progressive; the adverb and preposition are rendered in terms of direction.

> 45 e tue parole fien le nostre scorte.”  

and thy words shall be our escort.”

- *le nostre scorte* ‘our escorts’ → “our escort”: the plural becomes a collective singular.

> 46 “Lombardo fui, e fui chiamato Marco;  

“A Lombard was I and was called Mark;

- *Lombardo fui* ‘Lombard I was’ → “A Lombard was I”: English makes the designation a noun with an indefinite article and places the subject after the verb.
- *fui chiamato* ‘I was called’ → “was called”: the English shares the expressed subject of the preceding verb rather than repeating it.

> 47 del mondo seppi, e quel valore amai  
> 48 al quale ha or ciascun disteso l' arco;  

I had knowledge of the world, and loved that worth at which now every one hath unbent his bow;

- *del mondo seppi* ‘of the world I knew’ → “I had knowledge of the world”: the verb of knowing becomes a verb with a noun object, and the phrase naming what was known moves to the end.
- *quel valore amai* ‘that worth I loved’ → “loved that worth”: the object follows the verb.
- *ha or ciascun disteso l' arco* ‘has now each one slackened the bow’ → “now every one hath unbent his bow”: the adverb and subject precede the auxiliary; Okey takes the participle to mean releasing the tension of the bow, and makes the possessor explicit.

> 49 per montar su dirittamente vai.”  

for mounting up thou goest aright.”

- *dirittamente vai* ‘aright thou goest’ → “thou goest aright”: the adverb follows the verb.

> 50 Così rispose; e soggiunse: “Io ti prego  
> 51 che per me preghi, quando su sarai.”  

Thus answered he, and added: “I pray thee that thou pray for me, when thou art above.”

- *soggiunse* ‘he went on to say’ → “added”: English uses a single verb of addition for the continuation of the speech.
- *per me preghi* ‘for me thou pray’ → “thou pray for me”: the beneficiary follows the verb.
- *su sarai* ‘above thou wilt be’ → “thou art above”: the adverb follows the verb, and English uses the present in a temporal clause referring to the future.

> 52 Ed io a lui: “Per fede mi ti lego  
> 53 di far ciò che mi chiedi; ma io scoppio  

And I to him: “By my faith I bind me to thee to do that which thou askest of me,

- *Per fede* ‘by faith’ → “By my faith”: the possessive is supplied.
- *mi ti lego* ‘myself to thee I bind’ → “I bind me to thee”: both pronouns follow the verb, and the second is expressed with a preposition.
- *di far* ‘of doing’ → “to do”: an English infinitive replaces the prepositional complement.
- *mi chiedi* ‘of me thou askest’ → “thou askest of me”: the person asked follows the verb in a prepositional phrase.

> 54 dentro a un dubbio, s' io non me ne spiego.  

but I am bursting within at a doubt, if I free me not from it.

- *io scoppio* ‘I burst’ → “I am bursting”: the simple present becomes progressive.
- *non me ne spiego* ‘not myself from it I disentangle’ → “I free me not from it”: the figurative verb becomes one of freeing; the reflexive, the negative, and the phrase corresponding to the pronominal adverb follow the verb.

> 55 Prima era scempio, ed ora è fatto doppio  
> 56 nella sentenza tua, che mi fa certo,  
> 57 qui ed altrove, quello ov' io l' accoppio.  

First ’twas simple, and now is made double by thy discourse, which makes certain to me, both here and elsewhere, that whereto I couple it.

- *scempio* ‘single’ → “simple”: the English uses the opposite of compound rather than the direct opposite of double.
- *nella sentenza tua* ‘in the judgment of thine’ → “by thy discourse”: the preposition marks agency rather than inclusion, the judgment becomes the discourse expressing it, and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *mi fa certo* ‘makes me certain’ → “makes certain to me”: English treats the speaker as the person to whom something is made certain rather than as the person made certain.
- *qui ed altrove* ‘here and elsewhere’ → “both here and elsewhere”: the correlative is supplied.
- *ov' io l' accoppio* ‘where I it couple’ → “whereto I couple it”: the relative expressly marks the relation of one thing to another, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 58 Lo mondo è ben così tutto diserto  
> 59 d' ogni virtute, come tu mi suone,  
> 60 e di malizia gravido e coperto;  

The world is indeed so wholly desert of every virtue, even as thy words sound to me, and heavy and covered with sin;

- *tutto* ‘all’ → “wholly”: the expression of totality becomes an adverb of degree.
- *come tu mi suone* ‘as thou soundest to me’ → “even as thy words sound to me”: the comparison is reinforced, the speaker's words replace the speaker as subject, and the person addressed by the sound follows the verb.
- *di malizia gravido e coperto* ‘with wickedness pregnant and covered’ → “heavy and covered with sin”: the shared complement moves after the two adjectives; Okey renders pregnancy by heaviness and wickedness by sin.

> 61 ma prego che m' additi la cagione,  
> 62 sì ch' io la veggia, e ch' io la mostri altrui:  

but I pray that thou point the cause out to me, so that I may see it, and that I may show it to others;

- *m' additi la cagione* ‘to me thou point out the cause’ → “thou point the cause out to me”: the recipient moves to the end, and the direct object separates the English verb from its particle.
- *sì ch' io la veggia* ‘so that I it see’ → “so that I may see it”: the auxiliary renders the subjunctive of purpose, and the object follows the verb.
- *ch' io la mostri altrui* ‘that I it show to others’ → “that I may show it to others”: again the subjunctive is rendered with an auxiliary, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 63 chè nel cielo uno, ed un quaggiù la pone.”  

for one places it in the heavens and another here below.”

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating conjunction gives the reason.
- *nel cielo uno, ed un quaggiù la pone* ‘in the heaven one, and one down here it places’ → “one places it in the heavens and another here below”: the shared verb is expressed in the first clause rather than at the end of the second; its object follows it. Heaven becomes plural, the second ‘one’ becomes the contrasting “another”, and the second place adverb is rendered by two words in reverse order.

> 64 Alto sospir, che duolo strinse in “hui!”  
> 65 mise fuor prima, e poi cominciò: “Frate,  

A deep sigh, which grief compressed to “Alas!” he first gave forth, and then began:

- *Alto sospir* ‘deep sigh’ → “A deep sigh”: the indefinite article is supplied.
- *strinse in* ‘squeezed into’ → “compressed to”: the English uses a preposition of result with the verb of compression.
- *hui* ‘ah’ → “Alas!”: a cry is rendered by a conventional word of lament.
- *mise fuor prima* ‘put forth first’ → “he first gave forth”: the adverb moves before the verb, and English uses the idiom of giving forth a sound.

> 66 lo mondo è cieco, e tu vien ben da lui.  

“Brother, the world is blind, and verily thou comest from it.

- *tu vien ben da lui* ‘thou comest indeed from him’ → “verily thou comest from it”: the emphatic adverb moves before the subject, and the grammatically masculine pronoun referring to the world becomes neuter.

> 67 Voi che vivete ogni cagion recate  
> 68 pur suso al cielo, sì come se tutto  
> 69 movesse seco di necessitate.  

Ye who are living refer every cause up to the heavens alone, even as if they swept all with them of necessity.

- *che vivete* ‘who live’ → “who are living”: the simple present becomes progressive.
- *ogni cagion recate* ‘every cause ye carry’ → “refer every cause”: the object follows the verb, and literal carrying becomes attribution.
- *pur suso al cielo* ‘only up to the heaven’ → “up to the heavens alone”: the restrictive adverb comes last, and the singular becomes plural.
- *sì come se* ‘just as if’ → “even as if”: the comparison is reinforced with a different adverb.
- *tutto movesse seco* ‘everything it moved with itself’ → “they swept all with them”: the subject is plural to match the English antecedent, the object follows the verb, and moving becomes sweeping; the reflexive is rendered by an ordinary pronoun.

> 70 Se così fosse, in voi fora distrutto  
> 71 libero arbitrio, e non fora giustizia  
> 72 per ben, letizia, e per male, aver lutto.  

Were it thus, Freewill in you would be destroyed, and it were not just to have joy for good and mourning for evil.

- *Se così fosse* ‘if thus it were’ → “Were it thus”: English marks the condition by inversion instead of a conjunction, and places the adverb last.
- *in voi fora distrutto libero arbitrio* ‘in you would be destroyed free will’ → “Freewill in you would be destroyed”: the postponed subject moves to the front.
- *non fora giustizia* ‘there would not be justice’ → “it were not just”: the noun becomes a predicative adjective with an impersonal subject.
- *per ben, letizia, e per male, aver lutto* ‘for good, joy, and for evil, to have mourning’ → “to have joy for good and mourning for evil”: the shared infinitive moves to the front, and each phrase giving the ground of the recompense follows its noun.

> 73 Lo cielo i vostri movimenti inizia;  

The heavens set your impulses in motion;

- *Lo cielo* ‘the heaven’ → “The heavens”: the singular becomes plural.
- *i vostri movimenti inizia* ‘your movements it initiates’ → “set your impulses in motion”: the object follows the English verb; movements become impulses, and initiation is expressed by the idiom of setting something in motion.

> 74 non dico tutti, ma, posto ch' io il dica,  

I say not all, but suppose I said it,

- *posto ch' io il dica* ‘it being granted that I say it’ → “suppose I said it”: a participial concession with a subjunctive becomes an invitation to suppose, followed by a hypothetical past; the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 75 lume v' è dato a bene ed a malizia,  

a light is given you to know good and evil,

- *lume v' è dato* ‘light to you is given’ → “a light is given you”: the indefinite article is supplied, and the recipient follows the passive verb.
- *a bene ed a malizia* ‘for good and for wickedness’ → “to know good and evil”: Okey supplies an infinitive specifying what the light enables one to do; the two nouns become its objects, without a repeated preposition, and wickedness becomes the more general evil.

## Lines 76–108 — Free Will and Evil Leadership

> 76 e libero voler, che, se fatica  
> 77 nelle prime battaglie col ciel dura,  
> 78 poi vince tutto, se ben si nutrica.  

and Freewill, which, if it endure the strain in its first battlings with the heavens, at length gains the whole victory, if it be well nurtured.

- *se fatica … dura* ‘if hardship … it endures’ → “if it endure the strain”: the verb moves before its object and before the phrase naming the battles; the indicative becomes an English conditional subjunctive, and the object acquires a definite article.
- *nelle prime battaglie* ‘in the first battles’ → “in its first battlings”: the article becomes a possessive, and “battlings” names the acts of contending.
- *col ciel* ‘with heaven’ → “with the heavens”: the singular becomes an English plural with the article.
- *poi vince tutto* ‘then it conquers all’ → “at length gains the whole victory”: succession is rendered as eventual success; conquering becomes gaining a victory, and the object ‘all’ becomes a modifier of “victory”.
- *se ben si nutrica* ‘if well it nourishes itself’ → “if it be well nurtured”: the reflexive form is taken passively, with a subjunctive auxiliary; the adverb follows the auxiliary.

> 79 A maggior forza ed a miglior natura  
> 80 liberi soggiacete, e quella cria  
> 81 la mente in voi, che il ciel non ha in sua cura.  

Ye lie subject, in your freedom, to a greater power and to a better nature; and that creates in you mind which the heavens have not in their charge.

- *A maggior forza ed a miglior natura* ‘to greater power and to better nature’ → “to a greater power and to a better nature”: the complements move from the head of the clause to after the predicate, and both acquire indefinite articles.
- *liberi soggiacete* ‘free, you are subject’ → “Ye lie subject, in your freedom”: the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase with a possessive and follows rather than precedes the predicate.
- *cria la mente in voi* ‘creates the mind in you’ → “creates in you mind”: the prepositional phrase moves before the object, and the article disappears.
- *che il ciel non ha in sua cura* ‘which heaven does not have in its care’ → “which the heavens have not in their charge”: the singular becomes plural, with corresponding changes to verb and possessive; ‘care’ is rendered as “charge”.

> 82 Però, se il mondo presente disvia,  
> 83 in voi è la cagione, in voi si cheggia,  
> 84 ed io te ne sarò or vera spia.  

Therefore, if the world to-day goeth astray, in you is the cause, in you be it sought, and I now will be a true scout to thee therein.

- *il mondo presente* ‘the present world’ → “the world to-day”: the adjective becomes an expression of time.
- *si cheggia* ‘let it be asked for’ → “be it sought”: asking becomes seeking; the passive sense and the force of the subjunctive are retained.
- *te ne sarò or vera spia* ‘to thee of it I shall be now true scout’ → “now will be a true scout to thee therein”: the two pronoun complements move to the end, ‘of it’ becomes “therein”, and the time adverb moves before the future verb; the predicate noun acquires an article.

> 85 Esce di mano a lui, che la vagheggia  
> 86 prima che sia, a guisa di fanciulla  
> 87 che piangendo e ridendo pargoleggia,  
> 88 l' anima semplicetta, che sa nulla,  

From his hands who fondly loves her ere she is in being, there issues, after the fashion of a little child that sports, now weeping, now laughing, the simple, tender soul, who knoweth naught

- *Esce di mano a lui* ‘issues from the hand of him’ → “From his hands” … “there issues”: the source is put first, the singular becomes plural, and the possessor becomes possessive “his”; introductory “there” precedes the postponed verb.
- *la vagheggia* ‘regards her longingly’ → “fondly loves her”: the verb becomes a verb and adverb, and the object follows them.
- *prima che sia* ‘before she be’ → “ere she is in being”: the subjunctive of existence becomes indicative “is” with the added phrase “in being”.
- *fanciulla* ‘girl’ → “little child”: the English makes smallness explicit but does not specify sex.
- *piangendo e ridendo pargoleggia* ‘weeping and laughing plays the child’ → “sports, now weeping, now laughing”: the finite verb moves first and loses its explicit reference to childlike behaviour; the conjunction gives way to repeated “now”, marking alternation.
- *l' anima semplicetta* ‘the little simple soul’ → “the simple, tender soul”: the adjective moves before the noun, and its diminutive colouring is conveyed by a second adjective.

> 89 salvo che, mossa da lieto fattore,  
> 90 volentier torna a ciò che la trastulla.  

save that, sprung from a joyous maker, willingly she turneth to that which delights her.

- *mossa da lieto fattore* ‘moved by joyous maker’ → “sprung from a joyous maker”: being moved by an agent becomes originating from a source; the noun acquires an article.
- *torna* ‘returns’ → “turneth”: the English does not express the sense of turning back.
- *la trastulla* ‘her it amuses’ → “delights her”: delight replaces amusement, and the pronoun follows the verb.

> 91 Di picciol bene in pria sente sapore;  

First she tastes the savour of a trifling good;

- *Di picciol bene* ‘of small good’ → “of a trifling good”: the phrase moves from the head of the clause to follow “savour”; the English supplies an article and renders smallness as slight worth.
- *in pria* ‘at first’ → “First”: the time phrase becomes a single adverb at the head of the clause.
- *sente sapore* ‘perceives savour’ → “tastes the savour”: the verb specifies the sense involved, and the noun acquires an article.

> 92 quivi s' inganna, e retro ad esso corre,  
> 93 se guida o fren non torce suo amore.  

there she is beguiled and runneth after it, if guide or curb turn not her love aside.

- *s' inganna* ‘deceives herself’ → “she is beguiled”: the reflexive becomes passive.
- *retro ad esso corre* ‘after it runs’ → “runneth after it”: the verb moves before its complement.
- *non torce suo amore* ‘does not deflect her love’ → “turn not her love aside”: the negative follows an uninflected subjunctive rather than preceding an indicative; the sense of deflection is distributed between “turn” and “aside”.

> 94 Onde convenne legge per fren porre;  
> 95 convenne rege aver, che discernesse  
> 96 della vera cittade almen la torre.  

Wherefore ’twas needful to put law as a curb, needful to have a ruler who might discern at least the tower of the true city.

- *convenne legge per fren porre* ‘it was necessary law for bridle to set’ → “’twas needful to put law as a curb”: the infinitive moves before its object; the bridle is rendered by its restraining function, and “as” marks the role of law.
- *convenne rege aver* ‘it was necessary a king to have’ → “needful to have a ruler”: the repeated impersonal verb is reduced to an adjective sharing the earlier subject and copula; the infinitive precedes its object, and “ruler” is less specific than ‘king’.
- *discernesse* ‘should discern’ → “might discern”: the imperfect subjunctive of the relative clause is expressed through a modal of capacity.
- *della vera cittade almen la torre* ‘of the true city at least the tower’ → “at least the tower of the true city”: the dependent phrase moves after “tower”.

> 97 Le leggi son, ma chi pon mano ad esse?  

Laws there are, but who putteth his hand to them?

- *Le leggi son* ‘the laws exist’ → “Laws there are”: the article drops out, and the verb is given an existential “there” while “Laws” remains first.
- *pon mano ad esse* ‘puts hand to them’ → “putteth his hand to them”: the bare noun acquires a possessive.

> 98 Nullo: però che il pastor che precede  
> 99 ruminar può, ma non ha l' unghie fesse.  

None; because the shepherd that leads may chew the cud, but hath not the hoofs divided.

- *precede* ‘goes before’ → “leads”: going in front is rendered as leading.
- *ruminar può* ‘ruminate he can’ → “may chew the cud”: the modal moves before the infinitive, and the single verb becomes a verb with an object.
- *non ha l' unghie fesse* ‘does not have the hooves cloven’ → “hath not the hoofs divided”: the negative follows the verb, and ‘cloven’ is rendered as “divided”.

> 100 Per che la gente, che sua guida vede  
> 101 pure a quel ben ferire ond' ell' è ghiotta,  
> 102 di quel si pasce, e più oltre non chiede.  

Wherefore the people, that see their guide aiming only at that good whereof he is greedy, feed on that and ask no further.

- *la gente, che sua guida vede* ‘the people, that its guide sees’ → “the people, that see their guide”: Italian treats the collective as singular, English as plural; the object moves after the verb.
- *pure a quel ben ferire* ‘only at that good to strike’ → “aiming only at that good”: the infinitive after a verb of seeing becomes a participle and moves before its modifiers; striking at becomes aiming at.
- *ond' ell' è ghiotta* ‘of which she is greedy’ → “whereof he is greedy”: the feminine reference agreeing with the Italian noun for guide becomes masculine.
- *di quel si pasce* ‘on that it feeds itself’ → “feed on that”: the reflexive becomes intransitive, the complement follows the verb, and the verb takes plural agreement.
- *più oltre non chiede* ‘any further it does not ask’ → “ask no further”: the verb takes plural agreement and moves first; its negation is absorbed into the adverbial phrase.

> 103 Ben puoi veder che la mala condotta  
> 104 è la cagion che il mondo ha fatto reo,  
> 105 e non natura che in voi sia corrotta.  

Clearly canst thou see that evil leadership is the cause which hath made the world sinful, and not nature that may be corrupted within you.

- *Ben puoi veder* ‘well you can see’ → “Clearly canst thou see”: ‘well’ is taken as ‘clearly’, and the English subject follows the modal.
- *la mala condotta* ‘the bad conduct’ → “evil leadership”: the noun is taken in the active sense of directing others rather than of behaving; the article drops out.
- *il mondo ha fatto reo* ‘the world has made guilty’ → “hath made the world sinful”: the object moves after the verb, and guilt is rendered as sinfulness.
- *in voi sia corrotta* ‘in you be corrupt’ → “may be corrupted within you”: the subjunctive is expressed by “may”; “corrupted” gives the predicate a participial form, and the phrase locating the corruption moves to the end.

> 106 Soleva Roma, che il buon mondo feo,  
> 107 due soli aver, che l' una e l' altra strada  
> 108 facean vedere, e del mondo e di Deo.  

Rome, that made the good world, was wont to have two suns, which made plain to sight the one road and the other; that of the world, and that of God.

- *Soleva Roma … due soli aver* ‘was wont Rome … two suns to have’ → “Rome” … “was wont to have two suns”: the subject and its relative clause precede the finite verb, and the infinitive precedes its object.
- *il buon mondo feo* ‘the good world made’ → “made the good world”: the verb moves before the object.
- *l' una e l' altra strada* ‘the one and the other road’ → “the one road and the other”: the shared noun is attached to the first rather than the second member; the whole object moves after the verb.
- *facean vedere* ‘made see’ → “made plain to sight”: the causative infinitive becomes an adjective with a phrase specifying sight.
- *e del mondo e di Deo* ‘both of the world and of God’ → “that of the world, and that of God”: the first member loses its introductory conjunction, and each receives “that” to stand for the road.

## Lines 109–141 — The Sword and the Crook

> 109 L' un l' altro ha spento, ed è giunta la spada  
> 110 col pastorale; e l' un con l' altro insieme  
> 111 per viva forza mal convien che vada:  

One hath quenched the other; and the sword is joined to the crook; and the one together with the other must perforce go ill;

- *L' un l' altro ha spento* ‘the one the other hath quenched’ → “One hath quenched the other”: the first article drops out, and the object follows the verb.
- *è giunta la spada col pastorale* ‘is joined the sword with the crook’ → “the sword is joined to the crook”: the subject comes before the verb, and ‘with’ becomes “to”.
- *con l' altro insieme* ‘with the other together’ → “together with the other”: the adverb moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *per viva forza mal convien che vada* ‘of sheer necessity ill it must needs go’ → “must perforce go ill”: the impersonal *convien* with a subjunctive clause becomes a modal with an infinitive; the phrase of necessity is compressed into “perforce”, and “ill” follows “go”.

> 112 però che, giunti, l' un l' altro non teme.  

because, being joined, one feareth not the other.

- *giunti* ‘joined’ → “being joined”: “being” is supplied to introduce the participle.
- *l' un l' altro non teme* ‘the one the other feareth not’ → “one feareth not the other”: the first article drops out, and the object moves after the negated verb.

> 113 Se non mi credi, pon mente alla spiga,  
> 114 ch' ogni erba si conosce per lo seme.  

If thou believest me not, look well at the ear, for every plant is known by the seed.

- *non mi credi* ‘not me believest’ → “thou believest me not”: the object and negative both move after the verb.
- *pon mente alla spiga* ‘give heed to the ear’ → “look well at the ear”: attention is expressed by a verb of looking qualified by “well”.
- *ch'* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating conjunction gives the reason.
- *ogni erba si conosce* ‘every herb is known’ → “every plant is known”: “plant” is broader than ‘herb’, and the Italian *si* construction becomes an English passive with “is”.

> 115 In sul paese ch' Adige e Po riga  
> 116 solea valore e cortesia trovarsi,  
> 117 prima che Federico avesse briga;  

Over the land which the Adige and the Po water, worth and courtesy were wont to be found, ere Frederick met opposition;

- *In sul paese* ‘in the land’ → “Over the land”: the compound preposition is rendered by one suggesting extent.
- *ch' Adige e Po riga* ‘which Adige and Po waters’ → “which the Adige and the Po water”: articles are supplied for both rivers, and the singular verb becomes plural in agreement with the two names.
- *solea valore e cortesia trovarsi* ‘was wont worth and courtesy to be found’ → “worth and courtesy were wont to be found”: the subjects move before the finite verb, which becomes plural; the passive infinitive with *si* becomes “to be found”.
- *prima che Federico avesse briga* ‘before Frederick had strife’ → “ere Frederick met opposition”: ‘having strife’ becomes meeting opposition, and the imperfect subjunctive after *prima che* becomes a simple past.

> 118 or può sicuramente indi passarsi  
> 119 per qualunque lasciasse per vergogna  
> 120 di ragionar coi buoni o d' appressarsi.  

now, safely may it be traversed by whomsoever had, through shame, ceased to hold converse with good men, or to draw near them.

- *può sicuramente indi passarsi* ‘one may safely pass that way’ → “safely may it be traversed”: “safely” is moved forward, and an impersonal construction with an adverb of place becomes a passive whose subject “it” stands for the land.
- *lasciasse per vergogna di ragionar* ‘should cease for shame to talk’ → “had, through shame, ceased to hold converse”: the indefinite relative’s imperfect subjunctive becomes a past perfect indicative, the phrase of motive comes between auxiliary and participle, and the simple infinitive becomes the phrase “to hold converse”.
- *coi buoni* ‘with the good’ → “with good men”: “men” is supplied for the substantivized adjective, and the article disappears.
- *o d' appressarsi* ‘or to draw oneself near’ → “or to draw near them”: the reflexive verb becomes intransitive “draw near”, and “them” supplies the unexpressed complement.

> 121 Ben v' en tre vecchi ancora, in cui rampogna  
> 122 l' antica età la nuova, e par lor tardo  
> 123 che Dio a miglior vita li ripogna:  

Truly three elders yet are there in whom the olden times rebuke the new, and it seems to them long ere God removes them to the better life:

- *Ben v' en tre vecchi ancora* ‘indeed there are three old men still’ → “Truly three elders yet are there”: “elders” renders the substantivized adjective; both “three elders” and “yet” precede the verb, and “there” follows it.
- *rampogna l' antica età la nuova* ‘rebukes the ancient age the new’ → “the olden times rebuke the new”: the subject comes before the verb, and singular ‘age’ becomes plural “times”, with a corresponding change to the verb.
- *par lor tardo che* ‘it seems to them late that’ → “it seems to them long ere”: lateness becomes length of waiting, and the following clause is introduced as a clause of time.
- *a miglior vita li ripogna* ‘to a better life should take them back’ → “removes them to the better life”: the subjunctive becomes indicative “removes”; the object follows the verb, the destination comes last, and “the” makes that destination definite.

> 124 Corrado da Palazzo e il buon Gherardo  
> 125 e Guido da Castel, che me' si noma  
> 126 francescamente il semplice Lombardo.  

Corrado da Palazzo, and the good Gerard, and Guido da Castel, who is better named in French fashion the guileless Lombard.

- *me' si noma* ‘better is named’ → “is better named”: “better” comes between the auxiliary and the participle of the English passive.
- *francescamente* ‘French-fashion’ → “in French fashion”: a single adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *semplice* ‘simple’ → “guileless”: the English selects the sense of being without guile.

> 127 Di' oggimai che la Chiesa di Roma,  
> 128 per confondere in sè due reggimenti,  
> 129 cade nel fango, e sè brutta e la soma.”  

Say henceforth, that the Church of Rome, by confounding two powers in herself, falls into the mire, and fouls herself and her burden.”

- *per confondere in sè due reggimenti* ‘through confounding in herself two governments’ → “by confounding two powers in herself”: the preposition and infinitive become “by” with a gerund, “powers” renders ‘governments’, and the object moves before the phrase of place.
- *sè brutta* ‘herself fouls’ → “fouls herself”: the reflexive object follows the verb.
- *la soma* ‘the burden’ → “her burden”: a possessive replaces the definite article.

> 130 “O Marco mio,” diss' io, “bene argomenti;  
> 131 ed or discerno, perchè da retaggio  
> 132 li figli di Levì furono esenti;  

“O my Mark,” said I, “well thou reasonest, and now I perceive why Levi’s sons were exempt from inheriting;

- *Marco mio* ‘Mark mine’ → “my Mark”: the possessive precedes the name.
- *da retaggio li figli di Levì furono esenti* ‘from inheritance the sons of Levi were exempt’ → “Levi’s sons were exempt from inheriting”: the fronted complement of ‘exempt’ moves to the end and its noun becomes a gerund; the postposed ‘of Levi’ becomes a possessive before “sons”, displacing the article.

> 133 ma qual Gherardo è quel che tu, per saggio,  
> 134 di' ch' è rimaso della gente spenta,  
> 135 in rimproverio del secol selvaggio?”  

but what Gerard is that, who thou sayest is left behind for ensample of the extinct people, in reproof of the barbarous age?”

- *per saggio, di' ch' è rimaso* ‘for an example, thou sayest that he has remained’ → “thou sayest is left behind for ensample”: the phrase of purpose moves to the end and loses its article; “that” is omitted, and the perfect of ‘remain’ becomes a passive expression of the resulting state.
- *della gente spenta* ‘of the people extinguished’ → “of the extinct people”: the participle becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *del secol selvaggio* ‘of the age savage’ → “of the barbarous age”: the adjective comes before the noun, and “barbarous” renders ‘savage’.

> 136 “O tuo parlar m' inganna o e' mi tenta,”  
> 137 rispose a me; “chè, parlandomi tosco,  
> 138 par che del buon Gherardo nulla senta.  

“Either thy speech beguiles me, or it tempts me,” he answered me, “for thou, speaking to me in Tuscan, seemest to know naught of the good Gerard.

- *tuo parlar m' inganna* ‘thy speaking me beguiles’ → “thy speech beguiles me”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun, and the object follows the verb.
- *mi tenta* ‘me tempts’ → “tempts me”: the object follows the verb.
- *rispose a me* ‘answered to me’ → “he answered me”: English takes a direct object without the preposition.
- *parlandomi tosco* ‘speaking to me Tuscan’ → “speaking to me in Tuscan”: “in” is supplied before the name of the language.
- *par che del buon Gherardo nulla senta* ‘it seems that of the good Gerard thou knowest nothing’ → “thou, speaking to me in Tuscan, seemest to know naught of the good Gerard”: impersonal ‘it seems’ with a subjunctive clause becomes personal “seemest” with an infinitive; “naught” follows “know”, and the phrase specifying whom it concerns comes last.

> 139 Per altro soprannome io nol conosco,  
> 140 s' io nol togliessi da sua figlia Gaia.  

By other surname I know him not, except I take it from his daughter Gaia.

- *io nol conosco* ‘I not him know’ → “I know him not”: the object and negative both follow the verb.
- *s' io nol togliessi* ‘if I did not take it’ → “except I take it”: “except” expresses the negative condition without a separate negative, and “take” replaces the imperfect subjunctive.

> 141 Dio sia con voi, chè più non vegno vosco.  

God be with you, for no further I come with you.

- *più non vegno* ‘further I come not’ → “no further I come”: the negative is attached to “further” rather than to the verb.

## Lines 142–145 — The Light and the Leave-Taking

> 142 Vedi l' albòr, che per lo fummo raia,  
> 143 già biancheggiare, e me convien partirmi,  
> 144 l' angelo è ivi, prima ch' io gli appaia.”  

See the light, that beams through the smoke, now waxing bright; the angel is there, and it behoves me to depart ere I am seen of him.”

- *l' albòr* ‘the pale gleam’ → “the light”: the English uses a more general noun without the suggestion of whiteness.
- *che per lo fummo raia* ‘that through the smoke beams’ → “that beams through the smoke”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *già biancheggiare* ‘already to show white’ → “now waxing bright”: ‘already’ becomes “now”; the infinitive after the verb of seeing becomes an English participle, and showing white is rendered as growing bright.
- *e me convien partirmi* ‘and to me it is necessary to depart’ → “and it behoves me to depart”: the impersonal verb is given the formal subject “it”, the person affected follows the verb, and the pronominal infinitive is rendered by the simple “to depart”. The whole clause moves after the statement about the angel.
- *prima ch' io gli appaia* ‘before I appear to him’ → “ere I am seen of him”: appearing to someone is recast as a passive construction in which that person is the agent of seeing; the Italian subjunctive gives way to the English indicative.

> 145 Così tornò, e più non volle udirmi.  

So turned he back and no more would hear me.

- *tornò* ‘turned back’ → “turned he back”: the supplied subject follows the finite verb, separating it from “back”.
- *più non volle udirmi* ‘no longer did he wish to hear me’ → “no more would hear me”: “no more” combines the adverb and the negation, and the past tense of willing followed by an infinitive becomes the modal “would” with the bare verb “hear”.

## Summary — Italian Order and English Idiom

Okey often moves a verb before its object or restores a complement to the noun it qualifies. In *d’ iracondia van solvendo il nodo*, the opening ‘of anger’ comes last in “they are untying the knot of anger”. He also rearranges clauses: the comparison with the smoke waits until both the thickness and the harshness of the veil have been named. Yet he does not invariably smooth out an inversion. “A Lombard was I” keeps the emphasis of the opening *Lombardo*, and “Laws there are” keeps *Le leggi* first.

The choice of words can narrow a meaning or make an implication explicit. For *un modo*, ‘one manner’, Okey gives “one measure”; for *a bene ed a malizia*, ‘for good and for evil’, he gives “to know good and evil”, supplying the act of discernment. Elsewhere an English idiom leaves something out: *per non smarrirsi*, ‘in order not to lose oneself’, becomes “in order not to stray”, without a reflexive pronoun. Two routine adjustments generally pass unmarked in the comparison: English supplies subject pronouns where the Italian verb ending identifies the subject, and drops the article before a possessive, as in *il mio duca*, “my Leader”.

Changes of construction need not entail changes of image. In *van solvendo*, ‘they go loosening’, the English progressive “they are untying” drops the verb of motion but keeps both the gradual action and the knot. Conversely, *prima ch’ io gli appaia*, ‘before I appear to him’, becomes “ere I am seen of him”, shifting from appearing to being seen. Okey stays particularly close where English can carry the same concrete nouns or the same repetition: *due soli* remains “two suns”, *la spada* and *il pastorale* remain “the sword” and “the crook”, and the repeated *in voi* retains its insistent “in you”.
