# Purgatorio 31

## Lines 1–12 — A Confession Demanded

> 1 “O tu, che sei di là dal fiume sacro,”  

“O thou that art yon side the sacred stream,”

- *di là dal fiume sacro* ‘on the far side of the stream sacred’ → “yon side the sacred stream”: “yon side” expresses the relation without separate prepositions, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 2 volgendo suo parlare a me per punta  
> 3 che pur per taglio m' era paruto acro,  

her speech directing with the point towards me, which even with the edge had seemed sharp to me,

- *volgendo suo parlare* ‘directing her speaking’ → “her speech directing”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun, and the object is placed before the participle.
- *a me per punta* ‘towards me with the point’ → “with the point towards me”: the two phrases change places.
- *m' era paruto acro* ‘to me had seemed keen’ → “had seemed sharp to me”: “sharp” gives *acro* the sense suggested by the edge, and the pronoun moves from before the verb to after the adjective.

> 4 ricominciò, seguendo senza cunta,  

she began again, continuing without delay,

- *ricominciò* ‘she recommenced’ → “she began again”: the force of the prefix is given by a separate adverb.
- *seguendo* ‘following on’ → “continuing”: the participle is rendered in its sense of carrying on with speech.

> 5 “di', di', se questo è vero: a tanta accusa  
> 6 tua confession conviene esser congiunta.”  

“say, say, if this is true; to such accusation thy confession must be joined.”

- *a tanta accusa* ‘to so great an accusation’ → “to such accusation”: “such” replaces the explicit expression of degree.
- *tua confession conviene esser congiunta* ‘it is requisite for thy confession to be joined’ → “thy confession must be joined”: the Italian puts *tua confession* before the impersonal verb *conviene*; English makes it the subject of the modal “must”.

> 7 Era la mia virtù tanto confusa,  
> 8 che la voce si mosse e pria si spense  
> 9 che dagli organi suoi fosse dischiusa.  

My virtue was so confounded that the voice stirred and was spent ere it was set free from its organs.

- *Era la mia virtù* ‘was my virtue’ → “My virtue was”: the subject is placed before the verb.
- *si mosse* ‘moved itself’ → “stirred”: an intransitive verb replaces the pronominal form.
- *pria si spense che* ‘earlier it died out than’ → “was spent ere”: the pronominal verb becomes a passive expression, and the separated *pria … che* is rendered by one conjunction after it.
- *dagli organi suoi* ‘from the organs of it’ → “from its organs”: the postposed possessive precedes the noun in English, and the whole phrase moves after the verb.
- *fosse dischiusa* ‘it should be unclosed’ → “it was set free”: the imperfect subjunctive in the temporal clause becomes a simple past passive, and the image of opening is rendered as release.

> 10 Poco sofferse, poi disse; “Che pense?  

Short time she forebore, then said: “What thinkest thou?

- *Poco* ‘a little’ → “Short time”: the adverbial indication of duration becomes an adjective and an explicit noun.

> 11 Rispondi a me: chè le memorie triste  
> 12 in te non sono ancor dall' acqua offense.”  

Answer me, for the sad memories in thee are not yet destroyed by the water.

- *Rispondi a me* ‘answer to me’ → “Answer me”: the prepositional complement becomes a direct object.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *le memorie triste* ‘the memories sad’ → “the sad memories”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *non sono ancor* ‘not are yet’ → “are not yet”: the negative follows the auxiliary.
- *dall' acqua offense* ‘by the water impaired’ → “destroyed by the water”: the agent follows the participle, and “destroyed” is stronger than ‘impaired’.

## Lines 13–45 — Dante Confesses His Fault

> 13 Confusione e paura insieme miste  
> 14 mi pinsero un tal “sì” fuor della bocca,  
> 15 al quale intender fur mestier le viste.  

Confusion and fear, together mingled, drove forth from my mouth a “Yea” such that to understand it the eyes were needed.

- *mi pinsero un tal sì fuor della bocca* ‘drove me such a yes out of the mouth’ → “drove forth from my mouth a “Yea” such”: the dative *mi* becomes the possessive “my”; the phrase of motion moves ahead of the object, and *tal* moves from before the quoted word to after it.
- *al quale intender* ‘for the understanding of which’ → “that to understand it”: the relative construction gives way to a *such … that* clause, with “it” as the object of the infinitive.
- *fur mestier le viste* ‘were needful the powers of sight’ → “the eyes were needed”: the organs stand for the faculty; the subject precedes the verb, and necessity is expressed by a passive.

> 16 Come balestro frange, quando scocca  
> 17 da troppa tesa, la sua corda e l' arco,  
> 18 e con men foga l' asta il segno tocca:  

As a cross-bow breaks, when shot at too great tension, both its string and bow, and with less force the bolt hits the mark,

- *quando scocca da troppa tesa* ‘when it discharges from excessive tension’ → “when shot at too great tension”: the active finite verb becomes a passive participle, and *da*, indicating the source of the breakage, becomes “at”.
- *la sua corda e l' arco* ‘its string and the bow’ → “both its string and bow”: “both” is supplied, and the possessive does duty for both nouns.
- *foga* ‘impetus’ → “force”: the rendering stresses force rather than the rush of the discharge.
- *l' asta il segno tocca* ‘the shaft the mark touches’ → “the bolt hits the mark”: “bolt” specifies the kind of shaft, “hits” is more specific than ‘touches’, and the object follows the verb.

> 19 sì scoppia' io sott' esso grave carco,  
> 20 fuori sgorgando lagrime e sospiri,  
> 21 e la voce allentò per lo suo varco.  

so burst I under this heavy charge, pouring forth a torrent of tears and sighs, and my voice died away in its passage.

- *sott' esso grave carco* ‘under that very heavy load’ → “under this heavy charge”: the emphatic *esso* becomes the demonstrative “this”, and ‘load’ becomes “charge”.
- *fuori sgorgando lagrime e sospiri* ‘forth gushing tears and sighs’ → “pouring forth a torrent of tears and sighs”: “forth” follows the participle, and Okey makes the force of *sgorgando* explicit by supplying “a torrent of”.
- *la voce allentò* ‘the voice slackened’ → “my voice died away”: the article becomes a possessive, and a phrase suggesting extinction replaces the verb of slackening.
- *per lo suo varco* ‘through its passage’ → “in its passage”: “in” gives the place rather than the course.

> 22 Ond' ella a me: “Per entro i miei disiri,  
> 23 che ti menavano ad amar lo bene  
> 24 di là dal qual non è a che s' aspiri,  

Wherefore she to me: “Within thy desires of me which led thee to love the good beyond which is nought that may be aspired to,

- *Per entro i miei disiri* ‘through the midst of my desires’ → “Within thy desires of me”: “Within” compresses the compound preposition. Okey takes *miei* objectively: “of me” identifies the object of desire, while the supplied “thy” identifies the person desiring.
- *menavano* ‘were leading’ → “led”: the simple past does not mark the continuing action of the imperfect.
- *non è a che s' aspiri* ‘there is not anything to which one might aspire’ → “is nought that may be aspired to”: the negation is gathered into “nought”; impersonal *si* with an active subjunctive becomes a modal passive with the preposition at the end.

> 25 quai fossi attraversati o quai catene  
> 26 trovasti, per che del passare innanzi  
> 27 dovessiti così spogliar la spene?  

what pits didst find athwart thy path, or what chains that thou needs must strip thee of the hope of passing onward?

- *quai fossi attraversati o quai catene trovasti* ‘what ditches set crosswise or what chains didst thou find’ → “what pits didst find athwart thy path, or what chains”: “pits” replaces ‘ditches’; the verb moves between its two objects, and the participle becomes a prepositional phrase supplying “thy path”.
- *per che* ‘on account of which’ → “that”: a relative phrase expressing cause becomes a conjunction introducing the consequence.
- *del passare innanzi dovessiti così spogliar la spene* ‘of passing onward thou shouldst thus strip thyself of hope’ → “thou needs must strip thee of the hope of passing onward”: the phrase qualifying ‘hope’ moves from the beginning to the end; the imperfect subjunctive of obligation becomes “needs must”, the reflexive follows “strip”, and *così* has no separate equivalent.

> 28 E quali agevolezze o quali avanzi  
> 29 nella fronte degli altri si mostraro,  
> 30 per che dovessi lor passeggiare anzi?”  

And what allurements or what advantages were displayed to thee in the aspect of the others, that thou must needs wander before them?”

- *agevolezze* ‘facilities’ → “allurements”: what makes a course easy is rendered as what makes it attractive.
- *nella fronte degli altri si mostraro* ‘in the face of the others showed themselves’ → “were displayed to thee in the aspect of the others”: the verb moves ahead of the prepositional phrase, the reflexive becomes a passive, and “to thee” is supplied; “aspect” broadens the bodily *fronte*.
- *per che dovessi* ‘on account of which thou shouldst’ → “that thou must needs”: the causal relative becomes a conjunction of consequence, and the imperfect subjunctive becomes an emphatic modal expression.
- *lor passeggiare anzi* ‘them to walk before’ → “wander before them”: “wander” adds a suggestion of straying, and “before” precedes its pronoun.

> 31 Dopo la tratta d' un sospiro amaro,  
> 32 a pena ebbi la voce che rispose,  
> 33 e le labbra a fatica la formaro.  

After the heaving of a bitter sigh, scarce had I voice that answered, and my lips with labour gave it form.

- *la tratta d' un sospiro amaro* ‘the drawing of a sigh bitter’ → “the heaving of a bitter sigh”: English uses the idiom of heaving a sigh and places the adjective before the noun.
- *la voce* ‘the voice’ → “voice”: the noun is used without the definite article.
- *le labbra* ‘the lips’ → “my lips”: a possessive replaces the article.
- *la formaro* ‘it they formed’ → “gave it form”: the verb becomes a verb–noun phrase, and the preceding direct object becomes the recipient of “form”.

> 34 Piangendo dissi: “Le presenti cose  
> 35 col falso lor piacer volser miei passi,  
> 36 tosto che il vostro viso si nascose.”  

Weeping I said: “Present things with their false pleasure turned away my steps soon as your face was hidden.”

- *Le presenti cose* ‘the present things’ → “Present things”: the definite article is omitted.
- *col falso lor piacer* ‘with the false pleasure of theirs’ → “with their false pleasure”: the possessive comes before the adjective rather than after it.
- *volser* ‘turned’ → “turned away”: “away” makes the change of direction explicit.
- *si nascose* ‘hid itself’ → “was hidden”: the reflexive is rendered by a passive.

> 37 Ed ella: “Se tacessi, o se negassi  
> 38 ciò che confessi, non fora men nota  
> 39 la colpa tua: da tal giudice sassi.  

And she: “If thou wert silent, or if thou hadst denied what thou confessest, not less noted were thy fault; by such a judge ’tis known.

- *tacessi* ‘thou shouldst keep silence’ → “thou wert silent”: a verb becomes a copula and adjective, retaining the hypothetical construction.
- *negassi* ‘thou shouldst deny’ → “thou hadst denied”: the imperfect subjunctive, parallel to *tacessi*, becomes a past perfect.
- *non fora men nota la colpa tua* ‘not would be less known the fault of thine’ → “not less noted were thy fault”: “not less noted” is brought together before the verb; “were” has conditional force, “noted” replaces ‘known’, and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *sassi* ‘one knows it’ → “’tis known”: the impersonal construction with enclitic *si* becomes an English passive.

> 40 Ma quando scoppia dalla propria gota  
> 41 l' accusa del peccato, in nostra corte  
> 42 rivolge sè contra il taglio la rota.  

But when self-accusation of sin bursts from the cheeks in our Court, the grindstone is turned back against the edge.

- *scoppia dalla propria gota l' accusa del peccato* ‘bursts from one’s own cheek the accusation of the sin’ → “self-accusation of sin bursts from the cheeks”: the subject moves before the verb; *propria* is conveyed by “self-” on “accusation” rather than by a possessive on ‘cheek’. The singular cheek becomes plural, and the articles on ‘accusation’ and ‘sin’ drop out.
- *in nostra corte rivolge sè contra il taglio la rota* ‘in our court turns itself against the edge the wheel’ → “in our Court, the grindstone is turned back against the edge”: the translation attaches “in our Court” to the bursting forth of the accusation rather than to the turning of the stone. The subject moves to the front, “grindstone” specifies the kind of wheel, and the reflexive becomes a passive with “back” expressing reversal.

> 43 Tuttavia, perchè mo vergogna porte  
> 44 del tuo errore, e perchè altra volta  
> 45 udendo le Sirene sie più forte,  

Howbeit in order that now thou mayst bear shame for thy transgression, and that other time hearing the Sirens thou be of stouter heart,

- *perchè mo vergogna porte* ‘in order that now shame thou mayst bear’ → “in order that now thou mayst bear shame”: the object moves after the verb, and “mayst” expresses the subjunctive.
- *del tuo errore* ‘of thy error’ → “for thy transgression”: the preposition marks the occasion for shame, and “transgression” is stronger than ‘error’.
- *perchè* ‘in order that’ → “that”: the repeated conjunction is shortened in English, sharing the earlier “in order”.
- *sie più forte* ‘thou be stronger’ → “thou be of stouter heart”: the simple comparative adjective becomes a phrase specifying strength of heart.

## Lines 46–78 — Beatrice’s Rebuke and Dante’s Shame

> 46 pon giù il seme del piangere, ed ascolta:  

put away the seed of weeping, and hearken;

- *pon giù* ‘put down’ → “put away”: the direction ‘down’ becomes “away”.

> 47 sì udirai come in contraria parte  
> 48 mover doveati mia carne sepolta.  

so shalt thou hear how my buried flesh should have moved thee towards a contrary goal.

- *udirai* ‘thou wilt hear’ → “shalt thou hear”: the auxiliary precedes the subject after “so”.
- *in contraria parte* ‘in the opposite direction’ → “towards a contrary goal”: a direction becomes a goal approached; the phrase moves from before the verb to the end of the clause.
- *mover doveati* ‘to move thee it ought’ → “should have moved thee”: the modal comes first, the simple infinitive becomes a perfect infinitive, and the object pronoun, attached in Italian to the modal, follows “moved”.
- *mia carne sepolta* ‘my flesh buried’ → “my buried flesh”: the adjective precedes the noun, and the subject moves from after the verb to before it.

> 49 Mai non t' appresentò natura o arte  
> 50 piacer, quanto le belle membra in ch' io  
> 51 rinchiusa fui, e sono in terra sparte;  

Ne’er did nature and art present to thee pleasure so great as the fair members wherein I was enclosed, and are scattered to dust;

- *Mai non t' appresentò natura o arte* ‘never to thee presented nature or art’ → “Ne’er did nature and art present to thee”: one negative serves for *mai non*; English inserts “did” before the subject, puts the main verb and its indirect object after the subject, and substitutes “and” for the disjunctive *o*.
- *piacer, quanto* ‘pleasure, as much as’ → “pleasure so great as”: the comparison of amount is recast with an adjective of degree.
- *rinchiusa fui* ‘enclosed I was’ → “I was enclosed”: the auxiliary precedes the participle.
- *in terra sparte* ‘in earth scattered’ → “scattered to dust”: the participle comes first, and a phrase of place becomes one of result.

> 52 e se il sommo piacer sì ti fallio  
> 53 per la mia morte, qual cosa mortale  
> 54 dovea poi trarre te nel suo disio?  

and if the highest pleasure thus failed thee by my death, what mortal thing ought then to have drawn thee to desire it?

- *sì ti fallio* ‘thus thee failed’ → “thus failed thee”: the object follows the verb.
- *per la mia morte* ‘because of my death’ → “by my death”: the causal phrase is expressed with “by”.
- *dovea poi trarre te* ‘ought then to draw thee’ → “ought then to have drawn thee”: the simple infinitive becomes a perfect infinitive.
- *nel suo disio* ‘into desire for it’ → “to desire it”: the prepositional phrase becomes an infinitive, with the Italian possessive recast as its object.

> 55 Ben ti dovevi, per lo primo strale  
> 56 delle cose fallaci, levar suso  
> 57 di retro a me che non era più tale.  

Truly oughtest thou, at the first arrow of deceitful things, to rise up after me who was such no longer.

- *Ben ti dovevi … levar suso* ‘well oughtest thou … to lift thyself up’ → “Truly oughtest thou” … “to rise up”: emphatic *ben* becomes “Truly”; the reflexive construction, with *ti* before the modal, becomes the intransitive “rise”.
- *per lo primo strale* ‘through the first arrow’ → “at the first arrow”: “at” presents the stroke as the occasion.
- *delle cose fallaci* ‘of the things deceitful’ → “of deceitful things”: the definite article drops out and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *non era più tale* ‘was no longer such’ → “was such no longer”: the negative expression follows “such”.

> 58 Non ti dovean gravar le penne in giuso,  
> 59 ad aspettar più colpi, o pargoletta,  
> 60 o altra vanità con sì breve uso.  

Young damsel or other vain thing with so brief enjoyment, should not have weighed down thy wings to await more shots.

- *Non ti dovean gravar le penne in giuso* ‘ought not to weigh thy feathers downwards’ → “should not have weighed down thy wings”: the predicate moves after its subject, and the simple infinitive becomes a perfect infinitive; the dative *ti* becomes the possessive “thy”, *penne* is taken collectively as “wings”, and “down” precedes the object.
- *ad aspettar più colpi* ‘to await more blows’ → “to await more shots”: “shots” specifies the kind of blows; the infinitive phrase moves to the end.
- *o pargoletta, o altra vanità* ‘either a young girl or another vanity’ → “Young damsel or other vain thing”: the alternative subjects move to the front, the first *o* is unexpressed, and the abstract noun *vanità* becomes adjective and noun.
- *con sì breve uso* ‘with so brief a use’ → “with so brief enjoyment”: “enjoyment” interprets *uso*.

> 61 Nuovo augelletto due o tre aspetta;  
> 62 ma dinanzi dagli occhi dei pennuti  
> 63 rete si spiega indarno o si saetta.”  

The young bird waits two or three, but before the eyes of the full-fledged in vain the net is spread or arrow shot.”

- *Nuovo augelletto* ‘young little bird’ → “The young bird”: the definite article is supplied and the diminutive is not separately rendered.
- *due o tre aspetta* ‘two or three it waits’ → “waits two or three”: the verb precedes its object.
- *dei pennuti* ‘of the feathered ones’ → “of the full-fledged”: “full-fledged” specifies the state of feathering.
- *rete si spiega indarno* ‘a net is spread in vain’ → “in vain the net is spread”: the adverbial moves to the front, the bare noun takes a definite article, and passive *si* is rendered by an auxiliary.
- *o si saetta* ‘or one shoots’ → “or arrow shot”: the impersonal verb becomes a passive participle sharing “is” with “spread”, and “arrow” is supplied as subject.

> 64 Quali i fanciulli vergognando muti  
> 65 con gli occhi a terra, stannosi ascoltando,  
> 66 e sè riconoscendo, e ripentuti,  

As children, dumb with shame, stand listening with eyes to earth, self-confessing, and repentant,

- *Quali i fanciulli* ‘such as the children’ → “As children”: the comparative is reduced to “As” and the generic article drops out.
- *vergognando muti* ‘feeling shame, mute’ → “dumb with shame”: the adjective moves first and the gerund becomes a phrase giving the cause.
- *con gli occhi a terra* ‘with the eyes to earth’ → “with eyes to earth”: the article drops out and the phrase moves after “stand listening”.
- *stannosi ascoltando* ‘they stay listening’ → “stand listening”: *stare* is given the more specific “stand”; its pronominal particle has no separate rendering.
- *e sè riconoscendo* ‘and acknowledging themselves’ → “self-confessing”: the conjunction drops out, the reflexive becomes the first element of a compound, and “confessing” interprets ‘acknowledging’.
- *ripentuti* ‘repented’ → “repentant”: an adjective of state replaces the past participle.

> 67 tal mi stav' io. Ed ella disse: “Quando  
> 68 per udir sei dolente, alza la barba,  
> 69 e prenderai più doglia riguardando.”  

such stood I. And she said: “Since through hearing thou art grieving, lift up thy beard and more grief shalt thou receive by looking.”

- *mi stav' io* ‘I remained’ → “stood I”: the pronominal verb is rendered by a verb of posture without a reflexive.
- *Quando* ‘when’ → “Since”: the conjunction is taken causally.
- *per udir sei dolente* ‘through hearing thou art sorrowful’ → “through hearing thou art grieving”: the infinitive after the preposition becomes a gerund, and the predicate adjective becomes a progressive verb.
- *la barba* ‘the beard’ → “thy beard”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *prenderai più doglia riguardando* ‘thou wilt take more grief looking’ → “more grief shalt thou receive by looking”: the object moves first, with inversion of auxiliary and subject; “receive” replaces ‘take’, and “by” makes the relation of the gerund explicit.

> 70 Con men di resistenza si dibarba  
> 71 robusto cerro, o vero al nostral vento,  
> 72 o vero a quel della terra di Iarba,  
> 73 ch' io non levai al suo comando il mento;  

With less resistance is uprooted the sturdy oak, whether by wind of ours, or that which blows from Iarbas’ land, than at her command I lifted up my chin;

- *si dibarba* ‘is torn out by the roots’ → “is uprooted”: passive *si* becomes an English passive, and the Italian verb’s echo of *barba* is lost.
- *robusto cerro* ‘sturdy Turkey oak’ → “the sturdy oak”: the article is supplied and the more specific tree name is generalized.
- *o vero al nostral vento* ‘either by our wind’ → “whether by wind of ours”: the alternative is introduced by “whether”, and the possessive becomes a postposed phrase.
- *o vero a quel della terra di Iarba* ‘or by that of the land of Iarbas’ → “or that which blows from Iarbas’ land”: “by” is not repeated; origin is expressed by a supplied relative clause, and the possessor precedes “land”.
- *ch' io non levai al suo comando il mento* ‘than I raised at her command the chin’ → “than at her command I lifted up my chin”: the comparative *non* has no English negative; the command phrase moves before the subject, ‘raised’ becomes “lifted up”, and the article becomes a possessive.

> 74 e quando per la barba il viso chiese,  
> 75 ben conobbi il velen dell' argomento.  

and when by the beard she asked for my face, well I knew the venom of the argument.

- *il viso chiese* ‘the face she requested’ → “she asked for my face”: the object follows a prepositional verb, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *ben conobbi* ‘well I recognized’ → “well I knew”: the act of recognition is rendered as knowledge.

> 76 E come la mia faccia si distese,  
> 77 posarsi quelle prime creature  
> 78 da loro aspersion l' occhio comprese;  

And when my face was stretched forth, my sight perceived those primal creatures resting from their strewing,

- *come* ‘as’ → “when”: the temporal sense is made explicit.
- *si distese* ‘extended itself’ → “was stretched forth”: the pronominal verb becomes passive, with “forth” expressing direction.
- *posarsi quelle prime creature* ‘rest those first creatures’ → “those primal creatures resting”: the subject of the infinitive comes first in English, the infinitive of perception becomes a participle, and “primal” interprets ‘first’.
- *da loro aspersion* ‘from their sprinkling’ → “from their strewing”: “strewing” replaces the noun for sprinkling.
- *l' occhio comprese* ‘the eye perceived’ → “my sight perceived”: the organ becomes the faculty, the article becomes a possessive, and the main subject and verb move before what is perceived.

## Lines 79–111 — Repentance and the River Bath

> 79 e le mie luci, ancor poco sicure,  
> 80 vider Beatrice volta in su la fiera,  
> 81 ch' è sola una persona in due nature.  

and mine eyes, as yet hardly steadfast, saw Beatrice turned towards the beast, which is one sole person in two natures.

- *luci* ‘lights’ → “eyes”: the translation names directly what the Italian names figuratively.
- *ancor poco sicure* ‘still little sure’ → “as yet hardly steadfast”: “as yet” gives the temporal sense of *ancor*; “hardly steadfast” turns the small degree of assurance into a near lack of steadiness.
- *in su la fiera* ‘upon the beast’ → “towards the beast”: the preposition expresses the direction of the turn.
- *sola una persona* ‘sole a single person’ → “one sole person”: the numeral precedes the adjective in English.

> 82 Sotto suo velo ed oltre la riviera  
> 83 vincer pareami più sè stessa antica,  
> 84 vincer che l' altre qui quand' ella c' era.  

Under her veil and beyond the stream, to me she seemed to surpass more her ancient self, than she surpassed the others here when she was with us.

- *vincer pareami* ‘to surpass she seemed to me’ → “to me she seemed to surpass”: the infinitive moves after the finite verb, and the attached pronoun becomes an initial prepositional phrase.
- *sè stessa antica* ‘herself of old’ → “her ancient self”: the emphatic pronoun becomes a noun with a possessive, and the adjective moves before it.
- *vincer che l' altre* ‘to surpass than the others’ → “than she surpassed the others”: the repeated infinitive, which precedes the comparative conjunction in Italian, becomes a finite verb after it.
- *c' era* ‘was here’ → “was with us”: the locative is interpreted as presence in the speakers’ company.

> 85 Di penter sì mi punse ivi l' ortica,  
> 86 che di tutt' altre cose, qual mi torse  
> 87 più nel suo amor, più mi si fe' nimica.  

The nettle of repentance here so did sting me, that of all other things, that which turned me most to love of it became most hateful to me.

- *Di penter sì mi punse ivi l' ortica* ‘of repenting so me stung there the nettle’ → “The nettle of repentance here so did sting me”: the subject comes first and its modifier follows it; the substantival infinitive becomes an abstract noun. The place adverb changes from ‘there’ to “here” and moves before “so”; the simple past is expanded with “did”, and the object follows the verb.
- *qual mi torse più* ‘whichever me turned more’ → “that which turned me most”: the relative is expanded to antecedent and relative, the object follows the verb, and the comparative becomes a superlative.
- *nel suo amor* ‘into its love’ → “to love of it”: “to” marks the direction of the turning, and the possessive is unpacked to make the thing the object of love.
- *più mi si fe' nimica* ‘more to me made itself an enemy’ → “became most hateful to me”: the reflexive verb becomes “became”, being an enemy becomes being hateful, the comparative becomes a superlative, and “to me” moves to the end.

> 88 Tanta riconoscenza il cor mi morse,  
> 89 ch' io caddi vinto, e quale allora femmi  
> 90 salsi colei che la cagion mi porse.  

So much remorse gnawed at my heart that I fell vanquished, and what I then became, she knoweth who gave me the cause.

- *riconoscenza* ‘recognition’ → “remorse”: recognition of fault is rendered by the feeling it entails.
- *il cor mi morse* ‘the heart to me bit’ → “gnawed at my heart”: the verb moves before its object, biting becomes gnawing with the preposition “at”, and the personal dative becomes a possessive.
- *quale allora femmi* ‘of what sort then I made myself’ → “what I then became”: the reflexive construction becomes a verb of becoming; “what” asks after the resulting state.
- *salsi colei* ‘knows it herself, that woman’ → “she knoweth”: the subject moves before the verb; the demonstrative, the resumptive ‘it’, and the reflexive emphasis are not separately expressed.
- *la cagion mi porse* ‘the cause to me proffered’ → “gave me the cause”: the verb is generalized to “gave” and placed before both objects, with the recipient first.

> 91 Poi, quando il cor di fuor virtù rendemmi,  
> 92 la donna ch' io avea trovata sola  
> 93 sopra me vidi, e dicea: “Tiemmi, tiemmi.”  

Then when my heart restored to me the sense of outward things, the lady whom I had found alone I saw above me; and she said: “Hold me! Hold me!”

- *il cor* ‘the heart’ → “my heart”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *di fuor virtù rendemmi* ‘outward faculty restored to me’ → “restored to me the sense of outward things”: the verb and its attached pronoun come before the object; the faculty is specified as sense, and what is outside becomes the “things” perceived.
- *sopra me vidi* ‘above me I saw’ → “I saw above me”: the verb comes before the phrase of place.
- *dicea* ‘was saying’ → “said”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.

> 94 Tratto m' avea nel fiume infino a gola,  
> 95 e, tirandosi me dietro, sen giva  
> 96 sopr' esso l' acqua, lieve come spola.  

She had drawn me into the river up to my neck, and, pulling me after her, went along over the water light as a shuttle.

- *Tratto m' avea* ‘drawn me she had’ → “She had drawn me”: the auxiliary precedes the participle and the object follows it.
- *infino a gola* ‘up to throat’ → “up to my neck”: “neck” replaces ‘throat’, and a possessive is supplied.
- *tirandosi me dietro* ‘drawing to herself me behind’ → “pulling me after her”: “after her” combines the direction expressed by the adverb with the reference to the woman supplied by the reflexive.
- *sen giva* ‘went her way’ → “went along”: the pronominal idiom is rendered by a verb and adverb.
- *sopr' esso l' acqua* ‘right over the water’ → “over the water”: the reinforcing *esso* has no separate equivalent.

> 97 Quando fui presso alla beata riva,  
> 98 “*Asperges me*” sì dolcemente udissi,  
> 99 ch' io nol so rimembrar, non ch' io lo scriva.  

When I was nigh unto the blessed bank “*Asperges me*” so sweetly I heard that I cannot remember it much less describe it.

- *udissi* ‘was heard’ → “I heard”: the passive construction with *si* becomes active, with the hearer as subject.
- *nol so rimembrar* ‘it I know not how to recall’ → “I cannot remember it”: the construction with ‘know how’ becomes one of ability, and the object moves after the infinitive.
- *non ch' io lo scriva* ‘not that I should write it’ → “much less describe it”: the negative clause with a subjunctive becomes a phrase dependent on “cannot”; “much less” makes the gradation explicit, and describing replaces writing.

> 100 La bella donna nelle braccia aprissi,  
> 101 abbracciommi la testa, e mi sommerse  
> 102 ove convenne ch' io l' acqua inghiottissi;  

The fair lady opened her arms, clasped my head, and dipped me where I must needs swallow of the water;

- *nelle braccia aprissi* ‘in the arms opened herself’ → “opened her arms”: the reflexive and prepositional construction becomes transitive, with a possessive on the direct object.
- *abbracciommi la testa* ‘embraced me about the head’ → “clasped my head”: the head becomes the direct object of “clasped”, and the attached personal pronoun becomes a possessive.
- *mi sommerse* ‘me submerged’ → “dipped me”: the object moves after the verb, and “dipped” replaces ‘submerged’.
- *convenne ch' io l' acqua inghiottissi* ‘it was necessary that I the water should swallow’ → “I must needs swallow of the water”: impersonal necessity with a subjunctive clause becomes a personal modal construction; the object moves after the verb and becomes partitive with “of”.

> 103 indi mi tolse, e bagnato m' offerse  
> 104 dentro alla danza delle quattro belle,  
> 105 e ciascuna del braccio mi coperse.  

then drew me forth, and led me bathed within the dance of the four fair ones, and each did cover me with her arm.

- *mi tolse* ‘me lifted’ → “drew me forth”: the object follows the verb, and “forth” makes the movement out explicit.
- *bagnato m' offerse* ‘bathed, me she presented’ → “led me bathed”: presenting becomes leading; both the object and the description of its state follow the verb.
- *quattro belle* ‘four fair [women]’ → “four fair ones”: “ones” supplies a head for the substantival adjective.
- *del braccio mi coperse* ‘with the arm me covered’ → “did cover me with her arm”: the simple past is expanded with “did”, the object follows the verb, and the instrumental phrase moves to the end with a possessive in place of the article.

> 106 “Noi siam qui ninfe, e nel ciel siamo stelle;  

“Here we are nymphs and in heaven are stars;

- *Noi siam qui ninfe* ‘we are here nymphs’ → “Here we are nymphs”: the place adverb moves before the subject.

> 107 pria che Beatrice discendesse al mondo,  
> 108 fummo ordinate a lei per sue ancelle.  

ere Beatrice descended to the world we were ordained to her for her handmaids.

- *pria che Beatrice discendesse* ‘before Beatrice should descend’ → “ere Beatrice descended”: the temporal conjunction takes a simple past in English rather than a subjunctive.

> 109 Merrenti agli occhi suoi; ma nel giocondo  
> 110 lume ch' è dentro aguzzeranno i tuoi  
> 111 le tre di là, che miran più profondo.”  

We will lead thee to her eyes; but the three on the other side who deeper gaze, will sharpen thine eyes to the joyous light that is within.”

- *Merrenti* ‘we shall lead thee’ → “We will lead thee”: the inflected future becomes an auxiliary and infinitive, with the attached object a separate pronoun.
- *nel giocondo lume ch' è dentro* ‘in the joyous light that is within’ → “to the joyous light that is within”: the phrase moves from before the verb to the end, and “to” expresses the direction of vision rather than location.
- *aguzzeranno i tuoi* ‘will sharpen thine’ → “will sharpen thine eyes”: the verb and object move after the delayed subject and its relative clause; “eyes” is supplied for the noun implicit in the possessive.
- *le tre di là* ‘the three over there’ → “the three on the other side”: the subject moves before the verb, and the indication of place becomes more specific.
- *che miran più profondo* ‘who gaze more deeply’ → “who deeper gaze”: the relative clause moves with its antecedent, and the comparative adverb precedes the verb.

## Lines 112–145 — Beatrice’s Eyes and Unveiling

> 112 Così cantando cominciaro; e poi  
> 113 al petto del grifon seco menarmi,  
> 114 ove Beatrice volta stava a noi.  

Thus singing they began; and then did lead me with them up to the breast of the grifon, where Beatrice stood turned towards us.

- *al petto del grifon seco menarmi* ‘to the breast of the grifon with them they led me’ → “did lead me with them up to the breast of the grifon”: the simple past becomes a past with auxiliary “did”; the verb and its object come first, followed by the companions and then the destination. “up” is supplied.
- *volta stava* ‘turned stood’ → “stood turned”: the participle follows the verb.

> 115 Disser: “Fa che le viste non risparmi;  
> 116 posto t' avem dinanzi agli smeraldi,  
> 117 ond' Amor già ti trasse le sue armi.”  

They said: “Look that thou spare not thine eyes; we have placed thee before the emeralds whence Love once drew his shafts at thee.”

- *Fa che le viste non risparmi* ‘See to it that thy powers of sight thou spare not’ → “Look that thou spare not thine eyes”: the injunction takes an English idiomatic form; the object moves after the verb, and the faculties of sight become the eyes themselves, with a possessive in place of the definite article.
- *posto t’ avem* ‘placed thee we have’ → “we have placed thee”: the auxiliary precedes the participle, and the object follows it.
- *ti trasse le sue armi* ‘at thee drew his weapons’ → “drew his shafts at thee”: the pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase after the direct object, and the general term for weapons is narrowed to shafts.

> 118 Mille disiri più che fiamma caldi  
> 119 strinsermi gli occhi agli occhi rilucenti,  
> 120 che pur sopra il grifone stavan saldi.  

A thousand desires hotter than flame held mine eyes bound to the shining eyes, which remained ever fixed upon the grifon.

- *Mille* ‘thousand’ → “A thousand”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *più che fiamma caldi* ‘more than flame hot’ → “hotter than flame”: the separate mark of comparison becomes an adjective ending, and the adjective precedes the term of comparison.
- *strinsermi gli occhi* ‘bound for me the eyes’ → “held mine eyes bound”: the single verb becomes a verb with a participle expressing the resulting state; the personal pronoun becomes a possessive.
- *agli occhi rilucenti* ‘to the eyes shining’ → “to the shining eyes”: the adjective precedes its noun.
- *pur sopra il grifone stavan saldi* ‘ever upon the grifon stood firm’ → “remained ever fixed upon the grifon”: standing firm becomes remaining fixed; the adverb follows the verb, and the prepositional phrase comes last.

> 121 Come in lo specchio il sol, non altrimenti  
> 122 la doppia fiera dentro vi raggiava,  
> 123 or con uni, or con altri reggimenti.  

As the sun in a mirror, not otherwise the twofold beast was beaming within them, now with the attributes of one, now of the other nature.

- *in lo specchio il sol* ‘in the mirror the sun’ → “the sun in a mirror”: the sun comes first, and the mirror takes an indefinite article.
- *dentro vi raggiava* ‘within there it shone’ → “was beaming within them”: the imperfect becomes a past progressive; the locative becomes an explicit plural pronoun after “within”, and the phrase follows the verb.
- *con uni, or con altri reggimenti* ‘with one set, now with other modes of bearing’ → “with the attributes of one, now of the other nature”: the plural modifiers become pronouns dependent on “attributes”; the shared noun moves into the first member, and “nature” is supplied to identify what has the attributes.

> 124 Pensa, lettor, s' io mi maravigliava  
> 125 quando vedea la cosa in sè star queta,  
> 126 e nell' idolo suo si trasmutava.  

Think, reader, if I marvelled within me when I saw the thing itself remain motionless, and in its image it was changing.

- *s’ io mi maravigliava* ‘if I marvelled’ → “if I marvelled within me”: the Italian verb is pronominal; English gives the inward reference a separate phrase.
- *vedea* ‘was seeing’ → “saw”: the imperfect is rendered by the simple past.
- *la cosa in sè star queta* ‘the thing in itself stay still’ → “the thing itself remain motionless”: the prepositional phrase becomes an intensive pronoun attached to the noun; “motionless” makes the sense of stillness explicit.
- *nell’ idolo suo* ‘in the image of it’ → “in its image”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *si trasmutava* ‘transmuted itself’ → “it was changing”: the reflexive imperfect becomes an intransitive past progressive.

> 127 Mentre che, piena di stupore e lieta,  
> 128 l' anima mia gustava di quel cibo,  
> 129 che, saziando di sè, di sè asseta;  

While my soul, filled with wonderment and glad, was tasting of that food which, satisfying of itself, causes thirst of itself,

- *piena di stupore e lieta, l’ anima mia* ‘full of wonder and glad, the soul of mine’ → “my soul, filled with wonderment and glad”: the subject moves ahead of its description, with its possessive before the noun; the adjective ‘full’ becomes the participle “filled”.
- *gustava di quel cibo* ‘tasted of that food’ → “was tasting of that food”: the imperfect becomes a past progressive.
- *di sè asseta* ‘for itself makes thirsty’ → “causes thirst of itself”: the causative verb is unpacked into verb and noun, and the phrase naming the object of thirst follows them.

> 130 sè dimostrando di più alto tribo  
> 131 negli atti, l' altre tre si fero avanti,  
> 132 danzando al loro angelico caribo.  

the other three, showing them to be of the chiefest order in their bearing, drew forward, dancing to their angelic roundelay.

- *sè dimostrando di più alto tribo negli atti, l’ altre tre* ‘themselves showing of higher rank in their acts, the other three’ → “the other three, showing them to be of the chiefest order in their bearing”: the subject comes first; the fronted reflexive becomes “them” after the participle, with “to be” supplied. The comparative becomes a superlative with a definite article, and the plural acts become a collective “bearing” with a possessive.
- *si fero avanti* ‘made themselves forward’ → “drew forward”: an English verb of motion replaces the reflexive idiom.
- *angelico caribo* ‘angelic dance-song’ → “angelic roundelay”: the English names a particular kind of song.

> 133 “Volgi, Beatrice, volgi gli occhi santi,”  
> 134 era la lor canzone, “al tuo fedele  
> 135 che, per vederti, ha mossi passi tanti.  

“Turn, Beatrice, turn thy holy eyes,” was their song, “to thy faithful one, who to see thee hath moved so many steps.

- *gli occhi santi* ‘the eyes holy’ → “thy holy eyes”: the article becomes a possessive, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *al tuo fedele* ‘to thy faithful’ → “to thy faithful one”: “one” gives the substantivized adjective an English head noun.
- *passi tanti* ‘steps so many’ → “so many steps”: the quantifier precedes the noun.

> 136 Per grazia fa noi grazia che disvele  
> 137 a lui la bocca tua, sì che discerna  
> 138 la seconda bellezza che tu cele.”  

Of thy grace do us the grace that thou unveil thy mouth to him, that he may discern the second beauty which thou hidest.”

- *Per grazia* ‘by grace’ → “Of thy grace”: the preposition changes and the possessor is made explicit.
- *fa noi grazia* ‘do us grace’ → “do us the grace”: the noun takes a definite article.
- *disvele a lui la bocca tua* ‘unveil to him the mouth of thine’ → “thou unveil thy mouth to him”: the direct object precedes the recipient, with the possessive before its noun.
- *sì che discerna* ‘so that he discern’ → “that he may discern”: the purpose is expressed without a separate equivalent for ‘so’, and the subjunctive is rendered with “may”.

> 139 O isplendor di viva luce eterna,  

O glory of living light eternal,

- *isplendor* ‘splendour’ → “glory”: a less specifically visual noun replaces the term for brilliance.

> 140 chi pallido si fece sotto l' ombra  
> 141 sì di Parnaso, o bevve in sua cisterna,  

who that so pale hath grown beneath the shade of Parnassus, or hath drunk at its well,

- *chi pallido si fece … sì di Parnaso* ‘who pale became … so, of Parnassus’ → “who that so pale hath grown beneath the shade of Parnassus”: “that” introduces a relative clause, leaving “who” for the later main verb. The delayed degree word moves before “pale”, reuniting the shade with its modifier; the simple past of the reflexive construction becomes the present perfect of ‘grow’.
- *bevve in sua cisterna* ‘drank in its cistern’ → “hath drunk at its well”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, the preposition changes, and a well replaces a cistern.

> 142 che non paresse aver la mente ingombra,  
> 143 tentando a render te qual tu paresti  
> 144 là dove armonizzando il ciel t' adombra,  
> 145 quando nell' aere aperto ti solvesti?  

that would not seem to have mind encumbered, on trying to render thee as thou appearedest, when in the free air thou didst disclose thee, where heaven in its harmony shadows thee forth?

- *che non paresse* ‘that he should not seem’ → “that would not seem”: the imperfect subjunctive becomes a conditional with “would” in the recast question.
- *la mente* ‘the mind’ → “mind”: the definite article drops out.
- *tentando* ‘trying’ → “on trying”: the gerund takes a preposition.
- *là dove armonizzando il ciel t’ adombra* ‘there where harmonizing heaven shadows thee’ → “where heaven in its harmony shadows thee forth”: the place clause moves after the time clause, and ‘there’ drops out. The subject precedes the expression of manner; the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase with a possessive, the object follows the verb, and “forth” gives the verb the sense of figuring or representing.
- *nell’ aere aperto* ‘in the air open’ → “in the free air”: the adjective precedes its noun, and ‘open’ becomes “free”.
- *ti solvesti* ‘thyself thou didst unbind’ → “thou didst disclose thee”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb, the simple past takes auxiliary “didst”, and unbinding is interpreted as disclosure.

## Summary — Italian Order and English Idiom

Okey often keeps the sequence of clauses while rearranging the words within them. In *l’asta il segno tocca*, the object precedes the verb; in “the bolt hits the mark” it follows. A subject delayed until the end of a clause may move to the front, as “The nettle of repentance” does. Yet Okey does not invariably smooth out an inversion: “her speech directing” keeps an unusual order in English, and “so burst I” follows *sì scoppia’ io* closely. He also preserves emphatic repetitions such as *di’, di’* and “say, say”.

Changes of construction often let English express the same relation by different means. The impersonal *conviene* gives way to the personal modal in “thy confession must be joined”; the pronominal *si nascose* becomes the passive “was hidden”. Okey sometimes marks the continuing action of an imperfect, as when *gustava* becomes “was tasting”; elsewhere, as with *menavano* and “led”, he leaves duration unmarked. Two routine adjustments generally pass unremarked in the comparison: English supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending alone, and omits the Italian article before a possessive, as in *la mia virtù* and “My virtue”.

Okey’s choice of words can narrow or broaden the Italian: *armi* ‘weapons’ becomes “shafts”, while *cerro* loses its botanical specificity in “oak”. Additions often draw out what the Italian leaves implicit: “a torrent of” conveys the force of *sgorgando*, and “thine eyes” supplies the noun understood in *i tuoi*. Conversely, the reinforcing *esso* in *sopr’ esso l’acqua* has no separate equivalent in “over the water”. Even where the phrasing changes, Okey often retains the image itself: *il seme del piangere* remains “the seed of weeping”, and *il velen dell’argomento* is reproduced almost word for word as “the venom of the argument”.
