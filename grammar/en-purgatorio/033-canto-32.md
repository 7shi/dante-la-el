# Purgatorio 32

## Lines 1–15 — A Gaze Too Fixed

> 1 Tanto eran gli occhi miei fissi ed attenti  
> 2 a disbramarsi la decenne sete,  
> 3 che gli altri sensi m'eran tutti spenti;  

So fixed and intent were my eyes on satisfying their ten years’ thirst, that all my other senses were quenched;

- *Tanto eran gli occhi miei fissi ed attenti* ‘so much were the eyes of mine fixed and intent’ → “So fixed and intent were my eyes”: the adjectives move forward to join the adverb of degree, and the possessive comes before the noun.
- *a disbramarsi* ‘to sate themselves’ → “on satisfying”: the infinitive of purpose becomes an *-ing* form governed by “on”. The reflexive is not rendered as an object; the sense that the thirst is the eyes’ own is carried by the following possessive.
- *la decenne sete* ‘the ten-year thirst’ → “their ten years’ thirst”: a possessive replaces the article, and an adjective of duration becomes a genitive of measure.
- *gli altri sensi m'eran tutti spenti* ‘the other senses were to me all extinguished’ → “all my other senses were quenched”: the dative of the person affected becomes a possessive, “all” moves to the head of the noun phrase, and “quenched” renders the participle.

> 4 ed essi quinci e quindi avean parete  
> 5 di non caler, così lo santo riso  
> 6 a sè traeali con l' antica rete;  

and they on either side had a wall of unconcern, so the holy smile drew them to itself in the toils of old;

- *quinci e quindi* ‘on this side and on that’ → “on either side”: one distributive phrase replaces the paired expressions.
- *parete* ‘wall’ → “a wall”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *di non caler* ‘of not caring’ → “of unconcern”: a noun replaces the negated infinitive.
- *lo santo riso a sè traeali* ‘the holy smile to itself drew them’ → “the holy smile drew them to itself”: the phrase of direction moves after the verb and its object.
- *con l' antica rete* ‘with the old net’ → “in the toils of old”: “in” replaces the instrumental preposition, the singular net becomes plural “toils”, and the adjective becomes the following phrase “of old”.

> 7 quando per forza mi fu volto il viso  
> 8 ver la sinistra mia da quelle Dee,  
> 9 perch' io udia da loro un “Troppo fiso.”  

when perforce my face was turned toward my left by those goddesses, because I heard from them a: “Too fixedly.”

- *per forza* ‘by force’ → “perforce”: an adverb replaces the prepositional phrase.
- *mi fu volto il viso* ‘to me was turned the face’ → “my face was turned”: the dative becomes a possessive, and the subject moves before the passive verb.
- *la sinistra mia* ‘the left of mine’ → “my left”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *udia* ‘was hearing’ → “heard”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past.
- *Troppo fiso* ‘too fixed’ → “Too fixedly”: the adjectival form, used adverbially, becomes an explicit adverb of manner.

> 10 E la disposizion, ch' a veder ee  
> 11 negli occhi pur testè dal sol percossi,  
> 12 senza la vista alquanto esser mi fee;  

And that condition of the sight, which is in eyes but just smitten by the sun, made me remain a while without vision;

- *la disposizion* ‘the condition’ → “that condition”: a demonstrative replaces the definite article.
- *ch' a veder ee* ‘which, as regards seeing, is’ → “of the sight, which is”: the qualification within the relative clause becomes a phrase modifying “condition”, and the infinitive becomes a noun.
- *negli occhi* ‘in the eyes’ → “in eyes”: the definite article drops out in the generic English plural.
- *senza la vista alquanto esser mi fee* ‘without sight for a while to be me made’ → “made me remain a while without vision”: the causative verb and its object move to the front; ‘to be’ becomes the more durative bare infinitive “remain”. The time phrase now precedes “without vision”, and the article before the abstract noun drops out.

> 13 ma poi che al poco il viso riformossi  
> 14 (io dico al poco, per rispetto al molto  
> 15 sensibile, onde a forza mi rimossi),  

but after my sight re-formed itself to the lesser (I mean the lesser in respect to the greater object of sense wherefrom perforce I turned me away)

- *poi che* ‘after that’ → “after”: English uses the single word as a conjunction.
- *al poco il viso riformossi* ‘to the little the sight re-formed itself’ → “my sight re-formed itself to the lesser”: the phrase of adjustment moves to the end, the article before ‘sight’ becomes a possessive, and the comparative “lesser” makes explicit the contrast with what follows.
- *io dico al poco* ‘I say to the little’ → “I mean the lesser”: “mean” replaces the verb of saying; the preposition is not repeated, and the comparative is again explicit.
- *al molto sensibile* ‘to the much that is perceptible’ → “to the greater object of sense”: the comparative replaces the absolute expression of degree, and “object of sense” unpacks the substantival *sensibile*.
- *a forza* ‘by force’ → “perforce”: an adverb replaces the prepositional phrase.
- *mi rimossi* ‘myself I removed’ → “I turned me away”: the reflexive object follows the verb, and removal is expressed as turning away.

## Lines 16–48 — The Procession Turns and Surrounds the Tree

> 16 vidi in sul braccio destro esser rivolto  
> 17 lo glorioso esercito, e tornarsi  
> 18 col sole e con le sette fiamme al volto.  

I saw the glorious host had wheeled upon the right flank, and was returning with the sun and with the seven flames in its face.

- *in sul braccio destro esser rivolto lo glorioso esercito* ‘upon the right arm to be turned the glorious host’ → “the glorious host had wheeled upon the right flank”: the host comes first as the subject of a finite clause; the passive infinitive becomes an active past perfect, and the phrase giving the direction moves to the end. “flank” gives the military sense of ‘arm’, and “right” comes before its noun.
- *tornarsi* ‘to turn back’ → “was returning”: the second infinitive becomes a finite verb in the past progressive, without the reflexive pronoun.
- *al volto* ‘at the face’ → “in its face”: the preposition changes, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 19 Come sotto gli scudi per salvarsi  
> 20 volgesi schiera, e sè gira col segno  
> 21 prima che possa tutta in sè mutarsi:  

As under its shields a troop turns about to retreat, and wheels round with the standard ere it can wholly change front,

- *sotto gli scudi* ‘under the shields’ → “under its shields”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *per salvarsi* ‘to save itself’ → “to retreat”: the purpose is expressed by the manoeuvre rather than by its intended effect, and the phrase moves after the troop’s turning.
- *volgesi schiera* ‘turns itself a troop’ → “a troop turns about”: the subject comes before the verb, and the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb with an adverb.
- *sè gira* ‘itself it wheels’ → “wheels round”: an adverb of direction replaces the reflexive object.
- *col segno* ‘with the sign’ → “with the standard”: the noun is given its specifically military sense.
- *prima che possa tutta in sè mutarsi* ‘before it can, all of it, change itself about’ → “ere it can wholly change front”: the subjunctive after the Italian conjunction has no distinct English marking; the adjective agreeing with the troop becomes the adverb “wholly”, and “change front” gives the military sense of the reflexive expression.

> 22 quella milizia del celeste regno,  
> 23 che precedeva, tutta trapassonne  
> 24 pria che piegasse il carro il primo legno.  

that soldiery of the heavenly realm, which was in the van, passed all by us ere the car turned its pole.

- *celeste regno* ‘heavenly realm’ → “the heavenly realm”: English gives the realm its own definite article where the Italian article is already contained in *del*; the adjective and noun retain their order.
- *che precedeva* ‘which went before’ → “which was in the van”: a verb of movement becomes a verb of position with a military phrase.
- *tutta trapassonne* ‘all of it passed us by’ → “passed all by us”: the adjective referring to the soldiery moves after the verb, and the attached pronoun becomes the separate “us” after “by”.
- *pria che piegasse il carro il primo legno* ‘before the car should bend the foremost timber’ → “ere the car turned its pole”: the subject moves before the verb, the Italian subjunctive becomes a simple past, and the descriptive ‘foremost timber’ becomes the more specific “pole”, with a possessive in place of the article.

> 25 Indi alle rote si tornar le donne,  
> 26 e il grifon mosse il benedetto carco,  
> 27 sì che però nulla penna crollonne.  

Then to the wheels the ladies returned, and the grifon moved the hallowed burden, so that thereby no plume of it was ruffled.

- *si tornar le donne* ‘turned themselves back the ladies’ → “the ladies returned”: the subject precedes the verb, and an intransitive verb renders the reflexive.
- *il benedetto carco* ‘the blessed load’ → “the hallowed burden”: “hallowed” gives the sense of something made holy, and “burden” renders the thing borne.
- *nulla penna crollonne* ‘no feather of it stirred’ → “no plume of it was ruffled”: the attached ‘of it’ moves next to the noun, and an intransitive verb of movement becomes a passive describing disturbance of the plumage.

> 28 La bella donna che mi trasse al varco,  
> 29 e Stazio ed io seguitavam la rota  
> 30 che fe' l' orbita sua con minore arco.  

The fair lady who drew me across the ford, and Statius, and I, were following the wheel which made its orbit with the lesser arc.

- *mi trasse al varco* ‘me drew to the crossing’ → “drew me across the ford”: the object moves after the verb; English expresses passage across rather than motion to, and specifies the crossing as a ford.
- *seguitavam* ‘we followed’ → “were following”: the past progressive brings out the continuing action of the imperfect.
- *l' orbita sua* ‘the track of its own’ → “its orbit”: “orbit” renders the wheel’s track, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *con minore arco* ‘with smaller arc’ → “with the lesser arc”: English supplies the definite article.

> 31 Sì passeggiando l' alta selva, vota  
> 32 colpa di quella ch' al serpente crese,  
> 33 temprava i passi un' angelica nota.  

So pacing the lofty forest, empty through the fault of her who gave credence to the serpent, a melody of angels gave measure to our steps.

- *selva, vota colpa di quella* ‘forest, empty by fault of that woman’ → “forest, empty through the fault of her”: English makes the causal relation explicit with “through”, gives “fault” an article, and uses a personal pronoun for the demonstrative.
- *ch' al serpente crese* ‘who the serpent believed’ → “who gave credence to the serpent”: the verb becomes a verb-and-noun phrase, and the serpent, placed before the Italian verb, follows that phrase.
- *temprava i passi un' angelica nota* ‘regulated the steps an angelic note’ → “a melody of angels gave measure to our steps”: the subject moves to the front; the adjective becomes an “of” phrase, and the musical sound is rendered as a melody. The single verb becomes “gave measure to”, and a possessive replaces the article before ‘steps’.

> 34 Forse in tre voli tanto spazio prese  
> 35 disfrenata saetta, quanto eramo  
> 36 rimossi quando Beatrice scese.  

Haply in three flights so much space an arrow shot forth had covered, as we had advanced when Beatrice descended.

- *prese disfrenata saetta* ‘covered an unbridled arrow’ → “an arrow shot forth had covered”: the subject moves before the verb, the metaphorical adjective becomes a participial phrase after the noun, and the simple past becomes a past perfect.
- *quanto eramo rimossi* ‘as we were removed’ → “as we had advanced”: a description of the distance attained becomes an active past perfect of movement.

> 37 Io sentii mormorare a tutti: “Adamo”;  

I heard all murmur “Adam!”

- *mormorare a tutti* ‘murmur from all’ → “all murmur”: English makes “all” the understood subject of the bare infinitive after “heard”, placing it before the verb and dispensing with the preposition.

> 38 poi cerchiaro una pianta dispogliata  
> 39 di fiori e d' altra fronda in ciascun ramo.  

Then did they surround a tree despoiled of flowers, and of other foliage, in every bough.

- *cerchiaro* ‘they encircled’ → “did they surround”: the simple past is expressed with an auxiliary, inverted with the subject after “Then”.
- *d' altra fronda* ‘of other leaf’ → “of other foliage”: a singular noun for a leaf is rendered by a collective noun.

> 40 La coma sua, che tanto si dilata  
> 41 più quanto più è su, fora dagl' Indi  
> 42 nei boschi lor per altezza ammirata.  

Its crown of foliage, which more expands the loftier it is, would be marvelled at for its height by Indians in their woods.

- *La coma sua* ‘the crown its own’ → “Its crown of foliage”: the possessive moves before the noun, and “of foliage” spells out the arboreal sense of the figurative ‘crown of hair’.
- *tanto si dilata più quanto più è su* ‘so much more does it widen itself as it is farther up’ → “more expands the loftier it is”: the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb; the paired expressions of degree are compressed, and the adverb of position with its comparative becomes the comparative adjective “loftier”.
- *fora dagl' Indi nei boschi lor per altezza ammirata* ‘would be by the Indians in the woods of theirs for height admired’ → “would be marvelled at for its height by Indians in their woods”: the participle moves next to the auxiliary and becomes a verb with “at”; the phrase giving the reason moves before the agent and gains a possessive. The article before the people’s name drops out, and the possessive before “woods” precedes rather than follows its noun.

> 43 “Beato sei, grifon, che non discindi  
> 44 col becco d' esto legno dolce al gusto,  
> 45 poscia che mal si torce il ventre quindi.”  

“Blessed art thou, grifon, that with thy beak dost rend naught from this tree sweet to taste, since ill writhes the belly therefrom.”

- *non discindi col becco d' esto legno* ‘dost not tear off with the beak from this wood’ → “with thy beak dost rend naught from this tree”: the instrument moves before the verb and takes a possessive instead of the article; the negative becomes the object “naught”, and ‘wood’ is rendered as the living “tree”.
- *al gusto* ‘to the taste’ → “to taste”: the preposition and noun give way to an infinitive.
- *si torce* ‘twists itself’ → “writhes”: a single intransitive verb conveys the reflexive movement.

> 46 Così d' intorno all' arbore robusto  
> 47 gridaron gli altri; e l' animal binato:  
> 48 “Sì si conserva il seme d' ogni giusto.”  

Thus round about the sturdy tree the others cried; and the beast of two natures: “Thus is preserved the seed of all righteousness.”

- *arbore robusto* ‘tree sturdy’ → “sturdy tree”: the adjective precedes its noun.
- *gridaron gli altri* ‘cried the others’ → “the others cried”: the subject precedes the verb.
- *l' animal binato* ‘the twofold beast’ → “the beast of two natures”: an adjective becomes a phrase specifying in what sense the beast is twofold.
- *si conserva* ‘keeps itself’ → “is preserved”: the passive sense of the Italian pronominal construction is expressed with a passive auxiliary.
- *d' ogni giusto* ‘of every just one’ → “of all righteousness”: the translation takes the substantivized adjective abstractly rather than personally; “all” with a mass noun replaces ‘every’ with a countable singular.

## Lines 49–81 — The Tree Renews Itself and Dante Awakens

> 49 E volto al temo ch' egli avea tirato,  
> 50 trasselo al piè della vedova frasca;  

And having turned to the pole which he had drawn, he dragged it to the foot of the widowed bough;

- *volto* ‘turned’ → “having turned”: the participle becomes a perfect participial phrase.
- *trasselo* ‘drew he it’ → “he dragged it”: the object follows the verb in both languages, but the English chooses a more forceful verb rather than repeating the verb of drawing used just before.

> 51 e quel di lei a lei lasciò legato.  

and to it left bound that which came from it.

- *quel di lei* ‘that of her’ → “that which came from it”: the phrase of origin becomes a relative clause with an explicit verb; the object moves from before the verb to the end.
- *a lei* ‘to her’ → “to it”: English uses a neuter pronoun for the grammatically feminine antecedent.

> 52 Come le nostre piante, quando casca  
> 53 giù la gran luce mischiata con quella  
> 54 che raggia retro alla celeste lasca,  

As trees of our land when the great light falls down mingled with that which beams behind the celestial carp,

- *le nostre piante* ‘our plants’ → “trees of our land”: the noun is narrowed, and the possessive becomes a phrase of place with “land” supplied.
- *casca giù la gran luce* ‘falls down the great light’ → “the great light falls down”: the subject comes before the verb.
- *alla celeste lasca* ‘to the celestial carp’ → “the celestial carp”: English “behind” takes its object directly, without the preposition that follows *retro*.

> 55 turgide fansi, e poi si rinnovella  
> 56 di suo color ciascuna, pria che il sole  
> 57 giunga li suoi corsier sott' altra stella:  

burgeon forth, and each then is decked anew with its colour ere the sun yokes his steeds beneath another constellation,

- *turgide fansi* ‘swollen they make themselves’ → “burgeon forth”: the adjective and reflexive verb become an intransitive verb with an adverb.
- *poi si rinnovella di suo color ciascuna* ‘then renews itself in its colour each one’ → “each then is decked anew with its colour”: the subject moves from last to first; reflexive renewal becomes passive adornment, with the sense of renewal carried by “anew”.
- *pria che il sole giunga* ‘before the sun should yoke’ → “ere the sun yokes”: the Italian subjunctive after the temporal conjunction becomes an English indicative.
- *sott' altra stella* ‘under another star’ → “beneath another constellation”: the singular star is interpreted as a constellation.

> 58 men che di rose e più che di viole  
> 59 colore aprendo, s' innovò la pianta,  
> 60 che prima avea le ramora sì sole.  

opening out into a hue, less than of roses and more than of violets, the tree renewed itself, which before had its boughs so naked.

- *men che di rose e più che di viole colore aprendo* ‘less than of roses and more than of violets a colour opening’ → “opening out into a hue, less than of roses and more than of violets”: the participle moves to the front, its direct object becomes the complement of “into”, and the comparison follows the noun it qualifies.
- *s' innovò la pianta* ‘renewed itself the plant’ → “the tree renewed itself”: the subject precedes the reflexive verb, and the more general noun is narrowed.
- *le ramora sì sole* ‘the boughs so solitary’ → “its boughs so naked”: the article becomes a possessive, and the adjective is interpreted as describing bare boughs.

> 61 Io non lo intesi, e qui non si canta  
> 62 l' inno che quella gente allor cantaro,  

I understood it not, nor here is sung, the hymn which then that people sang,

- *Io non lo intesi* ‘I not it understood’ → “I understood it not”: the verb comes before the object and the negative follows both.
- *e* ‘and’ → “nor”: the negative coordination is expressed in the conjunction itself.
- *si canta* ‘one sings’ → “is sung”: the Italian *si* construction becomes an English passive.
- *quella gente allor cantaro* ‘that people then sang’ → “then that people sang”: the adverb moves before the subject; the Italian verb is explicitly plural with a singular collective noun, a distinction not marked in the English past tense.

> 63 nè la nota soffersi tutta quanta.  

nor did I endure its melody outright.

- *la nota soffersi tutta quanta* ‘the tune I endured all of it’ → “did I endure its melody outright”: after “nor” English uses auxiliary inversion and puts the object after the verb; the article becomes a possessive, and the expression of entirety agreeing with the noun becomes an adverb.

> 64 S' io potessi ritrar come assonnaro  
> 65 gli occhi spietati, udendo di Siringa,  
> 66 gli occhi a cui più vegghiar costò sì caro:  

If I could pourtray how the pitiless eyes did slumber hearing of Syrinx, the eyes whose longer vigil cost so dear,

- *assonnaro gli occhi spietati* ‘fell asleep the eyes pitiless’ → “the pitiless eyes did slumber”: the subject precedes the verb and the adjective precedes its noun; falling asleep becomes slumbering, with auxiliary “did”.
- *gli occhi a cui più vegghiar costò sì caro* ‘the eyes to which keeping watch more cost so dear’ → “the eyes whose longer vigil cost so dear”: the relative denoting those to whom the cost fell becomes possessive; the infinitive becomes a noun, and the comparison becomes an adjective specifying duration.

> 67 come pittor che con esemplo pinga,  
> 68 disegnerei com' io m' addormentai;  
> 69 ma qual vuol sia che l' assonnar ben finga.  

as a painter who paints from a model, I would depict how I fell asleep; be he who he may that can rightly image drowsiness.

- *come pittor* ‘as painter’ → “as a painter”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *con esemplo pinga* ‘with a model may paint’ → “paints from a model”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase and becomes indicative; the model is something painted from rather than with, and English supplies an article.
- *disegnerei* ‘I would draw’ → “I would depict”: the English verb is less specific about the mode of representation.
- *m' addormentai* ‘I put myself to sleep’ → “I fell asleep”: the reflexive verb becomes an intransitive expression for the change of state.
- *ma qual vuol sia* ‘but whoever he will, let him be’ → “be he who he may”: the adversative is omitted, the jussive verb comes first, and willingness is recast in terms of possibility.
- *l' assonnar ben finga* ‘the falling asleep well may portray’ → “can rightly image drowsiness”: the subjunctive is interpreted in terms of ability, the verb moves before its object, and the nominalized infinitive denoting a process becomes an abstract noun denoting a state.

> 70 Però trascorro a quando mi svegliai,  
> 71 e dico ch' un splendor mi squarciò il velo  
> 72 del sonno, ed un chiamar: “Surgi, che fai?”  

Wherefore I pass on to when I awoke, and I say that a bright light rent the veil of my sleep, and a call: “Arise, what doest thou?”

- *mi svegliai* ‘I woke myself’ → “I awoke”: the Italian reflexive has an intransitive English equivalent.
- *un splendor* ‘a radiance’ → “a bright light”: the single noun is expanded into adjective and noun.
- *mi squarciò il velo del sonno* ‘rent for me the veil of sleep’ → “rent the veil of my sleep”: the dative pronoun before the verb is expressed as a possessive modifying “sleep”.
- *un chiamar* ‘a calling’ → “a call”: the nominalized infinitive becomes an ordinary noun.
- *che fai?* ‘what do you do?’ → “what doest thou?”: the English uses a finite main verb inverted with its subject rather than the Italian order of interrogative followed by verb.

> 73 Quale a veder dei fioretti del melo,  
> 74 che del suo pomo gli angeli fa ghiotti  
> 75 e perpetue nozze fa nel cielo,  

As to behold some flowerets of the apple tree, which makes the angels greedy for its fruit, and makes perpetual marriage feast in heaven,

- *Quale* ‘such as’ → “As”: the Italian comparative form is rendered by a conjunction alone.
- *a veder* ‘to see’ → “to behold”: the verb of sight is rendered by a more formal verb.
- *dei fioretti* ‘of the little flowers’ → “some flowerets”: the partitive article becomes an indefinite quantifier.
- *del suo pomo gli angeli fa ghiotti* ‘for its apple the angels it makes greedy’ → “makes the angels greedy for its fruit”: the verb moves before its object, the complement of the adjective moves to the end, and the specific fruit becomes generic.
- *perpetue nozze fa* ‘perpetual nuptials it makes’ → “makes perpetual marriage feast”: the verb moves before its object, and the plural noun becomes a singular phrase that specifies a feast.

> 76 Pietro e Giovanni e Jacopo condotti,  
> 77 e vinti ritornaro alla parola,  
> 78 dalla qual furon maggior sonni rotti,  

Peter and John and James were brought, and, being overcome, came to themselves at the word by which greater slumbers had been broken,

- *condotti* ‘brought’ → “were brought”: English makes the participle a finite passive by supplying the auxiliary.
- *vinti* ‘overcome’ → “being overcome”: English expands the participle with “being”.
- *ritornaro alla parola* ‘returned at the word’ → “came to themselves at the word”: the unexpressed destination of the return is made explicit in an idiom for recovery of consciousness.
- *furon maggior sonni rotti* ‘were greater slumbers broken’ → “greater slumbers had been broken”: the subject moves before the auxiliary and the simple past passive becomes past perfect.

> 79 e videro scemata loro scuola,  
> 80 così di Moisè come d' Elia,  
> 81 ed al maestro suo cangiata stola:  

and saw their band diminished by Moses, as well as by Elias, and their Master’s raiment changed,

- *scemata loro scuola* ‘diminished their school’ → “their band diminished”: the object comes before its participle, and the noun is interpreted as the company of persons.
- *così di Moisè come d' Elia* ‘as much of Moses as of Elias’ → “by Moses, as well as by Elias”: the balanced comparison becomes an additive phrase, and “by” replaces the preposition marking what the company has lost.
- *al maestro suo cangiata stola* ‘on their master changed robe’ → “their Master’s raiment changed”: the dative becomes a possessive, the possessive adjective moves before “Master”, and the participle moves after its noun; a single garment becomes the collective “raiment”.

## Lines 82–114 — Beatrice’s Command and the Eagle’s Descent

> 82 tal torna' io, e vidi quella pia  
> 83 sopra me starsi, che conducitrice  
> 84 fu de' miei passi lungo il fiume pria.  

even so I came to myself, and saw that pitying one bending o’er me, who before was guide to my steps along the stream.

- *tal torna' io* ‘thus returned I’ → “even so I came to myself”: “even” strengthens the comparison, the subject moves before the verb, and the translation makes explicit the sense of returning to consciousness.
- *quella pia* ‘that compassionate one’ → “that pitying one”: the adjective becomes a participle, with “one” to carry it.
- *sopra me starsi* ‘above me to stand’ → “bending o’er me”: the infinitive after the verb of seeing becomes a participle and moves before the prepositional phrase; bending is more specific than the Italian, which describes her position.
- *che conducitrice fu de' miei passi lungo il fiume pria* ‘who guide was of my steps along the stream before’ → “who before was guide to my steps along the stream”: “before” moves from the end to precede the verb, “guide” follows the verb, and ‘of my steps’ becomes “to my steps”. The English noun does not mark the guide as female.

> 85 E tutto in dubbio dissi: “Ov' è Beatrice?”  
> 86 ond' ella: “Vedi lei sotto la fronda  
> 87 nuova sedersi in su la sua radice.  

And all perplexed I said: “where is Beatrice!” and she: “Behold her sitting beneath the new foliage upon its root.

- *tutto in dubbio* ‘wholly in doubt’ → “all perplexed”: an adjective replaces the prepositional phrase.
- *ond' ella* ‘whereupon she’ → “and she”: simple coordination replaces the connective marking her reply as consequent on the question.
- *Vedi lei sotto la fronda nuova sedersi* ‘see her beneath the foliage new sit’ → “Behold her sitting beneath the new foliage”: the infinitive after the verb of seeing becomes a participle placed immediately after “her”, and “new” precedes its noun.
- *in su* ‘up on’ → “upon”: a single preposition renders the compound expression.

> 88 Vedi la compagnia che la circonda;  
> 89 gli altri dopo il grifon sen vanno suso,  
> 90 con più dolce canzone e più profonda.”  

Behold the company that encircleth her; the others are mounting up after the grifon with sweeter and profounder song.”

- *che la circonda* ‘that her encircles’ → “that encircleth her”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *dopo il grifon sen vanno suso* ‘after the grifon go their way up’ → “are mounting up after the grifon”: the verb of motion becomes a progressive verb of ascent, without a separate equivalent for the pronominal sense of going away; the phrase naming whom they follow moves to the end.
- *con più dolce canzone e più profonda* ‘with sweeter song and more profound’ → “with sweeter and profounder song”: the second comparative joins the first before the noun, and English uses comparative endings rather than separate words for ‘more’.

> 91 E se più fu lo suo parlar diffuso  
> 92 non so, però che già negli occhi m' era  
> 93 quella ch' ad altro intender m' avea chiuso.  

And if her words extended farther I know not, because now before mine eyes was she, who had shut me off from heeding aught else.

- *se più fu lo suo parlar diffuso* ‘if further was her speaking extended’ → “if her words extended farther”: the substantival infinitive becomes a plural noun; the subject comes first, followed by an intransitive verb in place of the participial construction, and the comparative moves to the end.
- *però che già negli occhi m' era* ‘because already in the eyes to me was’ → “because now before mine eyes was”: “now” renders ‘already’; the figure changes from ‘in’ to “before”, and the dative of the person becomes the possessive “mine”.
- *quella ch' ad altro intender m' avea chiuso* ‘that one who to heeding anything else had closed me’ → “she, who had shut me off from heeding aught else”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative; the verb precedes its object in English and takes “off from” rather than ‘to’, while the object of “heeding” follows it.

> 94 Sola sedeasi in su la terra vera,  
> 95 come guardia lasciata lì del plaustro,  
> 96 che legar vidi alla biforme fiera.  

Alone sat she upon the bare earth, left there as guardian of the chariot, which I had seen the beast of two forms make fast.

- *sedeasi* ‘sat herself’ → “sat she”: the pronominal form becomes a simple intransitive verb.
- *in su la terra vera* ‘upon the true earth’ → “upon the bare earth”: “bare” interprets the adjective as indicating the earth itself, without a covering, rather than translating it as ‘true’.
- *come guardia lasciata lì del plaustro* ‘as guard left there of the chariot’ → “left there as guardian of the chariot”: the participial phrase moves forward, bringing “guardian” next to its complement.
- *che legar vidi alla biforme fiera* ‘which tie I saw by the two-formed beast’ → “which I had seen the beast of two forms make fast”: the simple past becomes a pluperfect. The translation takes the beast as the agent of the tying, makes it the subject of the English bare infinitive, and moves that infinitive after it. “Make fast” renders ‘tie’, and a following phrase replaces the adjective ‘two-formed’.

> 97 In cerchio le facevan di sè claustro  
> 98 le sette ninfe, con quei lumi in mano  
> 99 che son sicuri d' Aquilone e d' Austro.  

The seven nymphs in a ring made of them a fence about her, with those lights in their hands which are secure from north wind and from south.

- *In cerchio le facevan di sè claustro le sette ninfe* ‘in a circle for her made of themselves an enclosure the seven nymphs’ → “The seven nymphs in a ring made of them a fence about her”: the delayed subject comes first; “of them” renders the reflexive, “fence” makes the enclosure more concrete, and the dative referring to her becomes the spatial phrase “about her”.
- *in mano* ‘in hand’ → “in their hands”: the singular becomes a possessive plural.
- *d' Aquilone e d' Austro* ‘from Aquilo and from Auster’ → “from north wind and from south”: the names of the winds become directional terms, with “wind” expressed only for the first.

> 100 “Qui sarai tu poco tempo silvano,  
> 101 e sarai meco, senza fine, cive  
> 102 di quella Roma onde Cristo è Romano.  

“Here shalt thou be short time a forester, and with me everlastingly shalt be a citizen of that Rome whereof Christ is a Roman.

- *sarai tu* ‘wilt be thou’ → “shalt thou be”: the explicit pronoun stands between auxiliary and infinitive in English rather than after the finite verb.
- *poco tempo silvano* ‘a little time woodland-dwelling’ → “short time a forester”: duration is expressed as shortness, and the predicate descriptive of the addressee becomes a noun with an indefinite article.
- *sarai meco, senza fine, cive* ‘shalt be with me, without end, citizen’ → “with me everlastingly shalt be a citizen”: both modifiers move before the verb, an adverb replaces the prepositional phrase, and the predicate noun takes an article.
- *è Romano* ‘is Roman’ → “is a Roman”: the English uses a noun with an article rather than a predicate of nationality without one.

> 103 Però, in pro del mondo che mal vive,  
> 104 al carro tieni or gli occhi, e quel che vedi,  
> 105 ritornato di là, fa che tu scrive.”  

Therefore to profit the world that liveth ill, fix now thine eyes upon the car, and look that thou write what thou seest, when returned yonder.”

- *in pro del mondo* ‘for the benefit of the world’ → “to profit the world”: a purpose infinitive with a direct object replaces the prepositional phrase.
- *al carro tieni or gli occhi* ‘upon the car keep now the eyes’ → “fix now thine eyes upon the car”: “fix” renders ‘keep’, the phrase naming the object of attention moves to the end, and a possessive replaces the article.
- *e quel che vedi, ritornato di là, fa che tu scrive* ‘and that which thou seest, returned yonder, see to it that thou write’ → “and look that thou write what thou seest, when returned yonder”: the instruction moves ahead of the object of writing, “what” combines demonstrative and relative, and “when” makes the temporal force of the participial phrase explicit.

> 106 Così Beatrice; ed io, che tutto ai piedi  
> 107 de' suoi comandamenti era devoto,  
> 108 la mente e gli occhi, ov' ella volle, diedi.  

Thus Beatrice; and I, who was all obedient at the feet of her commands, gave mind and eyes whither she willed.

- *che tutto ai piedi de' suoi comandamenti era devoto* ‘who wholly at the feet of her commands was devoted’ → “who was all obedient at the feet of her commands”: the verb and its predicate move forward, and “obedient” interprets devotion specifically as obedience.
- *la mente e gli occhi, ov' ella volle, diedi* ‘the mind and the eyes, where she willed, I gave’ → “gave mind and eyes whither she willed”: the final verb comes before its objects, which lose their articles, and “whither” makes the direction explicit.

> 109 Non scese mai con sì veloce moto  
> 110 foco di spessa nube, quando piove  
> 111 da quel confine che più va remoto,  

Ne’er did fire from dense cloud descend, with motion so swift, when it falls from that confine which is most remote,

- *Non scese mai con sì veloce moto foco di spessa nube* ‘never descended with so swift a motion fire from dense cloud’ → “Ne’er did fire from dense cloud descend, with motion so swift”: the two-part negative becomes a single initial adverb with auxiliary inversion. The subject and its source phrase precede “descend”; the phrase of manner follows it, with “so swift” now after “motion”.
- *quando piove* ‘when it rains down’ → “when it falls”: a general verb of descent replaces the rain metaphor.
- *che più va remoto* ‘which goes farthest away’ → “which is most remote”: a construction with a verb of motion becomes a description of position.

> 112 com' io vidi calar l' uccel di Giove  
> 113 per l' arbor giù, rompendo della scorza,  
> 114 non che dei fiori e delle foglie nuove;  

as I saw Jove’s bird swoop down through the tree, rending its bark, likewise its flowers and its new leaves;

- *com' io vidi calar l' uccel di Giove* ‘as I saw descend the bird of Jove’ → “as I saw Jove’s bird swoop down”: the bird precedes the infinitive in English, the possessive precedes “bird”, and “swoop” specifies a manner of descent.
- *per l' arbor giù* ‘through the tree down’ → “down through the tree”: the directional adverb moves before the path phrase.
- *rompendo della scorza* ‘breaking some of the bark’ → “rending its bark”: the stronger verb suggests tearing, and a possessive replaces the partitive.
- *non che dei fiori e delle foglie nuove* ‘not to mention some of the flowers and of the leaves new’ → “likewise its flowers and its new leaves”: simple addition replaces the emphatic connective, possessives replace the partitives, and “new” precedes “leaves”.

## Lines 115–147 — The Assaults and Transformation of the Car

> 115 e ferì il carro di tutta sua forza,  

and he smote the car with all his might;

- *ferì* ‘he struck’ → “he smote”: Okey uses a more elevated verb for the blow.
- *di tutta sua forza* ‘of all his strength’ → “with all his might”: English uses “with” for the force exerted, and “might” for strength.

> 116 ond' ei piegò come nave in fortuna,  
> 117 vinta dall' onde, or da poggia or da orza.  

whereat it reeled like a vessel in a storm, beaten by the waves, now to starboard, now to larboard.

- *ei piegò* ‘it bent’ → “it reeled”: the verb describes unsteady motion rather than the inclination itself.
- *come nave in fortuna* ‘like ship in storm’ → “like a vessel in a storm”: “vessel” is broader than ‘ship’, and English supplies two indefinite articles.
- *vinta dall' onde* ‘overcome by the waves’ → “beaten by the waves”: the English suggests the buffeting as well as its effect.
- *or da poggia or da orza* ‘now to leeward, now to windward’ → “now to starboard, now to larboard”: the English names the two sides of the vessel rather than distinguishing them in relation to the wind.

> 118 Poscia vidi avventarsi nella cuna  
> 119 del trionfal veiculo una volpe,  
> 120 che d' ogni pasto buon parea digiuna.  

Then saw I a she-fox, that seemed fasting from all good food, leap into the body of the triumphal vehicle.

- *vidi avventarsi* ‘I saw hurl itself’ → “saw I” … “leap”: English puts the subject after “saw” and postpones the other verb until after the animal and its relative clause; a nonreflexive verb of motion replaces the reflexive.
- *nella cuna del trionfal veiculo* ‘into the cradle of the triumphal vehicle’ → “into the body of the triumphal vehicle”: “body” names the part directly rather than likening it to a cradle.
- *una volpe* ‘a fox’ → “a she-fox”: English makes the Italian noun’s feminine gender explicit and brings the noun forward as the object of “saw”.
- *che d' ogni pasto buon parea digiuna* ‘that of every meal good seemed fasting’ → “that seemed fasting from all good food”: the verb and its predicate come before their complement; ‘of’ becomes “from”, and individual meals become the mass noun “food”, with its adjective before it.

> 121 Ma, riprendendo lei di laide colpe,  
> 122 la donna mia la volse in tanta futa,  
> 123 quanto sofferson l' ossa senza polpe.  

But, rebuking her for foul sins, my Lady put her to flight, as swift as the fleshless bones did bear.

- *di laide colpe* ‘of foul faults’ → “for foul sins”: English gives the grounds for rebuke with “for”, and “sins” is more specifically moral than ‘faults’.
- *la donna mia* ‘the lady mine’ → “my Lady”: the postposed possessive comes before the noun.
- *la volse in tanta futa, quanto* ‘turned her to so great a flight as’ → “put her to flight, as swift as”: the object follows the verb, ‘turned’ becomes “put”, and the degree expressed by the adjective of ‘flight’ becomes a comparison of speed.
- *sofferson l' ossa senza polpe* ‘permitted the bones without flesh’ → “the fleshless bones did bear”: the subject precedes the verb; the phrase with ‘without’ becomes a prefixed adjective, and the simple past becomes “did bear”, in the sense of what the bones could sustain.

> 124 Poscia, per indi ond' era pria venuta,  
> 125 l' aquila vidi scender giù nell' arca  
> 126 del carro, e lasciar lei di sè pennuta.  

Then, from thence whence he first had come, I saw the eagle descend down into the body of the car, and leave it feathered with his plumage.

- *per indi ond' era pria venuta* ‘by that way by which it had come before’ → “from thence whence he first had come”: the route becomes a point of origin, ‘before’ becomes “first” and precedes the auxiliary, and English uses a masculine pronoun where the Italian participle agrees with the feminine noun for the eagle.
- *l' aquila vidi* ‘the eagle I saw’ → “I saw the eagle”: the object moves after the verb.
- *nell' arca* ‘into the chest’ → “into the body”: as with the earlier ‘cradle’, the English names the part of the car directly.
- *lasciar lei di sè pennuta* ‘leave it of himself feathered’ → “leave it feathered with his plumage”: the adjective precedes its complement, and what comes from the eagle himself is specified as “his plumage”.

> 127 E qual esce di cor che si rammarca,  
> 128 tal voce uscì del cielo, e cotal disse:  
> 129 “O navicella mia, com' mal sei carca!”  

And as a voice comes from a heart that sorroweth, such voice came from heaven, and thus it spake: “O my little bark, how ill art thou laden!”

- *qual esce di cor* ‘as comes forth from a heart’ → “as a voice comes from a heart”: English anticipates the noun of the following clause, puts it before the verb, and renders the sense of coming out by “comes from”.
- *uscì del cielo* ‘came forth from the heaven’ → “came from heaven”: the outward motion is again left to “from”, and English drops the article.
- *cotal disse* ‘such [words] it said’ → “thus it spake”: the demonstrative becomes an adverb of manner, with an intransitive verb of speaking.
- *navicella mia* ‘little ship mine’ → “my little bark”: the possessive comes first, and “little bark” renders the diminutive with a separate adjective. The Italian gives no pronoun for the speaker to address here.
- *sei* ‘thou art’ → “art thou”: English inverts subject and verb in the exclamation.

> 130 Poi parve a me che la terra s' aprisse  
> 131 tr' ambo le rote, e vidi uscirne un drago,  
> 132 che per lo carro su la coda fisse;  

Then it seemed to me that the earth opened ’twixt the two wheels, and I saw a dragon come forth that fixed his tail up through the car;

- *parve a me* ‘seemed to me’ → “it seemed to me”: English supplies an impersonal anticipatory “it”.
- *s' aprisse* ‘should open itself’ → “opened”: a nonreflexive simple past replaces the reflexive imperfect subjunctive dependent on ‘seemed’.
- *tr' ambo le rote* ‘between both the wheels’ → “’twixt the two wheels”: the numeral with a definite article replaces ‘both’.
- *vidi uscirne un drago* ‘I saw come out of it a dragon’ → “I saw a dragon come forth”: the dragon precedes the infinitive, and “forth” gives the outward direction without an explicit equivalent for ‘of it’.
- *per lo carro su la coda fisse* ‘through the car upward the tail fixed’ → “fixed his tail up through the car”: the verb and object come first, ‘up’ precedes the path phrase, and a possessive replaces the article with the body part.

> 133 e, come vespa che ritragge l' ago,  
> 134 a sè traendo la coda maligna  
> 135 trasse del fondo e gissen vago vago.  

and like a wasp, that draws back her sting, drawing to him his spiteful tail he wrenched out part of the bottom and went his vagrant way.

- *come vespa* ‘like wasp’ → “like a wasp”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *l' ago* ‘the needle’ → “her sting”: the English names the wasp’s sting directly and replaces the article by a possessive.
- *a sè traendo* ‘to himself drawing’ → “drawing to him”: the complement follows the participle, and the pronoun is not reflexive in form.
- *la coda maligna* ‘the tail malicious’ → “his spiteful tail”: the article becomes a possessive, the adjective comes first, and “spiteful” renders ‘malicious’.
- *trasse del fondo* ‘drew out some of the bottom’ → “he wrenched out part of the bottom”: the verb of pulling is strengthened, and the partitive is expressed with the noun “part”.
- *gissen vago vago* ‘went away wandering, wandering’ → “went his vagrant way”: a phrase with “way” conveys the departure and the wandering without repeating the adjective.

> 136 Quel che rimase, come di gramigna  
> 137 vivace terra, della piuma, offerta  
> 138 forse con intenzion sana e benigna,  

That which remained,—even as teeming land with grass,—with those plumes, haply offered with sincere and kind intent,

- *come di gramigna vivace terra* ‘as with couch grass vigorous land’ → “even as teeming land with grass”: “even” reinforces the comparison; the land precedes the phrase describing its cover, “teeming” suggests fertility, and “grass” loses the specific kind.
- *della piuma* ‘with the plumage’ → “with those plumes”: the collective singular becomes a plural with a demonstrative.
- *offerta forse* ‘offered perhaps’ → “haply offered”: the adverb precedes the participle.
- *con intenzion sana e benigna* ‘with intention sound and kindly’ → “with sincere and kind intent”: the adjectives precede the noun, and soundness of purpose is interpreted as sincerity.

> 139 si ricoperse, e funne ricoperta  
> 140 e l' una e l' altra rota e il temo, in tanto  
> 141 che più tiene un sospir la bocca aperta.  

did again cover itself, and both wheels and the pole were covered again by them, in such time that a sigh keeps the mouth open longer.

- *si ricoperse* ‘it covered itself over’ → “did again cover itself”: Okey takes the prefix in the sense of repetition, uses the expanded past with “did”, and puts the reflexive after the verb.
- *e funne ricoperta e l' una e l' altra rota e il temo* ‘and was with it covered both the one and the other wheel and the pole’ → “and both wheels and the pole were covered again by them”: the subjects come first, the two singular expressions for the wheels collapse into a plural, and the verb agrees with the compound subject rather than the first singular member. “Again” gives the repeated action, and “by them” refers back to the plumes.
- *in tanto che* ‘in so long that’ → “in such time that”: English supplies the noun ‘time’ for the elliptical measure of duration.
- *più tiene un sospir la bocca aperta* ‘longer holds a sigh the mouth open’ → “a sigh keeps the mouth open longer”: the subject precedes the verb, ‘holds’ becomes “keeps”, and the comparative of duration moves to the end.

> 142 Trasformato così il dificio santo  
> 143 mise fuor teste per le parti sue,  
> 144 tre sopra il temo, ed una in ciascun canto.  

Thus transformed, the sacred edifice put forth heads above its parts, three over the pole, and one at each corner.

- *Trasformato così* ‘transformed thus’ → “Thus transformed”: the adverb moves before the participle.
- *il dificio santo* ‘the edifice sacred’ → “the sacred edifice”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *mise fuor* ‘put out’ → “put forth”: “forth” expresses the outward emergence of the heads.
- *per le parti sue* ‘over its several parts’ → “above its parts”: the distributive sense of the preposition becomes a relation of height, and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *in ciascun canto* ‘in each corner’ → “at each corner”: English uses a preposition of position at a point.

> 145 Le prime eran cornute come bue;  
> 146 ma le quattro un sol corno avean per fronte:  

The first were horned like an ox, but the four had one single horn at the forehead;

- *come bue* ‘like ox’ → “like an ox”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *un sol corno avean* ‘one single horn had’ → “had one single horn”: the object moves after the verb.
- *per fronte* ‘for each forehead’ → “at the forehead”: English gives the location with “at” and an article rather than expressing the distribution with ‘for’.

> 147 simile mostro visto ancor non fue.  

such monster never yet was seen.

- *simile mostro* ‘a like monster’ → “such monster”: “such” expresses the comparison without an article.
- *visto ancor non fue* ‘seen as yet was not’ → “never yet was seen”: “never yet” combines negation with time, and the participle follows the auxiliary.

## Lines 148–160 — The Harlot and the Giant

> 148 Sicura, quasi rocca in alto monte,  
> 149 seder sopr' esso una puttana sciolta

- *Sicura, quasi rocca in alto monte* ‘secure, as if a fortress on a high mountain’ → “secure as a fortress on a steep hill”: the comparison follows rather than precedes the words for sitting; *quasi* becomes the simpler “as”, height becomes steepness, and “hill” replaces ‘mountain’. English supplies the two indefinite articles.
- *seder sopr' esso* ‘to sit upon it’ → “Seated upon it”: the infinitive dependent on the verb of appearing becomes a participle at the head of the sentence.
- *una puttana sciolta* ‘a harlot unrestrained’ → “a shameless harlot”: the adjective is interpreted as a lack of shame and placed before the noun.
- *m' apparve* ‘to me appeared’ → “appeared to me”: the pronoun follows the verb in a prepositional phrase.
- *con le ciglia intorno pronte* ‘with the brows all around ready’ → “with eyes quick around”: the brows stand for the eyes; the article drops out, and ‘ready’ becomes “quick” and comes before the adverb.

- *come perchè non gli fosse tolta* ‘as if in order that from him she might not be taken’ → “as though she should not be taken from him”: the purpose expressed by *perchè* is left implicit in “as though”; the imperfect subjunctive passive is rendered with “should”, and “from him” follows the verb.
- *vidi di costa a lei dritto un gigante* ‘I saw at the side of her upright a giant’ → “a giant I saw erect at her side”: the object moves from last to first, “erect” precedes the phrase of position, and the Italian phrase with a preposition and pronoun becomes a possessive construction.
- *baciavansi insieme alcuna volta* ‘they were kissing one another together at times’ → “from time to time each kissed the other”: the indication of frequency moves to the front; the plural imperfect with a reciprocal pronoun becomes a singular subject and simple past with “the other” as object. *Insieme* has no separate equivalent.

but, because her lustful and vagrant eye she turned upon me, that fierce paramour did scourge her from head to feet.

- *l' occhio cupido e vagante* ‘the eye desirous and wandering’ → “her lustful and vagrant eye”: the article becomes a possessive, and the modifiers move before the noun; “lustful” gives the desire a specifically sexual sense.
- *a me rivolse* ‘toward me she turned’ → “she turned upon me”: the phrase of direction follows the verb, with “upon” for ‘toward’.
- *la flagellò* ‘her he scourged’ → “did scourge her”: the simple past becomes a construction with “did”, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *dal capo infin le piante* ‘from the head as far as the soles’ → “from head to feet”: the articles disappear in the English idiom, “to” simplifies ‘as far as’, and the whole feet replace their soles.

> 158 disciolse il mostro, e trassel per la selva

Then filled with jealousy and cruel with rage, he loosed the monster, and dragged it so far through the wood, that of this alone he made a screen between me and the harlot and the strange beast.

- *di sospetto pieno* ‘of suspicion full’ → “filled with jealousy”: the adjective becomes a participle followed by its complement, and the suspicion is interpreted as jealousy.
- *d' ira crudo* ‘with wrath cruel’ → “cruel with rage”: the adjective precedes rather than follows its complement.
- *trassel* ‘dragged it’ → “dragged it so far”: the attached object pronoun becomes a separate word, and the following degree expression is brought forward to the verb.
- *per la selva tanto* ‘through the wood so much’ → “so far through the wood”: the degree is expressed specifically as distance, before rather than after the phrase of passage.
- *sol di lei* ‘only of her’ → “of this alone”: the feminine pronoun referring to the wood becomes a demonstrative, and “alone” follows it.
- *mi fece scudo alla puttana ed alla nuova belva* ‘for me he made a shield against the harlot and against the new beast’ → “he made a screen between me and the harlot and the strange beast”: “a screen” replaces the bare noun ‘shield’; “between” recasts the dative pronoun and the two phrases with *a* as the parties separated by the screen. *Nuova* is taken in the sense of ‘unfamiliar’ rather than ‘new’.

## Summary — Reordering the Sentence, Retaining the Image

Okey often brings a delayed subject forward, places an object after its verb, or draws a modifier closer to the word it qualifies. In *temprava i passi un’ angelica nota*, the melody comes last in Italian but first in English: “a melody of angels” becomes the subject of “gave measure to our steps”. The adjective *angelica* thus also becomes a phrase naming the singers. Yet Okey does not regularize every inversion: “Then to the wheels the ladies returned” retains the prominence of *alle rote*, and “Alone sat she” keeps the emphasis of *Sola*.

Changes of construction often turn on how the two languages express an action or identify the person it affects. The reflexive *mi svegliai* becomes the intransitive “I awoke”, while *si conserva* becomes the passive “is preserved”. In *mi fu volto il viso*, the person affected is marked by *mi*; in “my face was turned”, the possessive does that work. Two routine changes generally left unremarked in the comparison are the supplying of subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending, and the omission of articles before possessives: *i suoi corsier*, for instance, becomes “his steeds”.

Okey’s choice of words can sharpen one feature of an expression while leaving another implicit. He gives *segno* its military sense, “standard”, and renders *per salvarsi*, ‘to save itself’, by the manoeuvre “to retreat” rather than its purpose. Elsewhere he expands a compact expression: *quel di lei*, ‘that from it’, becomes “that which came from it”, with a verb supplied to make the relation clear. Even where the grammar changes, the image often stays close: *parete di non caler* turns a negated infinitive into an abstract noun but keeps the wall in “a wall of unconcern”.
