# Purgatorio 8

## Lines 1–12 — The Evening Hour and a Spirit’s Prayer

> 1 Era già l' ora che volge il disio  
> 2 ai naviganti, e intenerisce il core  
> 3 lo dì ch' han detto ai dolci amici addio;  

’Twas now the hour that turns back the desire of those who sail the seas and melts their heart, that day when they have said to their sweet friends adieu,

- *già* ‘already’ → “now”: the adverb marks the time without the force of ‘already’.
- *volge* ‘turns’ → “turns back”: the direction of the turning is made explicit.
- *ai naviganti* ‘to the seafarers’ → “of those who sail the seas”: the dative becomes a possessive phrase modifying “desire”; the substantivized participle is expanded into a relative clause, with “the seas” supplied.
- *intenerisce il core* ‘softens the heart’ → “melts their heart”: the image of softening becomes one of melting, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *lo dì ch’* ‘the day that’ → “that day when”: the article becomes demonstrative, and the temporal relative is rendered by “when”.

> 4 e che lo nuovo peregrin d' amore  
> 5 punge, se ode squilla di lontano,  
> 6 che paia il giorno pianger che si more:  

and that pierces the new pilgrim with love, if from afar he hears the chimes which seem to mourn for the dying day;

- *lo nuovo peregrin d’ amore punge* ‘the new pilgrim with love pierces’ → “pierces the new pilgrim with love”: the verb is brought forward from after its object and the phrase expressing what pierces him.
- *ode squilla di lontano* ‘hears a bell from afar’ → “from afar he hears the chimes”: the distance phrase is brought before the verb; the singular bell, without an article, becomes its plural sounds with a definite article.
- *paia* ‘may seem’ → “seem”: the subjunctive becomes indicative, and the verb is plural to agree with “the chimes”.
- *il giorno pianger che si more* ‘the day to lament that dies’ → “to mourn for the dying day”: the infinitive comes before its object and takes “for”; the relative clause with pronominal *si more* becomes a participle before the noun.

> 7 quand' io incominciai a render vano  
> 8 l' udire, ed a mirare una dell' alme  
> 9 surta, che l' ascoltar chiedea con mano.  

when I began to annul my sense of hearing, and to gaze on one of the spirits, uprisen, that craved a listening with its hand.

- *render vano* ‘make vain’ → “annul”: verb and result adjective become a single verb.
- *l’ udire* ‘the hearing’ → “my sense of hearing”: the nominalized infinitive is expanded into a phrase naming the faculty, with a possessive in place of the article.
- *a mirare* ‘to gaze at’ → “to gaze on”: *a* marks the infinitive; *mirare* takes its object directly, whereas “gaze” takes a preposition.
- *alme* ‘souls’ → “spirits”: a different noun is chosen.
- *l’ ascoltar chiedea* ‘the listening asked for’ → “craved a listening”: the verb precedes its object, asking becomes craving, and the definite article becomes indefinite.
- *con mano* ‘with hand’ → “with its hand”: the possessive is supplied.

> 10 Ella giunse e levò ambo le palme,  

It joined and lifted up both its palms,

- *Ella* ‘she’ → “It”: the feminine pronoun referring to the soul becomes neuter.
- *levò* ‘raised’ → “lifted up”: the simple verb becomes a verb with a directional particle.
- *ambo le palme* ‘both the palms’ → “both its palms”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 11 ficcando gli occhi verso l' oriente,  

fixing its eyes towards the east,

- *ficcando gli occhi* ‘planting the eyes’ → “fixing its eyes”: the more forceful verb is rendered by the English idiom for directing a gaze, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 12 come dicesse a Dio: “D' altro non calme.”  

as though ’twere saying to God: “For aught else I care not.”

- *come dicesse* ‘as if it said’ → “as though ’twere saying”: the simple imperfect subjunctive becomes a progressive subjunctive.
- *D’ altro non calme* ‘of anything else it matters not to me’ → “For aught else I care not.”: the impersonal *cal(e)* with enclitic *me* becomes personal “I care”; the preposition changes to “For”, and the negative follows the verb.

## Lines 13–45 — The Evening Hymn and the Angelic Guard

> 13 “*Te lucis ante*” sì devotamente  
> 14 le uscì di bocca, e con sì dolci note,  
> 15 che fece me a me uscir di mente.  

“*Te lucis ante*” so devoutly proceeded from its mouth, and with such sweet music, that it rapt me from my very sense of self.

- *le uscì di bocca* ‘to her came out of mouth’ → “proceeded from its mouth”: the dative identifying whose mouth it is becomes a possessive; the grammatically feminine pronoun becomes neuter. The simple verb of coming out becomes “proceeded from”.
- *sì dolci note* ‘so sweet notes’ → “such sweet music”: “such” qualifies the noun phrase in place of the adverb qualifying the adjective, and a singular mass noun replaces the plural.
- *fece me a me uscir di mente* ‘made me go out of mind to myself’ → “it rapt me from my very sense of self”: the causative verb and infinitive become one transitive verb. The repeated *me* gives way to an object and a possessive phrase: what I am carried away from is expressed as my consciousness of myself, with “very” added for emphasis.

> 16 E l' altre poi dolcemente e devote  
> 17 seguitar lei per tutto l' inno intero,  
> 18 avendo gli occhi alle superne rote.  

And the others then sweetly and devoutly accompanied it through the entire hymn, having their eyes fixed on the supernal wheels.

- *dolcemente e devote* ‘sweetly and devout’ → “sweetly and devoutly”: the adjective agreeing with the singers becomes a second adverb of manner.
- *seguitar lei* ‘followed her’ → “accompanied it”: following the lead singer is rendered as singing along; the feminine pronoun becomes neuter.
- *per tutto l' inno intero* ‘through all the hymn entire’ → “through the entire hymn”: the two expressions of completeness are reduced to one, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *gli occhi alle superne rote* ‘the eyes toward the supernal wheels’ → “their eyes fixed on the supernal wheels”: a possessive replaces the definite article, and a participle of sustained attention with “on” replaces the directional preposition.

> 19 Aguzza qui, lettor, ben gli occhi al vero,  
> 20 chè il velo è ora ben tanto sottile,  
> 21 certo, che il trapassar dentro è leggiero.  

Reader, here sharpen well thine eyes to the truth, for the veil now is indeed so thin, that of a surety to pass within is easy.

- *Aguzza qui, lettor* ‘sharpen here, reader’ → “Reader, here sharpen”: the vocative comes first, and the adverb now precedes the imperative.
- *gli occhi* ‘the eyes’ → “thine eyes”: the article becomes a possessive.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating conjunction replaces the subordinating one.
- *è ora ben tanto sottile* ‘is now indeed so thin’ → “now is indeed so thin”: the adverb of time moves before the verb.
- *certo, che* ‘certainly, that’ → “that of a surety”: the asseveration becomes a prepositional phrase and moves inside the result clause.
- *il trapassar dentro* ‘the passing through into the inside’ → “to pass within”: a bare English infinitive with “to” replaces the Italian infinitive used with an article; the sense of penetration in the verb is less explicit.

> 22 Io vidi quello esercito gentile  
> 23 tacito poscia riguardare in sue,  
> 24 quasi aspettando pallido ed umile;  

I saw that noble army thereafter silently gaze upward, as if in expectancy, pale and lowly;

- *esercito gentile* ‘army noble’ → “noble army”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *tacito poscia* ‘silent thereafter’ → “thereafter silently”: the adjective describing the army becomes an adverb modifying its gaze, and the two modifiers change places.
- *riguardare in sue* ‘look on high’ → “gaze upward”: the upward look is expressed by a verb of sustained looking and a directional adverb.
- *quasi aspettando* ‘as if waiting’ → “as if in expectancy”: a prepositional phrase naming a state replaces the participle.

> 25 e vidi uscir dell' alto, e scender giue  
> 26 due angeli con due spade affocate,  
> 27 tronche e private delle punte sue.  

and I saw two angels come forth from on high and descend below with two flaming swords, broken short and deprived of their points.

- *uscir dell' alto, e scender giue / due angeli* ‘come forth from the height and descend below two angels’ → “two angels come forth from on high and descend below”: the subject of both infinitives moves from after them to before them; “on high” replaces the noun phrase.
- *due spade affocate* ‘two swords afire’ → “two flaming swords”: the description of the swords becomes a prenominal participle.

> 28 Verdi, come fogliette pur mo nate,  
> 29 erano in veste, che da verdi penne  
> 30 percosse traean dietro e ventilate.  

Green, as tender leaves just born, was their raiment, which they trailed behind, fanned and smitten by green wings.

- *fogliette* ‘little leaves’ → “tender leaves”: an adjective of quality conveys the diminutive.
- *erano in veste* ‘they were in dress’ → “was their raiment”: the wearers cease to be the subject; their clothing becomes the singular subject, with a possessive supplied. The opening adjective consequently describes the raiment directly.
- *che da verdi penne / percosse traean dietro e ventilate* ‘which, struck by green wings, they drew behind and fanned’ → “which they trailed behind, fanned and smitten by green wings”: drawing behind becomes trailing. The two participles, separated by the finite verb in Italian, are brought together after it in reverse order, with their common agent last. The Italian participles are feminine plural, where the English refers them to the collective “raiment”.

> 31 L' un poco sopra noi a star si venne,  
> 32 e l' altro scese in l' opposita sponda,  
> 33 sì che la gente in mezzo si contenne.  

One came and alighted a little above us, and the other descended on the opposite bank, so that the people was contained in the middle.

- *L' un* ‘the one’ → “One”: the definite article drops out.
- *poco sopra noi a star si venne* ‘a little above us came to take his station’ → “came and alighted a little above us”: the place phrase moves to the end. The infinitive expressing the station taken up becomes a second finite verb expressing arrival there; the pronominal *si* has no separate equivalent.
- *in l' opposita sponda* ‘onto the opposite bank’ → “on the opposite bank”: the preposition gives the place of descent rather than marking motion to it.
- *in mezzo si contenne* ‘in the middle kept itself’ → “was contained in the middle”: the pronominal construction becomes passive, and the place phrase follows the verb.

> 34 Ben discerneva in lor la testa bionda;  
> 35 ma nelle faccie l' occhio si smarria,  
> 36 come virtù che al troppo si confonda.  

Clearly I discerned the fair hair of them; but in their faces the eye was dazed, like a faculty which by excess is confounded.

- *Ben discerneva in lor la testa bionda* ‘well I discerned in them the blond head’ → “Clearly I discerned the fair hair of them”: “Clearly” specifies the sense of the initial adverb. The visible feature becomes the hair rather than the head, the adjective precedes its noun, and “of them” expresses possession after the noun rather than locating the feature before it.
- *nelle faccie* ‘in the faces’ → “in their faces”: the article becomes a possessive.
- *si smarria* ‘lost itself’ → “was dazed”: a passive expression of bewilderment replaces the pronominal verb.
- *virtù* ‘faculty’ → “a faculty”: the indefinite article is supplied.
- *al troppo si confonda* ‘at the too-much should become confused’ → “by excess is confounded”: “excess” renders the substantivized expression for what is too much, and “by” marks it as the cause. The pronominal subjunctive of the comparison becomes an indicative passive.

> 37 “Ambo vegnon del grembo di Maria,”  
> 38 disse Sordello, “a guardia della valle,  
> 39 per lo serpente che verrà via via.”  

“Both come from Mary’s bosom,” said Sordello, “as guard of the vale, because of the serpent that straightway will come.”

- *del grembo di Maria* ‘from the bosom of Mary’ → “from Mary’s bosom”: the postnominal phrase becomes a preceding possessive.
- *a guardia* ‘for guard’ → “as guard”: a phrase of purpose is rendered as one of function.
- *verrà via via* ‘will come very soon’ → “straightway will come”: a single adverb renders the repeated expression and moves before the verb.

> 40 Ond' io, che non sapeva per qual calle,  
> 41 mi volsi intorno, e stretto m' accostai  
> 42 tutto gelato alle fidate spalle.  

Whereat I, who knew not by what way, turned me around, and placed me all icy cold close to the trusty shoulders.

- *stretto m' accostai / tutto gelato alle fidate spalle* ‘close I drew myself, all frozen, to the trusty shoulders’ → “placed me all icy cold close to the trusty shoulders”: reflexive placement replaces drawing near. “Icy cold” expresses the participle as a quality; “close” moves from before the verb to immediately before the phrase naming what is approached.

> 43 E Sordello anco: “Ora avvalliamo omai  
> 44 tra le grandi ombre, e parleremo ad esse;  
> 45 grazioso fia lor vedervi assai.”  

And Sordello again: “Now go we into the vale among the mighty shades, and we will speak to them; great joy will it be to them to see you.”

- *Ora avvalliamo omai* ‘now let us go down into the valley now’ → “Now go we into the vale”: the two temporal expressions become one. The exhortation is cast with the verb before “we”; the valley implicit in the Italian verb is made explicit as the destination, but the downward motion is no longer explicit.
- *ombre* ‘shadows’ → “shades”: English uses the term specific to departed spirits.
- *grazioso fia lor vedervi assai* ‘pleasing will be to them to see you, greatly’ → “great joy will it be to them to see you”: the predicate adjective becomes a predicate noun, and the final adverb of degree becomes an adjective before that noun. Anticipatory “it” stands for the infinitive that serves directly as subject in Italian.

## Lines 46–78 — Nino’s Greeting and Request

> 46 Solo tre passi credo ch' io scendesse,  
> 47 e fui di sotto, e vidi un che mirava  
> 48 pur me, come conoscer mi volesse.  

Only three steps methinks I descended, and was below, and saw one who was gazing only at me, as tho’ he would recognise me.

- *credo ch' io scendesse* ‘I believe that I descended’ → “methinks I descended”: the personal verb becomes impersonal; the conjunction is dropped, and English does not mark the subjunctive of the dependent verb.
- *mirava pur me* ‘was gazing at me alone’ → “was gazing only at me”: English places the restrictive word before the preposition required by “gazing”.
- *come conoscer mi volesse* ‘as if to recognise me he wished’ → “as tho’ he would recognise me”: the verb of wishing becomes a modal before the infinitive, with the object after it.

> 49 Tempo era già che l' aer s' annerava,  
> 50 ma non sì che tra gli occhi suoi e i miei  
> 51 non dichiarisse ciò che pria serrava.  

’Twas now the time when the air was darkening, yet not so dark but that what between his eyes and mine before was hidden, now grew clear.

- *Tempo era già che* ‘time it was already that’ → “’Twas now the time when”: English supplies an impersonal subject and a definite article, puts the verb before its complement, and uses a temporal relative; ‘already’ becomes “now”.
- *s' annerava* ‘was turning black’ → “was darkening”: a single intransitive verb renders the Italian pronominal verb.
- *ma non sì che … non* ‘but not so much that … not’ → “yet not so dark but that”: the adjective is supplied from the preceding verb, and “but that” takes the place of the negative result clause.
- *tra gli occhi suoi e i miei … ciò che pria serrava* ‘between the eyes of his and mine … that which it previously shut up’ → “what between his eyes and mine before was hidden”: the possessive moves before “eyes”; the phrase locating the act of disclosure is moved inside the relative clause, and the active verb, with the air as subject, becomes passive.
- *dichiarisse* ‘it made clear’ → “now grew clear”: what the air disclosed becomes the subject of an intransitive verb; “now” is supplied in contrast to “before”.

> 52 Ver me si fece, ed io ver lui mi fei:  

He advanced towards me, and I to him:

- *Ver me si fece* ‘towards me he made his way’ → “He advanced towards me”: a simple verb replaces the pronominal expression, and the direction follows the verb.
- *ver lui mi fei* ‘towards him I made my way’ → “to him”: the direction is retained but the repeated verb is understood rather than expressed.

> 53 Giudice Nin gentil, quanto mi piacque,  
> 54 quando ti vidi non esser tra i rei!  

Noble judge Nino how did I rejoice when I saw thee, and not among the damned!

- *Giudice Nin gentil* ‘Judge Nino noble’ → “Noble judge Nino”: the adjective moves before the title.
- *quanto mi piacque* ‘how much it pleased me’ → “how did I rejoice”: the experiencer becomes the subject, and an emphatic auxiliary is supplied.
- *ti vidi non esser tra i rei* ‘thee I saw not to be among the guilty’ → “I saw thee, and not among the damned”: the object follows the verb; the dependent infinitive is dropped, “and” is supplied, and “damned” gives ‘guilty’ a more specific sense.

> 55 Nullo bel salutar tra noi si tacque;  

No fair greeting was left unsaid between us;

- *bel salutar* ‘fair act of greeting’ → “fair greeting”: a noun replaces the substantivized infinitive.
- *tra noi si tacque* ‘between us was passed over in silence’ → “was left unsaid between us”: a passive phrase renders the Italian *si* construction, and the phrase naming the participants moves to the end.

> 56 poi domandò: “Quant' è che tu venisti  
> 57 a piè del monte per le lontane acque?”  

then he asked: “How long is it since thou camest to the foot of the mount over the far waters?”

- *Quant' è che* ‘how much is it that’ → “How long is it since”: English specifies duration and introduces its starting point with “since”.
- *a piè del monte* ‘at foot of the mount’ → “to the foot of the mount”: English marks the endpoint of motion with “to” and supplies the article before “foot”.
- *per le lontane acque* ‘through the far waters’ → “over the far waters”: the preposition of passage becomes one of traversal over a surface.

> 58 “O,” diss' io lui, “per entro i lochi tristi  
> 59 venni stamane, e sono in prima vita,  
> 60 ancor che l' altra sì andando acquisti.”  

“Oh,” said I to him, “from within the places of woe came I this morn, and am in my first life, albeit by this my journeying I gain the other.”

- *diss' io lui* ‘said I him’ → “said I to him”: the indirect object takes an explicit preposition.
- *per entro i lochi tristi* ‘through the sorrowful places’ → “from within the places of woe”: the route is expressed as a point of departure, and a phrase with a noun replaces the adjective.
- *stamane* ‘this morning’ → “this morn”: the single temporal adverb is expanded into a phrase.
- *in prima vita* ‘in first life’ → “in my first life”: the possessive is supplied.
- *l' altra sì andando acquisti* ‘the other by thus going I may gain’ → “by this my journeying I gain the other”: “this” and the possessive qualify a verbal noun in place of the adverb and gerund; the object moves after the verb, and English does not distinguish the concessive subjunctive.

> 61 E come fu la mia risposta udita,  
> 62 Sordello ed egli indietro si raccolse,  
> 63 come gente di subito smarrita.  

And when my answer was heard, Sordello and he shrank back like folk suddenly bewildered.

- *fu la mia risposta udita* ‘was my answer heard’ → “my answer was heard”: the subject moves before the auxiliary.
- *indietro si raccolse* ‘back drew himself’ → “shrank back”: the directional word follows an intransitive verb; the Italian singular, used of the two subjects, has no distinct singular form in the English past tense.
- *di subito smarrita* ‘of a sudden lost’ → “suddenly bewildered”: an adverb replaces the prepositional expression, and “bewildered” selects the figurative sense of the participle.

> 64 L' uno a Virgilio, e l' altro ad un si volse  
> 65 che sedea lì, gridando: “Su, Corrado,  
> 66 vieni a veder che Dio per grazia volse.”  

The one turned to Virgil, and the other to one who was seated there, crying: “Up, Conrad, come and see what God by his grace hath willed.”

- *L' uno a Virgilio, e l' altro ad un si volse* ‘the one to Virgil, and the other to one turned himself’ → “The one turned to Virgil, and the other to one”: the shared verb is moved into the first member of the pair and rendered without a reflexive pronoun.
- *sedea* ‘sat’ → “was seated”: the simple imperfect becomes a phrase describing a state.
- *vieni a veder* ‘come to see’ → “come and see”: the dependent infinitive becomes a coordinated imperative.
- *per grazia* ‘by grace’ → “by his grace”: the possessive is supplied.
- *volse* ‘willed’ → “hath willed”: the simple past becomes a present perfect.

> 67 Poi volto a me: “Per quel singular grado,  
> 68 che tu dei a colui, che sì nasconde  
> 69 lo suo primo perchè, che non gli è guado,  

Then turning to me: “By that especial grace which thou owest to him who so hideth his first purpose that there is no ford to it,

- *volto a me* ‘turned to me’ → “turning to me”: a present participle replaces the past participle.
- *singular grado* ‘singular favour’ → “especial grace”: “especial” expresses the exceptional character of the favour, and “grace” renders it as a gift.
- *lo suo primo perchè* ‘his first why’ → “his first purpose”: “purpose” interprets the interrogative used as a noun.
- *non gli è guado* ‘not to it is there a ford’ → “there is no ford to it”: the existential construction is reordered, with the negation attached to the noun and the pronominal complement placed last.

> 70 quando sarai di là dalle larghe onde,  
> 71 di' a Giovanna mia, che per me chiami  
> 72 là dove agl' innocenti si risponde.  

when thou art beyond the wide waters, tell my Giovanna that she pray for me there where the innocent are heard.

- *sarai* ‘thou wilt be’ → “thou art”: English uses the present for future time in the temporal clause.
- *di là dalle* ‘on the far side of the’ → “beyond the”: a single preposition renders the expression of position.
- *di' a Giovanna mia* ‘say to Giovanna mine’ → “tell my Giovanna”: the addressee becomes a direct object, and the possessive precedes the name.
- *che per me chiami* ‘that for me she call’ → “that she pray for me”: “pray” interprets the kind of calling, and the beneficiary follows the verb.
- *agl' innocenti si risponde* ‘to the innocent one answers’ → “the innocent are heard”: the indirect object of an impersonal active construction becomes the subject of a passive, and hearing replaces answering.

> 73 Non credo che la sua madre più m' ami,  
> 74 poscia che trasmutò le bianche bende,  
> 75 le quai convien che misera ancor brami.  

I do not think her mother loves me more, since she hath changed her white wimples, which hapless she must long for once again.

- *credo che* ‘believe that’ → “think”: the verb is rendered by a near equivalent and the conjunction is omitted.
- *più m' ami* ‘any longer me she loves’ → “loves me more”: both the object and the adverb follow the verb, whose subjunctive is unmarked in English; “more” has the temporal sense ‘any longer’ under the preceding negative.
- *trasmutò* ‘she changed’ → “she hath changed”: the simple past becomes a present perfect.
- *le bianche bende* ‘the white bands’ → “her white wimples”: a possessive replaces the article, and the noun specifies the kind of head covering.
- *convien che misera ancor brami* ‘it must be that, hapless, she again desire’ → “hapless she must long for once again”: the impersonal expression of necessity with a subjunctive becomes a personal modal construction; “long for” takes a preposition, and the adverb is expanded and placed last.

> 76 Per lei assai di lieve si comprende,  
> 77 quanto in femmina foco d' amor dura,  
> 78 se l' occhio o il tatto spesso non l' accende.  

By her right easily may be known, how long the fire of love doth last in woman, if eye and touch do not oft rekindle it.

- *Per lei* ‘through her’ → “By her”: “By” renders the means of knowing.
- *assai di lieve* ‘very much with ease’ → “right easily”: an intensified adverb replaces the phrase.
- *si comprende* ‘one understands’ → “may be known”: the impersonal construction becomes a modal passive, with knowing in place of understanding.
- *quanto in femmina foco d' amor dura* ‘how long in woman fire of love lasts’ → “how long the fire of love doth last in woman”: the prepositional phrase moves to the end, an article is supplied before “fire”, and the simple verb takes an auxiliary.
- *l' occhio o il tatto* ‘the eye or the touch’ → “eye and touch”: the articles are omitted and conjunction replaces disjunction.
- *spesso non l' accende* ‘often does not kindle it’ → “do not oft rekindle it”: the negative auxiliary precedes the adverb, with plural agreement in place of the Italian singular; the object follows the verb, and “rekindle” makes repetition explicit.

## Lines 79–111 — The Stars and the Serpent in the Vale

> 79 Non le farà sì bella sepoltura  
> 80 la vipera che il Milanese accampa,  
> 81 com' avria fatto il gallo di Gallura.”  

The viper that the Milanese blazons on his shield will not make her so fair a tomb as Gallura’s cock would have done.”

- *Non le farà sì bella sepoltura* ‘not for her will make so fair a tomb’ → “will not make her so fair a tomb”: the predicate comes after the subject and its relative clause; the negative follows the auxiliary, and the pronoun follows the main verb.
- *la vipera che il Milanese accampa* ‘the viper that the Milanese sets in the field’ → “The viper that the Milanese blazons on his shield”: the heraldic sense of the verb is rendered by a verb and a phrase specifying the shield.
- *com' avria fatto il gallo di Gallura* ‘as would have done the cock of Gallura’ → “as Gallura’s cock would have done”: the subject precedes the verb, and the phrase with *di* becomes a possessive before the noun.

> 82 Così dicea, segnato della stampa  
> 83 nel suo aspetto di quel dritto zelo,  
> 84 che misuratamente in core avvampa.  

Thus spake he, his countenance stamped with the mark of that righteous zeal which in due measure glows in the breast.

- *dicea* ‘was saying’ → “spake”: a simple past replaces the imperfect.
- *segnato della stampa nel suo aspetto* ‘marked with the impress in his countenance’ → “his countenance stamped with the mark”: the participle describes the speaker in Italian; English makes the countenance the subject of an absolute phrase and transfers the image of stamping from noun to verb.
- *misuratamente* ‘temperately’ → “in due measure”: an adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *in core avvampa* ‘in the heart blazes’ → “glows in the breast”: the verb comes first and is less intense; the breast takes the place of the heart.

> 85 Gli occhi miei ghiotti andavan pure al cielo,  
> 86 pur là dove le stelle son più tarde,  
> 87 sì come rota più presso allo stelo.  

My yearning eyes were again turned towards heaven, even there where the stars are slowest, like a wheel nearest the axle.

- *Gli occhi miei ghiotti* ‘the eyes of mine, avid’ → “My yearning eyes”: the possessive and the descriptive adjective both move before the noun; the image of appetite becomes one of longing.
- *andavan pure al cielo* ‘kept going toward heaven’ → “were again turned towards heaven”: active motion becomes passive turning, and *pure* is taken as marking renewal rather than continuance.
- *pur là* ‘just there’ → “even there”: the restrictive emphasis becomes an emphatic pointing out of the place.
- *le stelle son più tarde* ‘the stars are slower’ → “the stars are slowest”: the comparative is rendered as a superlative.
- *sì come rota* ‘just as a wheel’ → “like a wheel”: a preposition expresses the comparison without a separate intensifier.
- *più presso allo stelo* ‘closer to the shaft’ → “nearest the axle”: the comparative again becomes a superlative, and the shaft is named specifically as an axle.

> 88 E il duca mio: “Figliuol, che lassù guarde?”  

And my leader: “Son, what gazest thou at up there?”

- *Figliuol, che lassù guarde?* ‘Son, what up there dost thou gaze at?’ → “Son, what gazest thou at up there?”: the expression of place moves from before the verb to the end of the question.

> 89 Ed io a lui: “A quelle tre facelle,  
> 90 di che il polo di qua tutto quanto arde.”  

And I to him: “At those three torches, wherewith the whole pole here is flaming.”

- *A quelle tre facelle* ‘at those three little torches’ → “At those three torches”: the diminutive force of *facelle* is not separately expressed.
- *di che* ‘with which’ → “wherewith”: the preposition and relative pronoun become a single relative adverb.
- *il polo di qua tutto quanto* ‘the pole on this side, all of it’ → “the whole pole here”: the emphatic expression of entirety becomes an adjective before the noun, and the indication of side becomes one of place.
- *arde* ‘burns’ → “is flaming”: a progressive form replaces the simple present.

> 91 Ed egli a me: “Le quattro chiare stelle,  
> 92 che vedevi staman, son di là basse,  
> 93 e queste son salite ov' eran quelle.”  

And he to me: “The four bright stars which thou sawest this morn are low on the other side, and these are risen where they were.”

- *vedevi* ‘thou wast seeing’ → “thou sawest”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *son di là basse* ‘are on the other side low’ → “are low on the other side”: the adjective is placed before the expression of place.
- *ov' eran quelle* ‘where were those’ → “where they were”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun and moves before the verb.

> 94 Com' ei parlava, e Sordello a sè il trasse  
> 95 dicendo: “Vedi là il nostro avversaro”;  
> 96 e drizzò il dito, perchè in là guardasse.  

As he was speaking, lo Sordello drew him to himself, saying: “See there our adversary,” and pointed his finger so that he should look thither.

- *e* ‘and’ → “lo”: the conjunction introducing the main clause is taken as marking a sudden occurrence and rendered by an interjection.
- *a sè il trasse* ‘to himself him drew’ → “drew him to himself”: the verb comes first, followed by its object and then the reflexive phrase.
- *drizzò il dito* ‘directed the finger’ → “pointed his finger”: English uses the verb for the gesture and a possessive in place of the definite article.
- *perchè in là guardasse* ‘in order that thither he might look’ → “so that he should look thither”: the purpose subjunctive is expressed with a modal auxiliary, and the direction follows the verb.

> 97 Da quella parte, onde non ha riparo  
> 98 la picciola vallea, era una biscia,  
> 99 forse qual diede ad Eva il cibo amaro.  

On that side where the little vale hath no rampart, was a snake, perchance such as gave to Eve the bitter food.

- *Da quella parte* ‘from that side’ → “On that side”: a preposition of position replaces one of direction or origin.
- *onde* ‘whence’ → “where”: English identifies the place without expressing direction from it.
- *non ha riparo la picciola vallea* ‘has not a defence the little vale’ → “the little vale hath no rampart”: the subject moves before the verb, negation passes to the noun, and the general defence becomes a rampart.

> 100 Tra l' erba e i fior venia la mala striscia,  
> 101 volgendo ad or ad or la testa al dosso,  
> 102 leccando come bestia che si liscia.  

Through the grass and the flowers came the evil reptile, turning round now and again its head to its back, licking like a beast that sleeks itself.

- *Tra l' erba e i fior* ‘among the grass and the flowers’ → “Through the grass and the flowers”: English makes the phrase describe the path of motion.
- *venia la mala striscia* ‘was coming the evil streak’ → “came the evil reptile”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the creature is named directly rather than by the streak it makes.
- *volgendo* ‘turning’ → “turning round”: English adds a particle making the direction of the turn explicit.
- *la testa al dosso* ‘the head to the back’ → “its head to its back”: possessives replace the definite articles with both body parts.
- *come bestia* ‘like beast’ → “like a beast”: English supplies an indefinite article for the singular count noun.

> 103 Io non vidi, e però dicer non posso,  
> 104 come mosser gli astor celestiali,  
> 105 ma vidi bene e l' uno e l' altro mosso.  

I saw not, and therefore cannot tell, how the celestial falcons moved; but full well I saw both in motion.

- *dicer non posso* ‘tell I cannot’ → “cannot tell”: the modal precedes its infinitive.
- *come mosser gli astor celestiali* ‘how moved the goshawks celestial’ → “how the celestial falcons moved”: the subject moves before the verb and the adjective before its noun; a different bird-name replaces *astor*.
- *ma vidi bene* ‘but I saw well’ → “but full well I saw”: the adverb is intensified and placed before the subject and verb.
- *e l' uno e l' altro mosso* ‘both the one and the other moved’ → “both in motion”: one pronoun replaces the paired expressions, and a prepositional phrase replaces the participle.

> 106 Sentendo fender l' aere alle verdi ali,  
> 107 fuggì il serpente, e gli angeli dier volta  
> 108 suso alle poste rivolando eguali.  

Hearing the green wings cleave through the air, the serpent fled, and the angels wheeled around, flying in equal measure back to their posts.

- *fender l' aere alle verdi ali* ‘the air being cleft by the green wings’ → “the green wings cleave through the air”: the wings, marked by *a* as the agent of the infinitive, become its explicit subject before the verb; “through” expresses the passage through the air.
- *fuggì il serpente* ‘fled the serpent’ → “the serpent fled”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *gli angeli dier volta* ‘the angels gave a turn’ → “the angels wheeled around”: a verb and particle replace the verb and its noun object.
- *suso alle poste rivolando eguali* ‘upward to the posts flying back equal’ → “flying in equal measure back to their posts”: upward direction is omitted; the destination comes last and gains a possessive. The adjective describing the two fliers becomes an adverbial phrase qualifying their flight.

> 109 L' ombra, che s' era al giudice raccolta  
> 110 quando chiamò, per tutto quell' assalto  
> 111 punto non fu da me guardare sciolta.  

The shade that had drawn close to the judge when he called, through all that assault was not loosed a moment from gazing at me.

- *che s' era al giudice raccolta* ‘that had gathered itself to the judge’ → “that had drawn close to the judge”: an intransitive expression of approach replaces the reflexive, and the destination follows rather than intervening in the compound verb.
- *punto non fu … sciolta* ‘not a whit was … released’ → “was not loosed a moment”: the negative follows the auxiliary; *punto* is taken temporally and placed after the participle, which now stands next to the negated auxiliary.
- *da me guardare* ‘from me gazing at’ → “from gazing at me”: the infinitive governed by *da* becomes an *-ing* form after the preposition, and its object follows it.

## Lines 112–139 — The Praise and Prophecy of Malaspina

> 112 “Se la lucerna che ti mena in alto  
> 113 trovi nel tuo arbitrio tanta cera,  
> 114 quant' è mestiero infino al sommo smalto,”  

“So may that light which guideth thee on high, find in thy will as much wax as is needful to reach the enamelled summit,”

- *Se … trovi* ‘if … should find’ → “So may … find”: the conditional form of the wish becomes an explicit English optative, with the auxiliary before the subject.
- *la lucerna* ‘the lamp’ → “that light”: the article becomes demonstrative, and the lamp is named by what it gives.
- *ti mena* ‘thee leads’ → “guideth thee”: the verb expresses guidance rather than simply leading, and the object follows it.
- *arbitrio* ‘power of choice’ → “will”: the faculty of choosing is rendered by a single broader term.
- *tanta cera, quant' è mestiero* ‘so much wax as is need’ → “as much wax as is needful”: the correlatives become “as much … as,” and the noun expressing necessity becomes an adjective.
- *infino al sommo smalto* ‘as far as the highest enamel’ → “to reach the enamelled summit”: a verb of reaching replaces the preposition of extent; the adjective of height becomes the noun, and the noun for enamel becomes its modifier.

> 115 cominciò ella, “se novella vera  
> 116 di Valdimacra o di parte vicina  
> 117 sai, dilla a me, che già grande là era.  

it began, “if thou know true tidings of Valdimacra, or of neighbouring parts, tell it me who once was mighty there.

- *cominciò ella* ‘began she’ → “it began”: the feminine pronoun becomes neuter and precedes the verb.
- *se novella vera di Valdimacra o di parte vicina sai* ‘if news true of Valdimacra or of a part neighbouring thou know’ → “if thou know true tidings of Valdimacra, or of neighbouring parts”: the verb moves ahead of its object, both adjectives precede their nouns, and both singular nouns are rendered by plural forms.
- *dilla a me* ‘tell it to me’ → “tell it me”: the recipient is expressed without a preposition.
- *che già grande là era* ‘who once great there was’ → “who once was mighty there”: the verb moves before its complement, and “mighty” specifies the sense of *grande*.

> 118 Chiamato fui Corrado Malaspina:  
> 119 non son l' antico, ma di lui discesi;  

I was called Conrad Malaspina: not the elder am I, but descended from him:

- *Chiamato fui* ‘called was I’ → “I was called”: the participle follows the auxiliary instead of preceding it.
- *non son l' antico* ‘I am not the old one’ → “not the elder am I”: “elder” makes the distinction comparative, and the negated complement is put before the verb.
- *ma di lui discesi* ‘but from him I descended’ → “but descended from him”: the phrase of descent follows the verb, and the English reads as a participial complement of “am” rather than a new finite past verb.

> 120 a' miei portai l' amor che qui raffina.”  

to mine own I bore that love which here is purified.”

- *l' amor* ‘the love’ → “that love”: the article becomes demonstrative.
- *raffina* ‘grows refined’ → “is purified”: the intransitive verb is rendered by an explicit passive.

> 121 “O,” diss' io lui, “per li vostri paesi  
> 122 giammai non fui; ma dove si dimora  
> 123 per tutta Europa, ch' ei non sien palesi?  

“Oh,” said I to him, “through your lands I ne’er have been, but where do men dwell throughout Europe to whom they are not renowned?

- *diss' io lui* ‘said I him’ → “said I to him”: English marks the addressee with a preposition.
- *giammai non fui* ‘never was I’ → “I ne’er have been”: the English perfect replaces the simple past, and one negative renders the Italian negative concord.
- *dove si dimora* ‘where does one dwell’ → “where do men dwell”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal verb with a plural subject.
- *ch' ei non sien palesi* ‘that they be not known’ → “to whom they are not renowned”: the clause is attached by a personal relative to the supplied “men”; the subjunctive becomes indicative, and “renowned” is stronger than merely being known.

> 124 La fama che la vostra casa onora  
> 125 grida i signori e grida la contrada,  
> 126 sì che ne sa chi non vi fu ancora.  

The fame which honours your house proclaims abroad its lords, and proclaims the country, so that he knows of it who there hath never been.

- *che la vostra casa onora* ‘which your house honours’ → “which honours your house”: the Italian object-before-verb order is reversed; in Okey’s reading it is the fame that does the honouring.
- *grida i signori* ‘cries the lords’ → “proclaims abroad its lords”: the verb is expanded to express public proclamation, and a possessive replaces the article.
- *grida la contrada* ‘cries the district’ → “proclaims the country”: the repeated verb again becomes one of proclamation, and the territorial noun is rendered more broadly.
- *ne sa chi non vi fu ancora* ‘of it knows whoever has not been there yet’ → “he knows of it who there hath never been”: “he … who” divides the single *chi*; the phrase answering to *ne* follows the verb, and ‘not yet’ with a simple past becomes “never” with a perfect.

> 127 Ed io vi giuro, s' io di sopra vada,  
> 128 che vostra gente onrata non si sfregia  
> 129 del pregio della borsa e della spada.  

And I swear to you, so may I go on high, that your honoured race strips not itself of the glory of the purse and of the sword.

- *vi giuro* ‘to you I swear’ → “I swear to you”: the recipient follows the verb.
- *s' io di sopra vada* ‘if I should go above’ → “so may I go on high”: the conditional wish becomes an explicit optative, and the phrase of direction follows the verb.
- *gente onrata* ‘people honoured’ → “honoured race”: the collective noun is taken in the sense of lineage, and its adjective precedes it.
- *non si sfregia* ‘does not disfigure itself’ → “strips not itself”: loss is expressed as stripping rather than disfigurement, and the verb precedes both the negative and the reflexive.
- *pregio* ‘worth’ → “glory”: a term of value is rendered as one of distinction.

> 130 Uso e natura sì la privilegia,  
> 131 che, perchè il capo reo lo mondo torca,  
> 132 sola va dritta, e il mal cammin dispregia.”  

Custom and nature so do privilege it, that for all that the guilty head sets the world awry, it alone goeth straight and scorns the path of evil.”

- *sì la privilegia* ‘so it privileges’ → “so do privilege it”: English uses an auxiliary agreeing with the compound subject where Italian has a singular verb, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *perchè il capo reo lo mondo torca* ‘though the head guilty should twist the world’ → “for all that the guilty head sets the world awry”: the conjunction is taken concessively, the adjective moves before its noun, and the final subjunctive verb becomes an indicative verb with a resultative complement.
- *il mal cammin dispregia* ‘the evil path it scorns’ → “scorns the path of evil”: the object follows the verb, and the attributive adjective becomes an *of*-phrase.

> 133 Ed egli: “Or va, chè il sol non si ricorca  
> 134 sette volte nel letto che il Montone  
> 135 con tutti e quattro i piè copra ed inforca,  

And he: “Now depart, for the sun goeth not to rest seven times in the bed which the Ram covers and bestrides with all four feet,

- *va* ‘go’ → “depart”: the verb is made specifically one of leaving.
- *non si ricorca* ‘does not lie down again’ → “goeth not to rest”: the reflexive verb becomes a phrase of motion and rest, without an explicit equivalent for ‘again’.
- *il Montone con tutti e quattro i piè copra ed inforca* ‘the Ram with all four of its feet covers and bestrides’ → “the Ram covers and bestrides with all four feet”: the phrase specifying the feet follows both verbs instead of preceding them, and the joining *e* in *tutti e quattro* has no separate equivalent.

> 136 che cotesta cortese opinione  
> 137 ti fia chiavata in mezzo della testa  
> 138 con maggior chiovi che d' altrui sermone,  

ere this courteous opinion shall be nailed in the midst of thy head, with bigger nails than other men’s words,

- *che* ‘that’ → “ere”: English marks explicitly the temporal relation to the preceding negative.
- *cotesta* ‘that … of yours’ → “this”: the demonstrative loses its specific reference to the person addressed.
- *ti fia chiavata in mezzo della testa* ‘to thee shall be nailed in the midst of the head’ → “shall be nailed in the midst of thy head”: the dative of the person affected becomes a possessive modifying “head.”
- *maggior chiovi* ‘greater nails’ → “bigger nails”: the comparative is rendered specifically in terms of size.
- *d' altrui sermone* ‘of others’ speech’ → “other men’s words”: the indefinite possessor is expressed with a noun, and singular ‘speech’ becomes plural “words”; the Italian preposition has no separate equivalent.

> 139 se corso di giudizio non s' arresta.”  

if course of judgment be not stayed.”

- *non s' arresta* ‘does not come to a halt’ → “be not stayed”: the present indicative reflexive becomes a passive subjunctive, with the negative following the auxiliary.

## Summary — Close Images, Recast Syntax

Okey often keeps Dante’s images while changing the order in which they reach the reader. He brings *due angeli* forward as “two angels” before the two infinitives that precede it in Italian, and moves the object of *il mal cammin dispregia* after the verb in “scorns the path of evil”. Yet he retains the inversion of *venia la mala striscia* in “came the evil reptile”, and keeps such distinctive images as “the supernal wheels” and “the glory of the purse and of the sword”.

A change of construction can shift the emphasis without greatly changing the sense. The adjective *tacito* ‘silent’ describes the army; “silently” describes how it gazes. The impersonal *si comprende* ‘one understands’ becomes the modal passive “may be known”. Elsewhere a choice of words makes a detail more specific: *riparo* ‘defence’ becomes “rampart”, and the diminutive *fogliette* ‘little leaves’ becomes “tender leaves”, suggesting freshness rather than size. Okey sometimes supplies a detail, as with “back” in “turns back” for *volge* ‘turns’; sometimes he leaves one out, as with *suso* ‘upward’ in the angels’ return. He also condenses the double emphasis of *tutto l’inno intero* into “the entire hymn”.

Two routine differences generally go unremarked in the comparison. English supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the person in the verb ending alone, as in *Non credo* rendered by “I do not think”. English also omits the article that Italian commonly puts before a possessive: *la mia risposta* becomes “my answer”, not ‘the my answer’.
