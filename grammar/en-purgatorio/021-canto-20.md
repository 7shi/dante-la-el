# Purgatorio 20

## Lines 1–18 — Slow Steps and the Curse on the She-Wolf

> 1 Contra miglior voler voler mal pugna:  

Against a better will the will fights ill,

- *Contra miglior voler* ‘against better willing’ → “Against a better will”: the substantival infinitive becomes a noun with an indefinite article.
- *voler mal pugna* ‘willing ill fights’ → “the will fights ill”: the repeated infinitive becomes a noun, this time with the definite article, and the adverb follows the verb.

> 2 onde contra il piacer mio, per piacerli,  
> 3 trassi dell' acqua non sazia la spugna.  

wherefore, against my pleasure, to please him, I drew the sponge from the water unfilled.

- *contra il piacer mio* ‘against the pleasure of mine’ → “against my pleasure”: the postposed possessive comes before the noun.
- *per piacerli* ‘in order to please him’ → “to please him”: the English infinitive alone expresses the purpose marked by *per*.
- *trassi dell' acqua non sazia la spugna* ‘I drew from the water not sated the sponge’ → “I drew the sponge from the water unfilled”: the object comes immediately after the verb; the description of its state moves to the end, and the negated adjective becomes a single word denoting lack of fullness rather than of satiety.

> 4 Mossimi; e il duca mio si mosse per li  
> 5 lochi spediti pur lungo la roccia,  
> 6 come si va per muro stretto ai merli:  

I moved on, and my Leader moved on by the free spaces, ever along the rock, as one goes by a wall close to the battlements;

- *Mossimi* ‘I moved myself’ → “I moved on”: the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb with a particle of onward motion.
- *il duca mio* ‘the leader of mine’ → “my Leader”: the postposed possessive comes before the noun.
- *si mosse* ‘moved himself’ → “moved on”: again the reflexive gives way to the verb and particle.
- *per li lochi spediti* ‘through the places unobstructed’ → “by the free spaces”: the preposition indicates the route taken, *lochi* becomes “spaces”, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *pur* ‘only’ → “ever”: the translation takes the adverb in the sense of continuity.
- *per muro* ‘along a wall’ → “by a wall”: the preposition of passage becomes “by”, and English supplies an article before the singular noun.
- *stretto ai merli* ‘pressed close to the merlons’ → “close to the battlements”: the adjective agreeing with the person walking becomes an expression of position; “battlements” names the whole rather than its individual raised parts.

> 7 chè la gente, che fonde a goccia a goccia  
> 8 per gli occhi il mal che tutto il mondo occupa,  
> 9 dall' altra parte in fuor troppo s' approccia.  

for the people who distill through their eyes, drop by drop, the evil that fills the whole world, on the other side approach too near the edge.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *la gente, che fonde* ‘the populace, which pours out’ → “the people who distill”: the singular collective is construed as plural, the relative pronoun is personal, and the verb becomes one suggesting the emission of drops.
- *a goccia a goccia per gli occhi* ‘drop by drop through the eyes’ → “through their eyes, drop by drop”: the two phrases change places, and the definite article before the body part becomes a possessive.
- *tutto il mondo occupa* ‘the whole world occupies’ → “fills the whole world”: the verb precedes its object, and occupation is rendered as filling.
- *in fuor troppo s' approccia* ‘outward too much draws itself near’ → “approach too near the edge”: the reflexive singular becomes an intransitive plural agreeing with “the people”; excess is expressed as excessive nearness, and the outward direction becomes an explicit point of approach, “the edge”.

> 10 Maledetta sie tu, antica lupa,  
> 11 che più di tutte l'altre bestie hai preda,  
> 12 per la tua fame senza fine cupa!  

Accurst be thou, she-wolf of old, that hast more prey than all the other beasts, for thy hunger endlessly deep!

- *antica lupa* ‘ancient she-wolf’ → “she-wolf of old”: the preceding adjective becomes a following prepositional phrase.
- *più di tutte l'altre bestie hai preda* ‘more than all the other beasts hast prey’ → “hast more prey than all the other beasts”: the verb comes first, and “more” stands directly before the noun it quantifies.
- *senza fine cupa* ‘without end deep’ → “endlessly deep”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb modifying the adjective.

> 13 O ciel, nel cui girar par che si creda  
> 14 le condizion di quaggiù trasmutarsi,  
> 15 quando verrà per cui questa disceda?  

O heaven, in whose revolution it seems that conditions here below are thought to be changed, when will he come through whom she shall depart?

- *nel cui girar* ‘in whose turning’ → “in whose revolution”: the substantival infinitive becomes a noun.
- *par che si creda le condizion di quaggiù trasmutarsi* ‘it seems that one believes the conditions of here below to change’ → “it seems that conditions here below are thought to be changed”: the impersonal subjunctive becomes a passive with “conditions” as subject; the definite article and the preposition linking the conditions to ‘here below’ disappear. The final infinitive with *si*, which can denote undergoing change, is made explicitly passive.
- *quando verrà per cui* ‘when will come the one through whom’ → “when will he come through whom”: English supplies “he” as the antecedent implied by the relative construction and places it between the auxiliary and the main verb.
- *questa disceda* ‘this one may depart’ → “she shall depart”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative, and the subjunctive is rendered with a future auxiliary.

> 16 Noi andavam con passi lenti e scarsi,  
> 17 ed io attento all' ombre ch' io sentia  
> 18 pietosamente piangere e lagnarsi;  

We went on, with steps slow and scant, and I intent on the shades that I heard piteously weeping and complaining;

- *Noi andavam* ‘we were going’ → “We went on”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, with “on” conveying continued motion.
- *ch' io sentia pietosamente piangere e lagnarsi* ‘that I was hearing piteously weep and lament themselves’ → “that I heard piteously weeping and complaining”: the imperfect becomes a simple past; the two infinitives after the verb of perception become *-ing* forms, and the last verb has no reflexive pronoun in English.

## Lines 19–51 — Hugh Capet Praises Poverty and Names His Heirs

> 19 e per ventura udi': “Dolce Maria,”  
> 20 dinanzi a noi chiamar così nel pianto,  
> 21 come fa donna che in partorir sia;  

and by chance I heard one in front of us calling with tears: “Sweet Mary,” even as a woman who is in travail;

- *udi' … chiamar* ‘I heard … call’ → “I heard one” … “calling”: English supplies an object for the verb of hearing and uses a participle rather than an infinitive for what is heard.
- *Dolce Maria, dinanzi a noi* ‘Sweet Mary, in front of us’ → “in front of us” … “Sweet Mary,”: the words of the call are postponed until after the description of the calling.
- *così nel pianto* ‘thus in weeping’ → “with tears”: the adverb of manner is not separately rendered; the act of weeping becomes the tears that accompany the call.
- *come fa donna* ‘as does a woman’ → “even as a woman”: the comparison is reinforced by “even”, the verb standing for the act of calling is omitted, and the indefinite noun takes an article.
- *che in partorir sia* ‘who in giving birth may be’ → “who is in travail”: the verb moves forward and the subjunctive becomes indicative; the infinitive governed by a preposition becomes a noun phrase.

> 22 e seguitar: “Povera fosti tanto,  
> 23 quanto veder si può per quell' ospizio,  
> 24 ove sponesti il tuo portato santo.”  

and continuing: “So poor wast thou, as may be seen by that hostelry where thou didst lay down thy holy burden.”

- *seguitar* ‘to continue’ → “continuing”: like the earlier verb of calling, the infinitive dependent on the verb of hearing becomes a participle.
- *Povera fosti tanto* ‘poor wast thou so much’ → “So poor wast thou”: the degree word moves from after the verb to before the adjective.
- *quanto veder si può* ‘as much as one can see’ → “as may be seen”: the second term of the degree comparison is reduced to “as”, and the construction with *si* becomes an English modal passive.
- *sponesti* ‘thou laidst down’ → “thou didst lay down”: an auxiliary and plain verb render the simple past.
- *portato santo* ‘burden holy’ → “holy burden”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 25 Seguentemente intesi: “O buon Fabbrizio,  
> 26 con povertà volesti anzi virtute,  
> 27 che gran ricchezza posseder con vizio.”  

Following I heard: “O good Fabricius, thou didst desire to possess virtue with poverty, rather than great riches with iniquity.”

- *con povertà volesti anzi virtute, che gran ricchezza posseder con vizio* ‘with poverty thou didst wish rather for virtue than great wealth to possess with vice’ → “thou didst desire to possess virtue with poverty, rather than great riches with iniquity”: the infinitive, which in Italian goes with the second alternative, is moved up to govern both; the initial poverty phrase moves after “virtue”, and the separated terms of preference come together as “rather than”. Singular wealth becomes plural “riches”, and vice is rendered as “iniquity”.

> 28 Queste parole m'eran sì piaciute,  
> 29 ch' io mi trassi oltre per aver contezza  
> 30 di quello spirto, onde parean venute.  

These words were so pleasing to me, that I drew me forward to have knowledge of that spirit, from whom they seemed to have come.

- *m'eran sì piaciute* ‘had so pleased me’ → “were so pleasing to me”: the pluperfect becomes a past-tense description, with the experiencer moved to the end.
- *mi trassi oltre* ‘myself I drew farther on’ → “I drew me forward”: the reflexive follows the verb, and “forward” gives the direction of the movement.
- *onde* ‘whence’ → “from whom”: a relative adverb becomes a preposition with a personal relative pronoun.
- *parean venute* ‘seemed come’ → “they seemed to have come”: English makes the anterior action explicit with a perfect infinitive rather than a participle alone.

> 31 Esso parlava ancor della larghezza  
> 32 che fece Niccolao alle pulcelle,  
> 33 per condurre ad onor lor giovinezza.  

It went on to speak of the bounty which Nicholas gave to the maidens, to lead their youth to honour.

- *parlava ancor* ‘was speaking still’ → “went on to speak”: the imperfect and the following adverb are recast as a verb of continuation with an infinitive.
- *che fece Niccolao* ‘which made Nicholas’ → “which Nicholas gave”: the subject moves before the verb, and English treats bounty as something given rather than an act of generosity done.
- *per condurre ad onor lor giovinezza* ‘in order to lead to honour their youth’ → “to lead their youth to honour”: the purpose is expressed by the infinitive alone, and its direct object comes before the phrase of direction.

> 34 “O anima che tanto ben favelle,  
> 35 dimmi chi fosti,” dissi, “e perchè sola  
> 36 tu queste degne lode rinnovelle?  

“O spirit, that discoursest so much of good, tell me who thou wast,” said I, “and wherefore thou alone renewest these worthy lauds?

- *anima* ‘soul’ → “spirit”: a different noun is used.
- *che tanto ben favelle* ‘that so much good speakest’ → “that discoursest so much of good”: the verb comes first and takes a phrase with “of” rather than a direct object; its English form does not distinguish the Italian subjunctive.
- *perchè sola tu* ‘why alone thou’ → “wherefore thou alone”: the pronoun precedes the word singling it out.
- *queste degne lode rinnovelle* ‘these worthy lauds thou renewest’ → “renewest these worthy lauds”: the verb comes before its object, and its English form again does not distinguish the subjunctive.

> 37 Non fia senza mercè la tua parola,  
> 38 s' io ritorno a compier lo cammin corto  
> 39 di quella vita che al termine vola.”  

Thy words shall not be without reward, if I return to complete the short way of that life which is flying to its end.”

- *Non fia senza mercè la tua parola* ‘shall not be without reward thy word’ → “Thy words shall not be without reward”: the subject moves to the front and the singular becomes plural.
- *lo cammin corto* ‘the way short’ → “the short way”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *che al termine vola* ‘which to the end flies’ → “which is flying to its end”: the verb moves before the phrase of direction, the simple present becomes progressive, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 40 Ed egli: “Io 'l ti dirò, non per conforto  
> 41 ch' io attenda di là, ma perchè tanta  
> 42 grazia in te luce prima che sii morto.  

And he: “I will tell it thee, not for any solace that I expect from yonder, but because so much grace shineth in thee ere thou art dead.

- *Io 'l ti dirò* ‘I it to thee shall tell’ → “I will tell it thee”: both object pronouns follow the verb rather than preceding it.
- *non per conforto* ‘not for solace’ → “not for any solace”: “any” is supplied under the negative.
- *ch' io attenda di là* ‘that I may expect from there’ → “that I expect from yonder”: the subjunctive in the relative clause becomes an indicative.
- *in te luce* ‘in thee shines’ → “shineth in thee”: the verb precedes the prepositional phrase.
- *prima che sii morto* ‘before thou be dead’ → “ere thou art dead”: the temporal conjunction is rendered by a single word, and the subjunctive becomes indicative.

> 43 Io fui radice della mala pianta,  
> 44 che la terra cristiana tutta aduggia  
> 45 sì che buon frutto rado se ne schianta.  

I was the root of the evil tree which o’ershadows all Christian lands, so that rarely is good fruit plucked therefrom.

- *fui radice* ‘was root’ → “was the root”: English supplies a definite article for the predicate noun.
- *mala pianta* ‘evil plant’ → “evil tree”: the more general noun becomes a more specific one.
- *che la terra cristiana tutta aduggia* ‘which the Christian land in its entirety overshadows’ → “which o’ershadows all Christian lands”: the verb comes before its object; a definite collective singular becomes a plural, with “all” before the adjective and noun.
- *buon frutto rado se ne schianta* ‘good fruit rarely is from it broken off’ → “rarely is good fruit plucked therefrom”: the adverb moves to the front, with the passive auxiliary before the subject; the construction with *si* becomes an explicit passive, the verb of breaking off becomes one of plucking, and the pronoun of source is rendered by a final adverb.

> 46 Ma, se Doagio, Lilla, Guanto e Bruggia  
> 47 potesser, tosto ne saria vendetta;  
> 48 ed io la cheggio a lui che tutto giuggia.  

But if Douay, Lille, Ghent and Bruges had power, soon were vengeance taken for it, and I beseech this from him who judgeth all.

- *potesser* ‘could’ → “had power”: the imperfect subjunctive of ability becomes a verb with a noun object in the hypothetical clause.
- *tosto ne saria vendetta* ‘soon of it there would be vengeance’ → “soon were vengeance taken for it”: the conditional of being becomes a hypothetical passive with the supplied verb of taking; the pronoun preceding the Italian verb becomes a final prepositional phrase.
- *la cheggio a lui* ‘it I ask of him’ → “I beseech this from him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb and becomes demonstrative; the verb of asking becomes the stronger “beseech”, with the person asked construed as the source.
- *che tutto giuggia* ‘who all judges’ → “who judgeth all”: the object follows the verb.

> 49 Chiamato fui di là Ugo Ciapetta;  
> 50 di me son nati i Filippi e i Luigi,  
> 51 per cui novellamente Francia è retta.  

Hugh Capet was I called yonder; of me are born the Philips and the Lewises by whom of late France is ruled.

- *Chiamato fui di là Ugo Ciapetta* ‘called was I over there Hugh Capet’ → “Hugh Capet was I called yonder”: the name moves from last to first, the participle follows the auxiliary and subject, and the place phrase becomes a final adverb.
- *per cui* ‘through whom’ → “by whom”: English uses the preposition marking the agent of the passive.
- *novellamente* ‘newly’ → “of late”: a phrase of recent time replaces the adverb.

## Lines 52–84 — The Crown and the Avarice of My Race

> 52 Figlio fu' io d' un beccaio di Parigi.  

Son was I of a butcher of Paris.

- Literal.

> 53 Quando li regi antichi venner meno  
> 54 tutti, fuor ch' un, renduto in panni bigi,  

When the ancient kings came to an end, all save one given over to grey garments,

- *li regi antichi* ‘the kings ancient’ → “the ancient kings”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *venner meno* ‘came less’ → “came to an end”: the Italian verbal idiom is rendered by an English phrase denoting cessation.
- *renduto in panni bigi* ‘surrendered in garments grey’ → “given over to grey garments”: the Italian describes the one who had given himself up to religious life as being *in* the garments; English makes the garments the complement of “given over to” and puts the adjective first.

> 55 trovaimi stretto nelle mani il freno  
> 56 del governo del regno, e tanta possa  
> 57 di nuovo acquisto, e sì d' amici pieno,  

I found tight in my hands the reins of the government of the realm, and so much power from new possessions, and so rich in friends,

- *trovaimi* ‘I found myself’ → “I found”: the reflexive pronoun drops out, and “found” takes “the reins” directly as its object.
- *stretto nelle mani il freno* ‘gripped in the hands the rein’ → “tight in my hands the reins”: the participle becomes “tight”, the possessor of the hands is made explicit, and the singular becomes the idiomatic English plural.
- *di nuovo acquisto* ‘of new acquisition’ → “from new possessions”: the preposition is read as marking the source of the power, and the singular noun is rendered by a plural denoting the things acquired.
- *sì d’ amici pieno* ‘so of friends full’ → “so rich in friends”: “rich” replaces ‘full’ and precedes its complement, which it takes with “in”.

> 58 ch' alla corona vedova promossa  
> 59 la testa di mio figlio fu, dal quale  
> 60 cominciar di costor le sacrate ossa.  

that to my son’s head the widowed crown was promoted, from whom began the consecrated bones of those.

- *alla corona vedova promossa la testa di mio figlio fu* ‘to the crown widowed promoted the head of my son was’ → “to my son’s head the widowed crown was promoted”: the grammatical roles are reversed. In Italian the head is promoted to the crown; in English the crown is promoted to the head. The possessive and the adjective move before their nouns, and the auxiliary comes before the participle.
- *dal quale* ‘from which one’ → “from whom”: English uses the personal relative rather than the Italian combination of article and relative.
- *di costor le sacrate ossa* ‘of these the consecrated bones’ → “the consecrated bones of those”: the possessive phrase moves after the noun, and the near demonstrative becomes a distant one.

> 61 Mentre che la gran dote Provenzale  
> 62 al sangue mio non tolse la vergogna,  
> 63 poco valea, ma pur non facea male.  

So long as the great dowry of Provence had not taken shame from my race, it was of little worth, but yet it did no evil.

- *la gran dote Provenzale* ‘the great Provençal dowry’ → “the great dowry of Provence”: the adjective of origin becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *al sangue mio non tolse la vergogna* ‘from the blood of mine it did not take the shame’ → “had not taken shame from my race”: the simple past becomes a past perfect; the phrase naming what suffered the loss moves after the object. “Race” renders ‘blood’ in the sense of descent, the possessive moves before its noun, and ‘shame’ loses its article.
- *poco valea* ‘little was it worth’ → “it was of little worth”: the verb of valuation becomes “was of” with a noun, and the expression of amount follows the verb.
- *ma pur non facea male* ‘but yet it was not doing evil’ → “but yet it did no evil”: the imperfect is rendered by the simple past, and the negative shifts from the verb to the object.

> 64 Lì cominciò con forza e con menzogna  
> 65 la sua rapina; e poscia, per ammenda,  
> 66 Pontì e Normandia prese e Guascogna.  

There by force and fraud its rapine began; and then, for amends, Ponthieu and Normandy it seized, and Gascony.

- *cominciò … la sua rapina* ‘began … its rapine’ → “its rapine began”: English takes the noun phrase as the subject and puts it before the verb, after the phrase of means.
- *con forza e con menzogna* ‘with force and with falsehood’ → “by force and fraud”: “by” marks the means and serves both nouns; “fraud” renders falsehood as a practice.
- *prese* ‘took’ → “it seized”: the English verb expressly conveys forcible taking.

> 67 Carlo venne in Italia, e, per ammenda,  
> 68 vittima fe' di Curradino; e poi  
> 69 ripinse al ciel Tommaso, per ammenda.  

Charles came to Italy, and, for amends, made a victim of Conradin; and then thrust Thomas back to heaven, for amends.

- *in Italia* ‘into Italy’ → “to Italy”: English marks the destination rather than entry.
- *vittima fe’ di Curradino* ‘a victim made of Conradin’ → “made a victim of Conradin”: the verb moves before its complement, and English supplies the indefinite article before “victim”.
- *ripinse al ciel Tommaso* ‘thrust back to heaven Thomas’ → “thrust Thomas back to heaven”: the object moves up between the verb and the particle that renders its prefix; English also uses “heaven” without the article.

> 70 Tempo vegg' io, non molto dopo ancoi,  
> 71 che tragge un altro Carlo fuor di Francia,  
> 72 per far conoscer meglio e sè e i suoi.  

A time I see, not long after this day, that brings another Charles forth from France, to make both him and his better known.

- *Tempo vegg’ io* ‘time see I’ → “A time I see”: English adds an indefinite article and puts the expressed subject before the verb.
- *ancoi* ‘today’ → “this day”: the adverb becomes a demonstrative noun phrase.
- *tragge un altro Carlo fuor di Francia* ‘draws another Charles out of France’ → “brings another Charles forth from France”: “brings” replaces ‘draws’, and movement out of a place is expressed by “forth” with a phrase of origin.
- *per far conoscer meglio e sè e i suoi* ‘in order to cause to know better both himself and his own’ → “to make both him and his better known”: a bare infinitive of purpose renders *per* with the infinitive. The active infinitive after the causative becomes a passive participle, the persons to be known move before it, and the reflexive becomes “him”.

> 73 Senz' arme n' esce solo e con la lancia  
> 74 con la qual giostrò Giuda; e quella ponta  
> 75 sì, ch' a Fiorenza fa scoppiar la pancia.  

Forth he comes, alone, without an army, and with the lance wherewith Judas jousted; and that he couches so, that he makes the paunch of Florence to burst.

- *Senz’ arme n’ esce solo* ‘without arms thence he goes out alone’ → “Forth he comes, alone, without an army”: “Forth” conveys outward movement without the pronoun’s explicit reference to the place left; ‘goes out’ becomes “comes”. The opening phrase moves to the end and is read collectively as the absence of an army rather than of weapons.
- *con la qual* ‘with which’ → “wherewith”: the preposition and relative are combined in one word.
- *giostrò Giuda* ‘jousted Judas’ → “Judas jousted”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *quella ponta* ‘that he thrusts’ → “that he couches”: English names the positioning of the lance rather than its forward thrust.
- *a Fiorenza fa scoppiar la pancia* ‘to Florence he causes to burst the paunch’ → “he makes the paunch of Florence to burst”: the affected possessor becomes an “of” phrase attached to “paunch”, and the thing made to burst stands before the infinitive rather than after it.

> 76 Quindi non terra, ma peccato ed onta  
> 77 guadagnerà, per sè tanto più grave,  
> 78 quanto più lieve simil danno conta.  

Thence shall he win, not land, but sin and shame, for himself so much the more grievous, as he the more lightly counts such wrong.

- *non terra, ma peccato ed onta guadagnerà* ‘not land, but sin and shame he will gain’ → “shall he win, not land, but sin and shame”: the future verb moves before its objects, the auxiliary precedes the subject after “Thence”, and ‘gain’ becomes “win”.
- *tanto più grave* ‘so much more heavy’ → “so much the more grievous”: English adds the comparative marker “the” and renders weight figuratively as grievousness.
- *quanto più lieve simil danno conta* ‘by how much lighter such harm he reckons’ → “as he the more lightly counts such wrong”: “as” introduces the answering term of the comparison; the adjective expressing the weight assigned to the harm becomes an adverb modifying “counts”. The object follows the verb, and ‘harm’ is rendered as moral “wrong”.

> 79 L' altro, che già uscì preso di nave,  
> 80 veggio vender sua figlia e patteggiarne,  
> 81 come fanno i corsar dell' altre schiave.  

The other, who once came forth a captive from a ship, I see selling his daughter, and haggling over her, as pirates do with other bondwomen.

- *già* ‘already’ → “once”: the adverb marks an occasion in the past rather than completion by a given time.
- *uscì preso di nave* ‘went out captured from ship’ → “came forth a captive from a ship”: outward motion becomes “came forth”, the participle becomes a noun phrase, and “ship” takes an indefinite article.
- *vender sua figlia e patteggiarne* ‘to sell his daughter and to bargain about her’ → “selling his daughter, and haggling over her”: the two infinitives dependent on the verb of seeing become participles; “haggling” is more pointed than ‘bargaining’, and “over her” spells out the reference of the pronominal particle.
- *come fanno i corsar* ‘as do the pirates’ → “as pirates do”: the generic plural loses its article and moves before the verb.
- *dell’ altre schiave* ‘of the other female slaves’ → “with other bondwomen”: English uses “with” after “do”, omits the definite article, and expresses the feminine of the Italian noun lexically.

> 82 O avarizia, che puoi tu più farne,  
> 83 poscia ch' hai lo mio sangue a te sì tratto,  
> 84 che non si cura della propria carne?  

O avarice, what more canst thou do to us, since thou hast so drawn my race to thee, that it hath no care of its own flesh?

- *che puoi tu più farne* ‘what canst thou more do to us’ → “what more canst thou do to us”: “more” moves next to “what”; the enclitic attached to the infinitive is rendered by the separate phrase “to us”.
- *hai lo mio sangue a te sì tratto* ‘hast my blood to thee so drawn’ → “thou hast so drawn my race to thee”: “race” again renders ‘blood’ as descent. The participle and its adverb move next to the auxiliary, before the object and its destination.
- *non si cura della propria carne* ‘does not concern itself with its own flesh’ → “it hath no care of its own flesh”: the reflexive verb becomes “hath” with the noun “care”, and the verbal negative becomes “no” modifying that noun.

## Lines 85–117 — Captivity, Vengeance, and Cries Against Avarice

> 85 Perchè men paia il mal futuro e il fatto,  

In order that the ill to come and past, may seem less,

- *Perchè* ‘so that’ → “In order that”: the conjunction of purpose is expanded.
- *men paia* ‘less may seem’ → “may seem less”: the predicate moves after its subject, and the comparative follows the verb; “may” carries the force of the subjunctive.
- *il mal futuro e il fatto* ‘the evil future and the one done’ → “the ill to come and past”: the first adjective becomes an infinitive, and the second, an independently used participle with its own article, becomes a second modifier of “ill”.

> 86 veggio in Alagna entrar lo fiordaliso,  

I see the fleur-de-lys enter Alagna,

- *in Alagna entrar lo fiordaliso* ‘into Alagna enter the fleur-de-lys’ → “the fleur-de-lys enter Alagna”: the subject of the infinitive comes first, and the place follows the verb as its direct object rather than as a prepositional phrase.

> 87 e nel vicario suo Cristo esser catto.  

and in his vicar Christ made captive.

- *nel vicario suo* ‘in the vicar his’ → “in his vicar”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *esser catto* ‘to be captured’ → “made captive”: the explicit passive infinitive is replaced by a participle with an adjective.

> 88 Veggiolo un' altra volta esser deriso;  

A second time I see him mocked;

- *Veggiolo un' altra volta* ‘I see him another time’ → “A second time I see him”: the time phrase moves to the front, and “second” specifies the repetition.
- *esser deriso* ‘to be mocked’ → “mocked”: the infinitive of the passive auxiliary is left unexpressed.

> 89 veggio rinnovellar l' aceto e il fele,  

I see the vinegar and the gall renewed,

- *rinnovellar l' aceto e il fele* ‘be renewed the vinegar and the gall’ → “the vinegar and the gall renewed”: the infinitive with passive sense becomes a participle and follows the two nouns.

> 90 e tra vivi ladroni esser anciso.  

and him slain between living thieves.

- *tra vivi ladroni esser anciso* ‘between living thieves to be slain’ → “him slain between living thieves”: the understood subject of the infinitive is made explicit, the passive auxiliary is not expressed, and the place phrase moves to the end.

> 91 Veggio il nuovo Pilato sì crudele,  
> 92 che ciò nol sazia, ma, senza decreto,  
> 93 porta nel tempio le cupide vele.  

I see the new Pilate so cruel, that this sateth him not, but, lawlessly, he bears his greedy sails into the temple.

- *nol sazia* ‘not him sates’ → “sateth him not”: the object and the negative both follow the verb.
- *senza decreto* ‘without decree’ → “lawlessly”: a prepositional phrase becomes an adverb, interpreting the absence of a decree as lack of lawful authority.
- *porta nel tempio le cupide vele* ‘bears into the temple the greedy sails’ → “he bears his greedy sails into the temple”: the object precedes the destination, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 94 O Signor mio, quando sarò io lieto  
> 95 a veder la vendetta, che, nascosa,  
> 96 fa dolce l' ira tua nel tuo segreto?  

O my Lord, when shall I rejoice to see the vengeance, which, being hidden, maketh sweet thine anger in thy secret counsel?

- *Signor mio* ‘Lord mine’ → “my Lord”: the possessive comes first.
- *sarò io lieto* ‘shall I be glad’ → “shall I rejoice”: a verb and predicate adjective become a single verb of feeling.
- *nascosa* ‘hidden’ → “being hidden”: “being” is supplied before the participle.
- *l' ira tua* ‘the anger thine’ → “thine anger”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *nel tuo segreto* ‘in thy secret’ → “in thy secret counsel”: “counsel” is supplied as the noun, making “secret” its adjective.

> 97 Ciò ch' io dicea di quell' unica sposa  
> 98 dello Spirito Santo, e che ti fece  
> 99 verso me volger per alcuna chiosa,  

What I was saying of that only Bride of the Holy Ghost, and which made thee turn toward me for some gloss,

- *Ciò ch' io dicea* ‘that which I was saying’ → “What I was saying”: a single relative does duty for antecedent and relative together.
- *ti fece verso me volger* ‘thee made toward me turn’ → “made thee turn toward me”: the object follows the causative verb, and the direction follows the infinitive.

> 100 tant' è risposta a tutte nostre prece,  
> 101 quanto il dì dura; ma, quand' e' s' annotta,  
> 102 contrario suon prendemo in quella vece.  

so much is the answer to all our prayers, as long as the day lasts; but when the night cometh, a contrary sound we take up instead of that.

- *tant' è risposta* ‘so much is answer’ → “so much is the answer”: the definite article is supplied before the predicate noun.
- *quanto* ‘as much as’ → “as long as”: the second half of the comparison is made explicitly temporal.
- *quand' e' s' annotta* ‘when it grows dark’ → “when the night cometh”: the impersonal verb becomes a personal verb with “the night” as its subject.
- *contrario suon* ‘contrary sound’ → “a contrary sound”: the indefinite article is supplied.
- *prendemo* ‘we take’ → “we take up”: a particle is added to the verb.
- *in quella vece* ‘in that stead’ → “instead of that”: the demonstrative becomes the complement of “instead of”.

> 103 Noi ripetiam Pigmalione allotta,  
> 104 cui traditore e ladro e patricida  
> 105 fece la voglia sua dell' oro ghiotta;  

Then we rehearse Pygmalion, whom insatiate lust of gold made traitor, thief, and parricide,

- *Noi ripetiam Pigmalione allotta* ‘we repeat Pygmalion then’ → “Then we rehearse Pygmalion”: the time adverb moves to the front, and “rehearse” renders ‘repeat’ in the sense of recounting.
- *cui traditore e ladro e patricida fece* ‘whom traitor and thief and parricide made’ → “whom” … “made traitor, thief, and parricide”: the verb comes before the predicate nouns, and the first ‘and’ is dropped.
- *la voglia sua dell' oro ghiotta* ‘the desire his for gold greedy’ → “insatiate lust of gold”: the subject moves before the verb; the possessive disappears, ‘desire’ is strengthened to “lust”, and the final adjective is brought forward and rendered “insatiate”.

> 106 e la miseria dell' avaro Mida,  
> 107 che seguì alla sua domanda ingorda,  
> 108 per la qual sempre convien che si rida.  

and the misery of the avaricious Midas, which followed his greedy request, because of which ’tis right we forever laugh.

- *che seguì alla sua domanda ingorda* ‘which followed upon his request greedy’ → “which followed his greedy request”: “followed” takes a direct object, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *per la qual* ‘for which’ → “because of which”: the causal sense of the preposition is made explicit.
- *sempre convien che si rida* ‘always it is fitting that one laugh’ → “’tis right we forever laugh”: the time adverb moves into the subordinate clause and becomes “forever”; the impersonal main verb becomes a copula and adjective, the conjunction is dropped, and impersonal *si* becomes “we”.

> 109 Del folle Acam ciascun poi si ricorda,  
> 110 come furò le spoglie, sì che l' ira  
> 111 di Josuè qui par ch' ancor lo morda.  

The mad Achan then each one recalls, how he stole the spoils, so that Joshua’s wrath seems here yet to bite him.

- *Del folle Acam … si ricorda* ‘of the mad Achan … remembers’ → “The mad Achan” … “recalls”: the reflexive verb governing a phrase with *di* becomes a transitive verb with a direct object.
- *ciascun poi* ‘each one then’ → “then each one”: the adverb precedes the subject.
- *l' ira di Josuè* ‘the wrath of Joshua’ → “Joshua’s wrath”: a preceding possessive replaces the following prepositional phrase.
- *qui par* ‘here seems’ → “seems here”: the adverb follows the verb.
- *ch' ancor lo morda* ‘that it still bite him’ → “yet to bite him”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an infinitive, with the object after the verb.

> 112 Indi accusiam col marito Safira;  

Then we accuse Sapphira and her husband;

- *col marito Safira* ‘with the husband Sapphira’ → “Sapphira and her husband”: the accompanying phrase becomes a second, coordinated object after the first, and the article becomes a possessive.

> 113 lodiamo i calci ch' ebbe Eliodoro;  

we praise the kicks which Heliodorus had;

- *ebbe Eliodoro* ‘had Heliodorus’ → “Heliodorus had”: the subject moves before the verb.

> 114 ed in infamia tutto il monte gira  
> 115 Polinestor ch' ancise Polidoro.  

and all the mount doth circle in infamy Polymnestor who slew Polydorus.

- *in infamia tutto il monte gira Polinestor* ‘in infamy all the mount goes round Polymnestor’ → “all the mount doth circle in infamy Polymnestor”: the Italian construction takes Polymnestor as the subject who goes round the whole mount; the English wording instead makes “all the mount” read as the subject of transitive “doth circle”, with “in infamy” moved after the verb.

> 116 Ultimamente ci si grida: ‘Crasso,  
> 117 dicci, chè il sai, di che sapore è l' oro?’  

Last of all here we cry: ‘Crassus, tell us, for thou knowest, of what savour is gold?’

- *Ultimamente* ‘lastly’ → “Last of all”: the adverb is expanded into a phrase.
- *ci si grida* ‘here one cries’ → “here we cry”: the impersonal construction is rendered in the first-person plural.
- *il sai* ‘thou knowest it’ → “thou knowest”: the object pronoun is dropped.
- *l' oro* ‘the gold’ → “gold”: English uses no article for the substance in general.

## Lines 118–151 — The Mountain Quakes and the Shades Sing

> 118 Talor parla l' un alto e l' altro basso,  
> 119 secondo l' affezion ch' a dir ci sprona,  
> 120 ora a maggiore, ed ora a minor passo;  

Sometimes we discourse, the one loud the other low, according to the impulse which spurs us to speak, now with greater, now with lesser force;

- *parla l' un alto e l' altro basso* ‘one speaks loud and the other low’ → “we discourse, the one loud the other low”: the singular verb with its subject after it becomes a first-person plural verb; the two speakers are distinguished after the verb, without a conjunction.
- *affezion* ‘feeling’ → “impulse”: the feeling is named by its power to move the speaker.
- *ch' a dir ci sprona* ‘which to speak us spurs’ → “which spurs us to speak”: the finite verb comes before its object, and the infinitive moves to the end.
- *ora a maggiore, ed ora a minor passo* ‘now at greater, and now at lesser pace’ → “now with greater, now with lesser force”: degree of force replaces pace, with a corresponding change of preposition; the conjunction is dropped.

> 121 però al ben che il dì ci si ragiona,  
> 122 dianzi non er' io sol; ma qui da presso  
> 123 non alzava la voce altra persona.”  

therefore at the good we tell of here by day, I was not alone before, but here, near by, no other person was raising his voice.”

- *che il dì ci si ragiona* ‘that by day one speaks of here’ → “we tell of here by day”: the impersonal construction becomes first-person plural, the relative is unexpressed, and the time phrase moves from before the verb to the end.
- *dianzi non er' io sol* ‘before was I not alone’ → “I was not alone before”: the subject comes before the verb and the time adverb moves to the end.
- *non alzava la voce altra persona* ‘was not raising the voice any other person’ → “no other person was raising his voice”: the subject moves before the verb and carries the negative; a possessive replaces the definite article, and the progressive renders the imperfect.

> 124 Noi eravam partiti già da esso,  
> 125 e brigavam di soperchiar la strada  
> 126 tanto, quanto al poder n' era permesso;  

We were already parted from him, and striving to surmount the way so far as was permitted to our power,

- *eravam partiti già* ‘had departed already’ → “were already parted”: the English presents the resulting separation, with the adverb before the participle.
- *brigavam di* ‘we strove to’ → “striving to”: the finite imperfect becomes a participle taking its subject and auxiliary from the preceding clause.
- *tanto, quanto al poder n' era permesso* ‘as much as to our power was permitted’ → “so far as was permitted to our power”: the correlation of degree is rendered in terms of distance, and the phrase limiting what was permitted moves after the verb.

> 127 quand' io senti', come cosa che cada,  
> 128 tremar lo monte: onde mi prese un gelo,  
> 129 qual prender suol colui che a morte vada.  

when I felt the mountain quake, like a thing which is falling; whereupon a chill gripped me, as is wont to grip him who is going to death.

- *come cosa che cada* ‘like a thing that should fall’ → “like a thing which is falling”: the comparison moves after what was felt; an indicative progressive replaces the subjunctive of the comparison.
- *tremar lo monte* ‘quake the mountain’ → “the mountain quake”: the subject of the infinitive comes before it.
- *mi prese un gelo* ‘me seized a frost’ → “a chill gripped me”: the subject moves to the front and the object to the end; the cold is named as a sensation.
- *qual prender suol colui* ‘such as to seize is wont that man’ → “as is wont to grip him”: the verb of custom precedes its infinitive, and a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *che a morte vada* ‘who to death should go’ → “who is going to death”: the subjunctive characterizing such a man becomes an indicative progressive, followed by the destination.

> 130 Certo non si scotea sì forte Delo,  
> 131 pria che Latona in lei facesse il nido  
> 132 a partorir li due occhi del cielo.  

Of a surety, Delos was not shaken so violently, ere Latona made her nest therein to give birth to heaven’s two eyes.

- *non si scotea sì forte Delo* ‘did not shake so strongly Delos’ → “Delos was not shaken so violently”: the subject moves to the front; the pronominal imperfect becomes an English passive, and strength of movement is expressed as violence.
- *pria che Latona in lei facesse il nido* ‘before Latona in her should make the nest’ → “ere Latona made her nest therein”: the temporal conjunction takes a simple past rather than a subjunctive; the definite article becomes a possessive, and the reference to the island becomes an adverb placed last.
- *li due occhi del cielo* ‘the two eyes of heaven’ → “heaven’s two eyes”: the following *of*-phrase becomes a preceding possessive, displacing the article.

> 133 Poi cominciò da tutte parti un grido  
> 134 tal che il maestro in ver di me si feo,  
> 135 dicendo: “Non dubbiar, mentr' io ti guido.”  

Then began on all sides a shout, such that the Master drew toward me, saying: “Fear not while I do guide thee.”

- *da tutte parti* ‘from all sides’ → “on all sides”: the preposition indicates location rather than the directions from which the sound comes.
- *in ver di me si feo* ‘toward me made himself’ → “drew toward me”: an intransitive verb of motion replaces the reflexive idiom and precedes the direction phrase.
- *Non dubbiar* ‘do not doubt’ → “Fear not”: Okey takes the verb in the sense of being afraid rather than uncertain.
- *mentr' io ti guido* ‘while I thee guide’ → “while I do guide thee”: English adds the auxiliary and places the object after the main verb.

> 136 “*Gloria in excelsis,*” tutti, “*Deo,*”  
> 137 dicean, per quel ch' io da' vicin compresi,  
> 138 onde intender lo grido si poteo.  

“*Gloria in excelsis Deo,*” all were saying, by what I understood from those near by, whose cry could be heard.

- *“Gloria in excelsis,” tutti, “Deo,” dicean* ‘“Gloria in excelsis,” all, “Deo,” were saying’ → ““*Gloria in excelsis Deo,*” all were saying”: the subject no longer interrupts the Latin words; the progressive renders the imperfect.
- *per quel ch' io da' vicin compresi* ‘by that which I from the near ones understood’ → “by what I understood from those near by”: a fused relative replaces demonstrative and relative, the verb precedes the source phrase, and the substantivized adjective becomes a pronoun with an adverbial modifier.
- *onde intender lo grido si poteo* ‘whence one could make out the cry’ → “whose cry could be heard”: the connective becomes a possessive relative referring to the nearby speakers; the impersonal construction becomes passive, with the cry as subject, and making out the sound becomes hearing it.

> 139 Noi ci restammo immobili e sospesi,  
> 140 come i pastor che prima udir quel canto,  
> 141 fin che il tremar cessò, ed ei compièsi.  

Motionless we stood, and in suspense, like the shepherds who first heard that hymn, until the quaking ceased and it was ended.

- *Noi ci restammo immobili e sospesi* ‘we remained motionless and suspended’ → “Motionless we stood, and in suspense”: the first adjective is brought to the front; the pronominal verb of remaining is rendered by a verb of posture, and the second adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *canto* ‘song’ → “hymn”: the English noun is more specific.
- *fin che il tremar cessò* ‘until the trembling ceased’ → “until the quaking ceased”: an English verbal noun denoting the quake renders the substantivized infinitive.
- *ei compièsi* ‘it came to completion’ → “it was ended”: the pronominal verb becomes passive.

> 142 Poi ripigliammo nostro cammin santo,  
> 143 guardando l' ombre che giacean per terra,  
> 144 tornate già in su l' usato pianto.  

Then we took up again our holy way, looking at the shades, that lay on the ground already returned to their wonted plaint.

- *ripigliammo* ‘we resumed’ → “we took up again”: a phrasal verb and adverb express what the prefixed verb says in one word.
- *cammin santo* ‘way holy’ → “holy way”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *per terra* ‘on earth’ → “on the ground”: the English locative idiom uses a definite noun phrase.
- *tornate già* ‘returned already’ → “already returned”: the adverb precedes the participle.
- *in su l' usato pianto* ‘to the accustomed weeping’ → “to their wonted plaint”: a single preposition renders the compound expression, a possessive replaces the article, and lament takes the place of weeping.

> 145 Nulla ignoranza mai con tanta guerra  
> 146 mi fe' desideroso di sapere,  
> 147 se la memoria mia in ciò non erra,  
> 148 quanta pare' mi allor pensando avere;  

No ignorance, if my memory err not in this, did ever with so great assault give me yearning for knowledge as I then seemed to have, while pondering;

- *mai* ‘ever’ → “did ever”: the auxiliary is supplied in the negative construction and carries the past tense of the main verb.
- *con tanta guerra* ‘with so much war’ → “with so great assault”: sustained conflict becomes an assault, and quantity becomes degree.
- *mi fe' desideroso di sapere* ‘made me desirous to know’ → “give me yearning for knowledge”: making someone desirous becomes giving him a yearning; the infinitive becomes a noun governed by a preposition.
- *se la memoria mia in ciò non erra* ‘if my memory in this does not err’ → “if my memory err not in this”: the condition moves up to follow “No ignorance”; the possessive precedes the noun, the indicative becomes an English subjunctive, and the negative and the qualification follow the verb.
- *quanta pare' mi allor pensando avere* ‘as much as I seemed to myself then, thinking, to have’ → “as I then seemed to have, while pondering”: the degree comparison is reduced to “as”, the reference to the speaker as judge is left implicit, and the time adverb precedes the finite verb; the gerund moves after the infinitive and gains a temporal conjunction.

> 149 nè per la fretta domandarn' er' oso,  
> 150 nè per me lì potea cosa vedere:  

nor by reason of our haste was I bold to ask; nor of myself could I see aught there:

- *per la fretta* ‘because of the haste’ → “by reason of our haste”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *domandarn' er' oso* ‘to ask about it was I bold’ → “was I bold to ask”: the infinitive follows the expression of boldness, and its pronominal complement is left unexpressed.
- *lì potea cosa vedere* ‘there could I anything see’ → “could I see aught there”: the object follows the infinitive and the place adverb moves to the end.

> 151 così m' andava timido e pensoso.  

thus I went on timid and pensive.

- *m' andava* ‘I was going my way’ → “I went on”: the pronominal imperfect is rendered by a simple past with an adverb marking continuation.

## Summary — Close Echoes and Shifts of Emphasis

Okey often keeps Dante’s repetitions and even his inversions: the two instances of *voler* are answered by “a better will” and “the will”, each *per ammenda* by “for amends”, and *Figlio fu’ io* by “Son was I”. Elsewhere he changes the order without changing the image. In *trassi dell’ acqua non sazia la spugna*, the sponge comes last; in “I drew the sponge from the water unfilled”, it comes immediately after the verb, and its unfilled state has the final emphasis. Postposed adjectives and possessives regularly move before their nouns, as in *cammin santo*, “holy way”, and *il duca mio*, “my Leader”.

Changes of construction recur especially after verbs of perception: the infinitives *piangere e lagnarsi* become “weeping and complaining”, while *esser deriso* becomes simply “mocked”. An impersonal construction can give way to a personal one, as when *ci si grida*, ‘here one cries’, becomes “here we cry”. Some reorderings go further than a change of idiom: where Dante makes the head the subject of promotion to the crown, Okey reverses the relation in “to my son’s head the widowed crown was promoted”.

The English sometimes names what the Italian only indicates: the direction *in fuor*, ‘outward’, becomes a point of approach, “the edge”, and *nel tuo segreto*, ‘in thy secret’, becomes “in thy secret counsel”. Elsewhere a detail is left implicit: the object of *il sai*, ‘thou knowest it’, drops out in “thou knowest”. A choice of words can also shift the image without expanding it: *passo*, ‘pace’, becomes “force”, and *sangue*, ‘blood’, becomes “race” where descent is meant.

Two routine adjustments underlie the comparison without usually needing separate notice. English supplies subject pronouns where the Italian verb ending already gives the person, as when *ripigliammo* becomes “we took up again”. Conversely, the Italian article before a possessive is normally left out: *la tua fame* needs only “thy hunger”.
