# Purgatorio 1

## Lines 1–18 — Sailing Toward the Second Realm

> 1 Per correr miglior acqua alza le vele  
> 2 omai la navicella del mio ingegno,  

To course o’er better waters now hoists sail the little bark of my wit,

- *Per correr miglior acqua* ‘to run better water’ → “To course o’er better waters”: the water that is the direct object of the verb in Italian becomes the object of a preposition, and the singular becomes plural.
- *alza le vele omai* ‘hoists the sails now’ → “now hoists sail”: the adverb moves before the verb, and the English sailing phrase uses a singular noun without an article.
- *la navicella* ‘the little vessel’ → “the little bark”: the diminutive ending is rendered by a separate adjective, and “bark” is chosen for the vessel.
- *del mio ingegno* ‘of my inventive faculty’ → “of my wit”: “wit” renders the faculty of invention.

> 3 che lascia retro a sè mar sì crudele.  

leaving behind her a sea so cruel.

- *che lascia retro a sè* ‘which leaves behind itself’ → “leaving behind her”: the relative clause becomes a participial phrase, and the reflexive becomes a feminine personal pronoun referring to the vessel.
- *mar sì crudele* ‘sea so cruel’ → “a sea so cruel”: English supplies the indefinite article.

> 4 E canterò di quel secondo regno,  

And I will sing of that second realm,

- Literal.

> 5 dove l' umano spirito si purga  
> 6 e di salire al ciel diventa degno.  

where the human spirit is purged and becomes worthy to ascend to Heaven.

- *si purga* ‘purges itself’ → “is purged”: the Italian reflexive construction is rendered as a passive.
- *e di salire al ciel diventa degno* ‘and of ascending to the heaven becomes worthy’ → “and becomes worthy to ascend to Heaven”: the infinitive phrase follows rather than precedes the verb and adjective; English uses a *to*-infinitive as the complement of “worthy” and no article before “Heaven”.

> 7 Ma qui la morta poesì risurga,  
> 8 o sante Muse, poichè vostro sono,  

But here let dead poesy rise up again, O holy Muses, since yours am I,

- *la morta poesì risurga* ‘may the dead poesy rise again’ → “let dead poesy rise up again”: English puts “let” before the noun to express the wish conveyed by the subjunctive, omits the definite article, and expands the verb to “rise up again”.

> 9 e qui Calliopè alquanto surga,  

and here let Calliope rise somewhat,

- *Calliopè alquanto surga* ‘may Calliope somewhat rise’ → “let Calliope rise somewhat”: “let” with the bare infinitive renders the subjunctive, and the adverb moves after the verb.

> 10 seguitando il mio canto con quel suono,  

accompanying my song with that strain

- *seguitando* ‘following’ → “accompanying”: the participle is retained, but the verb is given the sense of musical accompaniment.
- *suono* ‘sound’ → “strain”: English chooses a specifically musical term.

> 11 di cui le Piche misere sentiro  
> 12 lo colpo tal che disperar perdono.  

whose stroke the wretched Pies felt so that they despaired of pardon.

- *di cui … lo colpo* ‘of which … the blow’ → “whose stroke”: the possessive relative replaces the prepositional phrase and the article, bringing the object noun forward from its place after the verb.
- *le Piche misere* ‘the Pies wretched’ → “the wretched Pies”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *tal che* ‘such that’ → “so that”: Italian qualifies the blow as such that a consequence followed; English gives the consequence after “felt” without an adjective qualifying “stroke”.
- *disperar perdono* ‘they despaired pardon’ → “they despaired of pardon”: English introduces the object of despair with a preposition.

> 13 Dolce color d' oriental zaffiro,  
> 14 che s' accoglieva nel sereno aspetto  
> 15 dell' aer puro infino al primo giro,  

Sweet hue of orient sapphire which was gathering on the clear forehead of the sky, pure even to the first circle,

- *color* ‘color’ → “hue”: English chooses a synonym.
- *s’ accoglieva* ‘was collecting itself’ → “was gathering”: an English intransitive progressive renders the Italian reflexive imperfect.
- *nel sereno aspetto* ‘in the serene aspect’ → “on the clear forehead”: “clear” takes the atmospheric sense of the adjective, and “forehead” makes the more general word for appearance or countenance a particular surface, hence “on” rather than ‘in’.
- *dell’ aer puro* ‘of the pure air’ → “of the sky, pure”: “sky” replaces ‘air’, and the adjective is set apart rather than incorporated into the English noun phrase.

> 16 agli occhi miei ricominciò diletto,  

to mine eyes restored delight,

- *agli occhi miei* ‘to the eyes of mine’ → “to mine eyes”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *ricominciò diletto* ‘began delight anew’ → “restored delight”: English expresses the renewed delight by a verb of giving something back.

> 17 tosto ch' i' uscii fuor dell' aura morta,  

soon as I issued forth from the dead air

- *tosto ch’* ‘as soon as’ → “soon as”: English uses the shortened form of the conjunction.
- *i’ uscii fuor* ‘I went out’ → “I issued forth”: the verb and its directional adverb are recast in a more formal phrase.
- *dell’ aura morta* ‘from the air dead’ → “from the dead air”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 18 che m' avea contristati gli occhi e il petto.  

which had afflicted eyes and heart.

- *m’ avea contristati* ‘had saddened for me’ → “had afflicted”: “afflicted” is broader than ‘saddened’; the pronoun marking the person affected is omitted, and the English participle does not show agreement with the following objects.
- *gli occhi e il petto* ‘the eyes and the breast’ → “eyes and heart”: both articles are omitted, and the breast is rendered figuratively as the heart.

## Lines 19–51 — The Four Stars and the Solitary Old Man

> 19 Lo bel pianeta che ad amar conforta  
> 20 faceva tutto rider l' oriente,  
> 21 velando i Pesci ch' erano in sua scorta.  

The fair planet which hearteneth to love was making the whole East to laugh, veiling the Fishes that were in her train.

- *che ad amar conforta* ‘which to love hearteneth’ → “which hearteneth to love”: the infinitive follows the verb that governs it.
- *faceva tutto rider l' oriente* ‘made all to laugh the East’ → “was making the whole East to laugh”: the progressive expresses the imperfect; “the whole East” gathers together the object and its modifier before the infinitive.
- *in sua scorta* ‘in its escort’ → “in her train”: the possessive is made personal and feminine, and “train” suggests attendants rather than expressly an escort.

> 22 Io mi volsi a man destra, e posi mente  
> 23 all' altro polo, e vidi quattro stelle  
> 24 non viste mai fuor che alla prima gente.  

I turned me to the right hand, and set my mind on the other pole, and saw four stars never yet seen save by the first people.

- *Io mi volsi* ‘I myself turned’ → “I turned me”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.
- *a man destra* ‘to hand right’ → “to the right hand”: the article is supplied and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *posi mente* ‘set mind’ → “set my mind”: the possessive is supplied.
- *non viste mai* ‘not seen ever’ → “never yet seen”: the negatives are gathered into “never” before the participle, and “yet” is supplied.
- *fuor che alla prima gente* ‘except to the first people’ → “save by the first people”: those to whom the stars were visible become the agents of “seen”.

> 25 Goder pareva il ciel di lor fiammelle.  

The heavens seemed to rejoice in their flames.

- *Goder pareva il ciel* ‘to rejoice seemed the heaven’ → “The heavens seemed to rejoice”: the singular becomes plural, and the postposed subject comes first, with the infinitive last.
- *di lor fiammelle* ‘in their little flames’ → “in their flames”: the diminutive is lost.

> 26 O settentrional vedovo sito,  
> 27 poichè privato sei di mirar quelle!  

O Northern widowed clime, since thou art bereft of beholding them!

- *sito* ‘region’ → “clime”: a more specifically geographical term replaces the general one.
- *privato sei* ‘bereft thou art’ → “thou art bereft”: the participle follows the finite verb.
- *di mirar quelle* ‘of beholding those’ → “of beholding them”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.

> 28 Com' io dal loro sguardo fui partito,  
> 29 un poco me volgendo all' altro polo  
> 30 là onde il Carro già era sparito,  

When I was parted from gazing at them, turning me a little to the other pole, there whence the Wain had already disappeared,

- *io dal loro sguardo fui partito* ‘I from the sight of them was parted’ → “I was parted from gazing at them”: the verb comes before its complement; the noun becomes an act of gazing, with the stars expressly its object.
- *un poco me volgendo* ‘a little me turning’ → “turning me a little”: the participle comes first, followed by the reflexive pronoun and then the expression of degree.
- *il Carro* ‘the Cart’ → “the Wain”: the conventional English designation replaces the ordinary noun.
- *già era sparito* ‘already was disappeared’ → “had already disappeared”: English forms the pluperfect with “had” rather than ‘was’ and places the adverb after the auxiliary.

> 31 vidi presso di me un veglio solo,  
> 32 degno di tanta riverenza in vista,  
> 33 che più non dee a padre alcun figliuolo.  

I saw near me an old man solitary, worthy of such great reverence in his mien, that no son owes more to a father.

- *tanta riverenza* ‘so much reverence’ → “such great reverence”: quantity is expressed as degree.
- *in vista* ‘in appearance’ → “in his mien”: the possessive is supplied, and “mien” specifies outward bearing.
- *non dee a padre alcun figliuolo* ‘owes not to father any son’ → “no son owes” … “to a father”: the negative passes from the verb to the subject, the subject moves before the verb, and the recipient moves to the end with an article supplied.

> 34 Lunga la barba e di pel bianco mista  
> 35 portava, a' suoi capegli simigliante,  
> 36 de' quai cadeva al petto doppia lista.  

Long he wore his beard and mingled with white hair, like unto his locks of which a double list fell on his breast.

- *Lunga la barba e di pel bianco mista / portava* ‘long the beard and with hair white mingled he wore’ → “Long he wore his beard and mingled with white hair”: “Long” remains in front, but the verb moves before its object; a possessive replaces the article, “mingled” precedes its complement, and “white” precedes “hair”.
- *a' suoi capegli simigliante* ‘unto his hairs like’ → “like unto his locks”: the adjective comes before its complement, and the plural “locks” denotes groups of hairs.
- *cadeva al petto doppia lista* ‘fell to the breast double strip’ → “a double list fell on his breast”: the subject moves before the verb and gains an article; “on” replaces the directional ‘to’, and a possessive replaces the article.

> 37 Li raggi delle quattro luci sante  
> 38 fregiavan sì la sua faccia di lume,  
> 39 ch' io 'l vedea come il sol fosse davante.  

The rays of the four holy lights adorned his face so with brightness, that I beheld him as were the sun before him.

- *quattro luci sante* ‘four lights holy’ → “four holy lights”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *fregiavan sì la sua faccia di lume* ‘adorned so his face with light’ → “adorned his face so with brightness”: the degree word follows the object, and “brightness” renders ‘light’ as a quality.
- *io 'l vedea* ‘I him saw’ → “I beheld him”: the object follows the verb, and “beheld” is more marked than ‘saw’.
- *come il sol fosse davante* ‘as if the sun were in front’ → “as were the sun before him”: inversion marks the hypothetical comparison, and “him” supplies the reference point for ‘in front’.

> 40 “Chi siete voi, che contro al cieco fiume  
> 41 fuggito avete la prigione eterna?”  
> 42 diss' ei, movendo quell' oneste piume.  

“Who are ye that against the dark stream have fled the eternal prison?” said he, moving those venerable plumes.

- *al cieco fiume* ‘the blind river’ → “the dark stream”: “dark” interprets the transferred adjective ‘blind’, and “stream” is less specific than ‘river’. The article contained in *al* is retained, but not its preposition, since “against” takes its object directly.
- *fuggito avete* ‘fled have’ → “have fled”: the auxiliary precedes the participle.
- *la prigione eterna* ‘the prison eternal’ → “the eternal prison”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *oneste* ‘honourable’ → “venerable”: the adjective expresses the reverence commanded rather than the dignity possessed.

> 43 “Chi v' ha guidati? o chi vi fu lucerna,  
> 44 uscendo fuor della profonda notte  
> 45 che sempre nera fa la valle inferna?  

“Who hath guided you? or who was a lamp unto you issuing forth from the deep night that ever maketh black the infernal vale?

- *v' ha guidati* ‘you hath guided’ → “hath guided you”: the object follows the participle, which in English does not mark agreement with the plural object.
- *vi fu lucerna* ‘unto you was lamp’ → “was a lamp unto you”: the indirect object moves after the predicate noun, which gains an article.
- *uscendo fuor della profonda notte* ‘going out forth from the deep night’ → “issuing forth from the deep night”: “issuing” expresses motion out in a single verb.
- *sempre nera fa* ‘ever black makes’ → “ever maketh black”: the result adjective follows the verb.
- *la valle inferna* ‘the vale infernal’ → “the infernal vale”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 46 Son le leggi d' abisso così rotte?  
> 47 o è mutato in ciel nuovo consiglio,  
> 48 che dannati venite alle mie grotte?”  

Are the laws of the pit thus broken, or is there some new counsel changed in Heaven that being damned ye come to my rocks?”

- *d' abisso* ‘of abyss’ → “of the pit”: “pit” replaces ‘abyss’ and takes a definite article.
- *è mutato in ciel nuovo consiglio* ‘is changed in heaven new counsel’ → “is there some new counsel changed in Heaven”: the existential construction supplies “there” and the determiner “some”, placing the subject before the participle and the place phrase.
- *dannati venite* ‘damned ye come’ → “being damned ye come”: “being” makes the participial construction explicit.
- *grotte* ‘grottoes’ → “rocks”: the translation takes the word in the sense of rocky places rather than hollows.

> 49 Lo duca mio allor mi diè di piglio,  
> 50 e con parole e con mano e con cenni,  
> 51 riverenti mi fe' le gambe e il ciglio.  

Then did my Leader lay hold on me, and with words, and with hand, and with signs, made reverent my knees and brow.

- *Lo duca mio allor mi diè di piglio* ‘the leader mine then on me took hold’ → “Then did my Leader lay hold on me”: the time adverb comes first, followed by the auxiliary “did”; the possessive precedes the noun, and the idiom “lay hold on” puts the personal object last.
- *riverenti mi fe' le gambe e il ciglio* ‘reverent for me he made the legs and the brow’ → “made reverent my knees and brow”: the verb precedes the result adjective, which no longer marks plural agreement; the personal dative becomes a possessive shared by both nouns, and “knees” specifies a part of the legs.

## Lines 52–84 — Virgil’s Plea for Passage

> 52 Poscia rispose lui: “Da me non venni.  

Then answered him: “Of myself I came not.

- *Da me* ‘from myself’ → “Of myself”: the preposition changes in the English expression for acting on one’s own initiative.
- *non venni* ‘not I came’ → “I came not”: the negative follows the verb.

> 53 Donna scese del ciel, per li cui preghi  
> 54 della mia compagnia costui sovvenni.  

A lady came down from Heaven through whose prayers I succoured this man with my company.

- *Donna* ‘lady’ → “A lady”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *del ciel* ‘from the heaven’ → “from Heaven”: the article is dropped.
- *per li cui preghi* ‘through the prayers of whom’ → “through whose prayers”: the relative possessive precedes the noun and takes the place of the article.
- *della mia compagnia costui sovvenni* ‘with my company this one I succoured’ → “I succoured this man with my company”: the verb comes first, followed by its object and then the phrase expressing the means; the demonstrative pronoun becomes a demonstrative with a noun.

> 55 Ma da ch' è tuo voler che più si spieghi  
> 56 di nostra condizion com' ella è vera,  
> 57 esser non puote il mio che a te si neghi.  

But since it is thy will that more be unfolded of our state, how it truly is, my will it cannot be that thou be denied.

- *da ch' è tuo voler* ‘since it is thy willing’ → “since it is thy will”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun, and English supplies the anticipatory subject.
- *che più si spieghi* ‘that more may unfold itself’ → “that more be unfolded”: the passive use of the pronominal verb is rendered by an English passive subjunctive.
- *com' ella è vera* ‘how it is true’ → “how it truly is”: the predicate adjective becomes an adverb placed before the verb.
- *esser non puote il mio* ‘be cannot mine’ → “my will it cannot be”: the noun understood after the possessive pronoun is repeated, the possessive phrase is moved to the front, and the infinitive follows the modal.
- *che a te si neghi* ‘that to thee it be denied’ → “that thou be denied”: the recipient becomes the subject of the passive; the thing denied is left unexpressed.

> 58 Questi non vide mai l' ultima sera,  
> 59 ma per la sua follia le fu sì presso,  
> 60 che molto poco tempo a volger era.  

He hath ne’er seen the last hour, but by his madness was so near to it, that very short time there was to turn.

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “He”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *non vide mai* ‘saw not ever’ → “hath ne’er seen”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, with the negative adverb between auxiliary and participle.
- *l' ultima sera* ‘the last evening’ → “the last hour”: a different division of time replaces ‘evening’.
- *le fu sì presso* ‘to it was so near’ → “was so near to it”: the preceding dative pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase after the adjective.
- *molto poco tempo* ‘very little time’ → “very short time”: duration is expressed as length rather than quantity.
- *a volger era* ‘to turn remained’ → “there was to turn”: the infinitive follows an existential construction with “there” instead of preceding the finite verb.

> 61 Sì come io dissi, fui mandato ad esso  
> 62 per lui campare, e non v' era altra via  
> 63 che questa per la quale io mi son messo.  

Even as I said, I was sent to him to rescue him, and no other way there was but this along which I have set me.

- *per lui campare* ‘in order him to save’ → “to rescue him”: the infinitive alone marks purpose, and its object follows it.
- *non v' era altra via* ‘not there was another way’ → “no other way there was”: the negative is attached to the noun phrase, which moves before the existential verb.
- *che questa* ‘than this’ → “but this”: English uses an expression of exception.
- *per la quale io mi son messo* ‘by which I myself am set’ → “along which I have set me”: the preposition specifies movement along a way; the reflexive perfect takes “have” rather than ‘be’, with the reflexive object after the participle.

> 64 Mostrato ho lui tutta la gente ria;  

I have shown him all the guilty people,

- *Mostrato ho lui* ‘shown have I to him’ → “I have shown him”: the auxiliary precedes the participle, and the recipient is expressed as an unmarked indirect object.
- *tutta la gente ria* ‘all the people wicked’ → “all the guilty people”: the adjective precedes the noun, and ‘wicked’ is rendered as “guilty”.

> 65 ed ora intendo mostrar quelli spirti,  
> 66 che purgan sè sotto la tua balìa.  

and now do purpose showing those spirits that purge them under thy charge.

- *intendo mostrar* ‘I intend to show’ → “do purpose showing”: “do” is supplied, “purpose” renders ‘intend’, and the infinitive becomes an *-ing* complement.
- *purgan sè* ‘purge themselves’ → “purge them”: the simple pronoun is used reflexively instead of the explicitly reflexive form.
- *balìa* ‘authority’ → “charge”: the rendering uses the idiom of being under someone’s charge rather than under their power.

> 67 Come io l' ho tratto, saria lungo a dirti:  

How I have brought him, ’twere long to tell thee:

- *io l' ho tratto* ‘I him have drawn’ → “I have brought him”: ‘drawn’ becomes “brought”, and the object follows the participle.
- *saria lungo* ‘would be long’ → “’twere long”: the conditional is rendered by a hypothetical subjunctive with an anticipatory subject.

> 68 dell' alto scende virtù che m' aiuta  
> 69 conducerlo a vederti ed a udirti.  

Virtue descends from on high which aids me to guide him to see thee and to hear thee.

- *dell' alto scende virtù* ‘from the height descends virtue’ → “Virtue descends from on high”: the postposed subject moves to the front, and the initial phrase becomes an adverbial idiom after the verb.
- *che m' aiuta* ‘which me aids’ → “which aids me”: the object follows the verb.

> 70 Or ti piaccia gradir la sua venuta:  

Now may it please thee to be gracious unto his coming:

- *ti piaccia* ‘thee may it please’ → “may it please thee”: the subjunctive of wish is expressed with a modal, and the dative pronoun follows the verb.
- *gradir la sua venuta* ‘to welcome his coming’ → “to be gracious unto his coming”: a transitive verb becomes a copular phrase with an adjective and a preposition.

> 71 libertà va cercando, che è sì cara,  
> 72 come sa chi per lei vita rifiuta.  

he seeketh freedom, which is so precious, as he knows who giveth up life for her.

- *libertà va cercando* ‘freedom he goes seeking’ → “he seeketh freedom”: the progressive expression becomes a simple present, and the object moves after the verb.
- *sì cara* ‘so dear’ → “so precious”: “precious” renders the adjective in the sense of value.
- *come sa chi* ‘as knows whoever’ → “as he knows who”: the single relative pronoun is resolved into an antecedent before “knows” and a relative after it.
- *per lei vita rifiuta* ‘for her life refuses’ → “giveth up life for her”: refusal is rendered as relinquishment; the verb moves before its object, and the phrase of motive comes last.

> 73 Tu il sai, chè non ti fu per lei amara  
> 74 in Utica la morte, ove lasciasti  
> 75 la vesta che al gran dì sarà sì chiara.  

Thou knowest it; since for her sake death was not bitter to thee in Utica, where thou leftest the raiment which at the great day shall be so bright.

- *il sai* ‘it knowest’ → “knowest it”: the object follows the verb.
- *non ti fu per lei amara in Utica la morte* ‘not to thee was for her bitter in Utica the death’ → “for her sake death was not bitter to thee in Utica”: the phrase of motive comes first and is expanded with “sake”; the subject loses its article and moves before the verb, the negative follows the verb, and the dative follows the adjective.
- *al gran dì* ‘on the great day’ → “at the great day”: the temporal preposition changes.

> 76 Non son gli editti eterni per noi guasti:  

The eternal laws by us are not violated,

- *Non son gli editti eterni per noi guasti* ‘not are the edicts eternal by us broken’ → “The eternal laws by us are not violated”: the subject moves before the auxiliary, with adjective before noun; the agent follows the subject, and the negative follows the auxiliary. ‘Edicts’ becomes the broader “laws”, and their being broken is expressed as being “violated”.

> 77 chè questi vive e Minos me non lega;  

for he doth live and Minos binds me not;

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating conjunction renders the causal subordinator.
- *questi vive* ‘this one lives’ → “he doth live”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative, and the simple present gains the auxiliary “doth”.
- *me non lega* ‘me not binds’ → “binds me not”: both the object and the negative move after the verb.

> 78 ma son del cerchio ove son gli occhi casti  
> 79 di Marzia tua, che in vista ancor ti prega,  
> 80 o santo petto, che per tua la tegni:  

but I am of the circle where are the chaste eyes of thy Marcia, who visibly yet doth pray thee, O holy breast, that thou hold her for thine own:

- *gli occhi casti* ‘the eyes chaste’ → “the chaste eyes”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *di Marzia tua* ‘of Marcia thine’ → “of thy Marcia”: the possessive precedes the name.
- *che in vista ancor ti prega* ‘who in her look still thee entreats’ → “who visibly yet doth pray thee”: the phrase referring to her look becomes an adverb of visibility; “yet” has the sense of ‘still’, “doth” is supplied, and the object follows the verb.
- *per tua la tegni* ‘as thine her thou hold’ → “thou hold her for thine own”: the verb moves before object and complement, and “own” reinforces the possessive.

> 81 per lo suo amore adunque a noi ti piega.  

for love of her then incline thee unto us.

- *per lo suo amore* ‘for her love’ → “for love of her”: the possessive becomes an *of*-phrase, taking her as the object of love.
- *a noi ti piega* ‘to us thyself bend’ → “incline thee unto us”: “incline” renders ‘bend’; the reflexive follows the imperative, and the phrase of direction comes last.

> 82 Lasciane andar per li tuoi sette regni:  

Let us go through thy seven kingdoms:

- Literal.

> 83 grazie riporterò di te a lei,  
> 84 se d' esser mentovato laggiù degni.”  

thanks of thee I will bear back to her, if thou deign to be mentioned there below.”

- *grazie riporterò di te a lei* ‘thanks I shall carry back of thee to her’ → “thanks of thee I will bear back to her”: the phrase “of thee” moves next to “thanks”, and the future ending is expressed by an auxiliary.
- *d' esser mentovato laggiù degni* ‘to be mentioned down there thou deign’ → “thou deign to be mentioned there below”: the finite verb precedes its infinitive complement, and the locative adverb is rendered by two words.

## Lines 85–117 — The Washing and the Girding With a Rush

> 85 “Marzia piacque tanto agli occhi miei,  
> 86 mentre ch' io fui di là,” diss' egli allora,  
> 87 “che quante grazie volse da me, fei.  

“Marcia was so pleasing to mine eyes while I was yonder,” said he then, “that every grace she willed of me I did.

- *piacque tanto* ‘pleased so much’ → “was so pleasing”: the simple past becomes a form of ‘be’ with a participial adjective, and the degree word comes before the adjective.
- *agli occhi miei* ‘to the eyes mine’ → “to mine eyes”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *di là* ‘on the other side’ → “yonder”: a single adverb replaces the prepositional expression.
- *quante grazie* ‘as many graces as’ → “every grace”: the relative expression of quantity becomes a distributive adjective with a singular noun.
- *volse da me* ‘she willed from me’ → “she willed of me”: the preposition changes.

> 88 Or che di là dal mal fiume dimora,  
> 89 più mover non mi può per quella legge  
> 90 che fatta fu quando me n' uscii fuora.  

Now that she dwells beyond the evil stream, no more may she move me, by that law which was made when I thence came forth.

- *di là dal mal fiume dimora* ‘beyond the evil river she dwells’ → “she dwells beyond the evil stream”: the verb comes before the expression of place, and ‘river’ becomes “stream”.
- *più mover non mi può* ‘any longer move me she cannot’ → “no more may she move me”: the negation is gathered into the initial “no more”; the modal precedes the subject and the infinitive, and the object follows the infinitive.
- *fatta fu* ‘made was’ → “was made”: the auxiliary precedes the participle.
- *me n' uscii fuora* ‘I went out from there’ → “I thence came forth”: the pronominal *me* has no separate equivalent; “thence” renders *ne*, and the outward movement expressed by both verb and adverb becomes “came forth”.

> 91 Ma se donna del ciel ti move e regge,  
> 92 come tu di', non c' è mestier lusinghe:  
> 93 bastiti ben che per lei mi richegge.  

But if a heavenly lady moves and directs thee, as thou sayest, no need is there for flattery: let it suffice thee that in her name thou askest me.

- *donna del ciel* ‘lady of heaven’ → “a heavenly lady”: an indefinite article is supplied, and the prepositional phrase becomes a preceding adjective.
- *ti move e regge* ‘thee moves and guides’ → “moves and directs thee”: the shared object follows the two verbs, and *regge* is rendered as direction.
- *non c' è mestier lusinghe* ‘there is no need of flatteries’ → “no need is there for flattery”: the negated noun is brought forward, “for” introduces its complement, and the plural becomes a mass noun.
- *bastiti ben* ‘may it well suffice thee’ → “let it suffice thee”: “let” expresses the force of the subjunctive; *ben* has no separate equivalent.
- *per lei* ‘on her account’ → “in her name”: the translation interprets the phrase as an appeal made in her name.
- *mi richegge* ‘me thou ask’ → “thou askest me”: the object follows the verb, and the Italian subjunctive is rendered by an English indicative.

> 94 Va dunque, e fa che tu costui ricinghe  
> 95 d' un giunco schietto, e che gli lavi il viso  
> 96 sì che ogni sucidume quindi stinghe:  

Go then, and look that thou gird this man with a smooth rush, and that thou bathe his face so that all filth may thence be wiped away:

- *fa che* ‘see to it that’ → “look that”: the injunction takes a different idiomatic form.
- *tu costui ricinghe* ‘thou this one gird again’ → “thou gird this man”: the object follows the verb, the demonstrative pronoun becomes “this man”, and the prefix suggesting renewed girding is not separately rendered.
- *d' un giunco schietto* ‘with a rush smooth’ → “with a smooth rush”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *gli lavi il viso* ‘for him thou wash the face’ → “thou bathe his face”: the personal dative becomes a possessive, and ‘wash’ becomes “bathe”.
- *ogni sucidume* ‘every uncleanness’ → “all filth”: the distributive expression becomes a mass expression.
- *quindi stinghe* ‘thou erase from there’ → “may thence be wiped away”: the active subjunctive becomes a modal passive, making the filth rather than the person washing the grammatical subject.

> 97 chè non si converria l' occhio sorpriso  
> 98 d' alcuna nebbia andar davanti al primo  
> 99 ministro, ch' è di quei di Paradiso.  

for ’twere not meet with eye obscured by any mist to go before the first minister of those that are of Paradise.

- *non si converria* ‘it would not be fitting’ → “’twere not meet”: the impersonal conditional is expressed by a subjunctive of ‘be’ and an adjective.
- *l' occhio sorpriso d' alcuna nebbia* ‘the eye overtaken by any mist’ → “with eye obscured by any mist”: “with” makes the attendant circumstance explicit, the article disappears, and “obscured” gives the effect of the mist rather than the image of being overtaken.
- *ch' è di quei di Paradiso* ‘who is of those of Paradise’ → “of those that are of Paradise”: the Italian relative clause modifies the minister and has a singular verb; the English relative clause modifies “those” and has a plural verb.

> 100 Questa isoletta intorno ad imo ad imo,  
> 101 laggiù, colà dove la batte l' onda,  
> 102 porta de' giunchi sopra il molle limo.  

This little isle all round about the very base, there, where the wave beats it, bears rushes on the soft mud.

- *isoletta* ‘islet’ → “little isle”: a separate adjective expresses the diminutive suffix.
- *intorno ad imo ad imo* ‘round about, at the bottom, at the bottom’ → “all round about the very base”: “all” reinforces the extent, and “very” conveys the emphasis of the repeated phrase.
- *laggiù, colà dove* ‘down there, there where’ → “there, where”: the two indications of place are reduced to one, without the downward indication.
- *la batte l' onda* ‘it beats the wave’ (object–verb–subject) → “the wave beats it”: the subject is placed first and the object last.
- *de' giunchi* ‘some rushes’ → “rushes”: the partitive is not separately expressed.

> 103 Null' altra pianta, che facesse fronda  
> 104 o indurasse, vi puote aver vita,  
> 105 però che alle percosse non seconda.  

No other plant that would put forth leaf or harden can live there, because it yields not to the buffetings.

- *facesse fronda o indurasse* ‘should make leaf or grow hard’ → “would put forth leaf or harden”: “would” carries the hypothetical force of both imperfect subjunctives, and the production of a leaf is expressed by “put forth”.
- *vi puote aver vita* ‘there can have life’ → “can live there”: ‘have life’ becomes a single verb, and the place adverb moves to the end.
- *alle percosse non seconda* ‘to the blows it does not yield’ → “it yields not to the buffetings”: the prepositional complement follows the verb instead of preceding it, the negative follows the verb, and ‘blows’ becomes “buffetings”.

> 106 Poscia non sia di qua vostra reddita;  
> 107 lo sol vi mostrerà, che surge omai,  
> 108 prender lo monte a più lieve salita.”  

Then be not this way your return; the sun, which now is rising, will show you how to take the mount at an easier ascent.”

- *non sia di qua* ‘let it not be by this way’ → “be not this way”: the negative follows the jussive “be”, and the route is expressed without a preposition.
- *vi mostrerà, che surge omai* ‘will show you, which rises now’ → “which now is rising, will show you”: the relative clause moves next to its antecedent; within it the time adverb precedes the verb and the simple present becomes progressive. The object of “show” follows the verb.
- *prender* ‘to take’ → “how to take”: “how” makes the manner of approach explicit.
- *a più lieve salita* ‘at easier ascent’ → “at an easier ascent”: the indefinite article is supplied.

> 109 Così sparì; ed io su mi levai  
> 110 senza parlare, e tutto mi ritrassi  
> 111 al duca mio, e gli occhi a lui drizzai.  

So he vanished; and I uplifted me without speaking, and drew me all back to my Leader, and directed mine eyes to him.

- *su mi levai* ‘up myself I raised’ → “I uplifted me”: the directional adverb is incorporated into the verb, and the reflexive object follows it.
- *tutto mi ritrassi* ‘wholly myself I drew back’ → “drew me all back”: both the pronoun and the expression of entirety follow the verb.
- *al duca mio* ‘to the leader mine’ → “to my Leader”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *gli occhi a lui drizzai* ‘the eyes to him I directed’ → “directed mine eyes to him”: the verb comes first, and an explicit possessive replaces the definite article.

> 112 Ei cominciò: “Figliuol, segui i miei passi:  
> 113 volgiamci indietro, chè di qua dichina  
> 114 questa pianura a' suoi termini bassi.”  

He began: “Son, follow thou my steps: turn we back, for this way the plain slopes down to its low bounds.”

- *segui* ‘follow’ → “follow thou”: the imperative is given an emphatic postverbal pronoun.
- *volgiamci indietro* ‘let us turn ourselves back’ → “turn we back”: the first-person plural exhortation takes an inverted English form, without a separate reflexive object.
- *dichina questa pianura* ‘slopes down this plain’ → “the plain slopes down”: the subject precedes the verb, and the demonstrative becomes a definite article.
- *a' suoi termini bassi* ‘to its bounds low’ → “to its low bounds”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 115 L' alba vinceva l' ôra mattutina  
> 116 che fuggia innanzi, sì che di lontano  
> 117 conobbi il tremolar della marina.  

The dawn was vanquishing the breath of morn which fled before her, so that from afar I recognised the trembling of the sea.

- *vinceva* ‘was overcoming’ → “was vanquishing”: the progressive conveys the continuing action of the imperfect, with a stronger verb of conquest.
- *l' ôra mattutina* ‘the morning breeze’ → “the breath of morn”: *ôra* is taken in the sense of ‘breeze’; “breath” personifies it, and the adjective becomes an ‘of’ phrase.
- *fuggia innanzi* ‘was fleeing ahead’ → “fled before her”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and “her” supplies an explicit reference to the dawn.
- *il tremolar della marina* ‘the trembling of the sea-surface’ → “the trembling of the sea”: the substantivized infinitive becomes an English verbal noun, and *marina* is rendered simply as “sea”.

## Lines 118–136 — The Dew and the Lowly Plant

> 118 Noi andavam per lo solingo piano,  
> 119 com' uom che torna alla perduta strada,  
> 120 che infino ad essa gli par ire in vano.  

We paced along the lonely plain, as one who returns to his lost road, and, till he reach it, seems to go in vain.

- *Noi andavam per* ‘we were going across’ → “We paced along”: the imperfect of the general verb of motion becomes a simple past specifying the manner of walking.
- *uom* ‘a man’ → “one”: an indefinite pronoun replaces the noun.
- *alla perduta strada* ‘to the lost road’ → “to his lost road”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *che infino ad essa* ‘who, as far as it’ → “and, till he reach it”: coordination replaces the second relative clause, and a clause with a verb of reaching replaces the phrase marking a spatial limit.
- *gli par ire* ‘to him it seems to go’ → “seems to go”: the man becomes the subject of the personal construction instead of being designated by an indirect-object pronoun.

> 121 Quando noi fummo dove la rugiada  
> 122 pugna col sole, per essere in parte  
> 123 dove ad orezza, poco si dirada,  

When we came there where the dew is striving with the sun, being at a place where, in the cool air, slowly it is scattered;

- *fummo dove* ‘we were where’ → “we came there where”: being at a point is rendered as arriving there, with an explicit “there”.
- *pugna col sole* ‘fights with the sun’ → “is striving with the sun”: the simple present becomes progressive, and ‘fights’ is softened to “striving”.
- *per essere in parte* ‘because of being in a place’ → “being at a place”: a participle replaces the infinitive governed by causal *per*, leaving the causal relation implicit.
- *ad orezza* ‘in the shade’ → “in the cool air”: Okey renders the coolness of the spot rather than its shelter from the sun.
- *poco si dirada* ‘it thins little’ → “slowly it is scattered”: degree becomes rate, and the pronominal verb for growing thin becomes a passive of dispersal.

> 124 ambo le mani in su l' erbetta sparte  
> 125 soavemente il mio maestro pose;  

both hands outspread, gently my Master laid upon the sweet grass;

- *ambo le mani … sparte* ‘both the hands … spread’ → “both hands outspread”: English drops the article after “both” and brings the participle next to its noun.
- *in su l' erbetta* ‘upon the little grass’ → “upon the sweet grass”: the phrase is moved from before the participle to after the verb, and “sweet” conveys the diminutive’s tenderness rather than its literal smallness.

> 126 ond' io che fui accorto di su' arte,  
> 127 porsi ver lui le guance lagrimose:  

wherefore I who was ware of his purpose, raised towards him my tear-stained cheeks:

- *fui accorto* ‘became aware’ → “was ware”: the English describes awareness as a state rather than marking the moment of recognition.
- *di su' arte* ‘of his art’ → “of his purpose”: what the master means to do replaces the reference to his manner of proceeding.
- *porsi* ‘held out’ → “raised”: the translation gives the gesture an upward direction.
- *le guance lagrimose* ‘the cheeks tearful’ → “my tear-stained cheeks”: a possessive replaces the article; the adjective moves before the noun and describes the marks of tears.

> 128 quivi mi fece tutto discoperto  
> 129 quel color che l' inferno mi nascose.  

there made he all revealed my hue which Hell had hidden.

- *mi fece tutto discoperto quel color* ‘he made quite uncovered that colour of mine’ → “made he all revealed my hue”: the verb precedes its subject after “there”; the personal clitic indicating whose colour it is becomes a possessive before the noun, displacing the demonstrative. “Revealed” renders ‘uncovered’, and “hue” renders ‘colour’.
- *l' inferno mi nascose* ‘Hell hid on me’ → “Hell had hidden”: the personal clitic is not separately rendered, and the simple past becomes a past perfect marking the earlier action.

> 130 Venimmo poi in sul lito diserto,  
> 131 che mai non vide navicar sue acque  
> 132 uomo che di tornar sia poscia esperto.  

We came then on to the desert shore, that never saw man navigate its waters who thereafter knew return.

- *in sul lito diserto* ‘onto the shore deserted’ → “on to the desert shore”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *navicar sue acque uomo* ‘sail its waters a man’ → “man navigate its waters”: the subject of the infinitive comes before it in English, with “man” used without an article.
- *di tornar sia poscia esperto* ‘of returning be afterwards experienced’ → “thereafter knew return”: the subjunctive of ‘be’ with an adjective becomes a past indicative verb of knowing; the infinitive governed by a preposition becomes its noun object, and the adverb moves before the verb.

> 133 Quivi mi cinse sì come altrui piacque:  

There he girded me even as it pleased Another:

- *mi cinse* ‘me he girded’ → “he girded me”: the object follows the verb.
- *sì come altrui piacque* ‘just as to another it pleased’ → “even as it pleased Another”: the person pleased comes after the verb as its direct object rather than before it as an indirect object.

> 134 o maraviglia! che qual egli scelse  
> 135 l' umile pianta, cotal si rinacque  
> 136 subitamente là onde la svelse.  

O marvel! that such as he plucked the lowly plant, even such did it forthwith spring up again, there whence he tore it.

- *qual egli scelse* ‘such as he chose’ → “such as he plucked”: Okey renders the physical act rather than the choice.
- *cotal si rinacque subitamente* ‘such it was reborn immediately’ → “even such did it forthwith spring up again”: “even” reinforces the correspondence; the simple past of rebirth becomes an inverted construction with “did” and a phrase for renewed growth, with the adverb brought before the main verb.
- *la svelse* ‘it he uprooted’ → “he tore it”: the object follows the verb, and the English verb does not itself specify removal by the root.

## Summary — Close in Phrasing, Freer in Construction

Okey often keeps the Italian sequence of phrases while rearranging the words within them. Adjectives commonly move before their nouns, object pronouns after their verbs, and subjects before verbs they follow in Italian: in *la batte l’onda*, for instance, the wave comes last, but in “the wave beats it” it comes first. Yet Okey does not consistently smooth away inversion. The placement of “Long” at the head of “Long he wore his beard” follows the emphatic position of *Lunga*, and “since yours am I” keeps the order of *poichè vostro sono*.

A change of construction can bring the English closer to an idiomatic phrase without much altering the sense. The relative clause *che lascia retro a sè* becomes “leaving behind her”; *si purga*, ‘purges itself’, becomes the passive “is purged”; and *va cercando*, ‘goes seeking’, is compressed to “seeketh”. Word choice can also sharpen or redirect an image: *suono*, ‘sound’, becomes the musical “strain”, while *aspetto*, ‘aspect’ or ‘countenance’, becomes the more specific “forehead”.

Small details are sometimes lost or supplied. The diminutive in *navicella* is expressed by “little” in “the little bark”, but *fiammelle* becomes simply “flames”. Conversely, “before him” supplies a reference point that *davante* leaves implicit. Two routine adjustments generally pass unremarked in the comparison: English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and drops the articles that Italian places before possessives, as in *i miei passi*, “my steps”. Such adjustments are compatible with considerable closeness: Okey preserves not only individual images but even the repeated conjunction and preposition in “and with words, and with hand, and with signs”.
