# Purgatorio 29

## Lines 1–12 — Little Steps Along the Riverbank

> 1 Cantando come donna innamorata,  
> 2 continuò col fin di sue parole:  
> 3 “*Beati, quorum tecta sunt peccata.*”  

At the end of her words, singing like an enamoured lady, she continued: “*Beati, quorum tecta sunt peccata.*”

- *col fin di sue parole* ‘with the end of her words’ → “At the end of her words”: the phrase moves from after the main verb to the beginning, and ‘with’ becomes the temporal “At”.
- *Cantando come donna innamorata* ‘singing like a lady in love’ → “singing like an enamoured lady”: the phrase no longer opens the sentence; English supplies an indefinite article, and the description of the lady precedes the noun instead of following it.

> 4 E come ninfe che si givan sole  
> 5 per le salvatiche ombre, disiando  
> 6 qual di veder, qual di fuggir lo sole,  

And, as nymphs who used to wend alone through the woodland shades, one desiring to see, another to flee the sun,

- *si givan* ‘were going their way’ → “used to wend”: “used to” gives the imperfect a habitual sense, and “wend” renders the verb without a separate equivalent for *si*.
- *salvatiche ombre* ‘wild shades’ → “woodland shades”: a noun used attributively replaces the adjective, making the association with the woods explicit.
- *disiando qual di veder, qual di fuggir lo sole* ‘desiring, one to see, one to flee the sun’ → “one desiring to see, another to flee the sun”: the first distributive pronoun moves before the participle, which is then understood rather than repeated after “another”; the repeated *qual* becomes the contrasting pair “one” and “another”.

> 7 allor si mosse contra il fiume, andando  
> 8 su per la riva, ed io pari di lei,  
> 9 picciol passo con picciol seguitando.  

she then advanced against the stream, walking on the bank, and I abreast of her, little step answering with little step.

- *allor si mosse* ‘then she moved’ → “she then advanced”: the subject precedes the adverb, and “advanced” renders the pronominal verb as forward motion.
- *contra il fiume* ‘against the river’ → “against the stream”: “stream” brings out the sense of movement against the flow.
- *andando su per la riva* ‘going up along the bank’ → “walking on the bank”: the verb specifies the manner of going, while “on” gives the location rather than the upward course along the bank.
- *pari di lei* ‘level with her’ → “abreast of her”: an idiom of relative position replaces the adjective of equality.
- *picciol passo con picciol seguitando* ‘little step with little [step] following’ → “little step answering with little step”: “answering” makes the correspondence between steps explicit and moves the participle before the phrase with “with”; the noun understood after the second adjective is repeated.

> 10 Non eran cento tra i suo' passi e i miei,  
> 11 quando le ripe igualmente dier volta,  

Not a hundred were her steps with mine, when both banks alike made a bend

- *Non eran cento* ‘there were not a hundred’ → “Not a hundred were”: the negated number moves before the verb, and the construction is no longer existential.
- *tra i suo' passi e i miei* ‘between her steps and mine’ → “her steps with mine”: “her steps” becomes the subject rather than part of a prepositional phrase, and coordination with ‘and’ gives way to accompaniment with “with”.
- *le ripe* ‘the banks’ → “both banks”: “both” makes their number explicit.
- *dier volta* ‘gave a turn’ → “made a bend”: both the verb and the noun change to the English turn of phrase for a change in the banks’ course.

> 12 per modo ch' a levante mi rendei.  

in such wise that I turned me to the east.

- *per modo ch'* ‘in a manner that’ → “in such wise that”: “wise” renders ‘manner’, and “such” makes the relation to the ensuing result explicit.
- *a levante mi rendei* ‘to the east I turned myself’ → “I turned me to the east”: the directional phrase moves after the verb, and the reflexive is expressed by “me” after “turned”.

## Lines 13–45 — Light and Song in the Forest

> 13 Nè ancor fu così nostra via molta,  
> 14 quando la donna tutta a me si torse,  
> 15 dicendo: “Frate mio, guarda, ed ascolta.”  

Nor yet was our way thus very far, when the lady turned her full round to me, saying, “Brother mine, look and hearken.”

- *così nostra via molta* ‘thus our way much’ → “our way thus very far”: the subject comes before the adverb, and an adjective of quantity becomes a phrase of distance, intensified by “very”.
- *tutta a me si torse* ‘wholly toward me herself turned’ → “turned her full round to me”: the verb comes first, “her” has reflexive force, and “full round” expresses the completeness of the turn.

> 16 ed ecco un lustro subito trascorse  
> 17 da tutte parti per la gran foresta,  
> 18 tal che di balenar mi mise in forse.  

And lo, a sudden brightness flooded on all sides the great forest, such that it set me in doubt if ’twere lightning.

- *un lustro subito* ‘a brightness sudden’ → “a sudden brightness”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *trascorse da tutte parti per la gran foresta* ‘passed swiftly from every side through the great forest’ → “flooded on all sides the great forest”: a verb of passage becomes transitive “flooded”, with the forest as its direct object; “on” replaces ‘from’.
- *di balenar mi mise in forse* ‘of lightning it put me in doubt’ → “it set me in doubt if ’twere lightning”: the prepositional phrase comes last and becomes an indirect question with a finite verb.

> 19 Ma perchè il balenar, come vien, resta,  
> 20 e quel durando più e più splendeva,  
> 21 nel mio pensar dicea: “Che cosa è questa?”  

But since lightning ceases even as it cometh, and that enduring, brighter and brighter shone, in my mind I said: “What thing is this?”

- *il balenar, come vien, resta* ‘the flashing, as it comes, ceases’ → “lightning ceases even as it cometh”: the substantivized infinitive becomes an English mass noun without an article; the main verb precedes the temporal clause, and “even” sharpens the coincidence.
- *più e più splendeva* ‘more and more it was shining’ → “brighter and brighter shone”: repeated comparatives of brightness replace adverbs of degree, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *nel mio pensar dicea* ‘in my thinking I was saying’ → “in my mind I said”: “mind” replaces the substantivized infinitive, and a simple past replaces the imperfect.

> 22 Ed una melodia dolce correva  
> 23 per l' aer luminoso; onde buon zelo  
> 24 mi fe' riprender l' ardimento d' Eva,  

And a sweet melody ran through the luminous air; wherefore righteous zeal made me reprove Eve’s daring,

- *una melodia dolce correva* ‘a melody sweet was running’ → “a sweet melody ran”: the adjective precedes the noun, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *per l' aer luminoso* ‘through the air luminous’ → “through the luminous air”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *buon zelo* ‘good zeal’ → “righteous zeal”: the adjective is given a more specifically moral sense.
- *mi fe' riprender* ‘me made reprove’ → “made me reprove”: the pronoun follows the causative verb.
- *l' ardimento d' Eva* ‘the daring of Eve’ → “Eve’s daring”: a possessive placed before the noun replaces the following prepositional phrase.

> 25 che, là dove ubbidia la terra e il cielo,  
> 26 femmina sola, e pur testè formata,  
> 27 non sofferse di star sotto alcun velo;  

who, there where heaven and earth obeyed, a woman alone and but then formed, did not bear to remain under any veil,

- *ubbidia la terra e il cielo* ‘obeyed the earth and the heaven’ → “heaven and earth obeyed”: the two subjects exchange places, lose their articles, and precede the verb; the Italian imperfect, singular before the compound subject, becomes a simple past.
- *femmina sola* ‘woman alone’ → “a woman alone”: English supplies the indefinite article in apposition.
- *pur testè formata* ‘only just formed’ → “but then formed”: “but” carries the restrictive sense of *pur*, while “then” renders the time relative to the past event.
- *di star* ‘to stay’ → “to remain”: English uses a near-synonym and an infinitive without a separate equivalent for the linking preposition *di*.

> 28 sotto il qual, se devota fosse stata,  
> 29 avrei quelle ineffabili delizie  
> 30 sentite prima, e più lunga fiata.  

under which, if she had been devout, I should have tasted those ineffable joys ere this, and for a longer time.

- *sotto il qual* ‘under the which’ → “under which”: the Italian article has no English counterpart.
- *se devota fosse stata* ‘if devout she had been’ → “if she had been devout”: the adjective follows the verb; the English past perfect in the conditional clause renders the Italian pluperfect subjunctive.
- *avrei quelle ineffabili delizie sentite* ‘I should have those ineffable delights felt’ → “I should have tasted those ineffable joys”: the participle moves next to the auxiliary and no longer marks agreement with the object; “tasted” gives the experience a more specific sensory verb, and “joys” replaces ‘delights’.
- *prima* ‘earlier’ → “ere this”: a phrase with an explicit point of reference replaces the comparative adverb.
- *più lunga fiata* ‘a longer time’ → “for a longer time”: English supplies a preposition of duration.

> 31 Mentr' io m' andava tra tante primizie  
> 32 dell' eterno piacer, tutto sospeso,  
> 33 e disioso ancora a più letizie,  

While I was going amid so many first-fruits of the eternal pleasance, all enrapt and still yearning for more joys,

- *io m' andava* ‘I was going my way’ → “I was going”: English keeps the progressive movement but gives the pronominal element no separate equivalent.
- *dell' eterno piacer* ‘of the eternal pleasure’ → “of the eternal pleasance”: the abstract noun is rendered by the more archaic “pleasance”.
- *tutto sospeso* ‘wholly suspended’ → “all enrapt”: the participle is interpreted as absorption rather than rendered by the image of suspension.
- *disioso ancora a più letizie* ‘desirous still of more joys’ → “still yearning for more joys”: the adverb moves forward, and the adjective with its complement becomes a participle with “for”.

> 34 dinanzi a noi, tal quale un foco acceso,  
> 35 ci si fe' l' aer sotto i verdi rami,  
> 36 e il dolce suon per canto era già inteso.  

the air in front of us under the green boughs, became even as a flaming fire to us, and the sweet sound was heard as a chant.

- *dinanzi a noi, tal quale un foco acceso* ‘before us, just like a fire kindled’ → “in front of us” and “even as a flaming fire”: the first phrase follows the English subject; the comparison moves after the verb, and a participle describing the fire as lit becomes an active modifier before the noun.
- *ci si fe' l' aer sotto i verdi rami* ‘to us became the air under the green boughs’ → “the air” … “under the green boughs, became” … “to us”: the subject and its phrase of place precede the verb; the pronominal verb is rendered by “became”, and the dative moves to the end of the comparison.
- *per canto era già inteso* ‘as song was already understood’ → “was heard as a chant”: the passive precedes its complement, “heard” gives *inteso* its auditory sense, “a chant” makes the song a count noun, and *già* is not separately rendered.

> 37 O sacrosante Vergini, se fami,  
> 38 freddi o vigilie mai per voi soffersi,  
> 39 cagion mi sprona, ch' io mercè ne chiami.  

O holy, holy, Virgins, if e’er for you I have endured fastings, cold, or vigils, occasion spurs me to crave my reward.

- *sacrosante* ‘most holy’ → “holy, holy”: repetition expresses the force of the compound adjective.
- *fami, freddi o vigilie mai per voi soffersi* ‘hungers, colds or vigils ever for you I suffered’ → “e’er for you I have endured fastings, cold, or vigils”: the adverb, prepositional phrase, and verb move before the objects; the simple past becomes a present perfect, ‘hungers’ is interpreted as “fastings”, and the plural ‘colds’ becomes a mass singular.
- *mi sprona* ‘me spurs’ → “spurs me”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *ch' io mercè ne chiami* ‘that I recompense for it should ask’ → “to crave my reward”: an infinitive governed by “spurs me” replaces the finite subjunctive clause; the object follows the verb and gains “my”, while *ne*, referring back to what was endured, has no separate equivalent.

> 40 Or convien ch' Elicona per me versi,  
> 41 ed Urania m' aiuti col suo coro,  
> 42 forti cose a pensar mettere in versi.  

Now ’tis meet that Helicon for me stream forth and Urania aid me with her choir to set in verse things hard to conceive.

- *per me versi* ‘for me pour’ → “for me stream forth”: the verb of pouring is interpreted intransitively, with “forth” expressing the flow.
- *m' aiuti* ‘me aid’ → “aid me”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *forti cose a pensar mettere in versi* ‘hard things to think put into verses’ → “to set in verse things hard to conceive”: the infinitive of putting precedes its object, “hard” follows “things” alongside its complement, and “conceive” interprets the act of thinking; singular “verse” replaces the plural.

> 43 Poco più oltre sette arbori d' oro  
> 44 falsava nel parere il lungo tratto  
> 45 del mezzo, ch' era ancor tra noi e loro;  

A little farther on, a delusive semblance of seven trees of gold was caused by the long space that was yet between us and them;

- *Poco più oltre* ‘little farther on’ → “A little farther on”: English supplies the indefinite article in the adverbial expression.
- *sette arbori d' oro falsava nel parere* ‘seven trees of gold it falsely represented to the sight’ → “a delusive semblance of seven trees of gold was caused”: the false appearance becomes the grammatical subject, with the trees in an “of” phrase; the active imperfect becomes a simple past passive.
- *il lungo tratto del mezzo* ‘the long stretch of the intervening space’ → “by the long space”: the Italian subject becomes the agent of the passive, and the two nouns are compressed into one.

## Lines 46–78 — The Candlesticks and Their Seven Bands

> 46 ma quando fui sì presso di lor fatto,  
> 47 che l' obbietto comun, che il senso inganna,  
> 48 non perdea per distanza alcun suo atto,  

but when I had drawn so nigh to them that the general similitude of things, which deceives the senses, lost not by distance any of its features,

- *fui sì presso di lor fatto* ‘I was made so near to them’ → “I had drawn so nigh to them”: the construction with ‘made’ becomes a pluperfect verb of approach; the verb now precedes the phrase of nearness.
- *l' obbietto comun* ‘the object common’ → “the general similitude of things”: Okey interprets the common object of perception as a likeness shared by things; the adjective moves before the noun, and “of things” is supplied.
- *che il senso inganna* ‘which the sense deceives’ → “which deceives the senses”: the verb moves before its object, and the singular becomes plural.
- *per distanza* ‘through distance’ → “by distance”: distance is expressed as the cause of the loss.
- *alcun suo atto* ‘any property of its own’ → “any of its features”: the singular noun qualified by ‘any’ becomes a plural governed by the partitive “any of”; “features” makes the properties specifically perceptible ones.

> 49 la virtù ch' a ragion discorso ammanna  
> 50 sì com' elli eran candelabri apprese,  
> 51 e nelle voci del cantare, “*Osanna*.”  

the faculty which prepares material for reason distinguished them as candlesticks, even as they were, and in the words of the chant, “Hosannah.”

- *a ragion discorso ammanna* ‘for reason readies discourse’ → “prepares material for reason”: what the faculty makes ready for reasoning is called “material” rather than ‘discourse’; the verb comes first and “for reason” last.
- *sì com' elli eran candelabri apprese* ‘even as they were, candlesticks it apprehended’ → “distinguished them as candlesticks, even as they were”: the verb moves forward, takes an explicit object “them” and a complement introduced by “as”; the clause affirming what they actually were moves to the end.
- *nelle voci del cantare* ‘in the voices of the singing’ → “in the words of the chant”: the voices are rendered by what they articulate, and the substantivized infinitive by the noun “chant.”

> 52 Di sopra fiammeggiava il bello arnese  
> 53 più chiaro assai che luna per sereno  
> 54 di mezza notte nel suo mezzo mese.  

Above, the fair pageant was flaming forth, brighter far than the moon in clear midnight sky in her mid month.

- *fiammeggiava il bello arnese* ‘flamed the fair apparatus’ → “the fair pageant was flaming forth”: the subject moves before the verb; the collective ‘apparatus’ is interpreted as a “pageant,” and the imperfect becomes a progressive with “forth” supplied.
- *più chiaro assai* ‘more brightly by much’ → “brighter far”: the comparative is compressed into one word, adjectival in form rather than explicitly adverbial.
- *che luna* ‘than moon’ → “than the moon”: the definite article is supplied.
- *per sereno di mezza notte* ‘through the clear of midnight’ → “in clear midnight sky”: the substantivized ‘clear’ becomes an adjective qualifying the supplied “sky”; “in” replaces ‘through,’ and the time phrase becomes a modifier before the noun.
- *nel suo mezzo mese* ‘in her middle month’ → “in her mid month”: “mid” expresses the midpoint of the month rather than identifying one month as the middle one.

> 55 Io mi rivolsi d' ammirazion pieno  
> 56 al buon Virgilio, ed esso mi rispose  

Full of wonderment I turned me to the good Virgil,

- *Io mi rivolsi* ‘I myself turned’ → “I turned me”: the reflexive follows the verb.
- *d' ammirazion pieno* ‘of wonder full’ → “Full of wonderment”: the adjective precedes its complement, and the whole description moves before the subject and verb.

> 57 con vista carca di stupor non meno.  

and he answered me with a face not less charged with amazement.

- *ed esso mi rispose* ‘and he me answered’ → “and he answered me”: the object follows the verb. These opening words of the English clause render the end of Italian line 56.
- *con vista* ‘with countenance’ → “with a face”: “face” renders the outward look, with an indefinite article supplied.
- *carca di stupor non meno* ‘laden with astonishment no less’ → “not less charged with amazement”: the comparison moves before the participle it qualifies.

> 58 Indi rendei l' aspetto all' alte cose,  
> 59 che si moveano incontro a noi sì tardi,  
> 60 che foran vinte da novelle spose.  

Then I turned my countenance back to the sublime things, which moved towards us so slowly, that they would be vanquished by new-wedded brides.

- *rendei l' aspetto* ‘I returned the gaze’ → “I turned my countenance back”: ‘returned’ is divided between “turned” and “back”; the direction of looking is expressed through the face, and the possessive replaces the definite article.
- *alte* ‘high’ → “sublime”: Okey chooses the figurative sense of height.
- *si moveano* ‘moved themselves’ → “moved”: English uses the intransitive verb without the pronominal marker.
- *incontro a noi* ‘to meet us’ → “towards us”: a preposition of direction renders the expression of encounter.
- *sì tardi* ‘so slow’ → “so slowly”: the plural adjective agreeing with the moving things becomes an adverb of manner.
- *novelle spose* ‘new brides’ → “new-wedded brides”: “wedded” makes explicit in what respect the brides are new.

> 61 La donna mi sgridò: “Perchè pur ardi  
> 62 sì nell' aspetto delle vive luci,  
> 63 e ciò che vien di retro a lor non guardi?”  

The lady cried to me: “Wherefore art thou so ardent only for the vision of these bright lights, and heedest not that which comes after them?”

- *mi sgridò* ‘me rebuked’ → “cried to me”: a transitive verb of reproof becomes a verb of utterance with a prepositional complement after it.
- *pur ardi sì* ‘only burnest so’ → “art thou so ardent only”: the verb of burning becomes a copula and adjective; “so” precedes the adjective and “only” moves after it.
- *nell' aspetto* ‘in the sight’ → “for the vision”: the object of ardour is introduced by “for” rather than ‘in.’
- *delle vive luci* ‘of the living lights’ → “of these bright lights”: the article becomes demonstrative, and ‘living’ is interpreted as brilliance.
- *ciò che vien di retro a lor non guardi* ‘that which comes behind them thou dost not look at’ → “heedest not that which comes after them”: looking becomes attending; the verb and negation move before the object, and following “after” replaces the spatial ‘behind.’

> 64 Genti vid' io allor, com' a lor duci,  
> 65 venire appresso, vestite di bianco:  

Then I beheld people, clad in white, following as after their leaders;

- *Genti vid' io allor* ‘people saw I then’ → “Then I beheld people”: the time adverb moves to the front and subject–verb–object order replaces object–verb–subject; “beheld” renders ‘saw.’
- *com' a lor duci* ‘as to their leaders’ → “as after their leaders”: “after” specifies the direction of the movement, and the comparison follows rather than precedes the expression of following.
- *venire appresso* ‘to come behind’ → “following”: a participle replaces the infinitive and absorbs the indication of relative position.
- *vestite di bianco* ‘dressed in white’ → “clad in white”: the description is moved forward to stand immediately after “people.”

> 66 e tal candor di qua giammai non fuci.  

and whiteness so pure here never was with us.

- *tal candor* ‘such whiteness’ → “whiteness so pure”: the degree expressed by ‘such’ is transferred to the supplied adjective “pure.”
- *di qua* ‘on this side’ → “here”: a spatial phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *giammai non* ‘never not’ → “never”: the two Italian marks of negation yield one English negative.
- *fuci* ‘was there’ → “was with us”: Okey gives the enclitic *-ci* a personal rendering rather than repeating the indication of place.

> 67 L' acqua splendeva dal sinistro fianco,  
> 68 e rendea a me la mia sinistra costa,  
> 69 s' io riguardava in lei, come specchio anco.  

Bright shone the water on my left flank, and reflected to me my left side, if I gazed therein, even as a mirror.

- *L' acqua splendeva* ‘the water shone’ → “Bright shone the water”: “Bright” brings out the luminosity of the verb, and the subject moves after it.
- *dal sinistro fianco* ‘on the left flank’ → “on my left flank”: the possessor replaces the article.
- *rendea* ‘gave back’ → “reflected”: a verb specific to an image replaces the more general verb of return.
- *s' io riguardava in lei* ‘if I looked into it’ → “if I gazed therein”: “gazed” renders the act of looking, and the preposition and pronoun become one adverb.
- *come specchio anco* ‘as a mirror also’ → “even as a mirror”: the final adverb becomes an intensifier before the comparison, and an article is supplied.

> 70 Quand' io dalla mia riva ebbi tal posta,  
> 71 che solo il fiume mi facea distante,  
> 72 per veder meglio ai passi diedi sosta,  

When I was so placed on my bank that the river alone kept me distant, to see better I gave halt to my steps,

- *dalla mia riva ebbi tal posta* ‘on my bank I had such a position’ → “I was so placed on my bank”: having a position becomes being placed; ‘such’ becomes the adverb “so,” and the bank phrase moves after the verb.
- *solo il fiume* ‘only the river’ → “the river alone”: the restrictive modifier follows the noun.
- *mi facea distante* ‘me made distant’ → “kept me distant”: making becomes maintaining a separation, and the object follows the verb.
- *ai passi diedi sosta* ‘to the steps I gave halt’ → “I gave halt to my steps”: the recipient phrase moves after the verb and its object, and “my” replaces the article.

> 73 e vidi le fiammelle andar davante,  
> 74 lasciando retro a sè l' aer dipinto,  
> 75 e di tratti pennelli avean sembiante;  

and I saw the flames advance, leaving the air behind them painted, and of trailing pennants they had the semblance;

- *le fiammelle* ‘the little flames’ → “the flames”: the diminutive is not expressed.
- *andar davante* ‘go in front’ → “advance”: one verb replaces the verb and adverb, stressing forward movement rather than position in front.
- *retro a sè l' aer dipinto* ‘behind themselves the air painted’ → “the air behind them painted”: the object moves before the phrase of position, and an ordinary pronoun replaces the reflexive.
- *di tratti pennelli* ‘of pennons drawn along’ → “of trailing pennants”: Okey takes *pennelli* in the sense of pennons rather than brushes; an active participle replaces the passive ‘drawn.’
- *avean sembiante* ‘had semblance’ → “they had the semblance”: the abstract noun takes a definite article.

> 76 sì che lì sopra rimanea distinto  
> 77 di sette liste, tutte in quei colori,  
> 78 onde fa l' arco il sole e Delia il cinto.  

so that the air above remained streaked with seven bands, all in those colours whereof the sun makes his bow, and Delia her girdle.

- *lì sopra* ‘there above’ → “the air above”: the previously mentioned air is made the explicit subject in place of the locative ‘there.’
- *rimanea distinto di sette liste* ‘remained marked off in seven strips’ → “remained streaked with seven bands”: “streaked” specifies the manner of marking, with “with” introducing the marks themselves.
- *fa l' arco il sole* ‘makes the bow the sun’ → “the sun makes his bow”: the subject moves before the verb and the object after it; a possessive replaces the article.
- *il cinto* ‘the girdle’ → “her girdle”: a possessive again replaces the article.

## Lines 79–111 — The Elders, the Four Creatures, and the Triumphal Car

> 79 Questi ostendali retro eran maggiori  
> 80 che la mia vista; e, quanto al mio avviso,  
> 81 dieci passi distavan quei di fuori.  

These banners streamed to the rearward far beyond my sight, and as I might judge, the outermost were ten paces apart.

- *retro eran maggiori che la mia vista* ‘rearward were greater than my sight’ → “streamed to the rearward far beyond my sight”: the comparison of extent becomes a verb of motion followed by phrases of direction and extent; “far” is supplied.
- *quanto al mio avviso* ‘as far as my judgment goes’ → “as I might judge”: the possessive and noun become a subject and a modal verb phrase.
- *dieci passi distavan quei di fuori* ‘ten paces stood apart those on the outside’ → “the outermost were ten paces apart”: the subject moves to the front and becomes a superlative; the measure of distance now falls between the verb and “apart”.

> 82 Sotto così bel ciel, com' io diviso,  
> 83 ventiquattro seniori, a due a due,  
> 84 coronati venian di fiordaliso.  

Beneath so fair a sky, as I describe, came four and twenty elders, two by two, crowned with flower-de-luce.

- *così bel ciel* ‘so fair sky’ → “so fair a sky”: English inserts the indefinite article between the adjective and the noun.
- *coronati venian di fiordaliso* ‘crowned came with flower-de-luce’ → “came” … “crowned with flower-de-luce”: the finite verb moves ahead of the subject, bringing the participle and its complement together at the end.

> 85 Tutti cantavan: “Benedetta tue  
> 86 nelle figlie d' Adamo, e benedette  
> 87 sieno in eterno le bellezze tue.”  

All were singing: “Blessed thou among the daughters of Adam, and blessed to all eternity be thy beauties.”

- *cantavan* ‘sang’ → “were singing”: the progressive makes the continuing action of the imperfect explicit.
- *nelle* ‘in the’ → “among the”: the preposition is rendered in its sense of inclusion in a group.
- *sieno in eterno* ‘be in eternity’ → “to all eternity be”: the time phrase precedes the subjunctive in English and takes the idiomatic form “to all eternity”.

> 88 Poscia che i fiori e l' altre fresche erbette,  
> 89 a rimpetto di me dall' altra sponda,  
> 90 libere fur da quelle genti elette,  

When the flowers and the other tender herbs opposite to me on the other bank, were free from those chosen people,

- *Poscia che* ‘after’ → “When”: the conjunction marks time without expressly saying ‘after’.
- *fresche erbette* ‘fresh little herbs’ → “tender herbs”: the adjective changes, and the diminutive is not rendered separately.
- *dall' altra sponda* ‘from the other bank’ → “on the other bank”: English states the location rather than the side from which the flowers face the speaker.
- *libere fur* ‘free were’ → “were free”: the predicate adjective follows the verb.
- *genti elette* ‘people chosen’ → “chosen people”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 91 sì come luce luce in ciel seconda,  
> 92 vennero appresso lor quattro animali,  
> 93 coronato ciascun di verde fronda.  

even as star follows star in the heavens, four creatures came after them, each one crowned with green leaves.

- *sì come luce luce in ciel seconda* ‘just as light light in heaven follows’ → “even as star follows star in the heavens”: “star” specifies the kind of light; the verb moves between subject and object, and the place phrase moves to the end, with a definite article and a plural noun.
- *vennero appresso lor quattro animali* ‘came after them four animals’ → “four creatures came after them”: the subject moves before the verb, and “creatures” is broader than ‘animals’.
- *coronato ciascun* ‘crowned each’ → “each one crowned”: the distributive pronoun precedes the participle and is expanded by “one”.
- *di verde fronda* ‘with green foliage’ → “with green leaves”: a collective singular becomes a countable plural.

> 94 Ognuno era pennuto di sei ali,  
> 95 le penne piene d' occhi; e gli occhi d' Argo,  
> 96 se fosser vivi, sarebber cotali.  

Everyone was plumed with six wings, the plumes full of eyes; and the eyes of Argus, were they living, would be such.

- *se fosser vivi* ‘if they were alive’ → “were they living”: inversion replaces the conditional conjunction, and “living” renders the adjective ‘alive’.

> 97 A descriver lor forme più non spargo  
> 98 rime, lettor; ch' altra spesa mi strigne  
> 99 tanto, che a questa non posso esser largo.  

To describe their form, reader, I spill no more rhymes; for other charges bind me so, that herein I cannot be lavish.

- *lor forme* ‘their forms’ → “their form”: the plural becomes singular.
- *più non spargo rime, lettor* ‘no longer do I spill rhymes, reader’ → “reader, I spill no more rhymes”: the address moves from after the clause about rhymes to before it, and the negative and the adverb preceding the verb are recast as “no more” before the noun.
- *ch'* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *altra spesa mi strigne* ‘another expense constrains me’ → “other charges bind me”: the singular noun and verb become plural, ‘expense’ becomes “charges”, and the object moves after the verb.
- *a questa* ‘on this one’ → “herein”: the preposition and demonstrative referring back to the expense become a single adverb.
- *esser largo* ‘be generous’ → “be lavish”: the sense of generosity is expressed specifically in terms of spending.

> 100 Ma leggi Ezechiel, che li dipigne  
> 101 come li vide dalla fredda parte  
> 102 venir con vento, con nube e con igne:  

But read Ezekiel who depicts them as he saw them coming from the cold region, with whirlwind, with cloud, and with fire;

- *li dipigne* ‘them depicts’ → “depicts them”: the object follows the verb.
- *li vide dalla fredda parte venir* ‘them he saw from the cold region come’ → “he saw them coming from the cold region”: the object follows the verb of perception, and the dependent infinitive becomes a participle placed before the phrase of origin.
- *vento* ‘wind’ → “whirlwind”: the translation specifies a kind of wind.

> 103 e quali i troverai nelle sue carte,  
> 104 tali eran quivi, salvo ch' alle penne  
> 105 Giovanni è meco, e da lui si diparte.  

and as thou shalt find them in his pages, such were they here, save that in the pinions John is with me, and differs from him.

- *quali i troverai* ‘of what sort them thou wilt find’ → “as thou shalt find them”: the predicative relative adjective becomes “as”, and the object follows the verb.
- *tali eran quivi* ‘such were here’ → “such were they here”: the subject pronoun is placed after the verb, retaining “such” at the head of the clause.
- *alle penne* ‘as to the wings’ → “in the pinions”: “in” marks the point of comparison, and “pinions” renders the wings.
- *da lui si diparte* ‘from him he departs’ → “differs from him”: the pronominal verb of departure is rendered by a verb of difference, with its complement moved after it.

> 106 Lo spazio dentro a lor quattro contenne  
> 107 un carro, in su due rote, trionfale,  
> 108 ch' al collo d' un grifon tirato venne.  

The space within the four of them contained a car triumphal, upon two wheels, which came drawn at the neck of a grifon.

- *dentro a lor quattro* ‘within them four’ → “within the four of them”: English puts the number first and adds an article and a partitive construction.
- *un carro, in su due rote, trionfale* ‘a car, upon two wheels, triumphal’ → “a car triumphal, upon two wheels”: the adjective is brought next to its noun, before the phrase about the wheels.
- *al collo d' un grifon tirato venne* ‘at the neck of a grifon drawn came’ → “came drawn at the neck of a grifon”: the finite verb comes first, then the participle, then its complement.

> 109 Esso tendea in su l' una e l' altr' ale  
> 110 tra la mezzana e le tre e tre liste,  
> 111 sì ch' a nulla fendendo facea male.  

And he stretched upwards one wing and the other, between the middle and the three and three bands, so that he did hurt to none by cleaving.

- *Esso* ‘he’ → “And he”: an opening conjunction is supplied.
- *l' una e l' altr' ale* ‘the one and the other wing’ → “one wing and the other”: the first article is dropped, and the noun is expressed with the first member rather than the second.
- *a nulla fendendo facea male* ‘to none, cleaving, did hurt’ → “he did hurt to none by cleaving”: the verb and its noun move before the recipient phrase; the gerund comes last, with “by” making its instrumental force explicit.

## Lines 112–144 — The Chariot and Its Attendants

> 112 Tanto salivan, che non eran viste;  

So high they rose that they were not seen;

- *Tanto salivan* ‘so much they were rising’ → “So high they rose”: the degree is expressed as height, and the imperfect as a simple past.

> 113 le membra d' oro avea, quanto era uccello,  
> 114 e bianche l' altre di vermiglio miste.  

his members had he of gold, so far as he was a bird, and the others white mingled with vermilion.

- *le membra d' oro avea* ‘the members of gold he had’ → “his members had he of gold”: the article becomes a possessive, and the verb and subject intervene between the noun and its material.
- *quanto era uccello* ‘insofar as he was bird’ → “so far as he was a bird”: English supplies the indefinite article before the predicate noun.
- *e bianche l' altre* ‘and white the others’ → “and the others white”: the adjective follows rather than precedes the pronoun.
- *di vermiglio miste* ‘with vermilion mingled’ → “mingled with vermilion”: the participle precedes its complement.

> 115 Non che Roma di carro così bello  
> 116 rallegrasse Affricano, o vero Augusto,  
> 117 ma quel del sol saria pover con ello:  

Not only Africanus, nor in sooth, Augustus, e’er rejoiced Rome with a car so fair, but that of the sun would be poor beside it,

- *Non che Roma di carro così bello rallegrasse Affricano* ‘not that Rome with a car so fair gladdened Africanus’ → “Not only Africanus” … “e’er rejoiced Rome with a car so fair”: Okey takes Africanus as subject and Rome as object rather than the reverse. He moves the car phrase to the end, supplies its indefinite article and “e’er”, and replaces the negative subjunctive clause with a statement introduced by “Not only”.
- *o vero Augusto* ‘or indeed Augustus’ → “nor in sooth, Augustus”: the alternative is joined by a negative conjunction.
- *con ello* ‘with it’ → “beside it”: the preposition makes the comparison explicit.

> 118 quel del sol, che sviando fu combusto,  
> 119 per l' orazion della Terra devota,  
> 120 quando fu Giove arcanamente giusto.  

that of the sun, which straying was consumed at the devout prayer of the earth, when Jove was mysteriously just.

- *fu combusto* ‘was burned up’ → “was consumed”: the English gives the result without naming burning.
- *per l' orazion* ‘because of the prayer’ → “at the devout prayer”: the causal preposition becomes the idiomatic “at”.
- *della Terra devota* ‘of the devout earth’ → “devout prayer of the earth”: Okey attaches the adjective to the prayer rather than to the earth.
- *fu Giove* ‘was Jove’ → “Jove was”: subject precedes verb.

> 121 Tre donne in giro, dalla destra rota,  
> 122 venian danzando: l' una tanto rossa  
> 123 ch' a pena fora dentro al foco nota;  

Three ladies came dancing in a round by the right wheel; one so red that hardly would she be noted in the fire;

- *Tre donne* ‘three women’ → “Three ladies”: the noun is more courtly.
- *in giro, dalla destra rota, venian danzando* ‘in a circle, on the side of the right wheel, they were coming dancing’ → “came dancing in a round by the right wheel”: both adverbial phrases follow the verbs; the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the indication of side is condensed to “by”.
- *l' una* ‘the one’ → “one”: the article is dropped.
- *a pena fora dentro al foco nota* ‘scarcely would she be within the fire discernible’ → “hardly would she be noted in the fire”: the adjective becomes a passive participle placed before the location, and “in” replaces the fuller expression of position.

> 124 l' altr' era come se le carni e l' ossa  
> 125 fossero state di smeraldo fatte;  
> 126 la terza parea neve testè mossa;  

the next was as if her flesh and bone had been made of emerald; the third seemed new fallen snow;

- *l' altr'* ‘the other’ → “the next”: distinction becomes succession.
- *le carni e l' ossa* ‘the fleshes and the bones’ → “her flesh and bone”: the articles give way to one possessive, and both nouns become singular.
- *fossero state di smeraldo fatte* ‘had been of emerald made’ → “had been made of emerald”: the material follows the participle, and English has no distinct subjunctive form here.
- *neve testè mossa* ‘snow just now moved’ → “new fallen snow”: Okey interprets the movement as falling and puts the modifiers before the noun.

> 127 ed or parevan dalla bianca tratte,  
> 128 or dalla rossa, e dal canto di questa  
> 129 l' altre togliean l' andare e tarde e ratte.  

and now seemed they led by the white, now by the red, and from the song of her the others took measure slow and quick.

- *parevan dalla bianca tratte* ‘they seemed by the white drawn’ → “seemed they led by the white”: the subject follows the verb, and the participle, interpreted as leadership rather than pulling, precedes the agent.
- *dal canto di questa* ‘from the song of this one’ → “from the song of her”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *togliean l' andare e tarde e ratte* ‘took their going, both slow and swift’ → “took measure slow and quick”: an unarticled noun of rhythm replaces the substantivized infinitive, and the first of the paired conjunctions disappears. The Italian adjectives agree with the women; in English they follow “measure”.

> 130 Dalla sinistra quattro facean festa,  
> 131 in porpora vestite, dietro al modo  
> 132 d' una di lor, ch' avea tre occhi in testa.  

By the left wheel, four clad in purple, made festival, following the lead of one of them, who had three eyes in her head.

- *Dalla sinistra* ‘on the left side’ → “By the left wheel”: English restores the noun understood after the feminine adjective.
- *facean festa, in porpora vestite* ‘were making festival, in purple clad’ → “clad in purple, made festival”: the clothing phrase precedes the finite verb, the participle precedes the color, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *dietro al modo* ‘after the manner’ → “following the lead”: the preposition becomes a participle, and manner is interpreted as guidance.
- *in testa* ‘in head’ → “in her head”: English supplies the possessive.

> 133 Appresso tutto il pertrattato nodo,  
> 134 vidi due vecchi in abito dispari,  
> 135 ma pari in atto, ed onesto e sodo:  

After all the group described, I saw two aged men, unlike in raiment, but like in bearing, and venerable and grave:

- *pertrattato nodo* ‘described knot’ → “group described”: the collective noun loses its metaphor, and the participle follows it.
- *due vecchi* ‘two old ones’ → “two aged men”: English supplies a noun for the substantivized adjective.
- *in abito dispari* ‘in dissimilar attire’ → “unlike in raiment”: dissimilarity is predicated of the men rather than of their attire.
- *in atto, ed onesto e sodo* ‘in bearing both honorable and steadfast’ → “in bearing, and venerable and grave”: the singular adjectives qualify the bearing in Italian; the English coordination makes them read as descriptions of the men and shifts the qualities to worthiness of respect and gravity.

> 136 l' un si mostrava alcun de' famigliari  
> 137 di quel sommo Ippocrate, che natura  
> 138 agli animali fe' ch' ell' ha più cari;  

one showed him to be of the familiars of that highest Hippocrates whom nature made for the creatures she holds most dear;

- *l' un* ‘the one’ → “one”: the article is dropped.
- *si mostrava alcun de' famigliari* ‘showed himself one of the familiars’ → “showed him to be of the familiars”: “him” does duty for the reflexive, the infinitive is supplied, and there is no separate equivalent for ‘one’ after it.
- *agli animali fe'* ‘for the animals made’ → “made for the creatures”: the beneficiary follows the verb, and its noun is rendered by a broader one.
- *ch' ell' ha più cari* ‘that she has dearest’ → “she holds most dear”: the relative pronoun is omitted, and having dear becomes holding dear.

> 139 mostrava l' altro la contraria cura  
> 140 con una spada lucida ed acuta,  
> 141 tal che di qua dal rio mi fe' paura.  

the other showed the contrary care, with a sword glittering and sharp, such that on this side the stream it made me afeard.

- *mostrava l' altro* ‘showed the other’ → “the other showed”: the subject precedes the verb.
- *lucida* ‘bright’ → “glittering”: a participial adjective suggests an active play of light.
- *di qua dal rio* ‘on this side of the brook’ → “on this side the stream”: English uses “this side” directly before the watercourse and chooses the less specific “stream”.
- *mi fe' paura* ‘gave me fear’ → “it made me afeard”: the noun becomes a predicate adjective, with the person affected as direct object.

> 142 Poi vidi quattro in umile paruta,  
> 143 e di retro da tutti un veglio solo  
> 144 venir, dormendo, con la faccia arguta.  

Then saw I four of lowly semblance; and behind all, an old man solitary, coming in a trance, with visage keen.

- *vidi* ‘I saw’ → “saw I”: subject and verb are inverted.
- *in umile paruta* ‘in humble appearance’ → “of lowly semblance”: the preposition of outward appearance becomes one of description.
- *di retro da tutti* ‘at the back of all’ → “behind all”: one preposition replaces the longer expression.
- *un veglio solo* ‘an old man alone’ → “an old man solitary”: “solitary” gives adjectival force to ‘alone’.
- *venir, dormendo* ‘come, sleeping’ → “coming in a trance”: the infinitive after the verb of seeing becomes a participle, and the accompanying participle is interpreted as a state of trance rather than sleep.
- *con la faccia arguta* ‘with the face keen’ → “with visage keen”: the article disappears, and a more formal noun replaces ‘face’.

## Lines 145–154 — Red Garlands and the Halt at Thunder

> 145 E questi sette col primaio stuolo  
> 146 erano abituati; ma di gigli  
> 147 dintorno al capo non facevan brolo,  
> 148 anzi di rose e d' altri fior vermigli:  

And these seven were arrayed as the first company; but of lilies around their heads no garland had they, rather of roses and of other red flowers;

- *col primaio stuolo erano abituati* ‘with the first company were clothed’ → “were arrayed as the first company”: the verb comes first, and “as” makes the first company the standard of comparison rather than rendering *col* as ‘with’.
- *dintorno al capo* ‘around the head’ → “around their heads”: the distributive singular becomes plural, with a possessive in place of the article.
- *non facevan brolo* ‘they made no garden’ → “no garland had they”: the image of a garden becomes “garland”; making becomes having, and the object precedes the verb and subject.
- *altri fior vermigli* ‘other flowers vermilion’ → “other red flowers”: the colour adjective is less specific and comes before the noun.

> 149 giurato avria poco lontano aspetto,  
> 150 che tutti ardesser di sopra dai cigli.  

one who viewed from short distance would have sworn that all were aflame above the eyes.

- *giurato avria* ‘sworn would have’ → “would have sworn”: the auxiliary precedes the participle, and the whole verb follows its subject instead of preceding it.
- *poco lontano aspetto* ‘a little-distant gaze’ → “one who viewed from short distance”: the gaze becomes a person with a relative clause; the description of the gaze becomes a phrase giving the distance from which the person viewed.
- *ardesser* ‘burned’ → “were aflame”: the imperfect subjunctive verb becomes a past copula with a predicative adjective, without a distinct subjunctive form.
- *di sopra dai cigli* ‘above the brows’ → “above the eyes”: the eyes replace the brows as the point of reference.

> 151 E quando il carro a me fu a rimpetto,  
> 152 un tuon s' udì; e quelle genti degne  
> 153 parvero aver l' andar più interdetto,  
> 154 fermandos' ivi con le prime insegne.  

And when the car was opposite to me, a thunder clap was heard; and those worthy folk seemed to have their further march forbidden, and halted there with the first ensigns.

- *a me fu a rimpetto* ‘to me was opposite’ → “was opposite to me”: the phrase referring to the speaker moves from before the verb to after “opposite”.
- *un tuon* ‘a thunder’ → “a thunder clap”: “clap” supplies a count noun for the single sound.
- *s' udì* ‘made itself heard’ → “was heard”: the Italian *si* construction is rendered by an English passive.
- *genti degne* ‘people worthy’ → “worthy folk”: the adjective precedes the noun, and a collective renders the Italian plural.
- *l' andar più interdetto* ‘the going farther forbidden’ → “their further march forbidden”: the substantivized infinitive becomes the more specific noun “march”, the article becomes a possessive, and the adverb of extent becomes a preceding adjective.
- *fermandos' ivi* ‘stopping there’ → “and halted there”: the pronominal gerund becomes a finite past-tense verb coordinated by the added “and”.

## Summary — Close in Detail, Freer in Construction

Okey often keeps Dante’s words but changes the order in which they reach the reader. Adjectives move before their nouns, as in *una melodia dolce*, “a sweet melody”; objects move after their verbs, as in *mi sprona*, “spurs me”. Yet he does not consistently smooth the Italian into ordinary English prose. In “a car triumphal” he keeps the adjective after its noun, though he brings the two closer together; in “his members had he of gold” he keeps the object before the verb. He also preserves repetitions such as *a due a due*, “two by two”, and *le tre e tre liste*, “the three and three bands”.

Changes of vocabulary often make a quality or action more specific: *buon zelo* (‘good zeal’) becomes “righteous zeal”, and *rendea* (‘gave back’) becomes “reflected”. Elsewhere Okey leaves a distinction unexpressed: “the flames” loses the diminutive of *le fiammelle*, and *già* (‘already’) has no counterpart in “was heard as a chant”. Conversely, “both banks” makes explicit the number implicit in *le ripe*, and “the left wheel” restores the noun understood in *la sinistra*.

Some departures reshape a whole clause. In *il lungo tratto del mezzo* the intervening space is the subject of an active verb; in English “a delusive semblance” is the subject, and the space reappears in “was caused by the long space”. At the close, *fermandos’ ivi* (‘stopping there’) becomes “and halted there”: a dependent gerund becomes a second finite verb. Two quieter adjustments recur throughout without being noted at each occurrence: Okey supplies subject pronouns where the Italian verb ending already gives the person, and omits the Italian article before a possessive, as in *la mia sinistra costa*, “my left side”.
