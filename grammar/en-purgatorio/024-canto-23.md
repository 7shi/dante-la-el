# Purgatorio 23

## Lines 1–15 — A Call to Move On and a Song in Tears

> 1 Mentre che gli occhi per la fronda verde  
> 2 ficcava io così, come far suole  
> 3 chi retro agli uccellin sua vita perde,  

While I was thus fixing mine eyes through the green leaves, even as he is wont to do who throws away his life after birds,

- *gli occhi per la fronda verde ficcava io così* ‘the eyes through the foliage green I fixed thus’ → “I was thus fixing mine eyes through the green leaves”: the postposed subject moves to the front, and the imperfect becomes a past progressive, with “thus” before the participle. The object and the prepositional phrase now follow the verb; the eyes take a possessive, and the collective singular becomes a plural with the adjective before it.
- *come far suole chi* ‘as is wont to do one who’ → “even as he is wont to do who”: “even” reinforces the comparison; the English separates the antecedent “he” from “who” and puts “to do” after “is wont” rather than before it.
- *retro agli uccellin sua vita perde* ‘after the little birds his life loses’ → “throws away his life after birds”: the verb comes first and expresses waste rather than simply loss. The phrase about the birds comes last, without the article or the diminutive.

> 4 lo più che padre mi dicea: “Figliuole,  
> 5 vienne oramai, chè il tempo che c' è imposto  
> 6 più utilmente compartir si vuole.”  

my more than father said to me: “Son, come now onward, for the time which is allotted to us must be more usefully apportioned.”

- *lo più che padre* ‘the more than father’ → “my more than father”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *mi dicea* ‘to me was saying’ → “said to me”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the indirect object follows the verb.
- *vienne oramai* ‘come on now’ → “come now onward”: the direction expressed by the attached particle is rendered separately as “onward”, after the time adverb.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes coordinating.
- *che c' è imposto* ‘which to us is imposed’ → “which is allotted to us”: the indirect object follows the participle, and the verb expresses allocation rather than imposition.
- *più utilmente compartir si vuole* ‘more usefully to apportion is needful’ → “must be more usefully apportioned”: the Italian puts the infinitive before the expression of necessity; English puts the modal first and makes the time the subject of a passive, with the adverbs before the participle.

> 7 Io volsi il viso e il passo non men tosto  
> 8 appresso ai savi, che parlavan sìe  
> 9 che l' andar mi facean di nullo costo.  

I turned my face, and my step not less quickly, towards the sages, who were discoursing so that they made the going of no cost to me.

- *il viso* ‘the face’ → “my face”: the possessive replaces the article.
- *il passo* ‘the step’ → “my step”: here too the possessive replaces the article.
- *appresso ai savi* ‘after the wise ones’ → “towards the sages”: following is rendered as direction towards, and a substantive adjective becomes a noun.
- *parlavan* ‘were speaking’ → “were discoursing”: the translation chooses a more formal verb.
- *l' andar mi facean di nullo costo* ‘the going to me they made of no cost’ → “they made the going of no cost to me”: the object moves after the verb, and the indirect object moves to the end.

> 10 Ed ecco piangere e cantar s' udie:  
> 11 “*Labia mea Domine*,” per modo  
> 12 tal che diletto e doglia parturìe.  

And lo, in tears and song was heard: “*Labia mea Domine*,” in such manner that it gave birth to joy and grief.

- *piangere e cantar s' udie* ‘weeping and singing were heard’ → “in tears and song was heard”: the two infinitives naming what was heard become nouns in a phrase of manner. The quoted words now supply what “was heard” refers to.
- *per modo tal* ‘in manner such’ → “in such manner”: the modifier precedes the noun.
- *diletto e doglia parturìe* ‘delight and grief it brought forth’ → “it gave birth to joy and grief”: the verb moves before what is brought forth and becomes a phrase with “to”; “joy” renders ‘delight’.

> 13 “O dolce padre, che è quel ch' i' odo?”  
> 14 comincia' io; ed egli: “Ombre che vanno  
> 15 forse di lor dover solvendo il nodo.”  

“O sweet Father, what is that which I hear?” began I; and he: “Shades that perchance go loosening the knot of their debt.”

- *vanno forse* ‘go perhaps’ → “perchance go”: the adverb moves before the verb.
- *di lor dover solvendo il nodo* ‘of their obligation untying the knot’ → “loosening the knot of their debt”: the phrase modifying the knot moves from before the gerund to after the noun. The obligation is construed as a debt, and the action is rendered as loosening rather than untying.

## Lines 16–48 — Forese Recognised by His Voice

> 16 Sì come i peregrin pensosi fanno,  
> 17 giugnendo per cammin gente non nota,  
> 18 che si volgono ad essa e non ristanno:  

Even as musing wayfarers do, who on overtaking strange folk by the way, turn round to them and stay not,

- *i peregrin pensosi fanno* ‘the wayfarers pensive do’ → “musing wayfarers do”: the article drops out, and the adjective after the noun becomes a participle before it.
- *giugnendo per cammin gente non nota* ‘overtaking on the road people not known’ → “on overtaking strange folk by the way”: “on” makes the participle explicitly temporal; the phrase of place moves after the object, and the negated participle becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *che si volgono ad essa* ‘who turn themselves toward it’ → “who” … “turn round to them”: the relative pronoun moves ahead of the intervening participial phrase; “round” conveys the reflexive turn, and the singular pronoun referring to the collective noun becomes plural.
- *non ristanno* ‘not stop’ → “stay not”: the negative follows the verb, and “stay” has the sense ‘halt’.

> 19 così di retro a noi, più tosto mota,  
> 20 venendo e trapassando, ci ammirava  
> 21 d' anime turba tacita e devota.  

so behind us, moving more quickly, coming, and passing by, a throng of spirits, silent and devout, was gazing upon us in wonder.

- *più tosto mota* ‘more swiftly moved’ → “moving more quickly”: the passive participle agreeing with the throng becomes an active one, and the comparative follows it.
- *ci ammirava* ‘us it regarded with wonder’ → “was gazing upon us in wonder”: the verb moves after its subject, the imperfect becomes a progressive, and the sense of wondering is expressed separately from the act of looking.
- *d' anime turba* ‘of souls a throng’ → “a throng of spirits”: the noun precedes its complement, and “spirits” renders ‘souls’.

> 22 Negli occhi era ciascuna oscura e cava,  
> 23 pallida nella faccia, e tanto scema  
> 24 che dall' ossa la pelle s' informava.  

Dark and hollow-eyed was each one, pallid of face, and so wasted away that the skin took form from the bones.

- *Negli occhi era ciascuna oscura e cava* ‘in the eyes was each one dark and hollow’ → “Dark and hollow-eyed was each one”: the adjectives move to the front; the opening phrase, which qualifies both in Italian, is absorbed into the compound “hollow-eyed”.
- *pallida nella faccia* ‘pallid in the face’ → “pallid of face”: the preposition changes and the article drops out.
- *tanto scema* ‘so diminished’ → “so wasted away”: the participial phrase specifies bodily wasting.
- *dall' ossa la pelle s' informava* ‘from the bones the skin shaped itself’ → “the skin took form from the bones”: the source of the shape moves to the end, and a verb with a noun object replaces the reflexive verb.

> 25 Non credo che così a buccia strema  
> 26 Eresitone fosse fatto secco,  
> 27 per digiunar, quando più n' ebbe tema.  

I do not believe that Erysichthon became thus withered to the very skin by hunger, when greatest fear he had thereof.

- *Non credo* ‘not I believe’ → “I do not believe”: English uses an auxiliary for the negation.
- *così a buccia strema Eresitone fosse fatto secco* ‘thus to the outermost skin Erysichthon were made dry’ → “Erysichthon became thus withered to the very skin”: the subject comes first; the subjunctive construction after the negated verb of belief becomes an indicative verb of change, “withered” renders ‘dry’, and the phrase giving the extent of the drying comes last.
- *per digiunar* ‘through fasting’ → “by hunger”: a noun naming the condition replaces the infinitive naming the deprivation of food.
- *quando più n' ebbe tema* ‘when most of it he had fear’ → “when greatest fear he had thereof”: the adverb of degree becomes an adjective modifying “fear”; the noun moves before the verb and the pronominal complement after it.

> 28 Io dicea fra me stesso pensando: “Ecco  
> 29 la gente che perdè Jerusalemme,  
> 30 quando Maria nel figlio die' di becco.”  

I said in thought within me: “Behold the people that lost Jerusalem when Mary fed on her child.”

- *Io dicea fra me stesso pensando* ‘I was saying within myself, thinking’ → “I said in thought within me”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase placed earlier, and the emphatic ‘myself’ becomes “me”.
- *Maria nel figlio die' di becco* ‘Mary into the son struck with her beak’ → “Mary fed on her child”: the idiom of pecking becomes a verb of feeding followed by its prepositional complement; ‘the son’ becomes the less specific “her child”, with the relationship made explicit by the possessive.

> 31 Parean l' occhiaie anella senza gemme:  
> 32 chi nel viso degli uomini legge *omo*,  
> 33 ben avria quivi conosciuto l' *emme*.  

Their eye-sockets seemed gemless rings: he who reads ‘omo’ in the face of man would clearly have recognised there the ‘m.’

- *Parean l' occhiaie* ‘seemed the eye-sockets’ → “Their eye-sockets seemed”: the subject moves before the verb, and a possessive replaces the article.
- *anella senza gemme* ‘rings without gems’ → “gemless rings”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *chi nel viso degli uomini legge omo* ‘whoever in the face of men reads omo’ → “he who reads ‘omo’ in the face of man”: the free relative becomes a pronoun followed by a relative clause; verb and object precede the phrase of place, and generic plural ‘men’ becomes generic singular “man”.
- *ben avria quivi conosciuto* ‘well would have there recognised’ → “would clearly have recognised there”: “clearly” interprets the opening adverb and moves inside the verb phrase; “there” moves after the participle.

> 34 Chi crederebbe che l' odor d' un pomo  
> 35 si governasse, generando brama,  
> 36 e quel d' un' acqua, non sapendo come?  

Who, not knowing the reason, would believe that the scent of fruit and that of water had thus wrought, by begetting desire?

- *l' odor d' un pomo* ‘the scent of a fruit’ → “the scent of fruit”: the individual fruit becomes a mass noun without an article.
- *si governasse* ‘should conduct itself’ → “had thus wrought”: the reflexive is taken in the sense ‘act, have an effect’; English supplies “thus” and uses a past perfect indicative for the imperfect subjunctive.
- *generando brama* ‘begetting desire’ → “by begetting desire”: the preposition makes the relation of means explicit.
- *e quel d' un' acqua* ‘and that of a water’ → “and that of water”: the second subject moves up beside the first, and “water” takes no indefinite article in English.
- *non sapendo come* ‘not knowing how’ → “not knowing the reason”: the closing qualification moves next to “Who”, and the interrogative adverb becomes a noun phrase expressing cause.

> 37 Già era in ammirar che sì gli affama,  
> 38 per la cagione ancor non manifesta  
> 39 di lor magrezza e di lor trista squama;  

Already I was in astonishment at what thus famishes them, because of the reason not yet manifest, of their leanness, and of their sad scurf,

- *in ammirar* ‘in wondering’ → “in astonishment at”: a noun of state replaces the infinitive, with “at” introducing its object.
- *che sì gli affama* ‘what so them famishes’ → “what thus famishes them”: “thus” interprets the adverb as one of manner, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *di lor magrezza e di lor trista squama* ‘of their leanness and of their sorry scale’ → “of their leanness, and of their sad scurf”: “scurf” renders the singular ‘scale’ collectively rather than as a single flake or layer.

> 40 ed ecco del profondo della testa  
> 41 volse a me gli occhi un' ombra, e guardò fiso,  
> 42 poi gridò forte: “Qual grazia m' è questa?”  

when lo, from the hollow of the head a shade turned its eyes to me and fixedly did gaze; then cried aloud: “What grace is this to me?”

- *ed ecco* ‘and behold’ → “when lo”: coordination becomes a temporal link to the preceding clause.
- *del profondo* ‘from the depth’ → “from the hollow”: a term for the cavity replaces the term for depth.
- *volse a me gli occhi un' ombra* ‘turned to me the eyes a shade’ → “a shade turned its eyes to me”: the subject moves before the verb and the direction after the object; the article before ‘eyes’ becomes a possessive.
- *guardò fiso* ‘gazed fixedly’ → “fixedly did gaze”: the adverb comes first, and the simple past is expanded with “did”.
- *gridò forte* ‘cried loudly’ → “cried aloud”: the English stresses audible utterance rather than explicitly giving its volume.
- *Qual grazia m' è questa* ‘what grace to me is this’ → “What grace is this to me”: the pronominal complement moves to the end.

> 43 Mai non l' avrei riconosciuto al viso;  
> 44 ma nella voce sua mi fu palese  
> 45 ciò che l' aspetto in sè avea conquiso.  

Never had I recognised him by the face, but in his voice, was revealed to me, that which was blotted out in his countenance.

- *Mai non l' avrei riconosciuto* ‘never would I have recognised him’ → “Never had I recognised him”: the past conditional becomes a pluperfect indicative; “Never” alone carries the Italian negative concord, and the object pronoun follows the participle.
- *al viso* ‘at the face’ → “by the face”: the preposition marks the means of recognition.
- *nella voce sua* ‘in the voice his’ → “in his voice”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *mi fu palese* ‘to me was manifest’ → “was revealed to me”: a passive verb replaces the predicate adjective, and the recipient follows it.
- *l' aspetto in sè avea conquiso* ‘the appearance in itself had effaced’ → “was blotted out in his countenance”: the active pluperfect becomes a simple past passive; the appearance, formerly the subject, becomes the place of effacement, with “his” identifying whose countenance it is.

> 46 Questa favilla tutta mi raccese  
> 47 mia conoscenza alla cambiata labbia,  
> 48 e ravvisai la faccia di Forese.  

This spark rekindled within me all my knowledge of the changed features, and I recognised the face of Forese.

- *tutta mi raccese mia conoscenza* ‘all of it for me rekindled my recognition’ → “rekindled within me all my knowledge”: the anticipatory adjective moves next to the noun it qualifies; the dative becomes a phrase of inward location after the verb, and ‘recognition’ is expressed as “knowledge”.
- *alla cambiata labbia* ‘as to the changed countenance’ → “of the changed features”: “of” makes the phrase the complement of “knowledge”, and a singular collective term becomes plural.

## Lines 49–81 — Forese’s Hunger and Thirst

> 49 “Deh non contendere all' asciutta scabbia,  
> 50 che mi scolora,” pregava, “la pelle,  
> 51 nè a difetto di carne ch' io abbia;  

“Ah stare not,” he prayed, “at the dry leprosy which discolours my skin, nor at any default of flesh that I may have,

- *non contendere* ‘do not give heed’ → “stare not”: Okey makes the attention specifically visual; the negative follows the verb.
- *all' asciutta scabbia* ‘to the dry scab’ → “at the dry leprosy”: the preposition changes with the verb, and Okey uses a different disease term.
- *pregava* ‘he was praying’ → “he prayed”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the reporting clause moves forward from inside the relative clause to immediately after “stare not”.
- *che mi scolora … la pelle* ‘which discolours the skin for me’ → “which discolours my skin”: the personal pronoun before the verb becomes a possessive before the noun.
- *nè a difetto* ‘nor to lack’ → “nor at any default”: the preposition again suits “stare”, and “any” is supplied.
- *ch' io abbia* ‘that I have’ → “that I may have”: the relative clause’s subjunctive is expressed by a modal auxiliary.

> 52 ma dimmi il ver di te, e chi son quelle  
> 53 due anime che là ti fanno scorta;  
> 54 non rimaner che tu non mi favelle.”  

but tell me sooth of thyself, and who those two spirits are that there make thy escort; abide thou not without speaking to me.”

- *il ver* ‘the true [thing]’ → “sooth”: the substantivized adjective becomes a noun without an article.
- *di te* ‘of thee’ → “of thyself”: the pronoun takes the English self-form.
- *chi son quelle due anime* ‘who are those two souls’ → “who those two spirits are”: the verb moves after its subject in the indirect question, and ‘souls’ becomes “spirits”.
- *là ti fanno scorta* ‘there to thee make escort’ → “there make thy escort”: the personal pronoun before the verb becomes a possessive modifying “escort”.
- *non rimaner che tu non mi favelle* ‘do not remain so that thou speak not to me’ → “abide thou not without speaking to me”: the negative subjunctive clause becomes “without” plus a gerund; “thou” appears with the imperative instead, and the recipient follows “speaking”.

> 55 “La faccia tua, ch' io lagrimai già morta,  
> 56 mi dà di pianger mo non minor doglia,”  
> 57 rispos' io lui, “veggendola sì torta.  

“Thy face,” answered I him, “which in death I wept for once, gives me now not less grief, even unto tears, seeing it so disfigured.

- *ch' io lagrimai già morta* ‘which I once wept for dead’ → “which in death I wept for once”: the adjective agreeing with the face becomes a prepositional phrase placed before the subject; ‘once’ moves after the verb phrase.
- *mi dà di pianger mo non minor doglia* ‘to me gives, for weeping, now no lesser grief’ → “gives me now not less grief, even unto tears”: the recipient follows the verb; the infinitive becomes a phrase of degree with a noun, moved to the end and amplified by “even”.
- *veggendola sì torta* ‘seeing it so twisted’ → “seeing it so disfigured”: Okey gives the effect on the face rather than the literal ‘twisted’.

> 58 Però mi di', per Dio, che sì vi sfoglia;  
> 59 non mi far dir mentr' io mi maraviglio,  
> 60 chè mal può dir chi è pien d' altra voglia.”  

Therefore tell me, in God’s name, what strips you so; make me not talk while I am marvelling, for ill can he speak who is full of other desire.”

- *per Dio* ‘by God’ → “in God’s name”: the brief adjuration is expanded.
- *che sì vi sfoglia* ‘what so strips you of leaves’ → “what strips you so”: the leaf-specific sense drops out; the object follows the verb and “so” comes last.
- *non mi far dir* ‘do not make me speak’ → “make me not talk”: the negative follows the causative verb and its object; ‘speak’ is rendered “talk”.
- *mentr' io mi maraviglio* ‘while I marvel’ → “while I am marvelling”: the pronominal simple present becomes a non-pronominal progressive.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating explanation replaces the subordinating conjunction.
- *mal può dir chi* ‘ill can speak whoever’ → “ill can he speak who”: the single indefinite relative becomes “he” and “who”, with “he” inserted between the auxiliary and the infinitive.
- *voglia* ‘wish’ → “desire”: Okey uses the stronger noun.

> 61 Ed egli a me: “Dall' eterno consiglio  
> 62 cade virtù nell' acqua, e nella pianta  
> 63 rimasa retro, ond' io sì m' assottiglio.  

And he to me: “From the eternal counsel virtue descends into the water, and into the tree left behind, whereby I thus do waste away.

- *cade virtù* ‘falls virtue’ → “virtue descends”: the subject precedes the verb, and ‘falls’ becomes “descends”.
- *pianta* ‘plant’ → “tree”: Okey specifies the kind of plant.
- *rimasa retro* ‘remaining behind’ → “left behind”: the participle of an intransitive verb is rendered by a passive participle.
- *sì m' assottiglio* ‘thus I thin myself’ → “thus do waste away”: the reflexive verb becomes an intransitive verb phrase with auxiliary “do”.

> 64 Tutta esta gente, che piangendo canta,  
> 65 per seguitar la gola oltra misura  
> 66 in fame e in sete qui si rifà santa.  

All this people, who weeping sing, sanctify themselves again in hunger and thirst, for having followed appetite to excess.

- *Tutta esta gente, che piangendo canta* ‘all this people, which weeping sings’ → “All this people, who weeping sing”: English treats the collective as persons in the plural rather than retaining singular agreement.
- *per seguitar la gola oltra misura* ‘for following the gullet beyond measure’ → “for having followed appetite to excess”: the simple infinitive becomes a perfect gerund, the bodily noun becomes the appetite it signifies, and ‘beyond measure’ becomes “to excess”; the whole phrase moves to the end.
- *in fame e in sete* ‘in hunger and in thirst’ → “in hunger and thirst”: the second preposition drops out, and the phrase follows rather than precedes the predicate.
- *qui si rifà santa* ‘here makes itself holy again’ → “sanctify themselves again”: ‘here’ is omitted; verb and predicate adjective become one verb, with plural agreement and reflexive pronoun.

> 67 Di bere e di mangiar n' accende cura  
> 68 l' odor ch' esce del pomo, e dello sprazzo  
> 69 che si distende su per la verdura.  

The scent which issues from the fruit, and from the spray that is diffused over the green, kindles within us a desire to eat and to drink.

- *Di bere e di mangiar* ‘of drinking and of eating’ → “to eat and to drink”: the activities reverse order and become infinitives following “desire” rather than a phrase at the head of the clause.
- *n' accende cura* ‘in us kindles longing’ → “kindles within us a desire”: the personal pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase after the verb, and the noun gains an indefinite article.
- *l' odor ch' esce* ‘the odour that comes out’ → “The scent which issues”: the subject, with its modifiers, moves before “kindles”; “scent” and “issues” replace the more literal noun and verb.
- *si distende su per la verdura* ‘spreads itself over the greenery’ → “is diffused over the green”: the pronominal verb becomes passive, and “the green” stands for the vegetation.

> 70 E non pure una volta, questo spazzo  
> 71 girando, si rinfresca nostra pena,  
> 72 io dico pena e dovrei dir sollazzo;  

And not once only, while circling this road, is our pain renewed, I say pain and ought to say solace;

- *non pure una volta* ‘not only one time’ → “not once only”: ‘one time’ becomes an adverb and the restrictive modifier follows it.
- *questo spazzo girando* ‘this space circling’ → “while circling this road”: “while” makes the temporal relation explicit, the object follows the gerund, and the space is interpreted as a road.
- *si rinfresca nostra pena* ‘refreshes itself our pain’ → “is our pain renewed”: the pronominal verb becomes a passive, its subject placed between auxiliary and participle after the initial negative; ‘refreshed’ becomes “renewed”.
- *dovrei dir* ‘I should say’ → “ought to say”: the conditional of obligation becomes “ought” with a to-infinitive.
- *sollazzo* ‘delight’ → “solace”: the English chooses the sense of comfort rather than enjoyment.

> 73 chè quella voglia all' arbore ci mena,  
> 74 che menò Cristo lieto a dire: ‘Eli,’  
> 75 quando ne liberò con la sua vena.”  

for that desire leads us to the tree, which led glad Christ to say: ‘Eli’ when he made us free with his blood.”

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the explanation becomes coordinate.
- *quella voglia all' arbore ci mena* ‘that desire to the tree us leads’ → “that desire leads us to the tree”: the verb comes before the object, and the destination comes last.
- *menò Cristo lieto* ‘led Christ glad’ → “led glad Christ”: the adjective describing Christ’s state as he was led becomes a modifier before the name.
- *ne liberò* ‘us freed’ → “he made us free”: a single verb becomes a causative construction, with the object between verb and adjective.
- *con la sua vena* ‘with his vein’ → “with his blood”: Okey gives what the vein contains rather than the vessel.

> 76 Ed io a lui: “Forese, da quel dì  
> 77 nel qual mutasti mondo a miglior vita,  
> 78 cinqu' anni non son volti infino a qui.  

And I to him: “Forese, from that day on which thou didst change the world for a better life, not five years have revolved till now.

- *nel qual* ‘in which’ → “on which”: English uses “on” of a particular day.
- *mutasti mondo a miglior vita* ‘thou changedst world to better life’ → “thou didst change the world for a better life”: the preterite is expressed with “didst”; articles are supplied, and “for” makes the change an exchange.
- *cinqu' anni non son volti* ‘five years have not turned’ → “not five years have revolved”: the negative is placed before the quantity rather than with the verb, and ‘turned’ becomes “revolved”.
- *infino a qui* ‘up to here’ → “till now”: a spatial expression is made explicitly temporal.

> 79 Se prima fu la possa in te finita  
> 80 di peccar più, che sorvenisse l' ora  
> 81 del buon dolor ch' a Dio ne rimarita,  

If power to sin more came to an end in thee ere the hour supervened of the holy sorrow which weds us anew to God,

- *prima fu la possa in te finita … che* ‘sooner was the power in thee ended … than’ → “power to sin more came to an end in thee ere”: the article drops out, the predicate follows the subject and its infinitive complement, and ‘was ended’ becomes the intransitive “came to an end”, followed by “in thee”; the two-part temporal comparison becomes the single conjunction “ere”.
- *sorvenisse l' ora* ‘should come upon [thee] the hour’ → “the hour supervened”: the subject precedes the verb, and the imperfect subjunctive of the temporal clause becomes a simple past.
- *buon dolor* ‘good sorrow’ → “holy sorrow”: the adjective is given a specifically religious sense.
- *ch' a Dio ne rimarita* ‘which to God us remarries’ → “which weds us anew to God”: the repeated action in the verb prefix becomes the separate adverb “anew”; the object follows the verb and “to God” comes last.

## Lines 82–114 — Nella’s Prayers and a Warning to Florence

> 82 come se' tu quassù venuto? Ancora  
> 83 io ti credea trovar laggiù di sotto,  
> 84 dove tempo per tempo si ristora.”  

how art thou come up here? I thought to find thee yet down below, where time for time is repaid.”

- *quassù venuto* ‘up here come’ → “come up here”: the participle comes before the adverb of place.
- *Ancora io ti credea trovar* ‘still I thee thought to find’ → “I thought to find thee yet”: “thee” follows the infinitive it belongs to, and “yet” is moved from the head of the clause, attaching it to where Dante expected to find him.
- *si ristora* ‘makes good itself’ → “is repaid”: the Italian construction with *si* has passive force; English uses an explicit passive and the verb of repayment.

> 85 Ed egli a me: “Sì tosto m' ha condotto  
> 86 a ber lo dolce assenzio de' martiri  
> 87 la Nella mia col suo pianger dirotto;  

And he to me: “Thus soon hath led me to drink the sweet wormwood of the torments, my Nella by her flood of tears;

- *Sì tosto m' ha condotto* ‘so soon me hath led’ → “Thus soon hath led me”: “Thus” renders the degree adverb, and the object follows the participle.
- *a ber lo dolce assenzio de' martiri* ‘to drink the sweet wormwood of torments’ → “to drink the sweet wormwood of the torments”: English supplies a definite article before “torments”.
- *la Nella mia* ‘Nella mine’ → “my Nella”: the possessive precedes the name instead of following it.
- *col suo pianger dirotto* ‘with her unrestrained weeping’ → “by her flood of tears”: “by” marks the means; the infinitive used as a noun and its qualifying adjective become the image of a “flood of tears”.

> 88 con suoi preghi devoti e con sospiri  
> 89 tratto m' ha della costa ove s' aspetta,  
> 90 e liberato m' ha degli altri giri.  

by her prayers devout and by sighs she hath brought me from the borders where they wait, and set me free from the other circles.

- *con suoi preghi devoti e con sospiri* ‘with her prayers devout and with sighs’ → “by her prayers devout and by sighs”: both instances of the preposition become “by”, marking the means.
- *tratto m' ha* ‘drawn me hath’ → “she hath brought me”: “brought” replaces ‘drawn’, with the auxiliary before the participle and the object after it.
- *della costa* ‘from the slope’ → “from the borders”: a singular term for a hillside becomes a plural term for limits.
- *ove s' aspetta* ‘where one waits’ → “where they wait”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal plural.
- *liberato m' ha* ‘freed me hath’ → “set me free”: the single participle becomes a verb with a predicative adjective, and English shares the preceding auxiliary instead of repeating it.

> 91 Tant' è a Dio più cara e più diletta  
> 92 la vedovella mia, che molto amai,  
> 93 quanto in bene operare è più soletta:  

So much more precious and beloved of God is my dear widow, whom I loved so well, as she is the more lonely in good works;

- *Tant' è a Dio più cara e più diletta* ‘so much is to God more dear and more beloved’ → “So much more precious and beloved of God is”: “precious” renders ‘dear’; “more” serves both adjectives, “of God” follows them, and the verb comes last.
- *la vedovella mia* ‘the little widow mine’ → “my dear widow”: “dear” conveys the affectionate diminutive, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *che molto amai* ‘whom much I loved’ → “whom I loved so well”: degree is expressed by “so well”, placed after the verb.
- *quanto in bene operare è più soletta* ‘as much as in doing well she is more alone’ → “as she is the more lonely in good works”: “as” completes the comparison, with “the” supplied before “more”; “lonely” renders the diminutive adjective, and the infinitive with its adverb becomes “good works”, placed last.

> 94 chè la Barbagia di Sardigna assai  
> 95 nelle femmine sue è più pudica  
> 96 che la Barbagia dov' io la lasciai.  

for the Barbagia of Sardinia is far more modest in its women than the Barbagia where I left her.

- *assai nelle femmine sue è più pudica* ‘much in the women its is more modest’ → “is far more modest in its women”: “far” stands immediately before the comparative it intensifies; the phrase specifying in whom the modesty is found follows the adjective, with the possessive before the noun.
- *dov' io la lasciai* ‘where I her left’ → “where I left her”: the object follows the verb.

> 97 O dolce frate, che vuoi tu ch' io dica?  

O sweet brother, what wouldst thou have me say?

- *che vuoi tu ch' io dica* ‘what willest thou that I say’ → “what wouldst thou have me say”: the present verb of wishing becomes “wouldst thou have”, and the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object pronoun with a bare infinitive.

> 98 Tempo futuro m' è già nel cospetto,  
> 99 cui non sarà quest' ora molto antica,  

Already in my vision is a time to come to which this hour shall not be very old,

- *Tempo futuro m' è già nel cospetto* ‘a future time is already before my sight’ → “Already in my vision is a time to come”: the adverb and phrase of sight move to the front; the Italian possessive relation expressed by the dative becomes “my”, and the adjective becomes an infinitive phrase after “time”.
- *cui non sarà quest' ora molto antica* ‘to which shall not be this hour very ancient’ → “to which this hour shall not be very old”: English places the subject before the verb and uses “old” for ‘ancient’.

> 100 nel qual sarà in pergamo interdetto  
> 101 alle sfacciate donne Fiorentine  
> 102 l' andar mostrando con le poppe il petto.  

when the brazen-faced women of Florence shall be forbidden from the pulpit to go abroad showing their breasts with the paps.

- *nel qual* ‘in which’ → “when”: a temporal relative adverb replaces the preposition and relative pronoun.
- *sarà in pergamo interdetto alle sfacciate donne Fiorentine* ‘shall be in the pulpit forbidden to the shameless Florentine women’ → “the brazen-faced women of Florence shall be forbidden from the pulpit”: the women, indirect object in Italian, become the subject of the passive; “brazen-faced” renders ‘shameless’, and the adjective of origin becomes “of Florence”. The pulpit is given as the source rather than the location of the prohibition, and follows the participle.
- *l' andar mostrando* ‘the going about showing’ → “to go abroad showing”: the nominalized infinitive, subject of the Italian passive, becomes an infinitive complement of “forbidden”; “abroad” makes the going out explicit.
- *con le poppe il petto* ‘with the paps the breast’ → “their breasts with the paps”: the direct object comes first, and its generic singular with the definite article becomes a plural with a possessive.

> 103 Quai Barbare fur mai, quai Saracine,  
> 104 cui bisognasse, per farle ir coperte,  
> 105 o spiritali o altre discipline?  

What Barbary, what Saracen women ever lived, to whom either spiritual, or other discipline were necessary, to make them go covered?

- *Quai Barbare fur mai, quai Saracine* ‘what barbarian women were there ever, what Saracen women’ → “What Barbary, what Saracen women ever lived”: both feminine plurals are rendered by modifiers sharing “women”; “Barbary” gives the first a geographical sense. The verb and adverb follow both groups, with “ever” before “lived” rather than after a form of ‘be’.
- *cui bisognasse, per farle ir coperte, o spiritali o altre discipline* ‘for whom there should be need, to make them go covered, of either spiritual or other disciplines’ → “to whom either spiritual, or other discipline were necessary, to make them go covered”: the two kinds of discipline are placed before the predicate; the Italian imperfect subjunctive of need becomes “were necessary”, and plural *discipline* becomes the singular “discipline”. The purpose clause moves from inside the relative clause to its end.

> 106 Ma se le svergognate fosser certe  
> 107 di quel che il ciel veloce loro ammanna,  
> 108 già per urlare avrian le bocche aperte:  

But if the shameless creatures were assured of what swift heaven is preparing for them, already would they have their mouths open to howl:

- *le svergognate* ‘the shameless ones’ → “the shameless creatures”: English supplies a noun for the feminine adjective used substantivally.
- *fosser certe* ‘were certain’ → “were assured”: an adjective of certainty becomes participial in form.
- *di quel che* ‘of that which’ → “of what”: the antecedent and relative are combined.
- *il ciel veloce loro ammanna* ‘the heaven swift for them prepares’ → “swift heaven is preparing for them”: the article drops out, the adjective precedes the noun, the simple present becomes progressive, and the dative pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase after the verb.
- *già per urlare avrian le bocche aperte* ‘already for howling they would have the mouths open’ → “already would they have their mouths open to howl”: the purpose infinitive moves to the end, with “to” for *per*; “their” replaces the definite article with the body part.

> 109 chè, se l' antiveder qui non m' inganna,  
> 110 prima fien triste che le guance impeli  
> 111 colui che mo si consola con nanna.  

for if prevision here beguile me not, they shall be sorrowing ere he shall clothe his cheeks with down, who now is soothed with lullaby.

- *l' antiveder* ‘the foreseeing’ → “prevision”: the nominalized infinitive becomes a noun without an article.
- *non m' inganna* ‘does not deceive me’ → “beguile me not”: the Italian present indicative becomes an English subjunctive after “if”; both object and negative follow the verb.
- *prima fien triste che* ‘sooner shall they be sad than’ → “they shall be sorrowing ere”: a form of ‘be’ with an adjective becomes a progressive verb, and the separated terms of the time comparison become the single conjunction “ere”.
- *le guance impeli* ‘the cheeks he cover with hair’ → “he shall clothe his cheeks with down”: the present subjunctive becomes a future construction, the object follows the verb with a possessive instead of an article, and the idea of growing hair is expanded into “clothe his cheeks with down”.
- *colui che* ‘he who’ → “who”: the antecedent has been moved forward as the subject of “shall clothe”, leaving the relative behind.
- *mo si consola con nanna* ‘now comforts himself with lullaby’ → “now is soothed with lullaby”: the pronominal verb is read passively, with “soothed” for ‘comforted’.

> 112 Deh, frate, or fa che più non mi ti celi;  

Pray brother, look that thou hide thee no longer from me;

- *Deh* ‘ah’ → “Pray”: the interjection becomes an explicit appeal.
- *or fa che* ‘now see to it that’ → “look that”: the injunction is rendered idiomatically, without a separate word for ‘now’.
- *più non mi ti celi* ‘any longer not from me thyself thou hide’ → “thou hide thee no longer from me”: “no longer” combines adverb and negative after the reflexive object; the dative becomes the final phrase “from me”.

> 113 vedi che non pur io, ma questa gente  
> 114 tutta rimira là dove il sol veli.”  

thou seest that not only I, but all this people are gazing where thou veilest the sun.”

- *questa gente tutta rimira* ‘this people all gazes’ → “all this people are gazing”: “all” moves before the demonstrative, and the singular simple present becomes a plural progressive with the collective subject.
- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “where”: the separate adverb of place is omitted.
- *il sol veli* ‘the sun thou veilest’ → “thou veilest the sun”: the object follows the verb.

## Lines 115–133 — Dante Names His Companions

> 115 Per ch' io a lui: “Se ti riduci a mente  
> 116 qual fosti meco e quale io teco fui,  
> 117 ancor fia grave il memorar presente.  

Wherefore I to him: “If thou bring back to mind what thou hast been with me and what I have been with thee, the present memory will still be grievous.

- *ti riduci a mente* ‘thou bringest back to mind for thyself’ → “thou bring back to mind”: the reflexive dative has no separate rendering.
- *qual fosti meco* ‘what thou wast with me’ → “what thou hast been with me”: the simple past becomes a present perfect.
- *e quale io teco fui* ‘and what I with thee was’ → “and what I have been with thee”: the same tense change is made, and the verb comes before the phrase of accompaniment.
- *ancor fia grave il memorar presente* ‘still will be grievous the remembering present’ → “the present memory will still be grievous”: the subject moves before the verb, the adjective before its noun, and the adverb between the auxiliary and the copula. The substantivized infinitive becomes a noun.

> 118 Di quella vita mi volse costui  
> 119 che mi va innanzi, l' altr' ier, quando tonda  
> 120 vi si mostrò la suora di colui—”  

From that life he who goeth before me did turn me, the other day, when full was shown to you the sister of him,”

- *mi volse costui che mi va innanzi* ‘me turned this one who to me goes ahead’ → “he who goeth before me did turn me”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the subject and its relative clause move before the verb. The relative clause recasts the dative and adverb as “before me”; the main clause uses an auxiliary for the simple past and puts its object after the verb.
- *l' altr' ier* ‘the day before yesterday’ → “the other day”: the expression is less precise in English.
- *tonda vi si mostrò* ‘round to you showed herself’ → “full was shown to you”: the adjective is rendered by the term for lunar fullness, the reflexive by a passive, and the dative comes after the verb.
- *di colui* ‘of that one’ → “of him”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.

> 121 (e il sol mostrai). “Costui per la profonda  
> 122 notte menato m' ha da' veri morti,  
> 123 con questa vera carne che il seconda.  

(and I pointed to the sun). “This one through the deep night hath led me from the truly dead, in this solid flesh which follows him.

- *il sol mostrai* ‘the sun I showed’ → “I pointed to the sun”: showing becomes pointing to, and the object moves after the verb and becomes the complement of a preposition.
- *menato m' ha* ‘led me hath’ → “hath led me”: the auxiliary precedes the participle and the object follows it.
- *veri morti* ‘true dead’ → “truly dead”: the adjective becomes an adverb.
- *con questa vera carne* ‘with this real flesh’ → “in this solid flesh”: the preposition of accompaniment becomes one of condition, and “solid” expresses physical substance rather than reality.
- *il seconda* ‘him follows’ → “follows him”: the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 124 Indi m' han tratto su li suoi conforti,  
> 125 salendo e rigirando la montagna,  
> 126 che drizza voi che il mondo fece torti.  

Thence his comforts have brought me up, ascending and circling the mount, which makes you straight whom the world made crooked.

- *m' han tratto su li suoi conforti* ‘me have drawn up his encouragements’ → “his comforts have brought me up”: the subject moves from last to first, and the object follows the participle. Drawing becomes bringing, and the encouragements are rendered as “comforts”.
- *rigirando* ‘turning round and round’ → “circling”: a single verb conveys the motion without separately marking repetition.
- *drizza voi* ‘straightens you’ → “makes you straight”: a single verb is expanded into a verb and a predicative adjective, with the object between them.

> 127 Tanto dice di farmi sua compagna,  
> 128 ch' io sarò là dove fia Beatrice;  
> 129 quivi convien che senza lui rimagna.  

So long he talks of making me his comrade, until I shall be there where Beatrice will be; there must I remain bereft of him.

- *Tanto dice di farmi sua compagna* ‘so long he says he will make me his companion’ → “So long he talks of making me his comrade”: the verb of saying with its dependent infinitive becomes talking of something, with a gerund after the preposition. “Comrade” renders the feminine noun without marking gender.
- *ch' io sarò là* ‘that I shall be there’ → “until I shall be there”: the conjunction is interpreted as marking the temporal limit of the preceding “So long”.
- *dove fia Beatrice* ‘where will be Beatrice’ → “where Beatrice will be”: the subject precedes the verb.
- *quivi convien che senza lui rimagna* ‘there it is necessary that without him I remain’ → “there must I remain bereft of him”: the impersonal verb and its subjunctive clause become a personal construction with a modal. The phrase expressing absence moves after the verb and becomes a participial phrase expressing loss.

> 130 Virgilio è questi che così mi dice  
> 131 (e addita' lo), e quest' altro è quell' ombra  
> 132 per cui scosse dianzi ogni pendice  
> 133 lo vostro regno che da sè lo sgombra.”  

Virgil is he who thus speaks to me (and I pointed to him) and this other is that shade for whom before in every scarp your realm did shake which now discharges him from itself.”

- *questi* ‘this one’ → “he”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *mi dice* ‘to me says’ → “speaks to me”: saying becomes speaking, and the dative comes after the verb as a prepositional phrase.
- *addita' lo* ‘I pointed him out’ → “I pointed to him”: the direct object becomes the complement of a preposition.
- *scosse dianzi ogni pendice lo vostro regno* ‘shook a little while ago every slope your realm’ → “before in every scarp your realm did shake”: the time adverb becomes the less specific “before”; the slopes, directly acted on in Italian, become places where the shaking occurred. The verb moves after its subject and is rendered intransitively, with an auxiliary for the simple past.
- *da sè lo sgombra* ‘from itself him clears away’ → “now discharges him from itself”: “now” is supplied; the object and the phrase of separation follow the verb, in that order.

## Summary — Reordered Syntax, Retained Images

Okey often changes the order of a clause while keeping its parts. In *dall' ossa la pelle s' informava*, the bones come first as the source of the skin’s shape; in “the skin took form from the bones”, they come last. Subjects often move before their verbs, objects after them, and possessives before their nouns. Yet Okey does not smooth away every inversion: “by her prayers devout” keeps the adjective after the noun, and “Thus soon hath led me” leaves the subject, “my Nella”, to follow.

Changes of construction can shift the emphasis. In *qui si rifà santa*, ‘here makes itself holy again’, the collective subject is singular; “sanctify themselves again” treats it as plural, combines verb and adjective in “sanctify”, and leaves out *qui*. Elsewhere Okey expands rather than compresses: *drizza voi*, ‘straightens you’, becomes “makes you straight”, echoing “made crooked” more closely than the two Italian verbs echo one another. He also varies his handling of the imperfect: *dicea* becomes the simple “said”, but *ci ammirava* becomes “was gazing upon us in wonder”, marking duration and distinguishing the gaze from the wonder it expresses.

Okey sometimes gives what an image signifies rather than its literal terms: *gola*, ‘throat’, becomes “appetite”, and *con la sua vena*, ‘with his vein’, becomes “with his blood”. At other points he keeps the image and changes only its form: *anella senza gemme*, ‘rings without gems’, becomes “gemless rings”. Small additions can sharpen a contrast, as when “now” in “now discharges him” answers the earlier “before”. More routinely, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the person by the verb ending alone, and omits the Italian article before a possessive, as in *lo vostro regno*, “your realm”.
