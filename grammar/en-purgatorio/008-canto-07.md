# Purgatorio 7

## Lines 1–18 — Sordello Greets Virgil

> 1 Poscia che l' accoglienze oneste e liete  
> 2 furo iterate tre e quattro volte,  

After the greetings dignified and glad had been repeated three and four times,

- *Poscia che* ‘after that’ → “After”: English uses the conjunction alone.
- *oneste* ‘honourable’ → “dignified”: the adjective is rendered in the sense of bearing or manner.
- *furo iterate* ‘were repeated’ → “had been repeated”: the simple past passive becomes a past perfect passive.

> 3 Sordel si trasse e disse: “Voi chi siete?”  

Sordello drew him back, and said: “Who art thou?”

- *si trasse* ‘himself drew back’ → “drew him back”: the pronoun follows the verb, and “him” is used in the older reflexive sense.
- *Voi chi siete?* ‘you who are?’ → “Who art thou?”: the interrogative comes first and the subject last; the Italian plural, which can address the two travellers, is rendered as an address to one.

> 4 “Prima che a questo monte fosser volte  
> 5 l' anime degne di salire a Dio,  
> 6 fur l' ossa mie per Ottavian sepolte.  

“Ere to this mount were turned those spirits worthy to ascend to God, my bones by Octavian had been buried.

- *Prima che … fosser volte* ‘before … were turned’ → “Ere … were turned”: the English conjunction takes no equivalent of *che*, and the Italian imperfect subjunctive has no distinct mood marking in English.
- *l' anime* ‘the souls’ → “those spirits”: the article becomes a demonstrative and the noun is rendered by a near synonym.
- *fur l' ossa mie per Ottavian sepolte* ‘were the bones of mine by Octavian buried’ → “my bones by Octavian had been buried”: the possessive precedes the noun, the subject and agent both precede the auxiliary, and the simple past passive becomes a past perfect passive.

> 7 Io son Virgilio; e per null' altro rio  
> 8 lo ciel perdei, che per non aver fè”:  

I am Virgil; and for no other sin did I lose heaven than for not having faith”:

- *lo ciel perdei* ‘heaven I lost’ → “did I lose heaven”: the object moves after the verb; the preceding negative phrase calls for inversion in English, with the past tense carried by “did”.
- *non aver* ‘not to have’ → “not having”: after the preposition, English uses an *-ing* form rather than an infinitive.

> 9 così rispose allora il duca mio.  

thus answered then my Leader.

- *il duca mio* ‘the leader of mine’ → “my Leader”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 10 Qual è colui che cosa innanzi sè  
> 11 subita vede, ond' ei si maraviglia,  
> 12 che crede e no, dicendo: “Ell' è, non è”;  

As one who seeth suddenly a thing before him whereat he marvels, who believes, and believes not, saying: “It is, it is not”;

- *Qual è colui* ‘as is the one’ → “As one”: the copula is omitted and the demonstrative pronoun becomes indefinite.
- *che cosa innanzi sè subita vede* ‘who a thing before himself sudden sees’ → “who seeth suddenly a thing before him”: the verb moves before its object; the adjective agreeing with the thing becomes an adverb modifying the seeing, the bare noun gains an article, and the reflexive becomes an ordinary pronoun.
- *che crede e no* ‘who believes and not’ → “who believes, and believes not”: English repeats the verb left understood after the conjunction.

> 13 tal parve quegli, e poi chinò le ciglia,  
> 14 ed umilmente ritornò ver lui,  
> 15 ed abbracciollo ove il minor s' appiglia.  

such seemed he, and forthwith bent his brow, and humbly turned back towards my Leader, and embraced him where the inferior clasps.

- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *poi* ‘then’ → “forthwith”: the translation adds immediacy to the indication of sequence.
- *le ciglia* ‘the brows’ → “his brow”: the plural becomes singular, and English uses a possessive rather than the article for the part of the body.
- *ritornò* ‘returned’ → “turned back”: a verb and adverb render the single verb.
- *lui* ‘him’ → “my Leader”: the translation names the referent instead of using the pronoun.
- *il minor* ‘the lesser’ → “the inferior”: the comparative is rendered by a term of relative rank.
- *s' appiglia* ‘attaches himself’ → “clasps”: English uses a non-reflexive verb of grasping without an expressed object.

> 16 “O gloria de' Latin,” disse, “per cui  
> 17 mostrò ciò che potea la lingua nostra,  
> 18 o pregio eterno del loco ond' io fui,  

“O glory of the Latins,” said he, “by whom our tongue showed forth all its power, O eternal praise of the place whence I sprang,

- *per cui* ‘through whom’ → “by whom”: English uses “by” to express agency.
- *mostrò ciò che potea la lingua nostra* ‘showed what it could do the tongue of ours’ → “our tongue showed forth all its power”: the subject moves before the verb and the possessive before the noun; “forth” expands the verb, and a noun phrase with an explicit “all” replaces the clause expressing what the tongue could do.
- *pregio eterno* ‘honour eternal’ → “eternal praise”: the adjective precedes the noun, and a word for honour or distinction is rendered by “praise”.
- *fui* ‘was’ → “sprang”: a verb of origin replaces the verb of being.

## Lines 19–51 — Virgil’s Abode and the Ascent by Night

> 19 qual merito o qual grazia mi ti mostra?  

what merit or what favour sheweth thee to me?

- *mi ti mostra* ‘to me thee sheweth’ → “sheweth thee to me”: both pronouns follow the verb, with the direct object first and the indirect object expressed with a preposition.

> 20 S' io son d'udir le tue parole degno,  
> 21 dimmi se vien d' inferno, e di qual chiostra.”  

If I am worthy to hear thy words, tell me if thou comest from Hell, and from what cloister.”

- *d'udir le tue parole degno* ‘of hearing thy words worthy’ → “worthy to hear thy words”: the adjective comes first, and its complement is a *to*-infinitive rather than an infinitive governed by *di*.
- *dimmi se vien* ‘tell me whether thou comest’ → “tell me if thou comest”: “if” introduces the indirect question.
- *di qual chiostra* ‘of what cloister’ → “from what cloister”: the preposition is taken in the sense of origin, parallel to the preceding “from Hell”.

> 22 “Per tutti i cerchi del dolente regno,”  
> 23 rispose lui, “son io di qua venuto.  

“Through all the circles of the woeful realm,” answered he him, “came I here.

- *regno* (in *del dolente regno*) ‘kingdom’ → “realm”: a different noun for the dominion.
- *rispose lui* ‘answered he’ → “answered he him”: besides the speaker, English names the person answered by a pronoun.
- *son io di qua venuto* ‘am I hither come’ → “came I here”: the compound past with *essere* becomes a simple past; the verb of motion now precedes the subject, and the expression of destination follows it.

> 24 Virtù del ciel mi mosse, e con lei vegno.  

A virtue from heaven moved me, and with it I come.

- *Virtù del ciel* ‘virtue of heaven’ → “A virtue from heaven”: the indefinite article is supplied, and the relation to heaven is expressed as one of origin.
- *mi mosse* ‘me moved’ → “moved me”: the object follows the verb.
- *con lei* ‘with her’ → “with it”: the pronoun agreeing with the feminine noun becomes neuter.

> 25 Non per far, ma per non far ho perduto  
> 26 di veder l' alto Sol che tu disiri,  
> 27 e che fu tardi da me conosciuto.  

Not for doing, but for not doing, have I lost the vision of the high Sun, whom thou desirest, and who too late by me was known.

- *ho perduto di veder l' alto Sol* ‘I have lost the seeing of the high Sun’ → “have I lost the vision of the high Sun”: the auxiliary precedes the subject after the opening negative contrast; the infinitive becomes a noun, with its former direct object introduced by “of”.
- *che fu tardi da me conosciuto* ‘who was late by me known’ → “who too late by me was known”: “too” makes the force of the lateness explicit, and the auxiliary is moved next to the participle.

> 28 Loco è laggiù non tristo da martiri,  
> 29 ma di tenebre solo, ove i lamenti  
> 30 non suonan come guai, ma son sospiri.  

Down there is a place not sad with torments, but with darkness alone, where the lamentations sound not as wailings, but are sighs.

- *Loco è laggiù* ‘a place is down there’ → “Down there is a place”: the indication of place comes first and the subject last.
- *da martiri* ‘from torments’ → “with torments”: the cause of the sadness is expressed with “with”.
- *di tenebre solo* ‘of darkness alone’ → “with darkness alone”: English uses the same preposition as for the torments, where Italian changes preposition.
- *non suonan* ‘not sound’ → “sound not”: the negative follows the verb.

> 31 Quivi sto io coi parvoli innocenti,  
> 32 dai denti morsi della morte, avante  
> 33 che fosser dall' umana colpa esenti.  

There do I abide with the innocent babes, bitten by the fangs of death, ere they were exempt from human sin.

- *sto io* ‘abide I’ → “do I abide”: an auxiliary carries the inversion after “There”.
- *parvoli innocenti* ‘little ones innocent’ → “innocent babes”: the adjective comes first, and “babes” gives a more specific age than ‘little ones’.
- *dai denti morsi della morte* ‘by the teeth bitten of death’ → “bitten by the fangs of death”: the participle comes first, bringing the noun and its dependent phrase together; ‘teeth’ becomes the more specific “fangs”.
- *avante che fosser dall' umana colpa esenti* ‘before that they were from human fault exempt’ → “ere they were exempt from human sin”: a single conjunction introduces the clause, with no distinct subjunctive form in English; the adjective precedes its complement, and ‘fault’ is rendered as “sin”.

> 34 Quivi sto io con quei che le tre sante  
> 35 virtù non si vestiro, e senza vizio  
> 36 conobber l' altre e seguir tutte e quante.  

There dwell I with those who clad them not with the three holy virtues, and without offence knew the others and followed them all.

- *sto io* ‘abide I’ → “dwell I”: the verb repeated from line 31 is varied in English.
- *le tre sante virtù non si vestiro* ‘the three holy virtues not themselves they clothed with’ → “clad them not with the three holy virtues”: the fronted virtues follow the verb in a prepositional phrase; “them” does reflexive duty, and the negative follows it.
- *senza vizio* ‘without vice’ → “without offence”: a term for fault or wrongdoing replaces ‘vice’.
- *seguir tutte e quante* ‘followed all and every one’ → “followed them all”: a pronoun resumes “the others”, and the emphatic double expression of totality is reduced to “all”.

> 37 Ma se tu sai e puoi, alcuno indizio  
> 38 dà noi, per che venir possiam più tosto  
> 39 là dove Purgatorio ha dritto inizio.”  

But if thou knowest and canst, give us some sign whereby we may most quickly come there where Purgatory has right beginning.”

- *alcuno indizio dà noi* ‘some indication give us’ → “give us some sign”: the direct object moves from first to last, and ‘indication’ is rendered as “sign”.
- *per che* ‘by which’ → “whereby”: the preposition and relative are combined.
- *venir possiam più tosto* ‘come we may sooner’ → “we may most quickly come”: the modal precedes the infinitive, and the comparative becomes a superlative adverbial phrase placed between them.

> 40 Rispose: “Loco certo non c' è posto:  

He answered: “No fixed place is set for us:

- *Loco certo non c' è posto* ‘a place fixed is not set for us’ → “No fixed place is set for us”: the negative passes from the verb to the determiner of the subject, the adjective precedes the noun, and the preverbal clitic is rendered by a final prepositional phrase.

> 41 licito m' è andar suso ed intorno;  
> 42 per quanto ir posso, a guida mi t'accosto.  

’tis permitted to me to go up and around; so far as I may go, as guide I place me beside thee.

- *licito m' è* ‘lawful to me it is’ → “’tis permitted to me”: the copula comes first, ‘lawful’ becomes a participle, and the personal complement comes last.
- *per quanto ir posso* ‘for as far as go I can’ → “so far as I may go”: English uses a correlative expression of extent and puts the modal before the infinitive; “may” suggests permission rather than simply capacity.
- *a guida* ‘for a guide’ → “as guide”: the role is expressed with “as”.
- *mi t'accosto* ‘myself to thee I draw near’ → “I place me beside thee”: the verb precedes its complements, and drawing near is expressed as taking a position beside someone.

> 43 Ma vedi già come dichina il giorno,  
> 44 ed andar su di notte non si puote;  
> 45 però è buon pensar di bel soggiorno.  

But see now how the day is declining, and ascend by night we cannot; therefore ’tis well to think of some fair resting-place.

- *già* ‘already’ → “now”: the adverb marks the present moment without explicitly saying ‘already’.
- *dichina il giorno* ‘declines the day’ → “the day is declining”: the subject precedes the verb and the simple present becomes progressive.
- *andar su* ‘go up’ → “ascend”: one verb replaces the verb and adverb.
- *non si puote* ‘one cannot’ → “we cannot”: the impersonal construction becomes first-person plural.
- *di bel soggiorno* ‘of fair sojourn’ → “of some fair resting-place”: the determiner “some” is supplied, and the expression for a stay is taken as naming the place for it.

> 46 Anime sono a destra qua rimote;  
> 47 se 'l mi consenti, io ti merrò ad esse,  
> 48 e non senza diletto ti fien note.”  

Here are souls on the right apart; if thou allow it I will lead thee to them, and not without joy will they be known to thee.”

- *Anime sono a destra qua rimote* ‘souls are on the right here set apart’ → “Here are souls on the right apart”: the local adverb opens the clause, the subject follows the verb, and the participial ‘set apart’ is rendered by “apart”.
- *'l mi consenti* ‘it to me thou grantest’ → “thou allow it”: the direct object follows the verb and the indirect object is omitted.
- *io ti merrò* ‘I thee shall lead’ → “I will lead thee”: the inflected future becomes an auxiliary construction and the object follows the main verb.
- *diletto* ‘delight’ → “joy”: a different noun is used.
- *ti fien note* ‘to thee they will be known’ → “will they be known to thee”: the personal complement comes last, and the future auxiliary precedes the subject after the fronted negative phrase.

> 49 “Com' è ciò?” fu risposto; “chi volesse  
> 50 salir di notte, fora egli impedito  
> 51 d'altrui? o non sarria che non potesse?”  

“How is that?” was answered; “he who wished to ascend by night, would he be hindered by others, or would he not ascend because he could not?”

- *chi volesse* ‘whoever might wish’ → “he who wished”: an explicit antecedent and relative replace the indefinite relative, and a past form conveys the hypothetical force of the Italian subjunctive.
- *fora egli impedito* ‘would-be he hindered’ → “would he be hindered”: an auxiliary construction replaces the single conditional form of ‘be’, with the subject between “would” and “be”.
- *d'altrui* ‘by another’ → “by others”: English chooses an explicitly plural form for the indefinite pronoun.
- *non sarria che non potesse* ‘would he not climb because he could not’ → “would he not ascend because he could not”: the conditional of ‘climb’ becomes an auxiliary with “ascend”, and the final subjunctive is represented by the modal “could”.

## Lines 52–84 — The Halt in the Flowering Valley

> 52 E il buon Sordello in terra fregò il dito,  
> 53 dicendo: “Vedi, sola questa riga  
> 54 non varcheresti dopo il sol partito;  

And the good Sordello drew his finger across the ground, saying: “Look, even this line thou wouldst not cross after the sun is set;

- *in terra fregò il dito* ‘on the ground rubbed the finger’ → “drew his finger across the ground”: the verb of rubbing becomes a verb of motion with “across”; the phrase of place moves to the end, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *sola questa riga* ‘this line alone’ → “even this line”: the restriction expressed by the adjective becomes an emphasis expressed by “even”.
- *dopo il sol partito* ‘after the sun departed’ → “after the sun is set”: the participle becomes a clause with “is”, and departure is rendered specifically as sunset.

> 55 non però che altra cosa desse briga,  
> 56 che la notturna tenebra, ad ir suso:  

not for that aught else than the darkness of night gave hindrance to going upward:

- *non però che* ‘not on this account, that’ → “not for that”: the demonstrative and conjunction together are rendered by a causal turn.
- *altra cosa desse briga, che la notturna tenebra* ‘another thing should give trouble, than the nocturnal darkness’ → “aught else than the darkness of night gave hindrance”: the exception is placed immediately after “aught else”, before the verb; the imperfect subjunctive becomes a simple past, and the adjective before the noun becomes an “of” phrase after it.
- *ad ir suso* ‘to go upward’ → “to going upward”: the infinitive becomes a gerund governed by the preposition.

> 57 quella col non poter la voglia intriga.  

that hampers the will with lack of power.

- *col non poter la voglia intriga* ‘with the not-being-able the will entangles’ → “hampers the will with lack of power”: the verb precedes its object, the instrumental phrase moves to the end, and the negated infinitive becomes a phrase with the noun “lack”.

> 58 Ben si poría con lei tornare in giuso,  
> 59 e passeggiar la costa intorno errando,  
> 60 mentre che l' orizzonte il dì tien chiuso.”  

Truly by night one might return downwards, and walk, wandering around the mountain side, while the horizon holds the day closed.”

- *si poría con lei* ‘one could with it’ → “by night one might”: the phrase with the pronoun is interpreted as a time phrase, names the night explicitly, and moves before the modal verb.
- *passeggiar la costa intorno errando* ‘walk the slope, about wandering’ → “walk, wandering around the mountain side”: the slope is specified as the side of the mountain and made the object of “around”; the participle moves forward.
- *il dì tien chiuso* ‘the day holds closed’ → “holds the day closed”: the object moves after the verb.

> 61 Allora il mio signor, quasi ammirando:  
> 62 “Menane, dunque,” disse, “là ove dici  
> 63 che aver si può diletto dimorando.”  

Then my Lord, as tho’ marvelling, said: “Lead us therefore where thou sayest we may have delight in tarrying.”

- *Menane, dunque, disse* ‘Lead us, therefore, he said’ → “said: “Lead us therefore”: the reporting verb moves from within the speech to its beginning.
- *là ove* ‘there where’ → “where”: the separate word of place is omitted.
- *dici che aver si può diletto* ‘thou sayest that have one can delight’ → “thou sayest we may have delight”: the conjunction is omitted, the impersonal construction becomes first-person plural, and the modal precedes the infinitive.
- *dimorando* ‘tarrying’ → “in tarrying”: the relation of the gerund to “delight” is expressed by a preposition.

> 64 Poco allungati c' eravam di lici,  
> 65 quand' io m' accorsi che il monte era scemo,  
> 66 a guisa che i vallon li sceman quici.  

Short way had we thence advanced, when I perceived that the mount was scooped out, after the fashion that valleys scoop them out here.

- *Poco allungati c' eravam di lici* ‘little removed ourselves we had from there’ → “Short way had we thence advanced”: the adverb of extent becomes a noun phrase, the reflexive verb of distancing oneself becomes an intransitive verb of advance, and the phrase of origin becomes an adverb placed before the participle.
- *io m' accorsi* ‘I became aware’ → “I perceived”: the reflexive construction is rendered by a simple transitive verb.
- *era scemo* ‘was diminished’ → “was scooped out”: the rendering specifies the shape of the diminution and uses a passive participle.
- *i vallon li sceman* ‘the valleys them diminish’ → “valleys scoop them out”: the generic article is omitted, the object follows the verb, and the verb is made more specific, as in the preceding line.

> 67 “Colà,” disse quell' ombra, “n' anderemo  
> 68 dove la costa face di sè grembo,  
> 69 e quivi il nuovo giorno attenderemo.”  

“There,” said the shade, “we will go where the mountain-side makes of itself a bosom, and there will await the new day.”

- *quell' ombra* ‘that shade’ → “the shade”: the demonstrative becomes a definite article.
- *n' anderemo* ‘we shall go away’ → “we will go”: the particle marking departure has no separate equivalent.
- *la costa* ‘the slope’ → “the mountain-side”: the English compound specifies what the slope belongs to.
- *il nuovo giorno attenderemo* ‘the new day we shall await’ → “will await the new day”: the object follows the verb.

> 70 Tra erto e piano era un sentiero sghembo,  
> 71 che ne condusse in fianco della lacca,  
> 72 là dove più che a mezzo muore il lembo.  

Neither steep nor level was a winding path, that led us to the side of that hollow, there where the valley’s edge more than half dies away.

- *Tra erto e piano* ‘between steep and level’ → “Neither steep nor level”: a position between two extremes is expressed by negating both.
- *un sentiero sghembo* ‘a path slanting’ → “a winding path”: obliqueness is interpreted as winding, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *ne condusse* ‘us led’ → “led us”: the object follows the verb.
- *in fianco della lacca* ‘on the flank of the hollow’ → “to the side of that hollow”: the preposition marks destination, “side” replaces the bodily image, and the article becomes demonstrative.
- *più che a mezzo muore il lembo* ‘more than halfway dies the rim’ → “the valley’s edge more than half dies away”: the subject moves before the verb, “the valley’s” identifies whose edge it is, and “away” gives the verb the sense of gradually disappearing.

> 73 Oro ed argento fino, cocco e biacca,  
> 74 indico legno lucido e sereno,  
> 75 fresco smeraldo in l' ora che si fiacca,  

Gold and fine silver, cramoisy and white, Indian wood bright and clear, fresh emerald at the moment it is split,

- *argento fino* ‘silver fine’ → “fine silver”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *cocco e biacca* ‘scarlet dye and white lead’ → “cramoisy and white”: the substances are rendered by colour names.
- *indico legno* ‘indigo, wood’ → “Indian wood”: the translation takes the first word as an adjective qualifying the second, rather than as a separate colour in the list.
- *in l' ora che si fiacca* ‘in the hour when it breaks’ → “at the moment it is split”: the time phrase is narrowed to an instant, the English needs no expressed ‘when’, and the pronominal verb is rendered as a passive.

> 76 dall' erba e dalli fior dentro a quel seno  
> 77 posti, ciascun saría di color vinto,  
> 78 come dal suo maggiore è vinto il meno.  

would each be surpassed in colour by the grass and by the flowers placed within that fold, as the less is surpassed by the greater.

- *dall' erba e dalli fior dentro a quel seno posti* ‘by the grass and by the flowers within that bosom placed’ → “by the grass and by the flowers placed within that fold”: the whole agent phrase moves after the predicate, the participle precedes its place phrase, and “fold” replaces the bodily image.
- *ciascun saría di color vinto* ‘each would be in colour vanquished’ → “would each be surpassed in colour”: “each” follows the conditional auxiliary, the phrase specifying the respect of comparison follows the participle, and defeat is rendered as being surpassed.
- *dal suo maggiore è vinto il meno* ‘by its greater is vanquished the less’ → “the less is surpassed by the greater”: the subject moves to the front and the agent to the end; the possessive relation is not expressed, and the verb again denotes comparison rather than defeat.

> 79 Non avea pur natura ivi dipinto,  
> 80 ma di soavità di mille odori  
> 81 vi facea un incognito e indistinto.  

Not only had Nature painted there, but of the sweetness of a thousand scents made there one, unknown and indefinable.

- *Non avea pur natura ivi dipinto* ‘not had only nature there painted’ → “Not only had Nature painted there”: “only” comes immediately after the negative, and the place adverb follows the participle.
- *vi facea un incognito e indistinto* ‘there made an unknown and indistinct [thing]’ → “made there one, unknown and indefinable”: the place adverb follows the verb; “one” stands on its own, with the adjectives following as a supplement, and ‘indistinct’ is interpreted as inability to define.

> 82 *Salve, Regina*, in sul verde e in sui fiori  
> 83 quivi seder cantando anime vidi,  
> 84 che per la valle non parean di fuori.  

There, seated on the grass and on the flowers, singing *Salve Regina*, saw I souls who because of the valley were not seen from without.

- *Salve, Regina, in sul verde e in sui fiori quivi seder cantando* ‘Salve, Regina, on the green and on the flowers there to sit singing’ → “There, seated on the grass and on the flowers, singing *Salve Regina*”: the place adverb moves to the front; the infinitive dependent on the verb of seeing becomes “seated” and precedes the phrase of place; ‘the green’ becomes specifically “the grass”, and the words sung move from the beginning to follow “singing”.
- *anime vidi* ‘souls I saw’ → “saw I souls”: the object moves after the verb and its expressed subject.
- *non parean di fuori* ‘did not appear from outside’ → “were not seen from without”: the intransitive verb of appearing becomes a passive verb of perception.

## Lines 85–117 — The Princes in the Hollow

> 85 “Prima che il poco sole omai s' annidi,”  

“Ere the little sun now sinks to his nest,”

- *s' annidi* ‘nestles down’ → “sinks to his nest”: the reflexive verb becomes a verb of motion with the nest expressed as its goal; the subjunctive after ‘ere’ becomes an English present indicative.

> 86 cominciò il Mantovan che ci avea volti,  

began the Mantuan who had led us aside,

- *ci avea volti* ‘us had turned’ → “had led us aside”: turning becomes leading aside, and the object follows the verb.

> 87 “tra color non vogliate ch' io vi guidi.  

“desire not that I guide you among them.

- *tra color* ‘among those’ → “among them”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the phrase moves from the head of the clause to the end.
- *non vogliate* ‘do not wish’ → “desire not”: the negative follows the verb.
- *vi guidi* ‘you guide’ → “guide you”: the object follows the verb.

> 88 Di questo balzo meglio gli atti e i volti  
> 89 conoscerete voi di tutti e quanti,  
> 90 che nella lama giù tra essi accolti.  

From this terrace ye will better know the acts and faces of them all, than if received among them down in the hollow.

- *Di questo balzo* ‘from this ledge’ → “From this terrace”: the ledge is rendered as a terrace.
- *gli atti e i volti* ‘the gestures and the faces’ → “the acts and faces”: “acts” is broader than visible gestures, and one article serves both nouns.
- *conoscerete voi* ‘will know ye’ → “ye will better know”: the expressed subject moves before the verb, and the verb comes before its objects rather than after them.
- *di tutti e quanti* ‘of all and every one’ → “of them all”: the emphatic double expression is condensed, with “them” supplied.
- *che nella lama giù tra essi accolti* ‘than in the hollow down among them received’ → “than if received among them down in the hollow”: “if” makes the implicit condition explicit; the participle comes first, and the two phrases of place are reversed.

> 91 Colui, che più sied' alto e fa sembianti  
> 92 d' aver negletto ciò che far dovea,  
> 93 e che non move bocca agli altrui canti,  

He who sits highest, and hath semblance of having left undone what he ought to have done, and who moves not his lips with the others’ songs,

- *Colui* ‘that one’ → “He”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *più sied' alto* ‘higher sits’ → “sits highest”: the comparison is rendered as a superlative, placed after the verb.
- *fa sembianti* ‘makes signs’ → “hath semblance”: making outward signs becomes having an appearance, with a singular noun for the plural.
- *d' aver negletto* ‘of having neglected’ → “of having left undone”: a verb and result complement replace the single participle.
- *ciò che far dovea* ‘that which to do he ought’ → “what he ought to have done”: “what” combines antecedent and relative; the infinitive follows the modal and is made perfect to mark the unperformed duty as past.
- *non move bocca* ‘does not move mouth’ → “moves not his lips”: the negative follows the verb; plural lips replace the singular mouth, with a possessive supplied.
- *agli altrui canti* ‘to the others’ songs’ → “with the others’ songs”: “with” expresses accompaniment in place of ‘to’.

> 94 Ridolfo imperador fu, che potea  
> 95 sanar le piaghe ch' hanno Italia morta,  
> 96 sì che tardi per altri si ricrea.  

was Rudolph the Emperor, who might have healed the wounds that were the death of Italy, so that too late through another is she succoured.

- *Ridolfo imperador fu* ‘Rudolph emperor was’ → “was Rudolph the Emperor”: the copula comes first, and the title takes an article.
- *potea sanar* ‘could heal’ → “might have healed”: the imperfect of ability with a present infinitive becomes a modal with a perfect infinitive, bringing out the unrealized possibility.
- *ch' hanno Italia morta* ‘that have killed Italy’ → “that were the death of Italy”: the active perfect becomes a past copula with a noun complement; Italy becomes the complement of “of” rather than the object of the verb.
- *tardi* ‘late’ → “too late”: “too” strengthens the adverb.
- *per altri* ‘by another’ → “through another”: agency is expressed as mediation.
- *si ricrea* ‘is restored’ → “is she succoured”: restoration becomes succour; the Italian *si* construction becomes an English passive with the subject between auxiliary and participle.

> 97 L' altro, che nella vista lui conforta,  
> 98 resse la terra dove l' acqua nasce,  
> 99 che Molta in Albia ed Albia in mar ne porta:  

The other, who looks to be comforting him, ruled the land where the water rises which the Moldau carries away into the Elbe, and the Elbe into the sea:

- *nella vista lui conforta* ‘in appearance him comforts’ → “looks to be comforting him”: the phrase of appearance becomes the finite verb, comforting becomes a progressive infinitive, and its object follows it.
- *in Albia ed Albia in mar ne porta* ‘into Elbe and Elbe into sea thence carries’ → “carries away into the Elbe, and the Elbe into the sea”: the shared verb comes before the destinations rather than after both; “away” renders the movement from the source indicated by *ne*, and the English place expressions take articles.

> 100 Ottacchero ebbe nome, e nelle fasce  
> 101 fu meglio assai, che Vincislao suo figlio  
> 102 barbuto, cui lussuria ed ozio pasce.  

Ottocar for name had he, and in swaddling clothes was better far than bearded Wenceslas his son, whom lust and sloth consume.

- *ebbe nome* ‘had name’ → “for name had he”: “for” is supplied, and the name phrase precedes the verb.
- *nelle fasce* ‘in the bands’ → “in swaddling clothes”: the bands are specified as swaddling clothes, without an article.
- *Vincislao suo figlio barbuto* ‘Wenceslas his son bearded’ → “bearded Wenceslas his son”: the adjective moves from after the apposition to before the name.
- *pasce* ‘feeds’ → “consume”: nourishment becomes consumption, and English uses a plural verb for the compound subject.

> 103 E quel nasetto, che stretto a consiglio  
> 104 par con colui ch' ha sì benigno aspetto,  
> 105 morì fuggendo e disfiorando il giglio:  

And that snub-nosed one, who seems close in counsel with him that hath so kindly a mien, died in flight and deflowering the lily:

- *quel nasetto* ‘that little nose’ → “that snub-nosed one”: the diminutive noun naming the person by a feature becomes an adjective describing the person; “snub” specifies shape rather than simply smallness.
- *stretto a consiglio par* ‘close in counsel seems’ → “seems close in counsel”: the verb precedes its complement, and ‘at counsel’ is expressed as “in counsel”.
- *colui* ‘that one’ → “him”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *sì benigno aspetto* ‘so kindly mien’ → “so kindly a mien”: English supplies an article after the intensified adjective.
- *fuggendo* ‘fleeing’ → “in flight”: the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase.

> 106 guardate là come si batte il petto.  

look there how he is beating his breast.

- *si batte il petto* ‘beats himself the breast’ → “he is beating his breast”: the reflexive marking the person affected becomes a possessive on the body part, and the simple present becomes progressive.

> 107 L' altro vedete ch' ha fatto alla guancia  
> 108 della sua palma, sospirando, letto.  

The other see, who, sighing, hath made a bed for his cheek with the palm of his hand.

- *ch' ha fatto alla guancia della sua palma, sospirando, letto* ‘who has made for the cheek of his palm, sighing, a bed’ → “who, sighing, hath made a bed for his cheek with the palm of his hand”: “sighing” moves before the verb and “a bed” immediately after it, with an article supplied. The cheek takes a possessive; the palm, expressed in Italian as the material of the bed, is introduced by “with”, and “of his hand” expands the possessive.

> 109 Padre e suocero son del mal di Francia:  

Father and father-in-law are they of the plague of France:

- *mal* ‘evil’ → “plague”: a more specific figurative noun replaces the general one.

> 110 sanno la vita sua viziata e lorda,  

they know his wicked and foul life,

- *la vita sua viziata e lorda* ‘the life his vicious and foul’ → “his wicked and foul life”: the possessive and both adjectives precede the noun; “wicked” renders the participial adjective meaning corrupted by vice.

> 111 e quindi viene il duol che sì li lancia.  

and hence comes the grief that pierceth them so.

- *sì li lancia* ‘so them pierces’ → “pierceth them so”: the object follows the verb, and the adverb of degree comes last.

> 112 Quel che par sì membruto, e che s' accorda  
> 113 cantando con colui dal maschio naso,  
> 114 d' ogni valor portò cinta la corda.  

He who seems so stout of limb, and accords his singing with him of the virile nose, was begirt with the cord of every worth.

- *Quel* ‘that one’ → “He”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *membruto* ‘stout-limbed’ → “stout of limb”: a phrase replaces the single adjective.
- *s' accorda cantando* ‘accords himself in singing’ → “accords his singing”: singing, expressed by a gerund of manner, becomes the object of the verb in place of the reflexive.
- *colui dal maschio naso* ‘that one with the manly nose’ → “him of the virile nose”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative, and “of” marks the distinguishing feature.
- *d' ogni valor portò cinta la corda* ‘of every worth wore girt the cord’ → “was begirt with the cord of every worth”: the active construction with the cord as object becomes a passive with the cord introduced by “with”; the phrase qualifying the cord moves from first to last.

> 115 E se re dopo lui fosse rimaso  
> 116 lo giovinetto che retro a lui siede,  
> 117 bene andava il valor di vaso in vaso;  

And if the lad who sits behind him had remained king after him, the worth would in truth have passed from vessel to vessel;

- *re dopo lui fosse rimaso* ‘king after him had remained’ → “had remained king after him”: the verb precedes its complements, and the whole predicate follows the subject instead of preceding it.
- *lo giovinetto che retro a lui siede* ‘the young lad who behind him sits’ → “the lad who sits behind him”: “lad” carries the suggestion of youth without a separate diminutive; the relative clause puts the verb before the phrase of place.
- *bene andava il valor* ‘well went the worth’ → “the worth would in truth have passed”: “in truth” takes the adverb as emphatic rather than as describing how well the worth went; the imperfect becomes a conditional perfect in the unreal consequence, “passed” specifies the movement, and the subject precedes the verb.

## Lines 118–136 — Kingdoms and Virtue Among the Heirs

> 118 che non si puote dir dell' altre erede.  

which may not be said of the other heirs.

- *non si puote dir* ‘one cannot say’ → “may not be said”: the impersonal construction with *si* becomes an English passive, with the negative after the modal.

> 119 Jacomo e Federico hanno i reami;  
> 120 del rettaggio miglior nessun possiede.  

James and Frederick have the realms: of the better heritage none hath possession.

- *del rettaggio miglior* ‘of the heritage better’ → “of the better heritage”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *nessun possiede* ‘none possesses’ → “none hath possession”: the verb becomes a phrase with a noun, to which the preceding “of the better heritage” is attached.

> 121 Rade volte risurge per li rami  
> 122 l' umana probitate: e questo vuole  
> 123 quei che la dà, perchè da lui si chiami.  

Rarely doth human probity rise through the branches: and this he wills who giveth it, so that it may be prayed for from him.

- *Rade volte risurge per li rami l' umana probitate* ‘few times rises again through the branches human probity’ → “Rarely doth human probity rise through the branches”: an adverb replaces the phrase of frequency; the subject loses its definite article and moves from the end to between the auxiliary and the main verb. The sense ‘again’ in *risurge* is not expressed.
- *e questo vuole quei che la dà* ‘and this wills he who gives it’ → “and this he wills who giveth it”: the subject pronoun moves before the verb, leaving its relative clause after the verb.
- *perchè da lui si chiami* ‘in order that from him it be called for’ → “so that it may be prayed for from him”: the purpose clause takes a modal passive for the Italian subjunctive with *si*; calling for something is interpreted as praying for it, and the phrase denoting its source moves to the end.

> 124 Anche al nasuto vanno mie parole,  
> 125 non men ch' all' altro, Pier, che con lui canta,  
> 126 onde Puglia e Provenza già si duole.  

Also to the big-nosed one my words do go, not less than to the other, Peter, who is singing with him, wherefore Apulia and Provence now moan.

- *al nasuto* ‘to the big-nosed’ → “to the big-nosed one”: English supplies a noun substitute for the adjective used substantivally.
- *vanno mie parole* ‘go my words’ → “my words do go”: the subject moves before the verb, which takes the auxiliary “do”.
- *che con lui canta* ‘who with him sings’ → “who is singing with him”: the simple present becomes progressive, and the phrase of accompaniment moves after the verb.
- *già si duole* ‘already grieves’ → “now moan”: “now” replaces ‘already’; the pronominal verb becomes a verb of audible lament, plural in agreement with the two names rather than singular as in Italian.

> 127 Tant' è del seme suo minor la pianta,  
> 128 quanto, più che Beatrice e Margherita,  
> 129 Costanza di marito ancor si vanta.  

So much is the plant degenerate from its seed as, more than Beatrice and Margaret, Constance yet boasts of her husband.

- *Tant' è del seme suo minor la pianta* ‘so much is than its seed inferior the plant’ → “So much is the plant degenerate from its seed”: the subject moves immediately after “is”; inferiority to the seed becomes degeneracy from it, with the possessive before “seed” rather than after it.
- *quanto* ‘by how much’ → “as”: English uses a conjunction to complete the comparison begun by “So much”.
- *di marito ancor si vanta* ‘of husband still boasts’ → “yet boasts of her husband”: the complement moves after the verb, with a possessive supplied; the Italian pronominal verb needs no reflexive in English.

> 130 Vedete il re della semplice vita  
> 131 seder là solo, Arrigo d' Inghilterra:  
> 132 questi ha ne' rami suoi migliore uscita.  

See the king of the simple life, sitting there alone, Henry of England: he in his branches hath better issue.

- *vedete … seder* ‘see … sit’ → “See” … “sitting”: the infinitive dependent on the verb of perception becomes a participle.
- *questi* ‘this one’ → “he”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *ha ne' rami suoi migliore uscita* ‘has in the branches his better outcome’ → “in his branches hath better issue”: the phrase about the branches moves before the verb, and the possessive before the noun; “issue” gives ‘outcome’ the more specific sense of offspring.

> 133 Quel che più basso tra costor s' atterra,  
> 134 guardando in suso, è Guglielmo marchese,  
> 135 per cui ed Alessandria e la sua guerra  
> 136 fa pianger Monferrato e Canavese.”  

That one who lower down humbleth himself among them, gazing upward, is William the Marquis, through whom Alessandria and its war make Montferrato and the Canavese to weep.

- *tra costor* ‘among these’ → “among them”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the phrase moves after the verb.
- *s' atterra* ‘lowers himself to the ground’ → “humbleth himself”: the rendering interprets the physical lowering as an act of humility; the reflexive follows the verb.
- *Guglielmo marchese* ‘William Marquis’ → “William the Marquis”: English supplies a definite article before the title.
- *ed Alessandria e la sua guerra* ‘both Alessandria and its war’ → “Alessandria and its war”: the first of the paired conjunctions is not rendered.
- *fa pianger Monferrato e Canavese* ‘makes weep Montferrato and Canavese’ → “make Montferrato and the Canavese to weep”: the causative verb becomes plural to agree with the compound subject; the names move before the infinitive, which takes “to”, and the second name gains a definite article.

## Summary — English Word Order and Italian Phrasing

Okey often moves a subject or object to give the English a more usual order: in *il nuovo giorno attenderemo* the object precedes the verb, while in “will await the new day” it follows. Two smaller adjustments are generally left unmarked in the comparison. Where an Italian verb ending identifies the subject, English commonly supplies a subject pronoun; where Italian puts an article before a possessive, English generally leaves it out, as in *le tue parole*, “thy words”. Yet Okey does not consistently smooth away Dante’s inversions: “thus answered then my Leader” keeps both adverbs in place and leaves the subject to the end.

Changes of construction often preserve a distinction without preserving its grammatical form. In *dichina il giorno*, “the day is declining”, the subject moves forward and the simple present becomes progressive. The impersonal *non si puote* becomes the personal “we cannot”, while the adjective *subita*, which describes the thing seen, becomes “suddenly”, describing the act of seeing. Okey sometimes supplies what Italian leaves understood: *crede e no* repeats no verb after the conjunction, but the English has “believes, and believes not”. Elsewhere he condenses an emphatic expression, as when *tutte e quante* becomes “them all”.

A change of word can make an image more specific: *denti* (‘teeth’) becomes “fangs”, and *era scemo* (‘was diminished’) becomes “was scooped out”. A pronoun, too, may give way to its referent, as when *ver lui* becomes “towards my Leader”. But Okey stays close where an Italian turn carries into English: *non senza diletto* remains “not without joy”, preserving the emphasis of the negative, and *di vaso in vaso* becomes “from vessel to vessel”, keeping both the image and the repetition.
