# Purgatorio 21

## Lines 1–9 — Natural Thirst and the Risen Christ

> 1 La sete natural che mai non sazia,  
> 2 se non con l' acqua onde la femminetta  
> 3 Sammaritana domandò la grazia,  

The natural thirst which never is sated, save with the water whereof the poor Samaritan woman asked the grace,

- *La sete natural* ‘the thirst natural’ → “The natural thirst”: the adjective comes before the noun.
- *mai non sazia* ‘never satisfies’ → “never is sated”: the single English negative renders *mai non*, and the active verb becomes passive: the thirst is itself unsatisfied.
- *se non* ‘if not’ → “save”: the two words become a single word of exception.
- *la femminetta Sammaritana* ‘the little woman Samaritan’ → “the poor Samaritan woman”: “poor” conveys the sympathetic force of the diminutive rather than smallness, and “Samaritan” comes before the noun.

> 4 mi travagliava, e pungeami la fretta  
> 5 per la impacciata via retro al mio duca,  

was burning within me, and haste was goading me along the encumbered way behind my Leader,

- *mi travagliava* ‘me it tormented’ → “was burning within me”: torment becomes burning; the object of the Italian verb becomes the object of “within”, and the progressive renders the imperfect.
- *pungeami la fretta* ‘was pricking me the haste’ → “haste was goading me”: the subject moves from after the verb and its attached pronoun to before them and loses its article; “goading” renders the figurative force of ‘pricking’.
- *per* ‘through’ → “along”: the preposition expresses movement along the way rather than passage through it.

> 6 e condoleami alla giusta vendetta;  

and I was grieving at the just penance;

- *condoleami* ‘I was sorrowing with’ → “I was grieving”: the pronominal verb becomes a simple intransitive verb, without the explicit suggestion of fellow feeling.
- *vendetta* ‘retribution’ → “penance”: the word for punishment is rendered by one for the penalty undergone.

> 7 ed ecco, sì come ne scrive Luca  
> 8 che Cristo apparve ai due ch' erano in via,  
> 9 già surto fuor della sepulcral buca,  

and lo, even as Luke writes to us that Christ appeared to the two who were on the way, already risen from the mouth of the tomb,

- *ne scrive Luca* ‘to us writes Luke’ → “Luke writes to us”: the subject comes before the verb and the recipient after it.
- *in via* ‘on way’ → “on the way”: English supplies the definite article.
- *fuor della sepulcral buca* ‘out of the sepulchral hole’ → “from the mouth of the tomb”: “from” renders ‘out of’; the hole becomes a “mouth”, and the adjective becomes the noun phrase “of the tomb”.

## Lines 10–42 — A Shade’s Greeting and Virgil’s Question

> 10 ci apparve un' ombra, e retro a noi venia  
> 11 da piè guardando la turba che giace;  

a shade appeared to us, and came on behind us, gazing at its feet on the prostrate crowd,

- *ci apparve un' ombra* ‘to us appeared a shade’ → “a shade appeared to us”: the subject moves before the verb and the indirect object after it.
- *retro a noi venia* ‘behind us it was coming’ → “came on behind us”: the verb moves before the phrase of place; the simple past with “on” renders the continuing motion of the imperfect.
- *da piè guardando* ‘at foot gazing’ → “gazing at its feet”: the participle comes first, and “its” assigns the feet to the shade, where the Italian has no possessive.
- *la turba che giace* ‘the crowd that lies’ → “on the prostrate crowd”: “on” introduces the object of the gaze, and an adjective before the noun replaces the relative clause.

> 12 nè ci addemmo di lei, sì parlò pria,  
> 13 dicendo: “Frati miei, Dio vi dea pace.”  

nor did we perceive it until it first spake, saying: “My brothers, God give you peace.”

- *nè ci addemmo di lei, sì parlò pria* ‘nor did we become aware of her, so spoke she first’ → “nor did we perceive it until it first spake”: the pronominal verb with *di* becomes a transitive verb with a neuter object; “until” makes the time relation explicit, and “first” moves before the verb.
- *Frati miei* ‘brothers mine’ → “My brothers”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 14 Noi ci volgemmo subito, e Virgilio  
> 15 rende' gli il cenno ch' a ciò si conface.  

Quickly we turned us, and Virgil gave back to him the sign that is fitting thereto.

- *Noi ci volgemmo subito* ‘we ourselves turned immediately’ → “Quickly we turned us”: the adverb moves to the front and expresses speed rather than immediacy; the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *rende' gli* ‘returned to him’ → “gave back to him”: a verb and particle render the single verb.
- *ch' a ciò si conface* ‘that to that befits itself’ → “that is fitting thereto”: the pronominal verb becomes a copula and adjective, and the complement moves to the end as a single adverb.

> 16 Poi cominciò: “Nel beato concilio  
> 17 ti ponga in pace la verace corte,  
> 18 che me rilega nell' eterno esilio.”  

Then began: “May the true court, which binds me in eternal exile, bring thee in peace to the council of the blest.”

- *Nel beato concilio* ‘in the blessed council’ → “to the council of the blest”: place becomes destination, and the adjective qualifying the council becomes a phrase naming its members; the whole phrase moves from first to last in the wish.
- *ti ponga in pace* ‘thee may it place in peace’ → “May” … “bring thee in peace”: the subjunctive is expressed by an initial auxiliary, ‘place’ becomes a verb of motion, and the object follows that verb.
- *la verace corte, che me rilega nell' eterno esilio* ‘the true court, which me binds in the eternal exile’ → “the true court, which binds me in eternal exile”: the subject and its relative clause move ahead of the main verb; within the clause the object follows the verb, and exile loses its article.

> 19 “Come,” diss' egli, e parte andavam forte,  
> 20 “se voi siete ombre che Dio su non degni,  
> 21 chi v' ha per la sua scala tanto scorte?”  

“How,” said he, and meantime we went sturdily, “if ye are shades that God deigns not above, who hath escorted you so far by his stairs?”

- *parte andavam forte* ‘meanwhile we were going strongly’ → “meantime we went sturdily”: the simple past replaces the imperfect, and “sturdily” gives the manner of going.
- *su non degni* ‘above deign not’ → “deigns not above”: the adverb moves to the end, the negative follows the verb, and the indicative replaces the subjunctive.
- *v' ha per la sua scala tanto scorte* ‘you has by his stairway so far escorted’ → “hath escorted you so far by his stairs”: the auxiliary and participle come together before the object, the phrase of route moves after the phrase of extent, and the singular stairway becomes plural.

> 22 E il dottor mio: “Se tu riguardi i segni  
> 23 che questi porta e che l' angel profila,  
> 24 ben vedrai che coi buon convien ch' ei regni.  

And my Teacher: “If thou lookest at the marks which this man bears, and which the angel outlines, clearly wilt thou see ’tis meet he reign with the good.

- *il dottor mio* ‘the teacher mine’ → “my Teacher”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *riguardi i segni* ‘regardest the marks’ → “lookest at the marks”: the direct object becomes the complement of “at”.
- *questi* ‘this one’ → “this man”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a determiner with an explicit noun.
- *ben vedrai* ‘well wilt thou see’ → “clearly wilt thou see”: the adverb is rendered in the sense of distinct perception.
- *che coi buon convien ch' ei regni* ‘that with the good it is meet that he reign’ → “’tis meet he reign with the good”: both conjunctions are left unexpressed, and the phrase of accompaniment moves after the verb it qualifies.

> 25 Ma perchè lei che dì e notte fila  
> 26 non gli avea tratta ancora la conocchia,  
> 27 che Cloto impone a ciascuno e compila,  

But since she who spins day and night, had not yet drawn for him the fibre which Clotho charges and packs on the distaff for each one,

- *dì e notte fila* ‘day and night spins’ → “spins day and night”: the time phrase moves after the verb.
- *non gli avea tratta ancora* ‘not for him had she drawn yet’ → “had not yet drawn for him”: the negative and “yet” come between auxiliary and participle, and the beneficiary follows the participle.
- *la conocchia* ‘the distaff’ → “the fibre”: the implement is taken to mean the material on it; the implement itself reappears later in “on the distaff”.
- *impone a ciascuno e compila* ‘lays on for each one and packs’ → “charges and packs on the distaff for each one”: the two verbs are brought together, the place of loading is made explicit, and the beneficiary moves to the end.

> 28 l' anima sua, ch' è tua e mia sirocchia,  
> 29 venendo su, non potea venir sola,  
> 30 però ch' al nostro modo non adocchia:  

his spirit, which is thy sister and mine, coming up, could not come alone, because it sees not after our fashion:

- *l' anima sua* ‘the soul his’ → “his spirit”: the possessive moves before the noun, and ‘soul’ is rendered by “spirit”.
- *tua e mia sirocchia* ‘thy and my sister’ → “thy sister and mine”: the noun follows the first possessive, and the second possessive stands independently.
- *al nostro modo non adocchia* ‘in our manner it does not look’ → “it sees not after our fashion”: the verb of looking is rendered by one of seeing, the negative follows it, and the phrase of manner moves to the end.

> 31 ond' io fui tratto fuor dell' ampia gola  
> 32 d' inferno, per mostrargli, e mostrerolli  
> 33 oltre, quanto il potrà menar mia scuola.  

wherefore I was brought forth from Hell’s wide jaws to guide him, and I will guide him onward, so far as my school can lead him.

- *tratto fuor* ‘drawn out’ → “brought forth”: both the participle and its adverb are recast.
- *dell' ampia gola d' inferno* ‘from the wide throat of Hell’ → “from Hell’s wide jaws”: the possessor moves before the noun, and a plural replaces the singular ‘throat’.
- *per mostrargli, e mostrerolli oltre* ‘to show him, and I shall show him further’ → “to guide him, and I will guide him onward”: showing is interpreted as guiding, with the Italian indirect object becoming the person guided; “onward” gives *oltre* a directional sense.
- *quanto il potrà menar mia scuola* ‘as far as him will be able to lead my school’ → “so far as my school can lead him”: the subject moves before the verb and the object after it; present “can” replaces the future of ability.

> 34 Ma dinne, se tu sai, perchè tai crolli  
> 35 diè dianzi il monte, e perchè tutti ad una  
> 36 parver gridare infino ai suoi piè molli?”  

But tell us, if thou knowest, why the mount gave before such shakings, and wherefore all seemed to shout with one voice down to its soft base.”

- *dinne* ‘tell to us’ → “tell us”: the attached indirect object becomes a separate pronoun after the verb.
- *perchè tai crolli diè dianzi il monte* ‘why such shakings gave just now the mountain’ → “why the mount gave before such shakings”: the subject moves before the verb and the object after it; “before” expresses anteriority without the explicit sense of recency.
- *perchè* ‘why’ → “wherefore”: the repeated interrogative is varied in English.
- *ad una parver gridare* ‘as one seemed to shout’ → “seemed to shout with one voice”: the phrase of concerted action moves after the infinitive and is made specifically vocal.
- *infino ai suoi piè molli* ‘as far as its soft feet’ → “down to its soft base”: “down” supplies the direction, the singular “base” replaces the plural ‘feet’, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 37 Sì mi diè domandando per la cruna  
> 38 del mio disio, che pur con la speranza  
> 39 si fece la mia sete men digiuna.  

Thus, by asking, did he thread the very needle’s eye of my desire, and with the hope alone my thirst was made less fasting.

- *Sì mi diè domandando per la cruna del mio disio* ‘so for me he struck, asking, through the needle’s eye of my desire’ → “Thus, by asking, did he thread the very needle’s eye of my desire”: the gerund moves before the finite verb and is marked as instrumental by “by”; *mi* has no separate equivalent, and striking through the eye becomes threading it, with “very” added for emphasis.
- *che pur con la speranza* ‘that merely with the hope’ → “and with the hope alone”: coordination replaces the result clause answering *Sì*, and the restrictive adverb moves after the noun.
- *si fece la mia sete men digiuna* ‘became my thirst less fasting’ → “my thirst was made less fasting”: the subject moves before the verb, and the pronominal expression of change becomes a passive.

> 40 Quei cominciò: “Cosa non è che sanza  
> 41 ordine senta la religione  
> 42 della montagna, o che sia fuor d' usanza.  

That spirit began: “The holy rule of the mount suffereth naught that is arbitrary, or that is outside custom.

- *Quei* ‘that one’ → “That spirit”: a noun is supplied for the demonstrative pronoun.
- *Cosa non è che sanza ordine senta* ‘a thing there is not that without order [it] experiences’ → “suffereth naught that is arbitrary”: the negative existential becomes the negative object “naught”; the relative verb becomes the main verb, and the phrase ‘without order’ becomes a relative clause with a predicate adjective.
- *la religione della montagna* ‘the religious observance of the mountain’ → “The holy rule of the mount”: the postposed subject moves to the front, and the single noun *religione* is interpreted by an adjective and noun.
- *o che sia fuor d' usanza* ‘or that be out of custom’ → “or that is outside custom”: the indicative replaces the subjunctive, and “outside” renders the compound preposition.

## Lines 43–75 — The Quake of a Soul Set Free

> 43 Libero è qui da ogni alterazione;  

Here it is free from all terrestrial change;

- *Libero è qui* ‘free is it here’ → “Here it is free”: the adverb moves to the front and the predicate adjective follows the verb.
- *da ogni alterazione* ‘from every alteration’ → “from all terrestrial change”: “all” with a mass noun replaces ‘every’ with a count noun; “terrestrial” is supplied.

> 44 di quel che il ciel da sè in sè riceve  
> 45 esserci puote, e non d' altro, cagione:  

that which Heaven receives into itself from itself may here operate as cause, and naught else:

- *di quel che il ciel da sè in sè riceve* ‘of that which the heaven from itself into itself receives’ → “that which Heaven receives into itself from itself”: the *di* construction is recast with “that which” as the subject of “may here operate”; the article disappears, the verb moves forward, and the two prepositional phrases exchange places.
- *esserci puote, e non d' altro, cagione* ‘there can be here, and not of aught else, a cause’ → “may here operate as cause, and naught else”: ‘being’ a cause becomes ‘operating as’ one; the modal comes first, and the exclusion is moved to the end and made parallel to the English subject rather than to the Italian *di* phrase.

> 46 perchè non pioggia, non grando, non neve,  
> 47 non rugiada, non brina più su cade  
> 48 che la scaletta dei tre gradi breve.  

since neither rain, nor hail, nor snow, nor dew, nor hoarfrost, falls any higher than the short little stairway of the three steps.

- *non pioggia, non grando, non neve, non rugiada, non brina* ‘not rain, not hail, not snow, not dew, not hoarfrost’ → “neither rain, nor hail, nor snow, nor dew, nor hoarfrost”: the repeated negatives become a correlative series.
- *più su cade* ‘higher up falls’ → “falls any higher”: the verb precedes the comparative, “any” is supplied, and ‘up’ is absorbed into “higher”.
- *la scaletta dei tre gradi breve* ‘the little stairway of the three steps, short’ → “the short little stairway of the three steps”: “short” moves ahead of the noun, whose diminutive ending is rendered by “little”.

> 49 Nuvole spesse non paion, nè rade,  
> 50 nè corruscar, nè figlia di Taumante,  
> 51 che di là cangia sovente contrade.  

Clouds, dense or thin, appear not, nor lightning flash, nor Thaumas’ daughter, who yonder oft changes her region.

- *Nuvole spesse non paion, nè rade* ‘dense clouds do not appear, nor thin ones’ → “Clouds, dense or thin, appear not”: the two adjectives are brought together after the noun and joined by “or” within the scope of a single negation.
- *corruscar* ‘flashing’ → “lightning flash”: the infinitive used as a noun becomes a noun phrase specifying what flashes.
- *figlia di Taumante* ‘daughter of Thaumas’ → “Thaumas’ daughter”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *di là cangia sovente contrade* ‘yonder changes often regions’ → “yonder oft changes her region”: the frequency adverb precedes the verb; the plural becomes singular with an added possessive.

> 52 Secco vapor non surge più avante  
> 53 ch' al sommo dei tre gradi ch' io parlai,  
> 54 ov' ha il vicario di Pietro le piante.  

Dry vapour rises not higher than the top of the three steps which I spake of, where Peter’s vicar hath his feet.

- *più avante ch' al sommo* ‘farther on than to the top’ → “higher than the top”: the comparison is made explicitly vertical, and the preposition of destination drops out.
- *ch' io parlai* ‘that I spoke’ → “which I spake of”: English supplies the preposition linking the steps to the verb of speaking.
- *ha il vicario di Pietro le piante* ‘has the vicar of Peter the soles’ → “Peter’s vicar hath his feet”: the subject precedes the verb, the possessive precedes “vicar”, and ‘the soles’ becomes “his feet”, with a possessive in place of the article.

> 55 Trema forse più giù poco od assai;  
> 56 ma, per vento che in terra si nasconda,  
> 57 non so come, quassù non tremò mai.  

It quakes perchance lower down little or much, but by reason of wind which is hidden in the earth, I know not how, it has never quaked up here.

- *per vento che in terra si nasconda* ‘through wind that in earth may hide itself’ → “by reason of wind which is hidden in the earth”: the causal preposition is expanded; the pronominal subjunctive becomes an indicative passive, followed by the place phrase with an article.
- *quassù non tremò mai* ‘up here it never quaked’ → “it has never quaked up here”: the place adverb moves to the end, and the simple past becomes a present perfect.

> 58 Tremaci quando alcuna anima monda  
> 59 sentesi, sì che surga, o che si mova  
> 60 per salir su, e tal grido seconda.  

It quakes here when some soul feeleth herself cleansed, so that she may rise up, or set forth, to mount on high, and such a shout follows her.

- *monda sentesi* ‘cleansed feels-herself’ → “feeleth herself cleansed”: the predicative adjective moves after the verb and its reflexive pronoun.
- *sì che surga, o che si mova* ‘so that she rise, or that she move herself’ → “so that she may rise up, or set forth”: “may” carries the subjunctive force for both verbs; ‘rise’ gains “up”, the second “that” is not repeated, and the reflexive verb becomes “set forth”.
- *tal grido seconda* ‘such a shout follows’ → “such a shout follows her”: English supplies both an article and an explicit object.

> 61 Della mondizia sol voler fa prova,  
> 62 che, tutta libera a mutar convento,  
> 63 l' alma sorprende, e di voler le giova.  

Of the cleansing the will alone gives proof, which fills the soul, all free to change her cloister, and avails her to will.

- *sol voler* ‘will alone’ → “the will alone”: the substantival infinitive takes an article in English, and the restriction follows the noun.
- *fa prova* ‘makes proof’ → “gives proof”: the English idiom changes the verb.
- *tutta libera a mutar convento* ‘wholly free to change cloister’ → “all free to change her cloister”: the phrase is moved after “the soul”, which it qualifies; “her” is supplied, and the word for a community is rendered by the name of its dwelling.
- *l' alma sorprende* ‘the soul seizes upon’ → “fills the soul”: the object, ‘the soul’, moves after the verb; being seized by the will is rendered as being filled by it.
- *di voler le giova* ‘in willing, to her it avails’ → “avails her to will”: the verb comes first, the indirect object becomes “her”, and the prepositional infinitive becomes a *to*-infinitive at the end.

> 64 Prima vuol ben; ma non lascia il talento  
> 65 che divina giustizia contra voglia,  
> 66 come fu al peccar, pone al tormento.  

She wills indeed before, but that desire permits it not which divine justice sets, counter to will, toward the penalty, even as it was toward the sin.

- *Prima vuol ben* ‘before, she wills indeed’ → “She wills indeed before”: the temporal adverb moves to the end of the clause.
- *non lascia il talento* ‘does not permit, the desire’ → “that desire permits it not”: the postposed subject moves before the verb, the article becomes demonstrative, and the object “it” is supplied.
- *contra voglia, come fu al peccar, pone al tormento* ‘against will, as it was toward sinning, sets toward the torment’ → “sets, counter to will, toward the penalty, even as it was toward the sin”: the verb moves ahead of both qualifications, and the comparison moves to the end. “Penalty” replaces ‘torment’, “even” reinforces the comparison, and the substantival infinitive ‘sinning’ becomes “the sin”.

> 67 Ed io che son giaciuto a questa doglia  
> 68 cinquecento anni e più, pur mo sentii  
> 69 libera volontà di miglior soglia.  

And I who have lain under this torment five hundred years and more, only now felt free will for a better threshold.

- *a questa doglia* ‘at this pain’ → “under this torment”: both the preposition and the noun change.
- *di miglior soglia* ‘of better threshold’ → “for a better threshold”: “for” expresses the direction of the will, and an indefinite article is supplied.

> 70 Però sentisti il tremoto, e li pii  
> 71 spiriti per lo monte render lode  
> 72 a quel Signor, che tosto su gl' invii.”  

Therefore didst thou feel the earthquake, and hear the pious spirits about the mount give praises to that Lord—soon may he send them above.”

- *sentisti* ‘thou feltest’ → “didst thou feel”: the simple past becomes an inverted construction with an auxiliary.
- *e li pii spiriti per lo monte render lode* ‘and the pious spirits about the mount give praise’ → “and hear the pious spirits about the mount give praises”: Italian lets the preceding verb of perception govern both objects; English supplies a second verb for hearing. Singular ‘praise’ becomes plural.
- *che tosto su gl' invii* ‘who soon up may send them’ → “soon may he send them above”: the relative clause becomes an independent wish with “he”; the object follows the verb, and the directional adverb comes last.

> 73 Così ne disse; e però ch' ei si gode  
> 74 tanto del ber quant' è grande la sete,  
> 75 non saprei dir quant' ei mi fece prode  

Thus he spake to us; and since we enjoy most the draught in proportion as our thirst is great, I could not tell how much he profited me.

- *ne disse* ‘to us he said’ → “he spake to us”: the pronominal complement follows the verb as a prepositional phrase, and ‘said’ becomes “spake”.
- *ei si gode tanto del ber quant' è grande la sete* ‘one takes as much pleasure in drinking as great is the thirst’ → “we enjoy most the draught in proportion as our thirst is great”: the impersonal construction becomes first-person plural. The substantive infinitive becomes the direct object “the draught”; the paired degree words become “most” and “in proportion as”, and ‘the thirst’ becomes “our thirst” and precedes its predicate.
- *non saprei dir* ‘I should not know how to say’ → “I could not tell”: conditional ‘know how’ plus an infinitive becomes a modal of ability with “tell”.
- *ei mi fece prode* ‘he did me good’ → “he profited me”: verb and noun become one transitive verb, with the pronoun following as direct object.

## Lines 76–108 — Statius Names Himself and Praises Virgil

> 76 E il savio duca: “Omai veggio la rete  
> 77 che qui vi piglia, e come si scalappia,  
> 78 per che ci trema, e di che congaudete.  

And the wise Leader: “Now I see the net that catches you here, and how one breaks through, wherefore it quakes here, and whereat ye make glad together.

- *Omai* ‘by now’ → “Now”: the sense of having come to this point is less explicit.
- *che qui vi piglia* ‘that here you catches’ → “that catches you here”: the object follows the verb, and the adverb comes last.
- *come si scalappia* ‘how one frees oneself from the snare’ → “how one breaks through”: the reflexive becomes an active verb with an adverb; escape from a snare is expressed as breaking through.
- *ci trema* ‘here it quakes’ → “it quakes here”: the adverb follows the verb.
- *di che congaudete* ‘at what you rejoice together’ → “whereat ye make glad together”: the preposition and pronoun are combined, and the single verb is expanded into a phrase.

> 79 Ora chi fosti piacciati ch' io sappia,  
> 80 e, perchè tanti secoli giaciuto  
> 81 qui sei, nelle parole tue mi cappia.”  

Now may it please thee that I know who thou wast; and why thou hast lain here so many ages, let me learn from thy words.”

- *chi fosti piacciati ch' io sappia* ‘who thou wast, may it please thee that I know’ → “may it please thee that I know who thou wast”: the indirect question moves from the beginning to the end of the request.
- *tanti secoli giaciuto qui sei* ‘so many centuries lain here hast thou’ → “thou hast lain here so many ages”: the auxiliary comes before the participle rather than after the adverb; the expression of duration moves to the end, and “ages” is less definite than ‘centuries’.
- *nelle parole tue mi cappia* ‘in thy words let it find room for me’ → “let me learn from thy words”: what is to be contained in the words becomes what the speaker is to learn; the indirect object becomes the learner, and the opening phrase of place becomes a final phrase of source.

> 82 “Nel tempo che il buon Tito con l' aiuto  
> 83 del sommo Rege vendicò le fora,  
> 84 ond' uscì il sangue per Giuda venduto,  

“What time the good Titus with help of the Highest King avenged the wounds whence issued the blood by Judas sold,

- *Nel tempo che* ‘in the time that’ → “What time”: the prepositional phrase and relative are replaced by an expression meaning ‘when’.
- *con l' aiuto* ‘with the help’ → “with help”: the article is omitted.
- *le fora* ‘the holes’ → “the wounds”: the openings are named as injuries.

> 85 col nome che più dura e più onora  
> 86 era io di là,” rispose quello spirto,  
> 87 “famoso assai, ma non con fede ancora.  

with the name which most endures, and honours most,” answered that spirit, “I was yonder, great in fame, but not yet with faith.

- *più onora* ‘most honours’ → “honours most”: the adverb follows the verb.
- *era io di là, rispose quello spirto* ‘was I yonder, answered that spirit’ → “answered that spirit, “I was yonder”: the reporting clause moves ahead of the statement it interrupts, and the subject of that statement precedes its verb.
- *famoso assai* ‘famous exceedingly’ → “great in fame”: the degree is expressed by an adjective, and the Italian adjective by a preposition and noun.
- *non con fede ancora* ‘not with faith yet’ → “not yet with faith”: the adverb moves next to the negative.

> 88 Tanto fu dolce mio vocale spirto,  
> 89 che, Tolosano, a sè mi trasse Roma,  
> 90 dove mertai le tempie ornar di mirto.  

So sweet was the music of my words, that me, a Toulousian, Rome drew to herself, where I did merit a crown of myrtle for my brow.

- *mio vocale spirto* ‘my vocal breath’ → “the music of my words”: the breath or utterance becomes its music; the possessive qualifies the newly explicit “words”.
- *Tolosano* ‘Toulousian’ → “a Toulousian”: the adjective becomes a noun in apposition, with an article.
- *a sè mi trasse Roma* ‘to herself me drew Rome’ → “me, a Toulousian, Rome drew to herself”: the object moves ahead of the apposition, the subject precedes the verb, and the phrase of direction comes last.
- *mertai le tempie ornar di mirto* ‘I merited to adorn the temples with myrtle’ → “I did merit a crown of myrtle for my brow”: the simple past is expressed with “did”; the infinitive and its object give way to a noun phrase supplying “a crown”, and the plural temples become a singular, explicitly possessed brow.

> 91 Stazio la gente ancor di là mi noma;  

Statius folk yonder still do name me;

- *la gente ancor di là mi noma* ‘the people still yonder names me’ → “folk yonder still do name me”: the singular collective with its article becomes articleless plural “folk”; the adverbs exchange places, the present is expanded with “do”, and the object follows the verb.

> 92 cantai di Tebe, e poi del grande Achille,  
> 93 ma caddi in via con la seconda soma.  

I sang of Thebes, and then of the great Achilles; but I fell by the way with the second burden.

- *in via* ‘on the way’ → “by the way”: the prepositional idiom changes.

> 94 Al mio ardor fur seme le faville,  
> 95 che mi scaldar, della divina fiamma,  
> 96 onde sono allumati più di mille:  

The sparks, which warmed me, from the divine flame whence more than a thousand have been kindled, were the seeds of my poetic fire:

- *Al mio ardor fur seme* ‘to my ardour were seed’ → “were the seeds of my poetic fire”: the predicate moves from before the subject to after its modifiers; singular ‘seed’ becomes a definite plural, and the opening phrase becomes an *of*-phrase after “seeds”. “Fire” renders ‘ardour’ with “poetic” supplied.
- *che mi scaldar* ‘which me warmed’ → “which warmed me”: the object follows the verb.
- *della divina fiamma* ‘of the divine flame’ → “from the divine flame”: the relation is made explicitly one of source.
- *onde sono allumati più di mille* ‘whence are lit more than a thousand’ → “whence more than a thousand have been kindled”: the subject moves before the passive; the present state of being lit is expressed as the result of kindling in the present perfect.

> 97 dell' Eneida dico, la qual mamma  
> 98 fummi, e fummi nutrice poetando;  
> 99 senz' essa non fermai peso di dramma.  

of the Æneid I speak, which was a mother to me, and was to me a nurse in poesy; without it I had not stayed the weight of a drachm.

- *la qual mamma fummi* ‘which a mother was to me’ → “which was a mother to me”: the predicate noun moves after the verb.
- *poetando* ‘in making poetry’ → “in poesy”: a gerund denoting an activity becomes a preposition and noun denoting its field.
- *non fermai peso di dramma* ‘I did not fix a drachm’s weight’ → “I had not stayed the weight of a drachm”: the simple past becomes a pluperfect with counterfactual force; ‘fix’ or ‘establish’ is rendered by the transitive “stayed”, and the weight is made definite.

> 100 E, per esser vivuto di là quando  
> 101 visse Virgilio, assentirei un sole  
> 102 più che non deggio al mio uscir di bando.”  

And to have lived yonder, when Virgil was alive, I would consent to one sun more than I owe to my coming forth from exile.”

- *per esser vivuto* ‘in order to have lived’ → “to have lived”: the preposition marking purpose is absorbed into the English infinitive.
- *visse Virgilio* ‘lived Virgil’ → “Virgil was alive”: the subject precedes the predicate, and the simple verb becomes a copula and adjective.
- *assentirei un sole* ‘I would assent one sun’ → “I would consent to one sun”: English supplies a preposition before the measure of time.
- *al mio uscir di bando* ‘to my going out of banishment’ → “to my coming forth from exile”: departure is phrased as coming forth rather than going out.

> 103 Volser Virgilio a me queste parole  
> 104 con viso che, tacendo, dicea: “Taci.”  

These words turned Virgil to me with a look that silently said: “Be silent.”

- *Volser Virgilio a me queste parole* ‘turned Virgil to me these words’ → “These words turned Virgil to me”: the subject moves from last place to first.
- *con viso* ‘with face’ → “with a look”: the face becomes its expression, with an indefinite article.
- *tacendo* ‘keeping silent’ → “silently”: the gerund becomes an adverb of manner.
- *Taci* ‘keep silence’ → “Be silent”: a single imperative becomes a copula and adjective.

> 105 Ma non può tutto la virtù che vuole:  

But the virtue which wills is not all powerful;

- *non può tutto la virtù che vuole* ‘cannot do everything the virtue which wills’ → “the virtue which wills is not all powerful”: the subject and its relative clause move before the predicate; the negated verb of ability with its object becomes a negated copula with an adjective of power.

> 106 chè riso e pianto son tanto seguaci  
> 107 alla passion da che ciascun si spicca,  
> 108 che men seguon voler nei più veraci.  

for laughter and tears follow so closely the passion from which each springs, that they least obey the will in the most truthful.

- *pianto* ‘weeping’ → “tears”: the act is rendered by a plural noun for its visible sign.
- *son tanto seguaci alla passion* ‘are so much followers of the passion’ → “follow so closely the passion”: the copular construction becomes a transitive verb, with the degree of adherence expressed adverbially.
- *ciascun si spicca* ‘each detaches itself’ → “each springs”: the reflexive verb of separation becomes an intransitive verb of origin.
- *men seguon voler* ‘less follow willing’ → “least obey the will”: the comparative becomes a superlative, ‘follow’ becomes “obey”, and the substantivized infinitive becomes a definite noun.
- *nei più veraci* ‘in the most truthful ones’ → “in the most truthful”: the adjective still designates people, without an expressed noun in English.

## Lines 109–136 — Statius Learns Virgil’s Name

> 109 Io pur sorrisi, come l' uom ch' ammicca:  

I did but smile, like one who makes a sign;

- *Io pur sorrisi* ‘I merely smiled’ → “I did but smile”: the simple past becomes “did” with the bare infinitive, and “but” carries the limiting force of *pur*.
- *come l' uom ch' ammicca* ‘like the man who winks’ → “like one who makes a sign”: the generic ‘the man’ becomes the indefinite “one”; the specific gesture of the eyes becomes the more general “makes a sign”.

> 110 per che l' ombra si tacque, e riguardommi  
> 111 negli occhi, ove il sembiante più si ficca.  

whereat the shade was silent and looked at me in the eyes, where most the soul is fixed.

- *per che* ‘on account of which’ → “whereat”: the causal link becomes an indication of what prompted the response.
- *si tacque* ‘fell silent’ → “was silent”: the English gives the state rather than the change into it.
- *il sembiante più si ficca* ‘the countenance most fixes itself’ → “most the soul is fixed”: Okey gives what the countenance expresses rather than the outward appearance itself, moves “most” before the subject, and changes the reflexive verb to a passive.

> 112 E “Se tanto lavoro in bene assommi,”  
> 113 disse, “perchè la faccia tua testeso  
> 114 un lampeggiar di riso dimostrommi?”  

And he said: “So may such great toil achieve its end; wherefore did thy face but now display to me a flash of laughter?”

- *disse* ‘said’ → “he said”: the reporting clause moves from within the speech to its beginning.
- *Se tanto lavoro in bene assommi* ‘so mayst thou bring so much toil to a good end’ → “So may such great toil achieve its end”: the optative force of *se* is made explicit by “may”; the toil becomes the subject rather than the object, quantity becomes degree, and a specifically good outcome becomes the toil’s own end.
- *la faccia tua* ‘the face of thine’ → “thy face”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *un lampeggiar di riso* ‘a flashing of laughter’ → “a flash of laughter”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun for a single flash, and the object moves after the verb and its indirect object.
- *dimostrommi* ‘showed me’ → “did thy face but now display to me”: the simple past is divided between the interrogative auxiliary and “display”; the attached indirect object becomes a phrase with “to”.

> 115 Or son io d' una parte e d' altra preso:  
> 116 l' una mi fa tacer, l' altra scongiura  
> 117 ch' io dica; ond' io sospiro, e sono inteso  

Now am I caught on either side; one makes me keep silence, the other conjures me to speak; wherefore I sigh and am understood

- *d' una parte e d' altra preso* ‘on one side and on the other caught’ → “caught on either side”: the participle comes first, and “either” condenses the two coordinated phrases.
- *l' una* ‘the one’ → “one”: the definite article drops out.
- *mi fa tacer* ‘me makes be silent’ → “makes me keep silence”: the object follows the causative verb, and the infinitive becomes a verb and noun.
- *scongiura ch' io dica* ‘adjures that I speak’ → “conjures me to speak”: the subject of the subjunctive clause becomes the object of “conjures”, followed by an infinitive.

> 118 dal mio maestro; e “Non aver paura,”  
> 119 mi disse, “di parlar; ma parla, e digli  
> 120 quel ch' ei domanda con cotanta cura.”  

by my Master, and he said to me, “Have no fear of speaking, but speak, and tell him that which he asketh with so great desire.”

- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “he said to me”: the reporting clause moves ahead of the direct speech, and the indirect object follows the verb as a prepositional phrase.
- *Non aver paura* ‘not to have fear’ → “Have no fear”: the Italian infinitive used for a negative command becomes an English imperative, with the negative transferred to the noun.
- *di parlar* ‘to speak’ → “of speaking”: the infinitive complement of ‘fear’ becomes a preposition with a gerund.
- *digli* ‘tell to him’ → “tell him”: the attached dative becomes a separate object without a preposition.
- *con cotanta cura* ‘with so much concern’ → “with so great desire”: degree replaces quantity, and the concern is interpreted as eagerness to know.

> 121 Ond' io: “Forse che tu ti maravigli,  
> 122 antico spirto, del rider ch' io fei;  
> 123 ma più d' ammirazion vo' che ti pigli.  

Wherefore I: “Perchance thou dost marvel, O ancient spirit, at the laugh I gave, but I desire that yet greater wonder seize thee.

- *Forse che tu ti maravigli* ‘perhaps it is that thou marvellest’ → “Perchance thou dost marvel”: the connective *che* falls away, and the reflexive present becomes an intransitive verb with “dost”.
- *antico spirto* ‘ancient spirit’ → “O ancient spirit”: “O” explicitly marks the vocative.
- *del rider ch' io fei* ‘at the laughing that I did’ → “at the laugh I gave”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a count noun, the relative pronoun is left unexpressed, and one ‘gives’ rather than ‘does’ a laugh.
- *più d' ammirazion vo' che ti pigli* ‘more of wonder I wish should seize thee’ → “I desire that yet greater wonder seize thee”: the main verb moves first; the partitive expression becomes an adjective modifying “wonder”, with “yet” supplied, and the object follows “seize”.

> 124 Questi, che guida in alto gli occhi miei,  
> 125 è quel Virgilio, dal qual tu togliesti  
> 126 forza a cantar degli uomini e de' Dei.  

He who guideth mine eyes on high, is that Virgil from whom thou drewest power to sing of men and gods.

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “He”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *guida in alto gli occhi miei* ‘guides on high the eyes of mine’ → “guideth mine eyes on high”: the directional phrase follows the object, and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *togliesti forza* ‘took strength’ → “drewest power”: “drewest” recasts the act of taking from a source, and “power” renders *forza* as an ability.
- *degli uomini e de' Dei* ‘of the men and of the gods’ → “of men and gods”: the generic articles disappear, and one preposition serves both nouns.

> 127 Se cagione altra al mio rider credesti,  
> 128 lasciala per non vera esser e credi  
> 129 quelle parole che di lui dicesti.”  

If thou didst believe other cause for my laughter, set it aside as untrue, and believe it was those words which thou spakest of him.”

- *cagione altra al mio rider credesti* ‘a cause other for my laughing thou believedst’ → “thou didst believe other cause for my laughter”: the verb, expressed with “didst”, precedes its object; “other” precedes the noun, and the substantivized infinitive becomes “laughter”.
- *lasciala per non vera esser* ‘leave it for not true being’ → “set it aside as untrue”: a phrasal verb replaces ‘leave’, with the pronoun between verb and particle; “as untrue” condenses the explanation ‘because it is not true’.
- *credi quelle parole* ‘believe those words’ → “believe it was those words”: Okey supplies “it was”, taking the demonstrative phrase to identify the cause of the laughter rather than simply the words to be believed.
- *di lui dicesti* ‘of him thou spakest’ → “thou spakest of him”: the prepositional phrase follows the verb.

> 130 Già si chinava ad abbracciar li piedi  
> 131 al mio dottor; ma egli disse: “Frate,  
> 132 non far, chè tu se' ombra, ed ombra vedi.”  

Already was he stooping to embrace my Teacher’s feet; but he said: “Brother, do not so, for thou art a shade and a shade thou seest.”

- *si chinava* ‘was bending himself down’ → “was he stooping”: an intransitive verb replaces the reflexive, and the auxiliary precedes the subject after “Already”.
- *li piedi al mio dottor* ‘the feet to my teacher’ → “my Teacher’s feet”: the Italian dative of the possessor becomes a possessive before the noun.
- *non far* ‘do not do’ → “do not so”: “so” stands for the action left implicit after the Italian negative command.
- *tu se' ombra, ed ombra vedi* ‘thou art shade, and shade thou seest’ → “thou art a shade and a shade thou seest”: English supplies an indefinite article at both occurrences of the singular count noun.

> 133 Ed ei surgendo: “Or puoi la quantitate  
> 134 comprender dell' amor ch' a te mi scalda,  
> 135 quando dismento nostra vanitate,  
> 136 trattando l' ombre come cosa salda.”  

And he, rising: “Now canst thou comprehend the measure of the love which warms me toward thee, when I forget our nothingness, and treat shades as a solid thing.”

- *puoi la quantitate comprender* ‘thou canst the quantity comprehend’ → “canst thou comprehend the measure”: the modal precedes the subject after “Now”, the object follows the infinitive, and “measure” replaces ‘quantity’.
- *ch' a te mi scalda* ‘that toward thee me warms’ → “which warms me toward thee”: both the object and the directional phrase move after the verb, with the object first.
- *nostra vanitate* ‘our emptiness’ → “our nothingness”: Okey takes *vanitate* in the sense of lack of substance.
- *trattando* ‘treating’ → “and treat”: a gerund expressing the manner of forgetting becomes a second finite verb coordinated with “forget”.
- *l' ombre* ‘the shades’ → “shades”: the definite article disappears in the English generic plural.
- *come cosa salda* ‘as thing solid’ → “as a solid thing”: English supplies an indefinite article and places the adjective before the noun.

## Summary — Italian Order in English Prose

Okey often moves a subject from the end of a clause to the beginning: *mi pungeami* is not the pattern of the English; in *pungeami la fretta*, ‘haste’ comes last, whereas “haste was goading me” puts it first and the object after the verb. Longer phrases move too: *Nel beato concilio* opens the Italian wish, but “to the council of the blest” closes the English. Yet Okey does not always smooth away an inversion: “of the Æneid I speak” keeps the emphasis of *dell' Eneida dico*, and “a shade thou seest” preserves the object-first order of *ombra vedi*.

A change of construction often accompanies a change of emphasis. The relative clause *che giace*, ‘that lies’, becomes the adjective in “the prostrate crowd”; the gerund *tacendo*, ‘keeping silent’, becomes “silently”. Where one Italian word does several jobs, Okey may distinguish its senses: *sentisti* serves for both bodily sensation and hearing, while the English uses “feel” and then supplies “hear”. Conversely, *che* can go unexpressed where English needs no connective, as in “’tis meet he reign with the good”. Two routine differences generally go unremarked in the comparison: English supplies subject pronouns where the Italian verb ending already indicates the subject, and omits the article before a possessive, as in *il dottor mio*, “my Teacher”.

Okey sometimes chooses a word for what an expression implies rather than for what it names: *le fora*, ‘the holes’, becomes “the wounds”, and *la conocchia*, ‘the distaff’, first appears as “the fibre”, with the implement named later in “on the distaff”. Elsewhere he keeps even an unusual combination of words. In *la mia sete men digiuna*, ‘my thirst less fasting’, he retains both the thirst and the language of fasting in “my thirst was made less fasting”. The grammar changes, but the image does not.
