# Purgatorio 27

## Lines 1–12 — The Angel’s Song and the Passage Through Fire

> 1 Sì come quando i primi raggi vibra  
> 2 là dove il suo Fattore il sangue sparse,  

As when he shoots forth his first beams there where his Creator shed his blood,

- *Sì come quando* ‘just as when’ → “As when”: the opening intensifier is not separately rendered.
- *i primi raggi vibra* ‘the first beams he darts’ → “he shoots forth his first beams”: the verb comes before its object, “shoots forth” renders the single verb, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *il sangue sparse* ‘the blood shed’ → “shed his blood”: the object follows the verb, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 3 cadendo Ibero sotto l' alta Libra,  
> 4 e l' onde in Gange da nona riarse,  

while Ebro falls beneath the lofty Scales, and Ganges’ waves by noonday heat are scorched,

- *cadendo Ibero* ‘Ebro falling’ → “while Ebro falls”: the gerund with its postposed subject becomes a finite clause introduced by a conjunction of time.
- *l’ onde in Gange* ‘the waves in Ganges’ → “Ganges’ waves”: the phrase of place becomes a possessive before the noun, displacing the article.
- *da nona riarse* ‘by noon scorched’ → “by noonday heat are scorched”: the time becomes a modifier of the supplied “heat”, and the participle, which qualifies the waves without an expressed verb, becomes a finite passive.

> 5 sì stava il sole: onde il giorno sen giva,  
> 6 quando l' angel di Dio lieto ci apparse.  

so stood the sun; wherefore the day was passing away when God’s glad angel appeared to us.

- *sen giva* ‘went its way’ → “was passing away”: the imperfect of the pronominal verb of going is rendered by the English progressive of “passing away”.
- *l’ angel di Dio lieto* ‘the angel of God, glad’ → “God’s glad angel”: the possessive replaces the phrase with *di* and displaces the article; the adjective, too, moves before the noun.
- *ci apparse* ‘to us appeared’ → “appeared to us”: the indirect-object pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase after the verb.

> 7 Fuor della fiamma stava in su la riva,  
> 8 e cantava: “*Beati mundo corde,*”  
> 9 in voce assai più che la nostra viva.  

Outside the flames on the bank he was standing and singing “*Beati mundo corde*” in a voice more piercing far than ours.

- *Fuor della fiamma* ‘outside the flame’ → “Outside the flames”: the singular becomes plural.
- *stava in su la riva* ‘stood upon the bank’ → “on the bank he was standing”: the phrase of place moves before the verb, and the imperfect becomes a progressive.
- *e cantava* ‘and sang’ → “and singing”: the second finite imperfect becomes a participle sharing the preceding auxiliary.
- *in voce* ‘in voice’ → “in a voice”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *assai più che la nostra viva* ‘much more than ours living’ → “more piercing far than ours”: the adjective is interpreted as “piercing” and moved before the comparison with “ours”; the degree modifier “far” follows rather than precedes the comparative.

> 10 Poscia: “Più non si va, se pria non morde,  
> 11 anime sante, il foco; entrate in esso,  
> 12 ed al cantar di là non siate sorde,”  

Then: “No farther may ye go, O hallowed souls, if first the fire bite not; enter therein and to the singing beyond be not deaf,”

- *Più non si va* ‘farther one does not go’ → “No farther may ye go”: the negation attaches to “farther”, and the impersonal present becomes a modal verb addressed to the hearers.
- *se pria non morde, … il foco* ‘if first bites not … the fire’ → “if first the fire bite not”: the delayed subject moves before the verb, the negative follows it, and the Italian indicative becomes an English subjunctive.
- *anime sante* ‘souls holy’ → “O hallowed souls”: the vocative moves out of the conditional clause to precede it; “O” is supplied, and “hallowed” comes before its noun.
- *in esso* ‘into it’ → “therein”: an adverb replaces the preposition and pronoun.
- *al cantar di là* ‘to the singing on the other side’ → “to the singing beyond”: “beyond” condenses the phrase of place.
- *non siate sorde* ‘not be deaf’ → “be not deaf”: the negative follows rather than precedes the imperative.

## Lines 13–45 — Fear of the Flame and the Name of Beatrice

> 13 ci disse come noi gli fummo presso:  

he said to us when we were nigh to him;

- *ci disse* ‘to us he said’ → “he said to us”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *come noi gli fummo presso* ‘as we came near to him’ → “when we were nigh to him”: the temporal conjunction becomes “when”; the Italian puts the pronoun for the person approached before the verb, whereas English puts “to him” after “nigh”.

> 14 per ch' io divenni tal quando lo intesi,  
> 15 quale è colui che nella fossa è messo.  

wherefore I became when I heard him, such as one who is laid in the grave.

- *tal quando lo intesi, quale* ‘such, when him I heard, as’ → “when I heard him, such as”: the time clause comes before both halves of the comparison instead of between them, and its object follows the verb.
- *colui che* ‘he who’ → “one who”: the demonstrative becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *nella fossa è messo* ‘in the pit is put’ → “is laid in the grave”: the passive verb precedes the phrase of place; “laid” and “grave” give the words their specifically funerary sense.

> 16 In su le man commesse mi protesi,  
> 17 guardando il foco, e immaginando forte  
> 18 umani corpi già veduti accesi.  

I bent forward over my clasped hands, gazing at the fire, and vividly imagining human bodies once seen burnt.

- *In su le man commesse* ‘over the hands clasped’ → “over my clasped hands”: the phrase follows the verb instead of opening the clause; the definite article becomes a possessive, and the participle precedes the noun.
- *mi protesi* ‘I stretched myself forward’ → “I bent forward”: an intransitive verb of posture replaces the reflexive.
- *guardando il foco* ‘looking at the fire’ → “gazing at the fire”: “gazing” gives the looking a more sustained quality.
- *immaginando forte* ‘imagining strongly’ → “vividly imagining”: the adverb precedes the participle and expresses vividness rather than strength.
- *già veduti accesi* ‘previously seen alight’ → “once seen burnt”: “once” places the sight at an unspecified past time, and “burnt” renders the state of being alight by a participle of burning.

> 19 Volsersi verso me le buone scorte,  
> 20 e Virgilio mi disse: “Figliuol mio,  
> 21 qui può esser tormento, ma non morte.  

The kindly escorts turned them toward me, and Virgil said to me: “My son, here may be torment but not death.

- *Volsersi verso me le buone scorte* ‘turned themselves toward me the good guides’ → “The kindly escorts turned them toward me”: the subject moves from the end to the beginning; “kindly” specifies the sense of ‘good’, “escorts” renders ‘guides’, and “them” has the reflexive force of ‘themselves’.
- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *Figliuol mio* ‘son of mine’ → “My son”: the possessive comes first.

> 22 Ricordati, ricordati . . . e, se io  
> 23 sopr' esso Gerion ti guidai salvo,  
> 24 che farò ora presso più a Dio?  

Remember thee, remember thee, . . . and if on Geryon I guided thee safely, what shall I do now nearer to God?

- *sopr' esso Gerion* ‘upon Geryon himself’ → “on Geryon”: the reinforcing pronoun has no separate equivalent.
- *ti guidai salvo* ‘thee I guided safe’ → “I guided thee safely”: the object follows the verb, and the adjective describing the person guided becomes an adverb modifying the guiding.
- *presso più a Dio* ‘near more to God’ → “nearer to God”: the comparative ending takes the place of the separate word for ‘more’.

> 25 Credi per certo che, se dentro all' alvo  
> 26 di questa fiamma stessi ben mill' anni,  
> 27 non ti potrebbe far d' un capel calvo;  

Of a surety believe, that if within the womb of these flames thou didst abide full a thousand years, they could not make thee bald of one hair;

- *Credi per certo* ‘believe for certain’ → “Of a surety believe”: the expression of certainty moves before the imperative and takes a different idiomatic form.
- *di questa fiamma* ‘of this flame’ → “of these flames”: both demonstrative and noun become plural.
- *stessi* ‘thou shouldst remain’ → “thou didst abide”: the imperfect subjunctive of the supposition is expressed by an English past with “didst”.
- *ben mill' anni* ‘fully a thousand years’ → “full a thousand years”: “full” is used adverbially for the intensifier.
- *non ti potrebbe far d' un capel calvo* ‘it could not thee make of one hair bald’ → “they could not make thee bald of one hair”: the subject becomes plural to agree with “flames”; the object follows “make”, and the phrase of deprivation follows “bald”.

> 28 e se tu credi forse ch' io t' inganni,  
> 29 fatti ver lei, e fatti far credenza  
> 30 con le tue mani al lembo de' tuoi panni.  

and if perchance thou thinkest that I beguile thee, get thee toward them, and get credence with thy hands on the hem of thy garments.

- *tu credi forse* ‘thou believest perhaps’ → “perchance thou thinkest”: the adverb comes first, and believing becomes thinking.
- *io t' inganni* ‘I thee deceive’ → “I beguile thee”: the object follows the verb, and the Italian subordinate subjunctive has no distinct English mood marking.
- *ver lei* ‘toward her’ → “toward them”: the feminine singular pronoun referring to the flame becomes plural.
- *fatti far credenza* ‘have assurance given to thee’ → “get credence”: the reflexive causative, with its second verb, is compressed into a verb of obtaining and its object.
- *al lembo* ‘at the hem’ → “on the hem”: the preposition of contact changes.
- *de' tuoi panni* ‘of thy clothes’ → “of thy garments”: the noun is given a more formal equivalent.

> 31 Pon giù omai, pon giù ogni temenza;  
> 32 volgiti in qua, e vieni oltre sicuro.”  

Put away now, put away all fear; turn thee hither, and onward come securely.”

- *Pon giù omai, pon giù* ‘put down now, put down’ → “Put away now, put away”: the repeated particle of downward motion becomes one of dismissal.
- *ogni temenza* ‘every fear’ → “all fear”: English treats fear collectively rather than distributively.
- *in qua* ‘in this direction’ → “hither”: a single directional adverb replaces the phrase.
- *vieni oltre sicuro* ‘come onward secure’ → “onward come securely”: the directional adverb moves before the verb, and the adjective describing the person becomes an adverb.

> 33 Ed io pur fermo e contro a coscienza.  

And I, yet rooted, and with accusing conscience.

- *pur fermo* ‘still motionless’ → “yet rooted”: “rooted” gives the adjective a figurative force.
- *contro a coscienza* ‘against conscience’ → “with accusing conscience”: opposition to conscience is recast as an accompanying circumstance, with “accusing” supplied.

> 34 Quando mi vide star pur fermo e duro,  
> 35 turbato un poco disse: “Or vedi, figlio,  
> 36 tra Beatrice e te è questo muro.”  

When he saw me stand yet rooted and stubborn, troubled a little he said: “Now look my son, ’twixt Beatrice and thee is this wall.”

- *mi vide* ‘me he saw’ → “he saw me”: the object follows the verb.
- *star pur fermo e duro* ‘remain still motionless and hard’ → “stand yet rooted and stubborn”: “stand” makes the posture explicit, “rooted” again renders immobility figuratively, and “stubborn” gives the sense rather than the image of ‘hard’.
- *Or vedi* ‘now see’ → “Now look”: the imperative of seeing becomes one of looking.
- *figlio* ‘son’ → “my son”: a possessive is supplied.

> 37 Come al nome di Tisbe aperse il ciglio  
> 38 Piramo in su la morte, e riguardolla,  
> 39 allor che il gelso diventò vermiglio:  

As at Thisbe’s name Pyramus opened his eyes at the point of death, and gazed at her, when the mulberry became red,

- *al nome di Tisbe* ‘at the name of Thisbe’ → “at Thisbe’s name”: a possessive before the noun replaces the following prepositional phrase.
- *aperse il ciglio Piramo* ‘opened the eyelid Pyramus’ → “Pyramus opened his eyes”: the subject comes first, the article becomes a possessive, and the singular eyelid is rendered by the plural organs of sight.
- *in su la morte* ‘upon death’ → “at the point of death”: “the point of” is supplied to express imminence.
- *riguardolla* ‘looked upon her’ → “gazed at her”: the rendering suggests a sustained look; the attached direct object becomes the object of “at”.
- *allor che* ‘then when’ → “when”: the separate temporal adverb drops out.
- *vermiglio* ‘vermilion’ → “red”: the specific hue becomes a general colour term.

> 40 così, la mia durezza fatta solla,  
> 41 mi volsi al savio duca, udendo il nome  
> 42 che nella mente sempre mi rampolla.  

so, my stubbornness being softened, I turned me to my wise Leader on hearing the name which ever springs up in my mind.

- *la mia durezza fatta solla* ‘my hardness made soft’ → “my stubbornness being softened”: the figurative noun is interpreted as a trait of will, and the participle with its adjective becomes a passive participial phrase.
- *al savio duca* ‘to the wise leader’ → “to my wise Leader”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *udendo* ‘hearing’ → “on hearing”: the preposition makes the temporal relation explicit.
- *nella mente sempre mi rampolla* ‘in the mind ever springs up for me’ → “ever springs up in my mind”: the phrase of place moves to the end, and the personal dative is absorbed into the possessive “my”.

> 43 Ond' ei crollò la testa e disse: “Come?  
> 44 volemci star di qua?” indi sorrise,  
> 45 come al fanciul si fa ch' è vinto al pome.  

Whereupon he shook his head, and said: “What? do we desire to stay this side?” then smiled as one does to a child that is won by an apple.

- *crollò la testa* ‘shook the head’ → “shook his head”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *Come* ‘how’ → “What”: the interrogative adverb becomes an English exclamation of surprise.
- *volemci star di qua* ‘will we stay here on this side’ → “do we desire to stay this side”: the question of wish takes auxiliary “do” and an infinitive with “to”; the locative clitic has no separate equivalent, and the final expression of place is rendered without a preposition.
- *come al fanciul si fa* ‘as to the child one does’ → “as one does to a child”: the impersonal construction takes “one” as subject, the indirect object follows the verb, and the generic definite article becomes indefinite.
- *al pome* ‘at the apple’ → “by an apple”: the preposition is interpreted instrumentally, and the definite article becomes indefinite.

## Lines 46–78 — Through the Fire to the Night Halt

> 46 Poi dentro al foco innanzi mi si mise,  
> 47 pregando Stazio che venisse retro,  
> 48 che pria per lunga strada ci divise.  

Then he entered into the fire in front of me, praying Statius that he would come behind, who for a long way before had separated us.

- *dentro al foco innanzi mi si mise* ‘into the fire, ahead of me, he put himself’ → “he entered into the fire in front of me”: the reflexive verb becomes a verb of motion and moves ahead of both phrases of place.
- *che venisse* ‘that he come’ → “that he would come”: “would” renders the imperfect subjunctive after the verb of request.
- *pria per lunga strada* ‘before, for a long way’ → “for a long way before”: the indication of time moves after that of distance, and English supplies “a”.
- *ci divise* ‘us he separated’ → “had separated us”: the simple past becomes a past perfect, and the object follows the verb.

> 49 Come fui dentro, in un bogliente vetro  
> 50 gittato mi sarei per rinfrescarmi,  
> 51 tant' era ivi lo incendio senza metro.  

When I was within, I would have flung me into molten glass to cool me, so immeasurable there was the burning.

- *in un bogliente vetro* ‘into a boiling glass’ → “into molten glass”: “molten” gives the state of the glass rather than the image of boiling; the substance takes no article in English, and the phrase moves after the verb of throwing.
- *gittato mi sarei* ‘flung myself I would have’ → “I would have flung me”: the auxiliary comes first and the reflexive is rendered by an ordinary object pronoun after the participle.
- *per rinfrescarmi* ‘in order to refresh myself’ → “to cool me”: the infinitive alone marks purpose, the verb is made specific to temperature, and “me” has reflexive force.
- *tant' era ivi lo incendio senza metro* ‘so much was the blaze there without measure’ → “so immeasurable there was the burning”: the final phrase becomes an adjective placed with “so” at the front; “there” precedes “was”, and the noun for the fire becomes a noun of action.

> 52 Lo dolce padre mio, per confortarmi,  
> 53 pur di Beatrice ragionando andava,  
> 54 dicendo: “Gli occhi suoi già veder parmi.”  

My sweet Father, to encourage me, went on discoursing ever of Beatrice, saying: “Already I seem to behold her eyes.”

- *Lo dolce padre mio* ‘the sweet father mine’ → “My sweet Father”: the possessive moves ahead of adjective and noun.
- *per confortarmi* ‘in order to comfort me’ → “to encourage me”: the infinitive alone marks purpose, and “encourage” takes the verb in the sense of giving heart.
- *pur di Beatrice ragionando andava* ‘ever of Beatrice discoursing he went’ → “went on discoursing ever of Beatrice”: the finite verb moves before the participle, “on” makes the continuing action explicit, and the adverb and topic move after “discoursing”.
- *Gli occhi suoi già veder parmi* ‘her eyes already to see it seems to me’ → “Already I seem to behold her eyes”: the impersonal construction becomes personal; the adverb moves to the front, “behold” renders ‘see’, and the object moves to the end with its possessive before the noun.

> 55 Guidavaci una voce che cantava  
> 56 di là; e noi, attenti pure a lei,  
> 57 venimmo fuor là dove si montava.  

A voice guided us, which was singing on the other side, and we, intent only on it, came forth, there where the ascent began.

- *Guidavaci una voce* ‘guided us a voice’ → “A voice guided us”: the subject moves before the verb, and the attached pronoun becomes a separate object.
- *che cantava di là* ‘that sang from over there’ → “which was singing on the other side”: the progressive renders the imperfect, and the indication of place is made more specific.
- *attenti pure a lei* ‘attentive solely to her’ → “intent only on it”: the adjective takes a different preposition, and the pronoun referring to the grammatically feminine voice becomes neuter.
- *si montava* ‘one went up’ → “the ascent began”: the impersonal verb becomes a noun serving as subject, and a verb of beginning is supplied.

> 58 “*Venite benedicti patris mei,*”  
> 59 sonò dentro ad un lume che lì era,  
> 60 tal che mi vinse e guardar nol potei.  

“*Venite benedicti patris mei,*” rang forth from within a light which was there, so bright that it vanquished me, and look upon it I could not.

- *sonò dentro ad* ‘sounded within’ → “rang forth from within”: the English describes the sound as issuing from the light rather than simply sounding in it.
- *lì era* ‘there was’ → “was there”: the adverb of place follows the verb.
- *tal che mi vinse* ‘such that it overcame me’ → “so bright that it vanquished me”: “bright” specifies the quality left unstated by *tal*, and the object follows the verb.
- *guardar nol potei* ‘look at it I could not’ → “look upon it I could not”: the object contained in *nol* (‘not it’) is placed with the infinitive, leaving the negation with the finite verb.

> 61 “Lo sol sen va,” soggiunse, “e vien la sera;  
> 62 non v' arrestate, ma studiate il passo,  
> 63 mentre che l' occidente non s' annera.”  

“The sun is sinking,” it added, “and the evening cometh; stay ye not but mend your pace while the west grows not dark.”

- *sen va* ‘goes away’ → “is sinking”: a progressive verb giving the direction of motion replaces the idiom of departure.
- *vien la sera* ‘comes the evening’ → “the evening cometh”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *non v' arrestate* ‘do not stop yourselves’ → “stay ye not”: the reflexive becomes an intransitive imperative, with an expressed subject and the negative after it.
- *studiate il passo* ‘press the step’ → “mend your pace”: an English idiom for quickening one’s steps replaces the Italian one, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *non s' annera* ‘does not blacken’ → “grows not dark”: the pronominal verb of change becomes a verb followed by an adjective.

> 64 Dritta salia la via per entro il sasso,  
> 65 verso tal parte, ch' io toglieva i raggi  
> 66 dinanzi a me del sol ch' era già basso.  

Straight the way mounted through the rock, toward such a quarter, that in front of me I stayed the rays of sun who already was low.

- *salia la via* ‘mounted the way’ → “the way mounted”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *per entro il sasso* ‘through the inside of the rock’ → “through the rock”: the single preposition absorbs the separate indication of interior passage.
- *verso tal parte* ‘toward such a direction’ → “toward such a quarter”: a directional noun is rendered by “quarter”, with an article supplied.
- *io toglieva i raggi dinanzi a me* ‘I took away the rays in front of me’ → “in front of me I stayed the rays”: the phrase of place moves ahead of the subject, and taking away is rendered as intercepting.
- *del sol ch' era già basso* ‘of the sun that was already low’ → “of sun who already was low”: the article is dropped, the relative becomes personal, and the adverb moves before the verb.

> 67 E di pochi scaglion levammo i saggi,  
> 68 che il sol corcar, per l' ombra che si spense,  
> 69 sentimmo retro ed io e li miei saggi.  

And of few steps made we assay, when I and my sages perceived that the sun had set behind us, because of the shadow which had vanished.

- *levammo i saggi* ‘we took samples’ → “made we assay”: the Italian idiom of taking samples becomes one of making trial; the definite plural noun becomes an unarticled singular, and the subject follows the verb.
- *il sol corcar* ‘the sun lie down’ → “that the sun had set”: the infinitive dependent on the verb of perception becomes a finite clause in the past perfect; “set” replaces the image of lying down.
- *per l' ombra che si spense* ‘by the shadow that went out’ → “because of the shadow which had vanished”: the sign by which they perceive becomes an explicit reason; the image of extinction gives way to disappearance, the simple past becomes past perfect, and the phrase moves to the end.
- *sentimmo retro ed io e li miei saggi* ‘we perceived behind, both I and my sages’ → “I and my sages perceived” … “behind us”: the compound subject moves before the verb, the first of the paired conjunctions drops out, and “behind” gains an explicit reference point in the clause about the sunset.

> 70 E pria che in tutte le sue parti immense  
> 71 fosse orizzonte fatto d' un aspetto,  
> 72 e notte avesse tutte sue dispense,  

And ere the horizon in all its stupendous range had become of one hue, and night held all her dominion,

- *in tutte le sue parti immense* ‘in all its immense parts’ → “in all its stupendous range”: the plural parts become a single extent, and an adjective of magnitude becomes one of wonder.
- *fosse orizzonte fatto d' un aspetto* ‘the horizon should have become of one appearance’ → “the horizon” … “had become of one hue”: the subject moves out from between auxiliary and participle to precede the phrase of extent; the pluperfect subjunctive becomes an English past perfect, and “hue” specifies an aspect of appearance.
- *notte avesse tutte sue dispense* ‘night should have all her allotments’ → “night held all her dominion”: the imperfect subjunctive becomes a simple past, having becomes holding, and the plural of what is allotted is interpreted collectively as the extent of night’s rule.

> 73 ciascun di noi d' un grado fece letto:  
> 74 chè la natura del monte ci affranse  
> 75 la possa del salir più che il diletto.  

each of us made a bed of a step; for the law of the mount took from us the power, rather than the desire, to ascend.

- *d' un grado fece letto* ‘of a step made bed’ → “made a bed of a step”: the material phrase moves after the object, which gains an article.
- *la natura del monte* ‘the nature of the mount’ → “the law of the mount”: an inherent property is rendered as a governing rule.
- *ci affranse* ‘for us broke down’ → “took from us”: the affected persons become the complement of “from”, and the verb expresses deprivation rather than the breaking of strength.
- *la possa del salir più che il diletto* ‘the power of ascending more than the pleasure’ → “the power, rather than the desire, to ascend”: the infinitive moves to the end, where it can complement both nouns; comparison of degree becomes a contrast, and pleasure is interpreted as desire.

> 76 Quali si fanno ruminando manse  
> 77 le capre, state rapide e proterve  
> 78 sopra le cime, avanti che sien pranse,  

As goats that have been agile and wanton upon the heights ere they are fed, grow tame while ruminating,

- *Quali si fanno ruminando manse le capre* ‘as become, ruminating, tame the goats’ → “As goats that” … “grow tame while ruminating”: the subject moves to the front without its article, the pronominal verb becomes “grow”, and the gerund follows the adjective with “while” supplied.
- *state rapide e proterve* ‘having been swift and unruly’ → “have been agile and wanton”: the participle becomes the finite verb of a relative clause; speed becomes agility, and unruliness is rendered as wantonness.
- *sopra le cime* ‘upon the summits’ → “upon the heights”: the more specific term for the tops becomes one for high ground.
- *avanti che sien pranse* ‘before they have eaten’ → “ere they are fed”: the compound subjunctive describing the goats as having eaten is rendered by a present passive.

## Lines 79–111 — The Night Watch and the Dream of Leah

> 79 tacite all' ombra, mentre che il sol ferve,  
> 80 guardate dal pastor, che in su la verga  
> 81 poggiato s' è, e lor poggiato serve;  

silent in the shade, when the sun is hot, guarded by the herd who has leaned upon his staff, and, leaning, minds them;

- *mentre che* ‘while’ → “when”: a conjunction of duration becomes one of time more generally.
- *ferve* ‘burns’ → “is hot”: the verb becomes a description of the sun’s state.
- *pastor* ‘shepherd’ → “herd”: Okey uses the older word for the person who tends the animals.
- *in su la verga poggiato s' è* ‘upon the staff leaned himself has’ → “has leaned upon his staff”: the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb, the auxiliary precedes the participle, the phrase of place follows, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *lor poggiato serve* ‘them, leaning, he serves’ → “leaning, minds them”: the participle comes first, the object follows the verb, and service is rendered more specifically as looking after the animals.

> 82 e quale il mandrian che fuori alberga,  
> 83 lungo il peculio suo queto pernotta,  
> 84 guardando perchè fiera non lo sperga:  

and like the shepherd who lodges in the open, holds silent vigil by night longside his flock, watching lest a wild beast scatter it;

- *quale* ‘such as’ → “like”: a preposition introduces the comparison.
- *mandrian* ‘herdsman’ → “shepherd”: the more general occupation is made specific.
- *fuori alberga* ‘outside lodges’ → “lodges in the open”: the adverb becomes a phrase following the verb.
- *lungo il peculio suo* ‘alongside the livestock of his’ → “longside his flock”: the possessive moves before the noun, and the livestock is specifically a flock. The whole phrase moves after the description of the vigil.
- *queto pernotta* ‘quiet he passes the night’ → “holds silent vigil by night”: the adjective describing the herdsman now qualifies the supplied noun “vigil”; the night implicit in the verb becomes a phrase of time.
- *perchè fiera non lo sperga* ‘so that a wild beast may not it scatter’ → “lest a wild beast scatter it”: “lest” carries the negative purpose without a separate negative, and the object follows the verb.

> 85 tali eravamo tutti e tre allotta,  
> 86 io come capra ed ei come pastori,  
> 87 fasciati quinci e quindi d' alta grotta.  

such were we then all three, I as a goat and they as shepherds, bounded by the high rock on this side and on that.

- *tutti e tre allotta* ‘all three then’ → “then all three”: the adverb moves before the expression of number; the Italian idiom needs no equivalent of *e* in English.
- *come capra* ‘as goat’ → “as a goat”: English supplies the indefinite article before the singular count noun.
- *fasciati* ‘enwrapped’ → “bounded”: enclosure is expressed as a limit rather than a wrapping.
- *quinci e quindi d' alta grotta* ‘on this side and on that with high rock’ → “by the high rock on this side and on that”: the phrase specifying the two sides moves after the rock; “by” introduces what bounds them, and English supplies the definite article.

> 88 Poco potea parer lì del di fuori;  

Little of the outside could there be seen,

- *Poco potea parer lì del di fuori* ‘little could appear there of the outside’ → “Little of the outside could there be seen”: “of the outside” is brought next to “Little”; appearing becomes being seen, and the adverb moves between the modal and the passive infinitive.

> 89 ma per quel poco vedev' io le stelle,  
> 90 di lor solere e più chiare e maggiori.  

but through that little I saw the stars brighter and bigger than their wont.

- *vedev' io* ‘saw I’ → “I saw”: the expressed subject moves before the verb; the simple past renders the Italian imperfect.
- *di lor solere e più chiare e maggiori* ‘than their wont both brighter and greater’ → “brighter and bigger than their wont”: the standard of comparison moves after both comparatives, the first half of the paired conjunction is dropped, and “bigger” specifies size.

> 91 Sì ruminando, e sì mirando in quelle,  
> 92 mi prese il sonno: il sonno che sovente,  
> 93 anzi che il fatto sia, sa le novelle.  

As I was thus ruminating, and thus gazing at them, sleep fell on me, sleep which oft doth know the news ere the fact come to pass.

- *Sì ruminando, e sì mirando in quelle* ‘thus ruminating, and thus gazing into those’ → “As I was thus ruminating, and thus gazing at them”: the two gerunds become a temporal clause with progressive verbs; the demonstrative referring back to the stars becomes a personal pronoun, and the gaze is directed “at” rather than into them.
- *mi prese il sonno* ‘me took sleep’ → “sleep fell on me”: the subject moves first, and the image of seizing becomes one of falling on someone.
- *il sonno* ‘the sleep’ → “sleep”: the repeated noun has no article in English.
- *anzi che il fatto sia* ‘before the fact be’ → “ere the fact come to pass”: being is rendered as happening, and the clause moves from before the verb of knowing to the end.
- *sa* ‘knows’ → “doth know”: the simple present is rendered with an auxiliary.

> 94 Nell' ora, credo, che dell' oriente  
> 95 prima raggiò nel monte Citerea,  
> 96 che di foco d' amor par sempre ardente,  

In the hour, methinks, when Cytherea, who seemeth ever burning with fire of love, first beamed from the east on the mount,

- *credo* ‘I believe’ → “methinks”: a personal verb of belief becomes an impersonal expression of seeming.
- *dell' oriente prima raggiò* ‘from the east first beamed’ → “first beamed from the east”: the phrase of origin moves after the verb.
- *nel monte Citerea* ‘on the mount Cytherea’ → “Cytherea” … “on the mount”: the subject moves from after the verb and its phrase of place to before both.
- *che di foco d' amor par sempre ardente* ‘who with fire of love seems ever burning’ → “who seemeth ever burning with fire of love”: the phrase naming the fire follows “burning”; the relative clause moves with its antecedent to precede “first beamed”.

> 97 giovane e bella in sogno mi parea  
> 98 donna vedere andar per una landa  
> 99 cogliendo fiori; e cantando dicea:  

meseemed to behold in a dream, a lady, young and fair, going along a plain gathering flowers; and singing she said:

- *giovane e bella … donna* ‘young and beautiful … lady’ → “a lady, young and fair”: the adjectives are brought together with their noun but placed after it, “fair” renders *bella*, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *in sogno* ‘in dream’ → “in a dream”: English supplies an article and places the phrase after the infinitive of seeing rather than before the verb of seeming.
- *vedere* ‘to see’ → “to behold”: a more formal verb is used, and the infinitive precedes its object rather than following it.
- *andar per una landa* ‘to go through an open tract’ → “going along a plain”: the infinitive after a verb of perception becomes a participle; passage through open country becomes movement along a plain.
- *dicea* ‘was saying’ → “said”: a simple past renders the imperfect.

> 100 “Sappia, qualunque il mio nome domanda,  
> 101 ch' io mi son Lia, e vo movendo intorno  
> 102 le belle mani a farmi una ghirlanda.  

“Know, whoso asketh my name, that I am Leah, and go moving my fair hands around to make me a garland.

- *Sappia, qualunque* ‘let whoever … know’ → “Know, whoso”: the third-person subjunctive of command becomes a direct imperative, with the person addressed set apart.
- *il mio nome domanda* ‘my name asks’ → “asketh my name”: the object follows its verb.
- *io mi son* ‘I myself am’ → “I am”: the reinforcing pronominal *mi* has no separate equivalent.
- *intorno le belle mani* ‘around the fair hands’ → “my fair hands around”: the adverb moves after the object of “moving”, and English makes possession explicit where Italian uses the definite article with a body part.

> 103 Per piacermi allo specchio qui m' adorno;  

To please me at the glass here I deck me;

- *Per piacermi* ‘to please myself’ → “To please me”: the reflexive enclitic becomes an unmarked object pronoun after the verb.
- *allo specchio* ‘at the mirror’ → “at the glass”: “glass” is used in the sense of a looking glass.
- *m' adorno* ‘myself I adorn’ → “I deck me”: the reflexive moves after the verb as an unmarked object pronoun, and “deck” renders adorning.

> 104 ma mia suora Rachel mai non si smaga  
> 105 dal suo miraglio, e siede tutto giorno.  

but Rachel my sister ne’er stirs from her mirror, and sitteth all day.

- *mia suora Rachel* ‘my sister Rachel’ → “Rachel my sister”: the name precedes the description of kinship.
- *mai non si smaga dal suo miraglio* ‘never does she turn away from her mirror’ → “ne’er stirs from her mirror”: the two Italian negative elements yield one English negative, and the pronominal verb is interpreted as not moving from the mirror.

> 106 Ell' è de' suoi begli occhi veder vaga,  
> 107 com' io dell' adornarmi con le mani:  
> 108 lei lo vedere, e me l' oprare appaga.”  

She is fain to behold her fair eyes, as I to deck me with my hands: her, contemplation; me, action, doth satisfy.”

- *è de' suoi begli occhi veder vaga* ‘is of her fair eyes seeing desirous’ → “is fain to behold her fair eyes”: the adjective comes immediately after “is”; its complement with *di* becomes a to-infinitive, and the verb of seeing, rendered “behold”, precedes its object.
- *dell' adornarmi* ‘of adorning myself’ → “to deck me”: the complement of the understood adjective becomes a to-infinitive; “deck” takes the unmarked pronoun in reflexive use.
- *con le mani* ‘with the hands’ → “with my hands”: the possessive replaces the definite article.
- *lo vedere* ‘the seeing’ → “contemplation”: the substantivized infinitive becomes an abstract noun that interprets the kind of seeing meant.
- *e me* ‘and me’ → “me”: the conjunction between the parallel phrases is dropped.
- *l' oprare* ‘the doing’ → “action”: the substantivized infinitive becomes an abstract noun without an article.
- *appaga* ‘satisfies’ → “doth satisfy”: the simple present is rendered with an auxiliary.

> 109 E già, per gli splendori antelucani,  
> 110 che tanto ai peregrin surgon più grati  
> 111 quanto tornando albergan men lontani,  

And now, at the brightness ere dayspring born, which rises the gratefuller to wayfarers as on their return they lodge less far from home,

- *già* ‘already’ → “now”: the adverb marks the present point in the sequence rather than saying it has already been reached.
- *per gli splendori antelucani* ‘through the pre-dawn splendours’ → “at the brightness ere dayspring born”: the prepositional relation changes, the plural becomes a singular mass noun, and the adjective expands into a phrase with “born” supplied.
- *che tanto … surgon più grati* ‘which by so much rise more welcome’ → “which rises the gratefuller”: the verb becomes singular to agree with “brightness”, and the measure expressed by *tanto* is conveyed by “the” with the comparative.
- *ai peregrin* ‘to the pilgrims’ → “to wayfarers”: the definite article drops out, the noun is rendered in its broader sense of travellers, and the phrase moves after the comparative.
- *quanto* ‘by how much’ → “as”: a conjunction conveys the second half of the proportional comparison.
- *tornando* ‘returning’ → “on their return”: the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase with a possessive and a noun.
- *men lontani* ‘less distant’ → “less far from home”: the adjective agreeing with the travellers becomes an adverb of distance, and the point from which distance is measured is made explicit.

## Lines 112–142 — Virgil Crowns Dante Over Himself

> 112 le tenebre fuggian da tutti i lati,  
> 113 e il sonno mio con esse; ond' io leva' mi,  
> 114 veggendo i gran maestri già levati.  

the shades of night were fleeing on every side, and my sleep with them; wherefore I arose, seeing the great Masters already risen.

- *le tenebre* ‘the darkness’ → “the shades of night”: the Italian plural is kept in “shades”, and “of night” is supplied.
- *da tutti i lati* ‘from all sides’ → “on every side”: the preposition changes, and the plural with ‘all’ becomes a singular with “every”.
- *il sonno mio* ‘the sleep of mine’ → “my sleep”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *io leva' mi* ‘I raised myself’ → “I arose”: an intransitive verb replaces the reflexive.

> 115 “Quel dolce pome, che per tanti rami  
> 116 cercando va la cura dei mortali,  
> 117 oggi porrà in pace le tue fami.”  

“That sweet fruit whereof the care of mortals goeth in search on so many boughs, this day shall give thy hungerings peace.”

- *Quel dolce pome, che* ‘that sweet fruit, which’ → “That sweet fruit whereof”: the relative, a direct object in Italian, takes the form “whereof” to go with the English noun “search”.
- *per tanti rami* ‘through so many boughs’ → “on so many boughs”: the preposition changes, and the phrase moves from before the expression of searching to after it.
- *cercando va la cura dei mortali* ‘seeking goes the care of mortals’ → “the care of mortals goeth in search”: the subject moves before the verb, and the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase following it.
- *oggi* ‘today’ → “this day”: the adverb becomes a noun phrase.
- *porrà in pace le tue fami* ‘will put at peace thy hungers’ → “shall give thy hungerings peace”: putting something into a state becomes giving something to a recipient; “peace” is now the direct object, and the hungers, rendered as “hungerings”, come before it.

> 118 Virgilio inverso me queste cotali  
> 119 parole usò, e mai non furo strenne  
> 120 che fosser di piacere a queste eguali.  

Words such as these did Virgil use to me, and never have there been gifts that were equal in sweetness to these.

- *Virgilio inverso me queste cotali parole usò* ‘Virgil toward me these such words used’ → “Words such as these did Virgil use to me”: the object is brought to the front, with its modifiers recast after the noun; the simple past is expanded with “did” before the subject, and the phrase of address moves to the end.
- *mai non furo strenne* ‘never were there New Year’s gifts’ → “never have there been gifts”: the Italian negative concord becomes a single negative, the simple past becomes an existential present perfect, and the particular kind of gift is left unspecified.
- *che fosser di piacere a queste eguali* ‘that might be in pleasure to these equal’ → “that were equal in sweetness to these”: the subjunctive becomes indicative; “equal” moves forward, and pleasure is expressed as “sweetness”.

> 121 Tanto voler sopra voler mi venne  
> 122 dell' esser su, ch' ad ogni passo poï  
> 123 al volo mi sentia crescer le penne.  

So greatly did desire upon desire come over me to be above, that at every step after I felt my pinions grow for the flight.

- *Tanto voler sopra voler mi venne* ‘so much desire upon desire came to me’ → “So greatly did desire upon desire come over me”: the amount of desire becomes an adverb of degree modifying the verb; the past is expressed with “did”, and ‘came to me’ becomes “come over me”.
- *dell' esser su* ‘of being up’ → “to be above”: a preposition and substantivized infinitive become an infinitive directly complementing “desire”.
- *al volo mi sentia crescer le penne* ‘for flight I felt the feathers grow on me’ → “I felt my pinions grow for the flight”: the flight phrase moves to the end; the relation to the speaker expressed by the clitic becomes the possessive “my”, and the more specific “pinions” precedes rather than follows “grow”.

> 124 Come la scala tutta sotto noi  
> 125 fu corsa, e fummo in sul grado superno,  
> 126 in me ficcò Virgilio gli occhi suoi,  

When the stairway was all sped beneath us, and we were upon the topmost step, on me did Virgil fix his eyes,

- *la scala tutta sotto noi fu corsa* ‘the stairway all beneath us was run’ → “the stairway was all sped beneath us”: “all” follows the auxiliary, the phrase of place follows the participle, and ‘run’ in the sense of traversed becomes “sped”.
- *grado superno* ‘step topmost’ → “topmost step”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *in me ficcò Virgilio gli occhi suoi* ‘into me fixed Virgil the eyes of his’ → “on me did Virgil fix his eyes”: the preposition changes; “did” carries the past tense before the subject, while “fix” follows it, and the possessive precedes “eyes”.

> 127 e disse: “Il temporal foco e l' eterno  
> 128 veduto hai, figlio, e sei venuto in parte  
> 129 dov' io per me più oltre non discerno.  

and said: “Son, the temporal fire and the eternal, hast thou seen, and art come to a place where I, of myself, discern no further.

- *veduto hai, figlio* ‘seen hast thou, son’ → “Son,” … “hast thou seen”: the vocative moves to the beginning of the speech, before the object, and the auxiliary precedes the participle.
- *in parte* ‘into a part’ → “to a place”: the phrase is rendered as a destination, with “place” for the region reached.
- *per me* ‘by myself’ → “of myself”: the prepositional idiom changes.
- *più oltre non discerno* ‘farther beyond I do not discern’ → “discern no further”: the verb comes first, and comparison and negation are condensed into “no further”.

> 130 Tratto t' ho qui con ingegno e con arte;  
> 131 lo tuo piacere omai prendi per duce:  
> 132 fuor sei dell' erte vie, fuor sei dell' arte.  

Here have I brought thee with wit and with art; now take thy pleasure for guide; forth art thou from the steep ways, forth art from the narrow.

- *Tratto t' ho qui* ‘drawn thee have I here’ → “Here have I brought thee”: “Here” comes first, the auxiliary precedes the participle, and “brought” replaces ‘drawn’, with the object following it.
- *ingegno* ‘ingenuity’ → “wit”: the capacity is expressed by “wit” in the sense of mental resourcefulness.
- *lo tuo piacere omai prendi* ‘thy pleasure henceforth take’ → “now take thy pleasure”: the forward-looking adverb becomes “now” and comes first; the object follows the imperative.
- *per duce* ‘as leader’ → “for guide”: the role is expressed as that of a guide.
- *fuor sei dell' erte vie, fuor sei dell' arte* ‘outside art thou of the steep ways, outside art thou of the narrow ones’ → “forth art thou from the steep ways, forth art from the narrow”: being outside is expressed by “forth … from”; “narrow” retains the Italian ellipsis of ‘ways’ (*arte* here is an adjective).

> 133 Vedi là il sol che in fronte ti riluce;  
> 134 vedi l' erbetta, i fiori e gli arbuscelli,  
> 135 che qui la terra sol da sè produce.  

Behold there the sun that shineth on thy brow; behold the tender grass, the flowers, and the shrubs, which the ground here of itself alone brings forth.

- *Vedi* ‘see’ → “Behold”: the imperative is rendered with a more pointed verb of looking.
- *in fronte ti riluce* ‘on the brow to thee shines’ → “shineth on thy brow”: the verb comes first, and the personal clitic becomes a possessive modifying “brow”.
- *vedi l' erbetta* ‘see the little grass’ → “behold the tender grass”: the imperative is again strengthened, and an adjective of quality conveys the diminutive.
- *gli arbuscelli* ‘the little trees’ → “the shrubs”: a distinct noun replaces the diminutive.
- *qui la terra* ‘here the earth’ → “the ground here”: the adverb follows the subject, and “ground” renders ‘earth’.
- *sol da sè* ‘alone of itself’ → “of itself alone”: the limiting word moves to the end.
- *produce* ‘produces’ → “brings forth”: a phrasal verb replaces the single verb.

> 136 Mentre che vegnan lieti gli occhi belli,  
> 137 che lagrimando a te venir mi fenno,  
> 138 seder ti puoi e puoi andar tra elli.  

While the glad fair eyes are coming, which weeping made me come to thee, thou canst sit thee down and canst go among them.

- *Mentre che* ‘while that’ → “While”: one conjunction serves for the two-word connective.
- *vegnan lieti gli occhi belli* ‘may come glad the eyes fair’ → “the glad fair eyes are coming”: the subjunctive of anticipated coming becomes an indicative progressive; the subject precedes the verb, and both adjectives precede “eyes”, with the predicative ‘glad’ now attributive.
- *a te venir mi fenno* ‘to thee come me they made’ → “made me come to thee”: the causative comes first, followed by its object, the infinitive, and the phrase of direction.
- *seder ti puoi* ‘seat thyself thou canst’ → “thou canst sit thee down”: the modal precedes the infinitive and reflexive, and “down” is supplied.

> 139 Non aspettar mio dir più, nè mio cenno.  

No more expect my word, nor my sign.

- *Non aspettar mio dir più* ‘do not await my saying any longer’ → “No more expect my word”: the negative and the expression of time come together before the imperative; ‘await’ becomes “expect”, and the substantivized infinitive becomes “word”.
- *cenno* ‘gesture’ → “sign”: the more general noun renders the gesture.

> 140 Libero, dritto e sano è tuo arbitrio,  
> 141 e fallo fora non fare a suo senno:  

Free, upright, and whole, is thy will, and ’twere a fault not to act according to its prompting;

- *dritto e sano* ‘straight and sound’ → “upright, and whole”: the adjectives are rendered by terms of rectitude and wholeness.
- *arbitrio* ‘power of choice’ → “will”: the faculty of deciding is expressed as “will”.
- *fallo fora* ‘a fault it would be’ → “’twere a fault”: the hypothetical copula precedes its complement, and the English supplies an anticipatory subject.
- *non fare a suo senno* ‘not to do according to its judgment’ → “not to act according to its prompting”: ‘do’ becomes intransitive “act”, and judgment is rendered as “prompting”.

> 142 per ch' io te sopra te corono e mitrio.”  

wherefore I do crown and mitre thee over thyself.

- *io te sopra te corono e mitrio* ‘I thee over thee crown and mitre’ → “I do crown and mitre thee over thyself”: the coordinated verbs, with auxiliary “do”, precede their shared object and the prepositional phrase; the second *te* takes the explicitly reflexive form “thyself”.

## Summary — Italian Order in English Prose

Okey often brings together words that Dante separates. In *aperse il ciglio Piramo*, the subject comes last; in “Pyramus opened his eyes” it comes first, and the singular *ciglio* (‘eyelid’) becomes the plural “eyes”. In *di lor solere e più chiare e maggiori*, the standard of comparison comes first; “brighter and bigger than their wont” places it after both comparatives. Yet Okey does not avoid inversion: “so stood the sun” keeps the order of *sì stava il sole*, and “Free, upright, and whole” preserves the three adjectives in their emphatic position before the verb.

Changes of construction often account for words supplied or left out. The gerund in *cadendo Ibero* becomes a finite clause, “while Ebro falls”; the impersonal *si montava* (‘one went up’) becomes “the ascent began”, with a noun for the movement and a verb marking its beginning. Two routine adjustments are not marked throughout the comparison: Okey generally supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject by the verb ending, and omits the Italian article before a possessive, as in *lo tuo piacere*, “thy pleasure”.

Okey sometimes gives a word a more specific sense: *viva* (‘living’) becomes “piercing” when applied to a voice. Elsewhere he generalizes, as when *vermiglio* becomes “red”. He supplies “from home” to give *men lontani* (‘less distant’) an explicit point of reference, but leaves the reinforcing *esso* in *sopr’ esso Gerion* without a separate equivalent. Such adjustments coexist with close verbal correspondences: Okey keeps the Latin phrases unchanged, echoes *Ricordati, ricordati* with “Remember thee, remember thee”, and preserves both verbs of *corono e mitrio* in “crown and mitre”, even as he rearranges the words around them.
