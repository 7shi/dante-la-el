# Purgatorio 3

## Lines 1–15 — Self-Reproach and the Upward Gaze

> 1 Avvegna che la subitana fuga  
> 2 dispergesse color per la campagna,  
> 3 rivolti al monte, ove ragion ne fruga,  

Although their sudden flight was scattering them o’er the plain, turned to the mount where justice probes us,

- *Avvegna che* ‘be it that’ → “Although”: the concessive formula becomes a single conjunction.
- *la subitana fuga* ‘the sudden flight’ → “their sudden flight”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *dispergesse color* ‘should scatter those’ → “was scattering them”: the imperfect subjunctive after the concessive conjunction becomes an indicative past progressive; a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *per la campagna* ‘across the open country’ → “o’er the plain”: the turn of phrase changes, with the English specifying level ground.
- *ove ragion ne fruga* ‘where justice us goads’ → “where justice probes us”: the verb of prodding becomes one of probing, and the object follows the verb.

> 4 io mi ristrinsi alla fida compagna;  

I drew me close to my faithful comrade;

- *mi ristrinsi* ‘myself I drew tight’ → “drew me close”: the reflexive follows the verb, and closeness is expressed separately.
- *alla fida compagna* ‘to the faithful company’ → “to my faithful comrade”: the possessive replaces the article, and the English names the individual rather than the companionship.

> 5 e come sare' io senza lui corso?  

and how should I have sped without him?

- *sare' io senza lui corso* ‘should I without him have run’ → “should I have sped without him”: English uses the perfect with ‘have’ rather than ‘be’, renders running as speeding, and puts the prepositional phrase last.

> 6 chi m'avria tratto su per la montagna?  

who would have brought me up the mountain?

- *m'avria tratto* ‘me would have drawn’ → “would have brought me”: bringing replaces drawing, and the object follows the verb.
- *su per la montagna* ‘up along the mountain’ → “up the mountain”: one English preposition does the work of the two Italian words of direction.

> 7 Ei mi parea da sè stesso rimorso:  

Gnawed he seemed to me by self-reproach.

- *Ei mi parea da sè stesso rimorso* ‘he to me seemed by himself bitten again’ → “Gnawed he seemed to me by self-reproach”: the participle moves from last to first, the experiencer follows ‘seemed’, and the reflexive agent becomes an abstract noun naming the feeling implicit in the verb.

> 8 o dignitosa coscienza e netta,  
> 9 come t' è picciol fallo amaro morso!  

O noble conscience and clear, how sharp a sting is a little fault to thee!

- *dignitosa* ‘dignified’ → “noble”: nobility replaces dignity.
- *netta* ‘clean’ → “clear”: the adjective changes to the English idiom for a conscience free of blame.
- *come t' è picciol fallo amaro morso* ‘how to thee is a little fault a bitter bite’ → “how sharp a sting is a little fault to thee”: the predicate moves forward and the pronoun phrase moves to the end; sharpness replaces bitterness and a sting replaces a bite.

> 10 Quando li piedi suoi lasciâr la fretta,  
> 11 che l' onestade ad ogni atto dismaga,  

When his feet had lost that haste which mars the dignity of every act,

- *piedi suoi* ‘feet of his’ → “his feet”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *lasciâr la fretta* ‘left haste’ → “had lost that haste”: leaving becomes losing, the simple past becomes a past perfect, and a demonstrative replaces the article.
- *che l' onestade ad ogni atto dismaga* ‘which the dignity in every act impairs’ → “which mars the dignity of every act”: the verb moves before its object, and the relation to each act is expressed by an ‘of’ phrase modifying ‘dignity’.

> 12 la mente mia, che prima era ristretta,  
> 13 lo intento rallargò, sì come vaga;  

my mind, that erewhile was centred within, widened its scope as in eager search,

- *mente mia* ‘mind of mine’ → “my mind”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *era ristretta* ‘was confined’ → “was centred within”: restriction becomes inward concentration, with the direction made explicit.
- *lo intento rallargò* ‘its attention widened again’ → “widened its scope”: the object follows the verb, attention becomes scope, the possessive replaces the article, and the sense of renewed widening is not separately expressed.
- *sì come vaga* ‘as one eager’ → “as in eager search”: the adjective describing the mind becomes a phrase describing an activity; searching is supplied.

> 14 e diedi il viso mio incontro al poggio,  
> 15 che inverso il ciel più alto si dislaga.  

and I set my face to the hillside which rises highest heavenward from the waters.

- *diedi il viso mio* ‘I gave the face of mine’ → “I set my face”: setting replaces giving, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *incontro al poggio* ‘towards the hill’ → “to the hillside”: direction is expressed by “to”, and the hillside is specified rather than the hill as a whole.
- *inverso il ciel più alto si dislaga* ‘towards heaven higher emerges from the waters’ → “rises highest heavenward from the waters”: the verb moves forward; its sense of emerging from water is distributed between “rises” and “from the waters”. The comparative is read as a superlative, and the phrase of direction becomes an adverb placed after “highest”.

## Lines 16–48 — The Shadow and the Limits of Reason

> 16 Lo sol, che retro fiammeggiava roggio,  
> 17 rotto m' era dinanzi, alla figura  
> 18 ch' aveva in me de' suoi raggi l' appoggio.  

The sun, that behind us was flaming red, was broken in front of me in the figure in which it had its beams stayed by me.

- *retro* ‘behind’ → “behind us”: the pronoun is supplied.
- *fiammeggiava* ‘flamed’ → “was flaming”: the imperfect is rendered by the past progressive.
- *rotto m' era dinanzi* ‘broken to me was in front’ → “was broken in front of me”: the auxiliary comes before the participle, and the dative pronoun becomes the complement of “in front of”.
- *alla figura* ‘according to the figure’ → “in the figure”: the preposition is changed.
- *ch' aveva in me de' suoi raggi l' appoggio* ‘that it had in me the stay of its rays’ → “in which it had its beams stayed by me”: the relative is made explicitly prepositional; the noun ‘stay’ becomes a participle, its dependent ‘of its rays’ becomes the object of “had”, and ‘in me’ becomes “by me” at the end.

> 19 Io mi volsi dallato con paura  
> 20 d' esser abbandonato, quando io vidi  
> 21 solo dinanzi a me la terra oscura.  

I turned me aside from fear of being forsaken, when I saw only before me the earth darkened.

- *mi volsi* ‘myself I turned’ → “I turned me”: the reflexive follows the verb.
- *con paura* ‘with fear’ → “from fear”: the accompanying feeling is expressed as the cause.
- *d' esser abbandonato* ‘of being abandoned’ → “of being forsaken”: a different participle is used.
- *la terra oscura* ‘the earth dark’ → “the earth darkened”: a participle replaces the adjective.

> 22 E il mio conforto: “Perchè pur diffidi?”  
> 23 a dir mi cominciò tutto rivolto;  

And my Comfort began to say to me, turning full round: “Why dost thou again distrust?

- *a dir mi cominciò* ‘to say to me he began’ → “began to say to me”: the finite verb comes before its infinitive, and the whole reporting phrase is moved before the question.
- *tutto rivolto* ‘wholly turned’ → “turning full round”: the past participle describing a position becomes a present participle describing a movement; “round” makes its direction explicit.
- *pur diffidi* ‘still dost thou distrust’ → “dost thou again distrust”: persistence is rendered as repetition, and the adverb follows the subject.

> 24 “non credi tu me teco, e ch' io ti guidi?  

believest thou not me with thee and that I do guide thee?

- *non credi tu* ‘not believest thou’ → “believest thou not”: the negative follows the subject.
- *ti guidi* ‘thee I guide’ → “do guide thee”: the object follows the verb, and the auxiliary “do” is supplied.

> 25 Vespero è già colà, dov' è sepolto  
> 26 lo corpo, dentro al quale io facea ombra:  
> 27 Napoli l' ha, e da Brandizio è tolto.  

It is already evening there, where the body buried lies within which I made shadow: Naples possesses it, and from Brindisi ’tis taken.

- *Vespero è già colà* ‘evening is already there’ → “It is already evening there”: the impersonal “It” takes subject position and “evening” follows the verb and adverb.
- *dov' è sepolto lo corpo* ‘where is buried the body’ → “where the body buried lies”: the subject moves up, and the passive ‘is buried’ is rendered by “buried lies”.
- *io facea ombra* ‘I used to make shadow’ → “I made shadow”: the simple past does not mark the imperfect’s habitual aspect.
- *l' ha* ‘it has’ → “possesses it”: the verb is more specific, and the object follows it.

> 28 Ora, se innanzi a me nulla s' adombra,  
> 29 non ti maravigliar più che de' cieli,  
> 30 che l' uno all' altro raggio non ingombra.  

Now, if before me no shadow falls, marvel not more than at the heavenly spheres, that one doth not obstruct the light from the other.

- *nulla s' adombra* ‘nothing is shadowed’ → “no shadow falls”: the negative pronoun and the verb of becoming shaded are recast as a noun subject and a verb of motion.
- *non ti maravigliar* ‘do not marvel’ → “marvel not”: the negative follows the verb; the Italian uses a reflexive infinitive for the negative command.
- *de' cieli* ‘at the heavens’ → “at the heavenly spheres”: the noun becomes an adjective qualifying the supplied “spheres”.
- *l' uno all' altro raggio non ingombra* ‘the one for the other a ray does not obstruct’ → “one doth not obstruct the light from the other”: the article before ‘one’ drops out, the verb precedes its complements, ‘ray’ becomes the collective “the light”, and the dative becomes a phrase with “from”.

> 31 A sofferir tormenti, caldi e gieli  
> 32 simili corpi la virtù dispone,  
> 33 che, come fa, non vuol che a noi si sveli.  

To suffer torments, heat and frost, bodies such as these that power disposes, which wills not that its workings be revealed to us.

- *caldi e gieli* ‘heats and frosts’ → “heat and frost”: both plurals become mass nouns in the singular.
- *simili corpi* ‘like bodies’ → “bodies such as these”: the qualification follows the noun and is expanded with a demonstrative.
- *la virtù* ‘the power’ → “that power”: the article becomes a demonstrative.
- *come fa, non vuol che a noi si sveli* ‘how it does so, it does not will that to us should be unveiled’ → “wills not that its workings be revealed to us”: the fronted clause of manner becomes the noun phrase “its workings” inside the subordinate clause; the negative follows “wills”, the reflexive construction becomes an English passive, and “to us” moves to the end.

> 34 Matto è chi spera che nostra ragione  
> 35 possa trascorrer la infinita via,  
> 36 che tiene una sustanzia in tre persone.  

Mad is he who hopes that our reason may compass that infinitude which one substance in three persons fills.

- *chi* ‘whoever’ → “he who”: the relative is given a separate antecedent.
- *possa trascorrer la infinita via* ‘may traverse the infinite way’ → “may compass that infinitude”: traversal becomes encompassing; the adjective becomes an abstract noun, ‘way’ disappears, and the article becomes a demonstrative.
- *che tiene una sustanzia in tre persone* ‘which occupies one substance in three persons’ → “which one substance in three persons fills”: the Italian places the subject after the verb; English puts it before the verb and renders occupation as filling.

> 37 State contenti, umana gente, al *quia*:  
> 38 chè, se potuto aveste veder tutto,  
> 39 mestier non era partorir Maria;  

Be ye content, O human race, with the *quia*! For if ye had been able to see the whole, no need was there for Mary to give birth;

- *State contenti* ‘remain content’ → “Be ye content”: remaining is rendered as being, and the imperative has an expressed subject.
- *umana gente* ‘human race’ → “O human race”: a vocative particle is supplied.
- *al quia* ‘at the quia’ → “with the *quia*”: English uses “with” after “content”.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “For”: a coordinating conjunction replaces the subordinating one.
- *potuto aveste veder tutto* ‘able ye had been to see everything’ → “ye had been able to see the whole”: the auxiliary precedes the expression of ability, and the indefinite pronoun becomes a noun phrase with an article.
- *mestier non era* ‘need was not’ → “no need was there”: the negative qualifies the noun rather than the verb, and existential “there” is supplied.
- *partorir Maria* ‘to give birth Mary’ → “for Mary to give birth”: the subject of the infinitive is introduced by “for” and precedes it.

> 40 e disiar vedeste senza frutto  
> 41 tai, che sarebbe lor disio quetato,  
> 42 ch' eternalmente è dato lor per lutto.  

and ye have seen such sages desire fruitlessly, whose desire had else been satisfied, which is given them for eternal grief.

- *disiar vedeste senza frutto tai* ‘desire ye saw without fruit such ones’ → “ye have seen such sages desire fruitlessly”: the simple past becomes a present perfect; the verb of seeing comes first, followed by its object and the infinitive; “sages” supplies a noun for the pronominal ‘such ones’, and the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *che sarebbe lor disio quetato* ‘that their desire would be quieted’ → “whose desire had else been satisfied”: “whose” incorporates the possessive into the relative; the subject precedes the auxiliary, ‘quieted’ becomes “satisfied”, and the conditional becomes a past counterfactual with “else” supplied.
- *ch' eternalmente è dato lor per lutto* ‘which eternally is given them for grief’ → “which is given them for eternal grief”: the adverb modifying the giving becomes an adjective modifying “grief”.

> 43 Io dico d' Aristotele e di Plato  
> 44 e di molti altri.” E qui chinò la fronte;  
> 45 e più non disse, e rimase turbato.  

I speak of Aristotle and of Plato, and of many others.” And here he bent his brow, and said no more, and remained troubled.

- *Io dico* ‘I say’ → “I speak”: the verb changes in the construction with ‘of’.
- *chinò la fronte* ‘he bent the brow’ → “he bent his brow”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *più non disse* ‘more he did not say’ → “said no more”: the negative and the adverb are combined after the verb.

> 46 Noi divenimmo intanto al piè del monte:  

We reached meanwhile the mountain’s foot:

- *Noi divenimmo* ‘we came’ → “We reached”: a transitive verb replaces the verb of arrival.
- *al piè del monte* ‘to the foot of the mountain’ → “the mountain’s foot”: the destination becomes a direct object without ‘to’, and a prenominal possessive replaces the phrase with ‘of’.

> 47 quivi trovammo la roccia sì erta,  
> 48 che indarno vi sarien le gambe pronte.  

there found we the cliff so steep that vainly there would legs be nimble.

- *quivi trovammo* ‘there we found’ → “there found we”: the expressed subject follows the verb.
- *la roccia* ‘the rock’ → “the cliff”: a more specific noun is used.
- *vi sarien le gambe pronte* ‘there would be the legs ready’ → “there would legs be nimble”: the subject comes between the auxiliary and “be”, the article is omitted, and readiness is rendered as nimbleness.

## Lines 49–81 — Asking the Souls for the Way Up

> 49 Tra Lerici e Turbia, la più diserta,  
> 50 la più romita via è una scala,  
> 51 verso di quella, agevole ed aperta.  

’Twixt Lerici and Turbia, the way most desolate, most solitary, is a stairway easy and free, compared with that.

- *la più diserta, la più romita via* ‘the most desolate, the most solitary way’ → “the way most desolate, most solitary”: the noun is brought forward, and the article is not repeated before the second superlative.
- *verso di quella* ‘in relation to that’ → “compared with that”: a participle makes the comparison explicit, and the phrase is moved after the adjectives.
- *aperta* ‘open’ → “free”: openness is rendered as freedom from obstruction.

> 52 “Or chi sa da qual man la costa cala,”  
> 53 disse il maestro mio fermando il passo,  
> 54 “sì che possa salir chi va senz' ala?”  

“Now who knows on which hand the scarp doth slope,” said my Master, halting his steps, “so that he may climb who wingless goes?”

- *da qual man* ‘from which hand’ → “on which hand”: the English gives position rather than direction from a side.
- *la costa cala* ‘the hillside descends’ → “the scarp doth slope”: “scarp” specifies a steep face, and downward movement becomes a description of its inclination.
- *il maestro mio* ‘the master of mine’ → “my Master”: the postposed possessive comes before the noun.
- *fermando il passo* ‘stopping the step’ → “halting his steps”: the singular becomes plural, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *possa salir chi va senz' ala* ‘may climb whoever goes without wing’ → “he may climb who wingless goes”: the postponed subject is split into “he” before the verb and a relative clause after it; the prepositional phrase becomes an adjective placed before “goes”.

> 55 E mentre ch' ei teneva il viso basso  
> 56 esaminando del cammin la mente,  
> 57 ed io mirava suso intorno al sasso,  

And while he held his visage low, searching in thought anent the way, and I was looking up about the rocks,

- *il viso basso* ‘the face low’ → “his visage low”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *esaminando del cammin la mente* ‘examining, concerning the way, the mind’ → “searching in thought anent the way”: the mind, the object examined, becomes the sphere of the search; the phrase concerning the way moves to the end.
- *mirava* ‘looked’ → “was looking”: the progressive expresses the continuing action of the imperfect.
- *al sasso* ‘the rock’ (after ‘about’) → “the rocks”: the singular becomes plural; English needs no further preposition after “about”.

> 58 da man sinistra m' apparì una gente  
> 59 d' anime, che movieno i piè ver noi,  
> 60 e non parevan, sì venivan lente.  

on the left hand appeared to me a throng of souls, who moved their feet towards us, and yet seemed not to advance, so slow they came.

- *da man sinistra* ‘from hand left’ → “on the left hand”: origin becomes position, an article is supplied, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *m' apparì* ‘to me appeared’ → “appeared to me”: the recipient follows the verb.
- *una gente d' anime* ‘a company of souls’ → “a throng of souls”: “throng” gives the collective noun a sense of multitude.
- *i piè* ‘the feet’ → “their feet”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *e non parevan* ‘and did not seem [to do so]’ → “and yet seemed not to advance”: “yet” makes the contrast explicit, and “to advance” supplies the action left understood.
- *sì venivan lente* ‘so they came slow’ → “so slow they came”: the adjective is brought next to its modifier rather than left after the verb.

> 61 “Leva,” diss' io, “maestro, gli occhi tuoi:  
> 62 ecco di qua chi ne darà consiglio,  
> 63 se tu da te medesmo aver nol puoi.”  

“Master,” said I, “lift up thine eyes, behold there one who will give us counsel, if of thyself thou mayest have it not.”

- *Leva, diss' io, maestro, gli occhi tuoi* ‘Lift, said I, master, the eyes of thine’ → “Master,” said I, “lift up thine eyes”: the address takes the initial place of the imperative; the verb, expanded by “up”, is reunited with its object, and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *di qua* ‘on this side’ → “there”: the reference to the side near the speaker becomes a pointing adverb of distance.
- *chi ne darà consiglio* ‘who will give us counsel’ → “one who will give us counsel”: the relative, which contains its own antecedent in Italian, is given the explicit antecedent “one”.
- *tu da te medesmo aver nol puoi* ‘thou from thyself have it canst not’ → “of thyself thou mayest have it not”: the phrase of source moves before the subject, the modal precedes the infinitive, and the object and negative follow “have”.

> 64 Guardò a loro, e con libero piglio  
> 65 rispose: “Andiamo in là, ch' ei vegnon piano;  
> 66 e tu ferma la speme, dolce figlio.”  

He looked at them, and with gladsome mien answered: “Go we thither, for slowly they come, and do thou confirm thy hope, sweet son.”

- *libero piglio* ‘free bearing’ → “gladsome mien”: an untroubled or unconstrained manner is interpreted as a cheerful expression.
- *in là* ‘over there’ → “thither”: a single directional adverb replaces the phrase.
- *ch' ei vegnon piano* ‘because they come slowly’ → “for slowly they come”: the reason is introduced by a coordinating conjunction, and the adverb moves before the subject.
- *tu ferma la speme* ‘thou make firm the hope’ → “do thou confirm thy hope”: the imperative takes emphatic “do”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 67 Ancora era quel popol di lontano,  
> 68 dico dopo li nostri mille passi,  
> 69 quanto un buon gittator trarria con mano,  

As yet that people were so far off (I mean after a thousand paces of ours) as a good slinger would carry with his hand,

- *era quel popol di lontano* ‘was that people at a distance’ → “that people were so far off”: the subject precedes the verb and takes plural agreement; “so” prepares for the comparison.
- *dico* ‘I say’ → “I mean”: the verb of speaking becomes one of clarification.
- *li nostri mille passi* ‘our thousand paces’ → “a thousand paces of ours”: the possessive moves after the noun into an “of” phrase, and “a” is supplied before “thousand”.
- *quanto* ‘as far as’ → “as”: the measure of distance has already been expressed by “so far off”.
- *un buon gittator trarria con mano* ‘a good thrower would throw with hand’ → “a good slinger would carry with his hand”: “slinger” is more specific than ‘thrower’; “carry” expresses the reach of the throw, and a possessive is supplied.

> 70 quando si strinser tutti ai duri massi  
> 71 dell' alta ripa, e stetter fermi e stretti,  
> 72 come a guardar, chi va dubbiando, stassi.  

when they all pressed close to the hard rocks of the steep cliff, and stood motionless and close, as he halts to gaze around who goes in dread.

- *si strinser tutti* ‘drew themselves close, all’ → “they all pressed close”: the reflexive becomes intransitive, and “all” moves before the verb.
- *dell' alta ripa* ‘of the high bank’ → “of the steep cliff”: height becomes steepness, and “cliff” specifies the kind of bank.
- *fermi e stretti* ‘still and pressed together’ → “motionless and close”: “close” expresses proximity rather than the act or state of being pressed together.
- *come a guardar, chi va dubbiando, stassi* ‘as, to look, one who goes doubting stops’ → “as he halts to gaze around who goes in dread”: the subject is split into “he” and a delayed relative clause, and the main verb moves forward; “around” is supplied, and the participle expressing doubt or misgiving becomes a phrase expressing fear.

> 73 “O ben finiti, o già spiriti eletti,”  
> 74 Virgilio incominciò, “per quella pace  
> 75 ch' io credo che per voi tutti si aspetti,  

“O ye whose end was happy, O spirits already chosen,” Virgil began, “by that same peace which I believe by you all is awaited,

- *O ben finiti* ‘O well-ended ones’ → “O ye whose end was happy”: the substantival participle becomes a pronoun with a relative clause; the adverb becomes a predicate adjective.
- *già spiriti eletti* ‘already spirits chosen’ → “spirits already chosen”: the adverb moves from before the noun to immediately before the participle.
- *quella pace* ‘that peace’ → “that same peace”: “same” adds emphasis to the demonstrative.
- *si aspetti* ‘may be awaited’ → “is awaited”: the Italian passive construction with *si* becomes a passive with “is”; the subjunctive dependent on the expression of belief has no distinct English counterpart.

> 76 ditene dove la montagna giace,  
> 77 sì che possibil sia l' andare in suso:  
> 78 chè perder tempo a chi più sa più spiace.”  

tell us where the mountain slopes, so that it may be possible to go upward; for time lost irks him most who knoweth most.”

- *giace* ‘lies’ → “slopes”: the verb is interpreted as describing the mountain’s inclination.
- *possibil sia l' andare in suso* ‘possible may be the going upward’ → “it may be possible to go upward”: anticipatory “it” takes subject position, the predicate adjective follows the verb, and the substantival infinitive becomes a deferred infinitive phrase.
- *perder tempo* ‘to lose time’ → “time lost”: the infinitive and its object become a noun modified by a past participle.
- *a chi più sa più spiace* ‘to whoever knows more it is more displeasing’ → “irks him most who knoweth most”: the verb comes first and takes a direct object; the Italian relative is divided into “him” and “who”, and both comparatives become superlatives placed after their verbs or objects.

> 79 Come le pecorelle escon del chiuso  
> 80 ad una, a due, a tre, e l' altre stanno  
> 81 timidette atterrando l' occhio e il muso;  

As sheep come forth from the pen, in ones, in twos, in threes, and the others stand all timid, casting eye and nose to earth,

- *le pecorelle* ‘the little sheep’ → “sheep”: both the definite article and the diminutive are lost.
- *ad una, a due, a tre* ‘one at a time, two at a time, three at a time’ → “in ones, in twos, in threes”: phrases with plural numerals express the successive group sizes.
- *timidette* ‘rather timid’ → “all timid”: “all” conveys emphasis rather than the softening force of the diminutive.
- *atterrando l' occhio e il muso* ‘lowering earthward the eye and the muzzle’ → “casting eye and nose to earth”: the direction contained in the verb becomes a final prepositional phrase, the articles disappear, and “nose” replaces ‘muzzle’.

## Lines 82–114 — The Flock Halts and Manfred Speaks

> 82 e ciò che fa la prima, e l' altre fanno,  
> 83 addossandosi a lei s' ella s' arresta,  
> 84 semplici e quete, e lo 'mperchè non sanno:  

and what the first one doeth, the others do also, huddling up to her if she stand still, silly and quiet, and know not why,

- *ciò che fa la prima* ‘that which does the first’ → “what the first one doeth”: “what” combines antecedent and relative; the subject moves before the verb, and “one” supplies a noun for the substantivized adjective.
- *e l' altre fanno* ‘the others too do’ → “the others do also”: the additive force of the second *e* is expressed by “also” after the verb.
- *addossandosi a lei* ‘pressing themselves against her’ → “huddling up to her”: an English intransitive phrase replaces the reflexive.
- *s' ella s' arresta* ‘if she stops herself’ → “if she stand still”: the reflexive verb of coming to a stop becomes a phrase of remaining at rest; the Italian indicative gives way to an English subjunctive.
- *semplici* ‘simple’ → “silly”: the adjective is rendered in the sense of lacking understanding.
- *lo 'mperchè non sanno* ‘the why they know not’ → “know not why”: the substantivized interrogative loses its article and follows the verb.

> 85 sì vid' io movere a venir la testa  
> 86 di quella mandria fortunata allotta,  
> 87 pudica in faccia, e nell' andare onesta.  

so saw I then the head of that happy flock move to come on, modest in countenance, in movement dignified.

- *sì vid' io movere a venir la testa di quella mandria fortunata allotta* ‘so saw I move to come the head of that flock fortunate then’ → “so saw I then the head of that happy flock move to come on”: “then” moves up to follow “saw I”; the infinitives follow their subject instead of preceding it, and “on” is added. “Happy” takes the sense of ‘fortunate’ and precedes its noun.
- *pudica in faccia* ‘modest in face’ → “modest in countenance”: “countenance” gives the face as expressive of a disposition.
- *e nell' andare onesta* ‘and in the going dignified’ → “in movement dignified”: the conjunction is dropped, and the infinitive used as a noun becomes a noun without an article.

> 88 Come color dinanzi vider rotta  
> 89 la luce in terra dal mio destro canto,  
> 90 sì che l' ombra era da me alla grotta,  

When those in front saw the light broken on the ground on my right side, so that the shadow was from me to the rock,

- *color dinanzi* ‘those ahead’ → “those in front”: a prepositional phrase renders the adverb.
- *vider rotta la luce* ‘saw broken the light’ → “saw the light broken”: the participle follows the object.
- *in terra* ‘on ground’ → “on the ground”: English supplies the article.
- *dal mio destro canto* ‘from my right side’ → “on my right side”: the preposition is rendered as one of position.
- *alla grotta* ‘to the cliff’ → “to the rock”: “rock” renders the contextual sense of *grotta*, the rock face.

> 91 restaro, e trasser sè indietro alquanto,  
> 92 e tutti gli altri che venieno appresso,  
> 93 non sapendo il perchè, fenno altrettanto.  

they halted, and drew them back somewhat; and all the others that came after, knowing not why, did the like.

- *trasser sè indietro* ‘drew themselves back’ → “drew them back”: the personal pronoun is used reflexively instead of an explicit reflexive form.
- *venieno appresso* ‘were coming after’ → “came after”: the simple past does not explicitly mark the ongoing action of the imperfect.
- *non sapendo il perchè* ‘not knowing the why’ → “knowing not why”: the negative follows the participle, and the substantivized *perchè* loses its article.
- *fenno altrettanto* ‘did as much’ → “did the like”: the English expression emphasizes sameness of action.

> 94 “Senza vostra domanda io vi confesso,  
> 95 che questo è corpo uman che voi vedete,  
> 96 per che il lume del sole in terra è fesso.  

“Without your question I confess to you, that this is a human body ye see, by which the sun’s light on the ground is cleft.

- *io vi confesso* ‘I to you confess’ → “I confess to you”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *corpo uman* ‘body human’ → “a human body”: English supplies the indefinite article and puts the adjective first.
- *che voi vedete* ‘that ye see’ → “ye see”: the object relative is unexpressed.
- *il lume del sole* ‘the light of the sun’ → “the sun’s light”: a possessive before the noun replaces the following prepositional phrase.
- *in terra* ‘on ground’ → “on the ground”: English supplies the article.

> 97 Non vi maravigliate; ma credete  
> 98 che, non senza virtù che dal ciel vegna,  
> 99 cerchi di soperchiar questa parete.”  

Marvel ye not, but believe that not without virtue which cometh from heaven, he seeks to surmount this wall.”

- *Non vi maravigliate* ‘do not marvel’ → “Marvel ye not”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, and the negative follows the verb and its expressed subject.
- *dal ciel vegna* ‘from heaven come’ → “cometh from heaven”: the verb moves before the phrase of origin, and the Italian subjunctive becomes indicative.
- *cerchi di soperchiar* ‘he seek to surmount’ → “he seeks to surmount”: English uses the indicative where Italian has a subjunctive dependent on the command to believe.

> 100 Così il maestro; e quella gente degna:  
> 101 “Tornate,” disse, “intrate innanzi dunque,”  
> 102 coi dossi delle man facendo insegna.  

So my Master; and that worthy people said: “Turn ye, enter then before us,” with the backs of their hands making sign.

- *il maestro* ‘the master’ → “my Master”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *quella gente degna* ‘that people worthy’ → “that worthy people”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *Tornate, disse* ‘Turn back, said’ → “said: “Turn ye”: the reporting verb moves from within the speech to before it, and the command is rendered without an explicit ‘back’.
- *intrate innanzi dunque* ‘enter ahead then’ → “enter then before us”: “then” moves forward, and “us” makes explicit the point of comparison in ‘ahead’.
- *coi dossi delle man* ‘with the backs of the hands’ → “with the backs of their hands”: a possessive replaces the article before the body part.

> 103 Ed un di loro incominciò: “Chiunque  
> 104 tu se', così andando volgi il viso,  
> 105 pon mente, se di là mi vedesti unque.”  

And one of them began: “Whoever thou art, thus while going turn thy face, give heed if e’er thou sawest me yonder.”

- *così andando* ‘thus going’ → “thus while going”: “while” makes the temporal relation of the gerund explicit.
- *il viso* ‘the face’ → “thy face”: a possessive replaces the article.
- *pon mente* ‘set mind’ → “give heed”: one verb–noun idiom replaces another.
- *di là mi vedesti unque* ‘over there me thou sawest ever’ → “e’er thou sawest me yonder”: the time adverb moves first, the object follows the verb, and the phrase of place becomes a single adverb at the end.

> 106 Io mi volsi ver lui, e guardail fiso:  
> 107 biondo era e bello e di gentile aspetto;  
> 108 ma l' un de' cigli un colpo avea diviso.  

I turned me to him, and steadfastly did look: golden-haired was he, and fair, and of noble mien; but one of his eyebrows a cut had cleft.

- *Io mi volsi ver lui* ‘I myself turned toward him’ → “I turned me to him”: the reflexive follows the verb as “me”, and ‘toward’ becomes “to”.
- *guardail fiso* ‘I looked at him fixedly’ → “steadfastly did look”: the object attached to the verb is dropped, the adverbial modifier moves first, and “did” carries the past tense.
- *biondo* ‘blond’ → “golden-haired”: the compound expressly names the hair and describes its colour as gold.
- *di gentile aspetto* ‘of noble appearance’ → “of noble mien”: “mien” gives appearance in the sense of bearing.
- *l' un de' cigli* ‘the one of the eyebrows’ → “one of his eyebrows”: the first article is dropped and the second becomes a possessive.
- *un colpo avea diviso* ‘a blow had divided’ → “a cut had cleft”: the blow is rendered by the cut it made, and “cleft” gives ‘divided’ the more specific sense of splitting.

> 109 Quando io mi fui umilmente disdetto  
> 110 d' averlo visto mai, ei disse: “Or vedi”:  

When I humbly had disclaimed ever to have seen him, he said: “Now look”;

- *io mi fui umilmente disdetto* ‘I had humbly denied’ → “I humbly had disclaimed”: the reflexive idiom becomes a non-reflexive verb, the Italian past anterior is rendered by the English past perfect, and the adverb precedes the auxiliary.
- *d' averlo visto mai* ‘of having him seen ever’ → “ever to have seen him”: an English perfect infinitive replaces the construction with *di*, the object moves after the participle, and “ever” moves to the front.

> 111 e mostrommi una piaga a sommo il petto.  

and he showed me a wound above his breast.

- *a sommo il petto* ‘at the top of the breast’ → “above his breast”: “above” replaces the phrase locating the wound at the uppermost part, and a possessive replaces the article.

> 112 Poi sorridendo disse: “Io son Manfredi,  
> 113 nepote di Costanza imperadrice;  
> 114 ond' io ti prego che quando tu riedi,  

Then smiling said: “I am Manfred, grandson of Empress Constance; wherefore I pray thee, that when thou returnest,

- *Costanza imperadrice* ‘Constance empress’ → “Empress Constance”: the title precedes the name.
- *io ti prego* ‘I thee pray’ → “I pray thee”: the object follows the verb.

## Lines 115–145 — Manfred’s Repentance and Plea for Prayers

> 115 vadi a mia bella figlia, genitrice  
> 116 dell' onor di Cicilia e d' Aragona,  
> 117 e dichi il vero a lei, s' altro si dice.  

thou go to my fair daughter, parent of the glory of Sicily and of Aragon, and tell her sooth, if other tale be told.

- *vadi* ‘that thou go’ → “thou go”: the Italian subjunctive continues a construction begun before line 115; the English likewise has a dependent request, not a new imperative.
- *genitrice* ‘mother’ → “parent”: the English noun is not specifically feminine.
- *dichi il vero a lei* ‘tell the truth to her’ → “tell her sooth”: the recipient moves before the thing told and loses its preposition; “sooth” renders the substantivized adjective without its article.
- *s’ altro si dice* ‘if something else is said’ → “if other tale be told”: the indefinite pronoun becomes a modifier of the supplied noun “tale”; the Italian indicative with *si* becomes an English subjunctive passive, with “told” for ‘said’.

> 118 Poscia ch' i' ebbi rotta la persona  
> 119 di due punte mortali, io mi rendei  
> 120 piangendo a quei che volentier perdona.  

After I had my body pierced by two mortal stabs, I gave me up weeping to him who willingly doth pardon.

- *Poscia ch’* ‘after that’ → “After”: one English conjunction does the work of the two Italian words.
- *ebbi rotta la persona* ‘I had pierced the body’ → “I had my body pierced”: the Italian participle precedes and agrees with its object; English puts the body before the participle, making it the thing acted on, and uses a possessive for the article.
- *di due punte mortali* ‘by two thrusts mortal’ → “by two mortal stabs”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *io mi rendei* ‘I surrendered myself’ → “I gave me up”: a phrasal verb renders the simple verb, with the object after the verb rather than before it.
- *volentier perdona* ‘willingly pardons’ → “willingly doth pardon”: the simple present is expanded with an auxiliary.

> 121 Orribil furon li peccati miei;  

Horrible were my transgressions;

- *peccati* ‘sins’ → “transgressions”: a different noun is used.

> 122 ma la bontà infinita ha sì gran braccia  
> 123 che prende ciò, che si rivolge a lei.  

but infinite goodness hath such wide arms that it accepteth all that turn to it.

- *la bontà infinita* ‘the goodness infinite’ → “infinite goodness”: the article drops out and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *sì gran braccia* ‘so great arms’ → “such wide arms”: degree is expressed by “such” modifying the noun phrase, and greatness is specified as width.
- *prende* ‘takes’ → “accepteth”: taking is rendered as acceptance.
- *ciò, che si rivolge a lei* ‘that which turns itself toward her’ → “all that turn to it”: the singular demonstrative becomes inclusive “all” with a plural verb; English uses intransitive “turn” for the reflexive, and neuter “it” for the feminine pronoun referring to goodness.

> 124 Se il pastor di Cosenza, che alla caccia  
> 125 di me fu messo per Clemente, allora  
> 126 avesse in Dio ben letta questa faccia,  

If Cosenza’s Pastor, who to chase of me was set by Clement, then had well read that page in God,

- *il pastor di Cosenza* ‘the pastor of Cosenza’ → “Cosenza’s Pastor”: the possessive precedes the noun and replaces the article and the *di* phrase.
- *alla caccia di me fu messo* ‘to the hunt of me was put’ → “to chase of me was set”: “chase” is used as a noun without the Italian article, and ‘put’ becomes “set”.
- *avesse in Dio ben letta questa faccia* ‘had in God well read this page’ → “had well read that page in God”: the locative moves to the end and the near demonstrative becomes a far one; the Italian pluperfect subjunctive is expressed by the English past perfect in the conditional clause.

> 127 l' ossa del corpo mio sarieno ancora  
> 128 in co' del ponte presso a Benevento,  
> 129 sotto la guardia della grave mora.  

the bones of my body would yet be at the bridge-head near Benevento, under the guard of the heavy cairn.

- *sarieno ancora* ‘would be still’ → “would yet be”: the adverb moves between the auxiliary and the verb.
- *in co’ del ponte* ‘at the head of the bridge’ → “at the bridge-head”: a compound replaces the noun followed by an *of* phrase.
- *grave mora* ‘heavy heap of stones’ → “heavy cairn”: a more specific noun denotes the stone heap.

> 130 Or le bagna la pioggia e move il vento  
> 131 di fuor del regno, quasi lungo il Verde,  
> 132 dov' ei le trasmutò a lume spento.  

Now the rain washes them, and the wind stirs them, beyond the Realm, hard by the Verde, whither he translated them with tapers quenched.

- *le bagna la pioggia e move il vento* ‘them wets the rain and moves the wind’ → “the rain washes them, and the wind stirs them”: both subjects move before their verbs; the object moves after the first verb and is repeated after the second. Wetting becomes washing, and moving becomes stirring.
- *di fuor del regno* ‘outside of the realm’ → “beyond the Realm”: a single preposition replaces the Italian phrase.
- *quasi lungo il Verde* ‘almost alongside the Verde’ → “hard by the Verde”: an expression of close proximity replaces the qualified ‘alongside’.
- *le trasmutò* ‘them he transferred’ → “translated them”: the object follows the verb; “translated” is used in the sense of transfer from place to place.
- *a lume spento* ‘with light extinguished’ → “with tapers quenched”: the singular light becomes plural objects that give light.

> 133 Per lor maledizion sì non si perde,  
> 134 che non possa tornar l' eterno amore,  
> 135 mentre che la speranza ha fior del verde.  

By curse of theirs man is not so lost, that eternal love may not return, so long as hope retaineth aught of green.

- *per lor maledizion* ‘by their curse’ → “By curse of theirs”: the possessive becomes a phrase after the noun.
- *sì non si perde* ‘one is not so lost’ → “man is not so lost”: “man” makes the generic subject explicit, and the degree word follows rather than precedes the negation.
- *non possa tornar l’ eterno amore* ‘may not return the eternal love’ → “eternal love may not return”: the subject moves from after the infinitive to before the modal, and the article drops out.
- *mentre che* ‘while’ → “so long as”: the conjunction is expanded into a phrase marking duration.
- *la speranza ha fior del verde* ‘the hope has a whit of green’ → “hope retaineth aught of green”: the article drops out; having becomes continuing to have, and “aught” renders the idiomatic *fior* as a quantity rather than literally as a flower.

> 136 Ver è che quale in contumacia muore  
> 137 di santa Chiesa, ancor che al fin si penta,  
> 138 stargli convien da questa ripa in fuore  

True is it, that he who dies in contumacy of Holy Church, even though at the last he repent, needs must stay outside this bank

- *Ver è che* ‘true is that’ → “True is it, that”: English supplies anticipatory “it” before the subject clause.
- *quale in contumacia muore di santa Chiesa* ‘whoever in contumacy dies of Holy Church’ → “he who dies in contumacy of Holy Church”: the relative gains an explicit antecedent, and the verb moves forward so that the two parts of the contumacy phrase stand together.
- *ancor che al fin si penta* ‘although at the end he repent himself’ → “even though at the last he repent”: the concessive is expressed in two English words, and the reflexive verb becomes intransitive.
- *stargli convien* ‘to stay is necessary for him’ → “needs must stay”: the impersonal construction with a dative becomes a personal one whose subject is the preceding “he who dies in contumacy of Holy Church”.
- *da questa ripa in fuore* ‘from this bank on the outside’ → “outside this bank”: one preceding preposition replaces the expressions on either side of the noun.

> 139 per ogni tempo, ch' egli è stato, trenta,  
> 140 in sua presunzion, se tal decreto  
> 141 più corto per buon preghi non diventa.  

thirtyfold for all the time that he hath lived in his presumption, if such decree be not shortened by holy prayers.

- *per ogni tempo, ch’ egli è stato, trenta* ‘for every time that he has been, thirty’ → “thirtyfold for all the time that he hath lived”: the multiplier is moved to the front and given the suffix *-fold*; “all the time” treats the duration as a whole, and ‘has been’ becomes “hath lived”.
- *più corto per buon preghi non diventa* ‘shorter through good prayers does not become’ → “be not shortened by holy prayers”: becoming shorter becomes a passive verb, in the subjunctive rather than the Italian indicative; the prayers follow the verb, and ‘good’ becomes “holy”.

> 142 Vedi oramai se tu mi puoi far lieto,  

Look now, if thou canst make me glad,

- *Vedi oramai* ‘see by now’ → “Look now”: “Look” renders the imperative, and the adverb loses the sense of ‘by this time’.
- *mi puoi far lieto* ‘me thou canst make glad’ → “thou canst make me glad”: the object moves from before the modal to after the infinitive.

> 143 rivelando alla mia buona Costanza  
> 144 come m' hai visto, ed anco esto divieto:  

by revealing to my good Constance how thou hast seen me, and also this ban:

- *rivelando* ‘revealing’ → “by revealing”: the preposition makes the gerund’s function as an expression of means explicit.
- *m’ hai visto* ‘me thou hast seen’ → “thou hast seen me”: the object moves after the participle.

> 145 chè qui per quei di là molto s' avanza.”  

for here, through those yonder, much advancement comes.”

- *per quei di là* ‘through those of over there’ → “through those yonder”: the adverb directly modifies “those”, without a counterpart to *di*.
- *molto s’ avanza* ‘one advances much’ → “much advancement comes”: the impersonal verb becomes a noun with “comes” as its verb, and the adverb of degree becomes a modifier of the noun.

## Summary — Close Echoes and Recast Phrases

Okey often changes the order of a phrase while keeping its words close to the Italian. In *vider rotta la luce*, ‘saw broken the light’, the participle precedes the object; in “saw the light broken” it follows. Object pronouns likewise tend to move after the verb, as in *m’ hai visto*, “thou hast seen me”. Yet Okey does not consistently favour ordinary English prose order: “golden-haired was he, and fair” keeps the opening adjective of *biondo era e bello*, and “so saw I” follows *sì vid’ io* almost word for word.

Okey’s choice of words sometimes narrows a meaning or changes an image. The *gittator*, a ‘thrower’, becomes a “slinger”, while *amaro morso*, a ‘bitter bite’, becomes “how sharp a sting” in the exclamation. Elsewhere he supplies what the Italian leaves understood: *retro*, ‘behind’, becomes “behind us”, and *non parevan* gains an explicit infinitive in “seemed not to advance”. Some distinctions go unexpressed: “sheep” loses the diminutive of *pecorelle*, and “widened” does not separately convey the sense of ‘again’ in *rallargò*.

A change of construction can redistribute the sense without adding a new idea. In *eternalmente è dato lor per lutto*, the adverb qualifies the giving; in “is given them for eternal grief”, the adjective qualifies the grief. The reverse shift, from noun to adverb, occurs when *senza frutto*, ‘without fruit’, becomes “fruitlessly”. Two quieter adjustments recur throughout: Okey generally supplies a subject pronoun where Italian gives the person by the verb ending alone, and drops the article before a possessive. Thus *disse* can become “he said”, while *il maestro mio* becomes “my Master”, with the possessive also moving before the noun.
