# Purgatorio 24

## Lines 1–9 — Talking and Walking at Speed

> 1 Nè il dir l' andar, nè l' andar lui più lento  
> 2 facea, ma ragionando andavam forte,  
> 3 sì come nave pinta da buon vento.  

Neither did our speech make the going, nor the going, it more slow; but, talking we went bravely on, even as a ship driven by a fair wind.

- *Nè il dir l' andar, nè l' andar lui più lento facea* ‘neither the speaking the going, nor the going it more slow made’ → “Neither did our speech make the going, nor the going, it more slow”: the noun “speech” replaces a substantivized infinitive, and “our” replaces the definite article. The shared verb moves from the end into the first clause, with “did” before the subject and “make” after it.
- *ragionando* ‘conversing’ → “talking”: a less specific verb is used.
- *andavam forte* ‘we were going fast’ → “we went bravely on”: the imperfect becomes a simple past; the adverb of speed becomes “bravely,” and “on” makes continued movement explicit.
- *sì come nave* ‘just as ship’ → “even as a ship”: “even as” renders the emphatic comparison, and English supplies an article.
- *pinta da buon vento* ‘pushed by good wind’ → “driven by a fair wind”: “driven” and “fair” are idiomatic of a ship and a favourable wind; English supplies an article.

> 4 E l' ombre, che parean cose rimorte,  
> 5 per le fosse degli occhi ammirazione  
> 6 traean di me, di mio vivere accorte.  

And the shades, that seemed things twice dead, drew in wonderment at me through the pits of their eyes, aware of my being alive.

- *rimorte* ‘dead again’ → “twice dead”: the sense of the prefix is expressed by a separate word that counts the deaths.
- *per le fosse degli occhi* ‘through the pits of the eyes’ → “through the pits of their eyes”: the phrase moves from before the object and verb to after them, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *ammirazione traean di me* ‘wonder they drew of me’ → “drew in wonderment at me”: the verb comes before its object; “in” makes the direction of the drawing explicit, and “at” gives the object of wonder.
- *di mio vivere accorte* ‘of my living aware’ → “aware of my being alive”: the adjective precedes its complement, and the substantivized infinitive becomes a phrase denoting a state.

> 7 Ed io, continuando il mio sermone,  
> 8 dissi: “Ella sen va su forse più tarda  
> 9 che non farebbe, per l' altrui cagione.  

And I, continuing my discourse, said: “Perchance he goeth upward more slowly than he would do, for another’s sake.

- *Ella* ‘she’ → “he”: the translation follows the sex of the referent rather than the gender of the Italian pronoun.
- *sen va su* ‘goes on her way up’ → “goeth upward”: the pronominal expression of motion is reduced to a simple verb, with “upward” for the direction.
- *forse* ‘perhaps’ → “Perchance”: the adverb moves from after the phrase of motion to the beginning of the clause.
- *più tarda* ‘slower’ → “more slowly”: a feminine adjective agreeing with the subject becomes an adverb modifying the verb.
- *che non farebbe* ‘than he would not do’ → “than he would do”: the Italian negative is expletive after a comparative and has no English counterpart.
- *per l' altrui cagione* ‘on another’s account’ → “for another’s sake”: ‘account’ or ‘cause’ becomes “sake” in the corresponding English idiom.

## Lines 10–42 — Piccarda and the Naming of the Fasting Souls

> 10 Ma dimmi, se tu 'l sai, ov' è Piccarda;  

But tell me, if thou knowest, where Piccarda is;

- *se tu 'l sai* ‘if thou it knowest’ → “if thou knowest”: the object pronoun is left out.
- *ov' è Piccarda* ‘where is Piccarda’ → “where Piccarda is”: the verb follows the subject in the English indirect question.

> 11 dimmi s' io veggio da notar persona  
> 12 tra questa gente che sì mi riguarda.”  

tell me if I see any person to be noted among this people who gaze so at me.”

- *da notar persona* ‘to note a person’ → “any person to be noted”: the noun comes first, the infinitive becomes passive, and “any” makes the indefinite sense explicit in the question.
- *che sì mi riguarda* ‘that so me regards’ → “who gaze so at me”: the relative and the plural verb refer to the individuals in the collective “people”; the adverb follows the verb, and the direct object becomes the object of “at”.

> 13 “La mia sorella, che tra bella e buona  
> 14 non so qual fosse più, trionfa lieta  
> 15 nell' alto Olimpo già di sua corona.”  

“My sister, who, whether she were more fair or more good I know not, now triumphs, rejoicing in her crown on high Olympus.”

- *che tra bella e buona non so qual fosse più* ‘who, between fair and good, I know not which she were more’ → “who, whether she were more fair or more good I know not”: the comparison becomes a *whether … or* clause, with “more” repeated before the adjectives; “I know not” moves to the end.
- *trionfa lieta* ‘triumphs glad’ → “triumphs, rejoicing”: the adjective describing the subject becomes a participle.
- *nell' alto Olimpo* ‘in the high Olympus’ → “on high Olympus”: the preposition changes, the article drops out, and the phrase moves to the end.
- *già di sua corona* ‘already of her crown’ → “now” … “in her crown”: the time adverb moves before “triumphs”; the complement of the Italian adjective becomes a phrase with “in” after “rejoicing”.

> 16 Sì disse prima, e poi: “Qui non si vieta  
> 17 di nominar ciascun, da ch' è sì munta  
> 18 nostra sembianza via per la dieta.”  

Thus spake he at first, and then: “Here ’tis not forbidden to name each one, since our features are so wrung by abstinence.

- *prima* ‘first’ → “at first”: the adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *non si vieta* ‘one does not forbid’ → “’tis not forbidden”: the impersonal construction with *si* becomes a passive with anticipatory “’tis”.
- *da ch' è sì munta nostra sembianza via* ‘since so drained away is our appearance’ → “since our features are so wrung”: the singular collective noun becomes plural and precedes its verb; “wrung” renders the image of draining, without a separate word for *via*.
- *per la dieta* ‘through the diet’ → “by abstinence”: the regimen is rendered by what it entails, with a change of preposition and no article.

> 19 Questi (e mostrò col dito) è Bonagiunta,  
> 20 Bonagiunta da Lucca; e quella faccia  

This (and he showed with his finger) is Bonagiunta, Bonagiunta of Lucca;

- *Questi* ‘this man’ → “This”: the English demonstrative does not itself mark a person.
- *col dito* ‘with the finger’ → “with his finger”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *da Lucca* ‘from Lucca’ → “of Lucca”: origin is expressed as an identifying byname.

> 21 di là da lui, più che l' altre trapunta,  
> 22 ebbe la santa Chiesa in le sue braccia:  

and that visage, beyond him, shrivelled more than the others, held Holy Church within its arms:

- *di là da lui* ‘on the far side of him’ → “beyond him”: the locative expression is condensed.
- *più che l' altre trapunta* ‘more than the others quilted’ → “shrivelled more than the others”: the participle comes first, and the figurative texture is rendered by its effect on the face.
- *ebbe la santa Chiesa in le sue braccia* ‘had the holy Church in its arms’ → “held Holy Church within its arms”: “held” makes the sense of possession more specific, the article before “Holy Church” drops out, and “within” renders the simpler locative *in*.

> 23 dal Torso fu, e purga per digiuno  
> 24 l' anguille di Bolsena e la vernaccia.”  

from Tours sprang he, and by fasting purges the eels of Bolsena and the sweet wine.”

- *dal Torso fu* ‘from Tours he was’ → “from Tours sprang he”: a verb of origin replaces the verb of being, with the expressed subject after it.
- *purga per digiuno* ‘purges by fasting’ → “by fasting purges”: the phrase of means precedes the verb.
- *la vernaccia* ‘the vernaccia’ → “the sweet wine”: a description replaces the name of the wine.

> 25 Molti altri mi nomò ad uno ad uno;  
> 26 e del nomar parean tutti contenti,  
> 27 sì ch' io però non vidi un atto bruno.  

Many others he named to me, one by one, and all did seem glad at the naming, so that I saw therefore not one black look.

- *mi nomò* ‘to me he named’ → “he named to me”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *del nomar parean tutti contenti* ‘at the naming seemed all content’ → “all did seem glad at the naming”: “all” moves before the verb, the phrase giving the occasion moves to the end, and “did seem glad” uses an auxiliary and a different adjective.
- *io però non vidi un atto bruno* ‘I therefore did not see a single dark gesture’ → “I saw therefore not one black look”: the adverb follows the verb, the negative attaches to “one” rather than to the verb, and a dark gesture or expression is narrowed to a “black look”.

> 28 Vidi per fame a vôto usar li denti  
> 29 Ubaldin dalla Pila, e Bonifazio  
> 30 che pasturò col rocco molte genti.  

I saw Ubaldino della Pila using his teeth for very hunger on the void; and Boniface who pastured many peoples with the rook.

- *per fame a vôto usar li denti Ubaldin dalla Pila* ‘for hunger on emptiness use the teeth Ubaldino della Pila’ → “Ubaldino della Pila using his teeth for very hunger on the void”: the person seen moves before the action, the infinitive becomes a participle, and the possessive replaces the article before the body part. Both adverbial phrases move after “teeth”; “very” adds emphasis, and “the void” gives *a vôto* a definite noun.
- *pasturò col rocco molte genti* ‘pastured with the rook many peoples’ → “pastured many peoples with the rook”: the object precedes the phrase of instrument.

> 31 Vidi messer Marchese, ch' ebbe spazio  
> 32 già di bere a Forlì con men secchezza,  
> 33 e sì fu tal che non si sentì sazio.  

I saw Messer Marchese, who once had leisure to drink at Forlì with less thirst, and yet was so craving that he never felt sated.

- *ebbe spazio già di bere* ‘had room once to drink’ → “once had leisure to drink”: “once” moves before the verb, and “leisure” gives *spazio* a temporal sense.
- *con men secchezza* ‘with less dryness’ → “with less thirst”: the bodily condition is rendered by the sensation.
- *e sì fu tal* ‘and so was such’ → “and yet was so craving”: “yet” makes the contrast explicit, and “so craving” specifies the quality left implicit in *tal*.
- *non si sentì sazio* ‘did not feel himself sated’ → “he never felt sated”: “never” gives the negative temporal scope, and “felt” needs no reflexive object in English.

> 34 Ma, come fa chi guarda e poi s' apprezza  
> 35 più d' un che d' altro, fe' io a quel da Lucca,  
> 36 che più parea di me aver contezza.  

But as he doth who looks, and then esteems one more than another, so did I to him of Lucca who seemed to have most knowledge of me.

- *come fa chi guarda* ‘as does whoever looks’ → “as he doth who looks”: the indefinite relative is divided into an antecedent before the verb and a relative after it.
- *s' apprezza più d' un che d' altro* ‘sets greater store by one than by another’ → “esteems one more than another”: a transitive verb replaces the pronominal construction with prepositional complements, and “one” comes before “more”.
- *fe' io* ‘did I’ → “so did I”: “so” is supplied to resume the comparison.
- *a quel da Lucca* ‘to that one from Lucca’ → “to him of Lucca”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative, and “of” renders origin.
- *che più parea di me aver contezza* ‘who more seemed of me to have knowledge’ → “who seemed to have most knowledge of me”: “most” makes the comparison superlative and stands directly before “knowledge”, whose complement moves after it.

> 37 Ei mormorava, e non so che “Gentucca”  
> 38 sentiva io là ov' ei sentia la piaga  
> 39 della giustizia che sì li pilucca.  

He was muttering, and something like “Gentucca,” I heard there where he was feeling the wounds of Justice, which so doth pluck them.

- *Ei mormorava* ‘he muttered’ → “He was muttering”: the progressive conveys the continuing action of the imperfect.
- *non so che “Gentucca”* ‘I know not what “Gentucca”’ → “something like “Gentucca,””: uncertainty expressed by a finite clause becomes an indefinite expression of approximation.
- *sentiva io* ‘heard I’ → “I heard”: the expressed subject precedes the verb.
- *ei sentia la piaga* ‘he felt the wound’ → “he was feeling the wounds”: the imperfect becomes a progressive, and the singular noun becomes plural.
- *della giustizia* ‘of the justice’ → “of Justice”: the article is omitted.
- *che sì li pilucca* ‘which so them plucks’ → “which so doth pluck them”: the object follows the verb, and “doth” carries the tense.

> 40 “O anima,” diss' io, “che par sì vaga  
> 41 di parlar meco, fa sì ch' io t' intenda,  
> 42 e te e me col tuo parlare appaga.”  

“O soul,” said I, “that seemeth yearning so to talk with me, speak so that I may understand thee, and satisfy me and thee with thy speech.”

- *che par sì vaga* ‘that seemest so desirous’ → “that seemeth yearning so”: an adjective becomes a participle, “so” follows it, and “seemeth” agrees in the third person with “soul” rather than in the second with the addressee.
- *di parlar meco* ‘of speaking with me’ → “to talk with me”: the complement is expressed with a *to*-infinitive.
- *fa sì ch' io t' intenda* ‘make it so that I understand thee’ → “speak so that I may understand thee”: “speak” specifies the action, “may” conveys the subjunctive, and the object follows the verb.
- *e te e me col tuo parlare appaga* ‘and thee and me with thy speaking satisfy’ → “and satisfy me and thee with thy speech”: the imperative moves before its objects, their order is reversed, and a noun replaces the nominalized infinitive.

## Lines 43–75 — The Prophecy, the New Style, and Forese’s Question

> 43 “Femmina è nata, e non porta ancor benda,”  
> 44 cominciò ei, “che ti farà piacere  
> 45 la mia città, come ch' uom la riprenda.  

“A woman is born and wears not yet the wimple,” he began, “who will make my city pleasing to thee, however man may rebuke it.

- *Femmina è nata* ‘woman is born’ → “A woman is born”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *non porta ancor benda* ‘does not wear yet a veil’ → “wears not yet the wimple”: the negative follows the verb; the bare noun receives a definite article and the more specific clothing term.
- *cominciò ei* ‘began he’ → “he began”: the expressed subject moves before the verb.
- *ti farà piacere la mia città* ‘to thee will cause to please my city’ → “will make my city pleasing to thee”: the city, the subject of the Italian infinitive, becomes the object of “make”; the infinitive becomes an adjective, and the person pleased moves to the end.
- *come ch' uom la riprenda* ‘however one blame it’ → “however man may rebuke it”: the indefinite subject is rendered by generic “man”; “may” conveys the concessive subjunctive, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 46 Tu te n' andrai con questo antivedere;  

Thou shalt go hence with this prophecy;

- *te n' andrai* ‘wilt take thyself away from here’ → “shalt go hence”: the pronominal verb of departure becomes “go” with an adverb of departure; the future is expressed by “shalt”.
- *antivedere* ‘foreseeing’ → “prophecy”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun for what is foretold.

> 47 se nel mio mormorar prendesti errore,  
> 48 dichiariranti ancor le cose vere.  

if thou hast taken my muttering in error, the real facts will make it yet clear to thee.

- *nel mio mormorar prendesti errore* ‘in my muttering thou tookest error’ → “thou hast taken my muttering in error”: the simple past becomes a present perfect; the muttering becomes the direct object, while the Italian object, ‘error’, moves into a prepositional phrase.
- *dichiariranti ancor le cose vere* ‘will make clear to thee yet the things true’ → “the real facts will make it yet clear to thee”: the postposed subject moves first, with ‘things’ interpreted as “facts” and the adjective placed before it. English supplies the object “it”, puts “yet” before “clear”, and moves the attached indirect pronoun to a final prepositional phrase.

> 49 Ma di' s' io veggio qui colui che fuore  
> 50 trasse le nuove rime, cominciando:  
> 51 ‘*Donne, ch' avete intelletto d' Amore.*’”  

But tell if I see here him who invented the new rhymes beginning: ‘*Ladies that have intelligence of Love.*’”

- *fuore trasse* ‘forth drew’ → “invented”: the verb and its adverb become a single verb of origination.

> 52 Ed io a lui: “Io mi son un che, quando  
> 53 amor mi spira, noto, ed a quel modo  
> 54 che ditta dentro, vo significando.”  

And I to him: “I am one who, when Love inspires me take note, and go setting it forth after the fashion which he dictates within me.”

- *Io mi son un che* ‘I for my part am one who’ → “I am one who”: the reinforcing pronominal *mi* has no separate equivalent.
- *amor mi spira* ‘Love breathes into me’ → “Love inspires me”: the image of breathing is rendered by a transitive verb of inspiration, with its object after it.
- *noto* ‘I note’ → “take note”: a verb becomes a verb–noun phrase.
- *a quel modo che ditta dentro* ‘in that manner which he dictates within’ → “after the fashion which he dictates within me”: the demonstrative becomes a definite article, and “me” makes explicit whose interior is meant. The whole phrase moves after the expression it qualifies.
- *vo significando* ‘I go expressing’ → “go setting it forth”: the gerund is rendered by a phrasal verb, with “it” supplied as its object.

> 55 “O frate, issa veggio,” disse, “il nodo  
> 56 che il Notaro, e Guittone e me ritenne  
> 57 di qua dal dolce stil nuovo ch' i' odo.  

“O brother,” said he, “now I see the knot which kept back the Notary, and Guittone, and me, short of the sweet new style that I hear.

- *disse* ‘he said’ → “said he”: the reporting clause moves from after ‘now I see’ to after the address.
- *che il Notaro, e Guittone e me ritenne* ‘which the Notary, and Guittone and me held back’ → “which kept back the Notary, and Guittone, and me”: the verb moves before its three objects.
- *di qua dal* ‘on this side of the’ → “short of the”: the spatial expression becomes an idiom for not reaching something.

> 58 Io veggio ben come le vostre penne  
> 59 di retro al dittator sen vanno strette,  
> 60 che delle nostre certo non avvenne.  

Truly I see how your pens follow close after him who dictates, which certainly befell not with ours.

- *Io veggio ben* ‘I see well’ → “Truly I see”: the adverb qualifying sight becomes an initial affirmation.
- *di retro al dittator sen vanno strette* ‘behind the dictator go their way close’ → “follow close after him who dictates”: the pronominal verb of motion becomes “follow”; the phrase naming the one followed moves after the verb, and the agent noun becomes a pronoun with a relative clause. The agreeing adjective is rendered adverbially.
- *che delle nostre certo non avvenne* ‘which in the case of ours certainly did not happen’ → “which certainly befell not with ours”: the phrase concerning the other pens moves to the end and takes “with”; the negative follows the verb.

> 61 E qual più a guardar oltre si mette,  
> 62 non vede più dall' uno all' altro stilo”;  
> 63 e quasi contentato si tacette.  

And he who sets himself to search farther, has lost all sense of difference between the one style and the other”; and, as if satisfied, he was silent.

- *qual più a guardar oltre si mette* ‘whoever further to look beyond sets himself’ → “he who sets himself to search farther”: the indefinite relative is expanded, the reflexive verb moves before its infinitive, and “search farther” combines the act of looking with the two indications of going further.
- *non vede più dall' uno all' altro stilo* ‘sees no more from the one to the other style’ → “has lost all sense of difference between the one style and the other”: Okey takes the negative as a loss of discrimination rather than as seeing no further distinction. The present becomes a present perfect, “sense of difference” is supplied, and the ‘from … to’ construction becomes “between the one style and the other”, with “style” attached to the first term.
- *quasi contentato* ‘as though contented’ → “as if satisfied”: the participle of contentment is rendered by one of satisfaction.
- *si tacette* ‘fell silent’ → “was silent”: the pronominal verb marking a cessation of speech becomes a description of the resulting state.

> 64 Come gli augei che vernan lungo il Nilo  
> 65 alcuna volta in aer fanno schiera,  
> 66 poi volan più in fretta e vanno in filo:  

As birds that winter along the Nile sometimes make of themselves an aerial squadron, then fly in greater haste and go in file;

- *gli augei* ‘the birds’ → “birds”: English leaves the generic plural without an article.
- *alcuna volta* ‘at some time’ → “sometimes”: the noun phrase becomes an adverb of frequency.
- *in aer fanno schiera* ‘in air form a rank’ → “make of themselves an aerial squadron”: the phrase of place becomes an adjective before the noun, ‘rank’ becomes “squadron”, and “of themselves” makes explicit what forms the group.
- *volan più in fretta* ‘fly more in haste’ → “fly in greater haste”: the comparison is transferred to an adjective modifying “haste”.

> 67 così tutta la gente che lì era,  
> 68 volgendo il viso, raffrettò suo passo,  
> 69 e per magrezza e per voler leggiera.  

so all the people that were there, facing round, quickened their pace, fleet through leanness and desire.

- *tutta la gente che lì era* ‘all the throng that there was’ → “all the people that were there”: the singular collective takes plural agreement in English, and the adverb moves after the verb.
- *volgendo il viso* ‘turning the face’ → “facing round”: the noun becomes part of the verb’s meaning, and “round” expresses the turn.
- *raffrettò suo passo* ‘hastened its step’ → “quickened their pace”: “pace” expresses the rate of movement rather than the step, and the possessive is plural to agree with “people”.
- *e per magrezza e per voler leggiera* ‘both through leanness and through willing light’ → “fleet through leanness and desire”: the final adjective moves first and is interpreted as speed rather than lightness; English drops the first conjunction, uses the preposition only once, and renders the substantivized infinitive by “desire”.

> 70 E come l' uom che di trottare è lasso  
> 71 lascia andar li compagni, e si passeggia  
> 72 fin che si sfoghi l' affollar del casso:  

And as one who is weary of running lets his comrades go by, and walks until the panting of his chest be eased;

- *l' uom* ‘the man’ → “one”: the generic noun becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *di trottare è lasso* ‘of trotting is weary’ → “is weary of running”: the complement moves after the adjective, and “running” is less specific about the gait.
- *lascia andar li compagni* ‘lets go the companions’ → “lets his comrades go by”: the companions move before the infinitive, the article becomes a possessive, and “by” supplies the direction relative to the walker.
- *si passeggia* ‘walks along at his ease’ → “walks”: the pronominal expression is reduced to a simple verb.
- *fin che si sfoghi l' affollar del casso* ‘until it spend itself, the panting of the chest’ → “until the panting of his chest be eased”: the subject moves before the verb, the pronominal subjunctive becomes a passive subjunctive, and the article before the body part becomes a possessive.

> 73 sì lasciò trapassar la santa greggia  
> 74 Forese, e retro meco sen veniva,  
> 75 dicendo: “Quando fia ch' io ti riveggia?”  

so Forese let the holy flock pass by, and came on behind with me, saying: “When shall it be that I see thee again?”

- *lasciò trapassar la santa greggia Forese* ‘let pass by the holy flock Forese’ → “Forese let the holy flock pass by”: the delayed subject moves before the finite verb, and the flock moves before the infinitive.
- *retro meco sen veniva* ‘behind with me was coming along’ → “came on behind with me”: the pronominal imperfect becomes a simple past with “on”, and both adverbial expressions move after the verb.
- *fia* ‘will be’ → “shall it be”: English supplies the impersonal “it” and inverts it with the future auxiliary in the question.
- *ch' io ti riveggia* ‘that I thee see again’ → “that I see thee again”: the object follows the verb, the prefix becomes a separate adverb, and the Italian subjunctive has no distinct English form here.

## Lines 76–108 — Forese’s Prophecy and the Second Tree

> 76 “Non so,” rispos' io lui, “quant' io mi viva;  

“I know not,” answered I him, “how long I may live,

- *rispos' io lui* ‘answered I to him’ → “answered I him”: *lui* indicates the person to whom the reply is made; English makes him the direct object.
- *quant' io mi viva* ‘how long I live’ → “how long I may live”: the subjunctive in the indirect question is rendered with “may”; the pronominal *mi* has no separate equivalent.

> 77 ma già non fia il tornar mio tanto tosto,  
> 78 ch' io non sia col voler prima alla riva:  

yet my return will not be so soon but that I be not before with my desire at the bank:

- *ma già non fia il tornar mio tanto tosto* ‘but indeed will not be the returning of mine so soon’ → “yet my return will not be so soon”: the contrast becomes “yet”, without a separate word for the emphatic *già*; the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun, and the possessive and subject move forward.
- *ch' io non sia* ‘that I be not’ → “but that I be not”: “but” is supplied in the clause dependent on the preceding negative.
- *col voler prima* ‘with the will beforehand’ → “before with my desire”: the adverb moves forward, and the substantivized infinitive denoting willing becomes “my desire”, with a possessive in place of the article.

> 79 però che il loco, u' fui a viver posto,  
> 80 di giorno in giorno più di ben si spolpa,  
> 81 ed a trista ruina par disposto.”  

for the place where I was put to live, is day by day more stripped of good, and seems doomed to woeful ruin.”

- *però che* ‘because’ → “for”: the reason is introduced by a coordinating rather than a subordinating conjunction.
- *fui a viver posto* ‘I was to live placed’ → “I was put to live”: the participle moves next to its auxiliary, ahead of the infinitive.
- *di giorno in giorno più di ben si spolpa* ‘from day to day more of good strips itself bare’ → “is day by day more stripped of good”: the pronominal verb, literally evoking the stripping of flesh, becomes a passive; its auxiliary moves forward and “of good” follows the participle.
- *a trista ruina par disposto* ‘to woeful ruin seems disposed’ → “seems doomed to woeful ruin”: the destination moves after the predicate, and “doomed” is stronger than ‘disposed’ or ‘tending’.

> 82 “Or va,” diss' ei, “chè quei che più n' ha colpa  
> 83 vegg' io a coda d' una bestia tratto  
> 84 in ver la valle, ove mai non si scolpa.  

“Now go,” said he, “for him who is most in fault I see dragged at the tail of a beast, towards the vale where sin is never cleansed.

- *quei che più n' ha colpa* ‘him who most has blame for it’ → “him who is most in fault”: having blame becomes being at fault; *n'*, referring back to what the blame is for, is not separately rendered.
- *vegg' io* ‘see I’ → “I see”: subject and verb change order.
- *a coda d' una bestia tratto* ‘at the tail of a beast dragged’ → “dragged at the tail of a beast”: the participle moves forward, and English gives “tail” an article.
- *ove mai non si scolpa* ‘where one never clears oneself of guilt’ → “where sin is never cleansed”: the impersonal pronominal construction becomes a passive with an explicit subject, “sin”; what is cleansed is now the sin rather than the person.

> 85 La bestia ad ogni passo va più ratto,  
> 86 crescendo sempre, fin ch' ella il percuote,  
> 87 e lascia il corpo vilmente disfatto.  

Faster goes the beast at every step, increasing ever till it dashes him, and leaves his body hideously disfigured.

- *La bestia ad ogni passo va più ratto* ‘the beast at every step goes faster’ → “Faster goes the beast at every step”: the comparative moves to the front, followed by verb, subject, and prepositional phrase.
- *il percuote* ‘him it strikes’ → “it dashes him”: the object follows the verb, and “dashes” gives a more specific sense to the blow.
- *il corpo vilmente disfatto* ‘the body basely undone’ → “his body hideously disfigured”: the article becomes a possessive; the adverb and participle describe a hideous disfigurement rather than an ignominious undoing.

> 88 Non hanno molto a volger quelle rote,  
> 89 (e drizzò gli occhi al ciel) che ti fia chiaro  
> 90 ciò che il mio dir più dichiarar non puote.  

Yon wheels (and he lifted his eyes up to the heavens) have not long to revolve ere that shall be clear to thee which my words may no further declare.

- *Non hanno molto a volger quelle rote* ‘have not much to turn those wheels’ → “Yon wheels” … “have not long to revolve”: the postposed subject moves to the front, with the parenthesis now between subject and verb; “long” makes the measure of time explicit.
- *drizzò gli occhi al ciel* ‘he directed the eyes toward heaven’ → “he lifted his eyes up to the heavens”: directing becomes lifting up, the article becomes a possessive, and the singular destination becomes plural.
- *che ti fia chiaro ciò* ‘before there will be clear to thee that’ → “ere that shall be clear to thee”: the postposed demonstrative becomes the subject before the verb; the recipient moves after “clear”.
- *che il mio dir più dichiarar non puote* ‘which my speaking further declare cannot’ → “which my words may no further declare”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a plural noun, and the negative modal and adverb are rearranged before “declare”.

> 91 Tu ti rimani omai, chè il tempo è caro  
> 92 in questo regno, sì ch' io perdo troppo,  
> 93 venendo teco sì a paro a paro.”  

Now remain thou behind, for time is precious in this realm, so that I lose too much coming with thee thus at equal pace.”

- *Tu ti rimani omai* ‘thou stayest back now’ → “Now remain thou behind”: the instruction is cast as an inverted imperative, the pronominal verb is expressed by “remain … behind”, and the time adverb moves to the front.
- *il tempo* ‘the time’ → “time”: English uses the abstract noun without the article.
- *sì a paro a paro* ‘thus abreast, abreast’ → “thus at equal pace”: the repeated expression for keeping level is rendered by a single phrase specifying pace.

> 94 Qual esce alcuna volta di galoppo  
> 95 lo cavalier di schiera che cavalchi,  
> 96 e va per farsi onor del primo intoppo,  

As a horseman sometimes comes forth at a gallop from a troop that is riding, and goes to win the honour of the first encounter,

- *Qual esce alcuna volta di galoppo lo cavalier* ‘as comes out sometimes at a gallop the horseman’ → “As a horseman sometimes comes forth at a gallop”: the generic definite becomes indefinite and moves before the verb, with the adverb now between them.
- *di schiera che cavalchi* ‘from a troop that may be riding’ → “from a troop that is riding”: English supplies an article and uses an indicative progressive for the subjunctive describing an unspecified troop.
- *per farsi onor del primo intoppo* ‘to make honour for himself from the first encounter’ → “to win the honour of the first encounter”: the reflexive beneficiary disappears into “win”, and honour receives a definite article.

> 97 tal sì partì da noi con maggior valchi;  
> 98 ed io rimasi in via con esso i due,  
> 99 che fur del mondo sì gran maliscalchi.  

so parted he from us with greater strides; and I was left by the way with the two who were such great marshals of the world.

- *tal sì partì da noi* ‘so indeed he departed from us’ → “so parted he from us”: one “so” serves the comparison without separately rendering the reinforcing *sì*; the subject follows the verb.
- *rimasi in via* ‘remained on the way’ → “was left by the way”: the active verb becomes passive, and “by” replaces the preposition of location on the path.
- *con esso i due* ‘with the very two’ → “with the two”: the invariable reinforcing *esso* has no separate equivalent.
- *fur del mondo sì gran maliscalchi* ‘were of the world so great marshals’ → “were such great marshals of the world”: the genitive moves after the noun, and the degree expression becomes “such great”.

> 100 E quando innanzi a noi entrato fue,  
> 101 che gli occhi miei si fero a lui seguaci  
> 102 come la mente alle parole sue,  

And when he had advanced so far ahead of us, that mine eyes made such pursuit of him, as my mind did of his words,

- *innanzi a noi entrato fue* ‘ahead of us entered was’ → “he had advanced so far ahead of us”: the compound verb is rendered by an English past perfect with “had” and moved before the phrase of place; “so far” makes the following result clause explicit.
- *gli occhi miei si fero a lui seguaci* ‘the eyes of mine made themselves followers of him’ → “mine eyes made such pursuit of him”: the possessive moves forward, and the reflexive with a predicate describing the eyes becomes a verb with the abstract object “pursuit”; “such” anticipates the comparison.
- *come la mente alle parole sue* ‘as the mind to the words of his’ → “as my mind did of his words”: the first article becomes a possessive, the second possessive moves forward, and “did” takes up the preceding verb; “of” continues the English construction with “pursuit”.

> 103 parvermi i rami gravidi e vivaci  
> 104 d' un altro pomo, e non molto lontani,  
> 105 per esser pure allora volto in làci.  

the laden and green boughs of another tree appeared to me, and not very far away, for I was but then come round thither.

- *parvermi i rami gravidi e vivaci d' un altro pomo* ‘appeared to me the boughs laden and lively of another fruit tree’ → “the laden and green boughs of another tree appeared to me”: the whole subject moves before the verb and the adjectives before their noun; “green” interprets *vivaci* as visible freshness, and “tree” does not specify a fruit tree.
- *non molto lontani* ‘not very distant’ → “not very far away”: an adjective agreeing with the boughs becomes an adverbial expression of distance.
- *per esser pure allora volto in làci* ‘through being only then turned thither’ → “for I was but then come round thither”: the causal preposition with an infinitive becomes a finite clause, and turning is expressed as coming round.

> 106 Vidi gente sott' esso alzar le mani  
> 107 e gridar non so che verso le fronde;  
> 108 quasi bramosi fantolini e vani  

I saw people beneath it lifting up their hands, and crying out something towards the foliage, like spoilt and greedy children,

- *alzar le mani* ‘raise the hands’ → “lifting up their hands”: the infinitive after the verb of perception becomes a participle, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *gridar non so che* ‘cry I know not what’ → “crying out something”: the second infinitive likewise becomes a participle; an indefinite pronoun replaces the speaker’s explicit profession of ignorance.
- *le fronde* ‘the leafy branches’ → “the foliage”: the plural becomes a collective singular.
- *quasi bramosi fantolini e vani* ‘like eager little children and vain’ → “like spoilt and greedy children”: both adjectives now precede the noun and their order is reversed; “spoilt” interprets *vani* and “greedy” specifies the eagerness of *bramosi*. The diminutive of *fantolini* is not separately marked.

## Lines 109–141 — The Tree’s Warning and the Upward Way

> 109 che pregano, e il pregato non risponde,  

who beg, and he of whom they beg, answers not,

- *il pregato* ‘the one begged of’ → “he of whom they beg”: the substantivized passive participle becomes a pronoun with an active relative clause.
- *non risponde* ‘not answers’ → “answers not”: the negative follows the verb.

> 110 ma per fare esser ben la voglia acuta,  
> 111 tien alto lor disio e nol nasconde.  

but to make their longing full keen, holds what they desire on high, and hides it not.

- *fare esser ben la voglia acuta* ‘make be very the longing keen’ → “make their longing full keen”: the infinitive ‘be’ drops out, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the intensifier moves next to the adjective after the object.
- *tien alto lor disio* ‘holds high their desire’ → “holds what they desire on high”: the noun becomes a relative clause denoting the thing desired, and the expression of height follows the object.
- *nol nasconde* ‘not it hides’ → “hides it not”: both object and negative follow the verb, in reverse order.

> 112 Poi si partì sì come ricreduta;  

Then they departed as though undeceived;

- *si partì* ‘it departed’ → “they departed”: the singular verb, referring to the group collectively, becomes plural.
- *sì come ricreduta* ‘as if disabused’ → “as though undeceived”: the feminine singular participle agrees with the collective in Italian; the English participle applies to the plural subject without a mark of agreement.

> 113 e noi venimmo al grande arbore adesso,  
> 114 che tanti preghi e lagrime rifiuta.  

and now we came to the great tree which mocks so many prayers and tears.

- *noi venimmo al grande arbore adesso* ‘we came to the great tree now’ → “now we came to the great tree”: the adverb moves from after the destination to before the subject.
- *che tanti preghi e lagrime rifiuta* ‘which so many prayers and tears refuses’ → “which mocks so many prayers and tears”: the verb moves before its object, and refusal becomes mockery.

> 115 “Trapassate oltre senza farvi presso;  

“Pass onward without drawing nigh to it;

- *farvi presso* ‘making yourselves near’ → “drawing nigh to it”: an intransitive expression replaces the reflexive construction, and the object of approach is made explicit.

> 116 legno è più su che fu morso da Eva,  
> 117 e questa pianta si levò da esso.”  

higher up is a tree which was eaten of by Eve, and this plant was raised from it.”

- *legno è più su* ‘a tree is higher up’ → “higher up is a tree”: the place and the subject exchange positions around the verb; English supplies the indefinite article.
- *fu morso* ‘was bitten’ → “was eaten of”: the verb of biting becomes a partitive expression with a verb of eating.
- *si levò* ‘rose’ → “was raised”: the pronominal verb of rising becomes a passive.

> 118 Sì tra le frasche non so chi diceva;  

Thus amid the branches some one spake;

- *tra le frasche* ‘among the leafy twigs’ → “amid the branches”: the noun loses its suggestion of foliage.
- *non so chi diceva* ‘I know not who was saying’ → “some one spake”: the speaker is made indefinite without the narrator’s statement of ignorance; the imperfect of ‘say’ becomes the simple past of ‘speak’.

> 119 per che Virgilio e Stazio ed io, ristretti,  
> 120 oltre andavam dal lato che si leva.  

wherefore Virgil and Statius and I, close together, went forward by the side which rises.

- *per che* ‘for which reason’ → “wherefore”: a single connective replaces the prepositional phrase.
- *ristretti* ‘drawn close’ → “close together”: the participle becomes a description of position.
- *oltre andavam* ‘onward we were going’ → “went forward”: the adverb follows the verb, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.

> 121 “Ricordivi,” dicea, “dei maledetti  
> 122 nei nuvoli formati, che satolli  
> 123 Teseo combattêr coi doppi petti;  

“Remember,” he said, “the accursed ones formed in the clouds, who when gorged, fought Theseus with their double breasts;

- *Ricordivi, dicea, dei maledetti* ‘let it come to mind to you, he was saying, of the accursed’ → “Remember,” he said, “the accursed ones”: a personal imperative with a direct object replaces the construction with a dative pronoun and a complement introduced by *di*; “ones” supports the adjective. The imperfect of the reporting verb becomes a simple past.
- *nei nuvoli formati* ‘in the clouds formed’ → “formed in the clouds”: the participle precedes its complement.
- *satolli* ‘sated’ → “when gorged”: the adjective becomes an explicitly temporal phrase, with a stronger participle.
- *Teseo combattêr* ‘Theseus they fought’ → “fought Theseus”: the object follows the verb.
- *coi doppi petti* ‘with the double breasts’ → “with their double breasts”: a possessive replaces the definite article.

> 124 e degli Ebrei, ch' al ber si mostrâr molli,  
> 125 per che no' i volle Gedeon compagni,  
> 126 quando ver Madian discese i colli.”  

and the Hebrews who showed themselves soft at the drinking, wherefore Gideon would have them not for comrades when he came down the hills to Midian.”

- *degli Ebrei* ‘of the Hebrews’ → “the Hebrews”: like the first object of the imperative, the second loses its preposition.
- *al ber si mostrâr molli* ‘at the drinking showed themselves soft’ → “showed themselves soft at the drinking”: the phrase specifying the occasion moves to the end.
- *per che* ‘for which reason’ → “wherefore”: the prepositional phrase becomes a single connective.
- *no' i volle Gedeon compagni* ‘not them wanted Gideon as companions’ → “Gideon would have them not for comrades”: the subject moves before the verb, object and negative follow it in reverse order, and ‘wanted’ with a predicative noun becomes “would have” with a “for” phrase.
- *ver Madian discese i colli* ‘toward Midian descended the hills’ → “came down the hills to Midian”: the directional phrase moves to the end, ‘toward’ becomes “to”, and the single verb becomes “came down”.

> 127 Sì accostati all' un de' due vivagni  
> 128 passammo, udendo colpe della gola,  
> 129 seguite già da miseri guadagni.  

Thus we passed close against one of the two margins, hearing sins of gluttony, once followed by woeful gains.

- *accostati all' un de' due vivagni passammo* ‘drawn close to one of the two margins we passed’ → “we passed close against one of the two margins”: the main verb moves to the front, and a phrase of position replaces the participle of approach.
- *colpe della gola* ‘faults of the throat’ → “sins of gluttony”: “sins” gives the moral sense of ‘faults’, and the vice is named directly rather than by the bodily term.
- *seguite già* ‘followed once’ → “once followed”: the temporal adverb precedes the participle.

> 130 Poi, rallargati per la strada sola,  
> 131 ben mille passi e più ci portaro oltre,  
> 132 contemplando ciascun senza parola.  

Then, spread out along the solitary way, full a thousand paces and more bore us onward, each in contemplation without a word.

- *rallargati* ‘spread out again’ → “spread out”: the sense of renewed separation is not expressed.
- *per la strada sola* ‘along the way solitary’ → “along the solitary way”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *ci portaro oltre* ‘us bore onward’ → “bore us onward”: the object follows the verb.
- *contemplando ciascun* ‘contemplating each’ → “each in contemplation”: the distributive pronoun moves first, and the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase with a noun.
- *senza parola* ‘without word’ → “without a word”: the indefinite article is supplied.

> 133 “Che andate pensando sì voi sol tre?”  
> 134 subita voce disse; ond' io mi scossi,  
> 135 come fan bestie spaventate e poltre.  

“What go ye thus pondering on ye lone three,” sudden voice did say; wherefore I startled as frightened and timid beasts do.

- *andate pensando sì voi sol tre* ‘go pondering thus you three alone’ → “go ye thus pondering on ye lone three”: the subject is expressed both after “go” and in the final address; the adverb moves before the participle, “on” is supplied with “pondering”, and “lone” precedes the numeral.
- *disse* ‘said’ → “did say”: the simple past becomes a form with an auxiliary.
- *ond' io mi scossi* ‘whence I shook myself’ → “wherefore I startled”: the connective is made explicitly causal, and an intransitive verb replaces the reflexive.
- *come fan bestie spaventate e poltre* ‘as do beasts frightened and timid’ → “as frightened and timid beasts do”: the adjectives move before the noun and the verb after the subject.

> 136 Drizzai la testa per veder chi fossi;  

I raised my head to see who it was,

- *Drizzai la testa* ‘I straightened the head’ → “I raised my head”: the movement is rendered as lifting, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *chi fossi* ‘who it might be’ → “who it was”: the Italian subjunctive of the indirect question becomes an English indicative.

> 137 e giammai non si videro in fornace  
> 138 vetri o metalli sì lucenti e rossi,  

and never in a furnace were glasses or metals seen so glowing and red,

- *giammai non* ‘never … not’ → “never”: the single English negative replaces Italian negative concord.
- *in fornace* ‘in furnace’ → “in a furnace”: the indefinite article is supplied.
- *si videro … vetri o metalli* ‘were seen … glasses or metals’ → “were glasses or metals seen”: the Italian *si* passive becomes an auxiliary passive, with the subject between auxiliary and participle after the initial negative.
- *lucenti* ‘shining’ → “glowing”: the English adjective suggests incandescence more specifically.

> 139 com' io vidi un che dicea: “S' a voi piace  
> 140 montare in su, qui si convien dar volta;  
> 141 quinci si va chi vuole andar per pace.”  

as I saw one who said: “If it please you to mount upward, here must a turn be given; hence goeth he who desires to go for peace.”

- *un* ‘a [person]’ → “one”: an independent pronoun renders the elliptical Italian expression.
- *dicea* ‘was saying’ → “said”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *a voi piace* ‘to you it pleases’ → “it please you”: the experiencer follows the verb without a preposition, and the Italian indicative is rendered by the English subjunctive after “If”.
- *si convien dar volta* ‘it is necessary to give a turn’ → “must a turn be given”: the impersonal expression of necessity becomes a modal, and the active infinitive becomes passive, with “a turn” as subject.
- *si va chi vuole* ‘one goes, whoever wishes’ → “goeth he who desires”: English makes the person specified in the relative clause the explicit subject of the main verb rather than retaining the impersonal *si* construction.

## Lines 142–154 — The Fragrant Breeze and the Blessing on Just Hunger

> 142 L' aspetto suo m' avea la vista tolta:  

His countenance had bereft me of sight;

- *L' aspetto suo* ‘the aspect of him’ → “His countenance”: the possessive comes before the noun, and the more general word for appearance is rendered by a word for the face.
- *m' avea la vista tolta* ‘from me had the sight taken’ → “had bereft me of sight”: the participle comes directly after the auxiliary; the person from whom sight is taken becomes the direct object, and the thing taken becomes an *of*-phrase without the article.

> 143 per ch' io mi volsi retro a' miei dottori,  
> 144 com' uom che va secondo ch' egli ascolta.  

wherefore I turned me back to my Teachers, like one who goeth according as he listens.

- *per ch'* ‘for which reason’ → “wherefore”: the preposition and relative are condensed into one connective.
- *io mi volsi* ‘I myself turned’ → “I turned me”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.
- *com' uom che* ‘like a man who’ → “like one who”: the indefinite “one” replaces the generic noun.

> 145 E quale, annunziatrice degli albori,  
> 146 l' aura di maggio movesi ed olezza,  
> 147 tutta impregnata dall' erba e da' fiori:  

And as the May breeze, herald of the dawn, stirs and breathes forth sweetness, all impregnate with grass and with flowers,

- *quale* ‘such as’ → “as”: the first half of the correlative comparison is expressed by the conjunction alone.
- *annunziatrice degli albori* ‘announcer of the first lights’ → “herald of the dawn”: the apposition moves from before the subject to after it; “herald” has no feminine marking, and singular “dawn” replaces the plural.
- *l' aura di maggio* ‘the breeze of May’ → “the May breeze”: the month becomes a noun modifier before “breeze”.
- *movesi* ‘moves itself’ → “stirs”: an intransitive verb replaces the pronominal form.
- *olezza* ‘is fragrant’ → “breathes forth sweetness”: the single verb becomes a phrase with an explicit object.
- *dall' erba e da' fiori* ‘by the grass and by the flowers’ → “with grass and with flowers”: the prepositions mark what the breeze is impregnated with rather than the source of its fragrance; both articles disappear.

> 148 tal mi sentii un vento dar per mezza  
> 149 la fronte, e ben senti' mover la piuma,  
> 150 che fe' sentir d' ambrosia l' orezza.  

such a wind felt I give on the middle of my brow, and right well I felt the pinions move which wafted ambrosial fragrance to my senses;

- *tal mi sentii un vento* ‘such I felt a wind’ → “such a wind felt I”: the noun is brought forward next to “such”, the subject follows the verb, and the pronoun marking the speaker as personally affected has no separate equivalent.
- *dar per mezza la fronte* ‘strike across the middle of the forehead’ → “give on the middle of my brow”: the English retains “give” in the sense of striking, but uses “on” for the point of contact; the adjective agreeing with the forehead becomes the noun “middle” with an *of*-phrase, and the possessive replaces the bodily part’s definite article.
- *ben senti'* ‘well I felt’ → “right well I felt”: “right” adds an explicit intensifier.
- *mover la piuma* ‘move the feather’ → “the pinions move”: the subject of the infinitive comes before it, and a singular feather or wing is rendered by a plural.
- *che fe' sentir d' ambrosia l' orezza* ‘which made one perceive of ambrosia the fragrant air’ → “which wafted ambrosial fragrance to my senses”: the causative with an infinitive of perception becomes a verb of conveyance with an explicit recipient; the air is rendered by its fragrance, without the article, and the *of*-phrase becomes an adjective.

> 151 E senti' dir: “Beati cui alluma  
> 152 tanto di grazia, che l' amor del gusto  
> 153 nel petto lor troppo disir non fuma,  
> 154 esuriendo sempre quanto è giusto.”  

And I heard say: “Blessed are they who are illumined by so much grace, that the love of taste kindleth not too great desire in their breasts, and who hunger always so far as is just.”

- *Beati cui alluma tanto di grazia* ‘blessed those whom so much of grace illumines’ → “Blessed are they who are illumined by so much grace”: the English supplies the copula and an explicit antecedent for the relative. The object of the active verb becomes the subject of a passive, while the Italian subject becomes a *by*-phrase; the partitive construction becomes “so much grace”.
- *nel petto lor troppo disir non fuma* ‘in the breast of them excessive desire does not smoke’ → “kindleth not too great desire in their breasts”: the verb of smoking is interpreted transitively as kindling desire; the negated verb comes before its object, and the phrase of place moves to the end. The distributive singular becomes a plural with the possessive before it.
- *esuriendo* ‘hungering’ → “and who hunger”: a gerund becomes a coordinated relative clause with a finite verb.
- *quanto è giusto* ‘as much as is just’ → “so far as is just”: the measure of hunger is phrased in terms of extent rather than amount.

## Summary — Close Phrasing and Shifts of Construction

Okey often keeps the Italian image while changing the order in which its parts appear. In *Teseo combattêr*, the object comes first; in “fought Theseus”, it follows the verb. The subject *Forese*, delayed until after *lasciò trapassar la santa greggia*, comes first in “Forese let the holy flock pass by”. Yet Okey does not invariably prefer ordinary English order: “so parted he from us” gives the English an inversion of its own. At times both image and phrasing stay close, as in *il nodo*, “the knot”, and *con maggior valchi*, “with greater strides”.

Changes of construction often preserve the sense without preserving the part of speech or the grammatical emphasis. The adjective *lieta* becomes the participle “rejoicing”; the gerund *esuriendo* becomes the relative clause “and who hunger”. In *cui alluma tanto di grazia*, grace does the illuminating; “who are illumined by so much grace” makes the recipients the subject. Word choice can also steer the reading: “the sweet wine” describes rather than names *la vernaccia*, while “mocks” goes beyond *rifiuta* (‘refuses’). A larger interpretive step comes with *non vede più*, where “has lost all sense of difference” turns a statement about what one sees into a judgment about discrimination.

Some additions and omissions are small but recurrent. “Within me” specifies whose interior *dentro* denotes, whereas “some one” conveys the uncertainty of *non so chi* without retaining the narrator’s ‘I know not who’. The expletive *non* in *che non farebbe* has no English counterpart in “than he would do”. Two routine adjustments generally pass unmarked in the comparison: Okey supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the person in the verb ending, as in *Vidi*, “I saw”, and drops the definite article before possessives, as in *La mia sorella*, “My sister”.
