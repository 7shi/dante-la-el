# Purgatorio 6

## Lines 1–15 — The Dice Winner and the Throng

> 1 Quando si parte il giuoco della zara,  
> 2 colui che perde si riman dolente,  
> 3 ripetendo le volte, e tristo impara;  

When the game of dice breaks up, he who loses stays sorrowing, repeating the throws, and sadly learns:

- *si parte il giuoco* ‘breaks up the game’ → “the game of dice breaks up”: the subject, which follows the Italian pronominal verb, comes before the English phrasal verb.
- *della zara* ‘of zara’ → “of dice”: the name of the particular dice game is rendered by the general term.
- *si riman dolente* ‘remains sorrowful’ → “stays sorrowing”: the pronominal form has no counterpart in English, and a participle takes the place of the adjective.
- *le volte* ‘the turns’ → “the throws”: the noun is made specific to the game.
- *tristo* ‘sad’ → “sadly”: the adjective describing the loser becomes an adverb modifying the verb.

> 4 con l' altro se ne va tutta la gente:  

with the other all the folk go away:

- *se ne va tutta la gente* ‘goes away all the folk’ → “all the folk go away”: the collective subject is moved before the verb and takes plural rather than singular agreement.

> 5 qual va dinanzi, e qual di retro il prende,  
> 6 e qual da lato gli si reca a mente.  

one goes in front, another plucks him from behind, and another at his side recalls him to his mind.

- *qual … qual … qual* ‘one … one … one’ → “one” … “another” … “another”: English varies the repeated distributive pronoun.
- *e* ‘and’ → “another plucks him from behind”: the conjunction introducing the second person is dropped.
- *di retro il prende* ‘from behind him takes hold of’ → “plucks him from behind”: the verb is more specific; both the object and the phrase of position follow it.
- *da lato* ‘at the side’ → “at his side”: the possessive specifies whose side.
- *gli si reca a mente* ‘to him brings himself to mind’ → “recalls him to his mind”: the reflexive object referring to the petitioner becomes a nonreflexive “him”; the indirect object referring to the winner is expressed by the possessive in “his mind”.

> 7 Ei non s' arresta, e questo e quello intende;  

He halts not and attends to this one and to that:

- *s' arresta* ‘stops himself’ → “halts”: a simple intransitive verb replaces the pronominal form.
- *questo e quello intende* ‘this one and that one heeds’ → “attends to this one and to that”: the verb precedes its complements and takes a preposition, repeated before the second demonstrative.

> 8 a cui porge la man più non fa pressa;  

those to whom he stretches forth his hand press no more;

- *a cui* ‘to whom’ → “those to whom”: an explicit plural antecedent is supplied for the relative.
- *porge la man* ‘extends the hand’ → “he stretches forth his hand”: the simple verb becomes a verb with a particle, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *più non fa pressa* ‘no longer makes pressure’ → “press no more”: the verb and noun become a single verb, now plural to agree with “those”; the negative expression follows it.

> 9 e così dalla calca si difende.  

and so he saves him from the crowd.

- *dalla calca si difende* ‘from the crowd defends himself’ → “he saves him from the crowd”: the phrase with ‘from’ moves to the end, ‘defends’ becomes “saves”, and the object “him” has the force of the Italian reflexive.

> 10 Tal era io in quella turba spessa;  

Such was I in that dense throng,

- *turba spessa* ‘throng dense’ → “dense throng”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 11 volgendo a loro e qua e là la faccia,  
> 12 e promettendo, mi sciogliea da essa.  

turning my face to them, now here, now there, and by promising freed me from them.

- *volgendo a loro e qua e là la faccia* ‘turning to them both here and there the face’ → “turning my face to them, now here, now there”: the direct object moves next to the verb, the article becomes a possessive, and repeated “now” replaces the paired conjunctions.
- *promettendo* ‘promising’ → “by promising”: the preposition makes the gerund’s instrumental force explicit.
- *mi sciogliea da essa* ‘myself I was freeing from it’ → “freed me from them”: the imperfect is rendered by a simple past; the reflexive precedes the Italian verb but follows the English verb as “me”, and the singular pronoun for the throng becomes a plural referring to its members.

> 13 Quivi era l' Aretin, che dalle braccia  
> 14 fiere di Ghin di Tacco ebbe la morte,  

There was the Aretine who by the savage arms of Ghin di Tacco met his death;

- *dalle braccia fiere* ‘from the arms fierce’ → “by the savage arms”: the preposition expresses agency rather than source, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *ebbe la morte* ‘had the death’ → “met his death”: English changes the verb and substitutes a possessive for the article.

> 15 e l' altro che annegò correndo in caccia.  

and the other who was drowned as he ran in chase.

- *annegò* ‘drowned’ → “was drowned”: the intransitive Italian verb is rendered by a passive.
- *correndo* ‘running’ → “as he ran”: the gerund becomes a finite temporal clause.

## Lines 16–48 — Prayer and Heaven’s Decree

> 16 Quivi pregava con le mani sporte  
> 17 Federico Novello, e quel da Pisa  
> 18 che fe' parer lo buon Marzucco forte.  

There was praying with outstretched hands Federigo Novello, and he of Pisa who made the good Marzucco show fortitude.

- *pregava* ‘prayed’ → “was praying”: the progressive brings out the continuing action of the imperfect.
- *con le mani sporte* ‘with the hands stretched forth’ → “with outstretched hands”: the article drops and the participle precedes the noun.
- *quel da Pisa* ‘that one from Pisa’ → “he of Pisa”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative, and origin is expressed by “of”.
- *che fe' parer lo buon Marzucco forte* ‘who made the good Marzucco appear strong’ → “who made the good Marzucco show fortitude”: Marzucco comes before the infinitive rather than after it; appearing strong becomes showing a quality, with a noun in place of the adjective.

> 19 Vidi Cont' Orso; e l' anima divisa  
> 20 dal corpo suo per astio e per inveggia,  
> 21 come dicea, non per colpa commisa—  

I saw Count Orso, and the soul severed from its body through hatred and envy, so it said, and not for any sin committed—

- *divisa* ‘divided’ → “severed”: the participle more specifically denotes a cutting apart.
- *dal corpo suo* ‘from the body of its own’ → “from its body”: the possessive precedes the noun rather than following it.
- *per astio e per inveggia* ‘through hatred and through envy’ → “through hatred and envy”: the preposition is not repeated.
- *come dicea* ‘as it was saying’ → “so it said”: the comparison becomes a parenthetic assertion, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *non per colpa commisa* ‘not for fault committed’ → “and not for any sin committed”: the conjunction and “any” are supplied, and “sin” gives the more specifically moral sense of *colpa*.

> 22 Pier dalla Broccia dico: e qui provveggia,  
> 23 mentr' è di qua, la donna di Brabante,  
> 24 sì che però non sia di peggior greggia.  

Pierre de la Brosse I mean: and here let the Lady of Brabant take heed, while she is on earth, so that for this she be not of a worser herd.

- *dico* ‘I say’ → “I mean”: the verb expresses identification rather than the act of speaking.
- *provveggia, mentr' è di qua, la donna di Brabante* ‘let her take heed, while she is on this side, the Lady of Brabant’ → “let the Lady of Brabant take heed, while she is on earth”: English expresses the exhortative subjunctive with “let”, brings the delayed subject into that construction, and makes the place meant by ‘on this side’ explicit.
- *però non sia* ‘on that account she be not’ → “for this she be not”: the adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *di peggior greggia* ‘of worse flock’ → “of a worser herd”: the indefinite article is supplied, and “herd” replaces ‘flock’.

> 25 Come libero fui da tutte e quante  
> 26 quell' ombre, che pregar pur ch' altri preghi,  
> 27 sì che s' avacci il lor divenir sante,  

When I was free from all those shades whose one prayer was that others should pray, so that their way to blessedness be sped,

- *Come libero fui* ‘as soon as free I was’ → “When I was free”: the temporal link is less emphatic, and the adjective follows the verb.
- *tutte e quante quell' ombre* ‘all and every one of those shades’ → “all those shades”: one quantifier does the work of the emphatic pair.
- *che pregar pur* ‘who prayed only’ → “whose one prayer was”: the relative subject becomes a possessive, the verb becomes a noun with a copula, and ‘only’ is expressed by “one”.
- *ch' altri preghi* ‘that another pray’ → “that others should pray”: the indefinite singular becomes plural, and “should” expresses the subjunctive.
- *s' avacci il lor divenir sante* ‘be hastened their becoming holy’ → “their way to blessedness be sped”: the subject precedes the verb, and the nominal infinitive with its adjective becomes a figure of movement toward a destination.

> 28 io cominciai: “E' par che tu mi neghi,  
> 29 o luce mia, espresso in alcun testo,  
> 30 che decreto del cielo orazion pieghi;  

I began: “It seemeth that thou, O my Light, deniest expressly in a certain passage, that prayer may bend heaven’s decree;

- *che tu mi neghi, o luce mia* ‘that thou deniest to me, O light of mine’ → “that thou, O my Light, deniest”: the indirect object drops, the vocative moves between subject and verb, and the possessive precedes its noun.
- *espresso* ‘express’ → “expressly”: the Italian adjective used adverbially becomes an English adverb.
- *in alcun testo* ‘in some text’ → “in a certain passage”: the indefinite determiner becomes “a certain”, and “passage” specifies a portion of a text.
- *che decreto del cielo orazion pieghi* ‘that decree of heaven prayer bend’ → “that prayer may bend heaven’s decree”: the object moves from before the subject to after the verb, the possessive replaces the *di* phrase, and “may” expresses the subjunctive.

> 31 e questa gente prega pur di questo.  

and these people pray but for this.

- *questa gente prega* ‘this people prays’ → “these people pray”: the singular collective construction becomes plural.

> 32 Sarebbe dunque loro speme vana?  
> 33 o non m'è il detto tuo ben manifesto?”  

Can then their hope be vain? or are not thy words right clear to me?”

- *Sarebbe dunque loro speme vana* ‘would their hope, then, be vain’ → “Can then their hope be vain”: the conditional becomes a question of possibility with “Can”; “then” intervenes between the auxiliary and the subject.
- *non m'è il detto tuo ben manifesto* ‘is not to me the saying of thine quite manifest’ → “are not thy words right clear to me”: the singular saying becomes plural “words”, with a plural verb; the possessive precedes the noun, and the experiencer moves from before the verb to the end of the question.

> 34 Ed egli a me: “La mia scrittura è piana,  
> 35 e là speranza di costor non falla,  
> 36 se ben si guarda con la mente sana.  

And he to me: “My writing is plain and the hope of them is not deceived if well thou considerest with mind whole.

- *e là speranza di costor non falla* ‘and there the hope of these does not fail’ → “and the hope of them is not deceived”: “the” corresponds to a reading *la* rather than the printed *là* ‘there’; the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the active verb becomes a passive.
- *se ben si guarda* ‘if well one looks’ → “if well thou considerest”: the impersonal construction becomes a direct address, and looking is rendered as considering.
- *con la mente sana* ‘with the mind sound’ → “with mind whole”: the article drops, and “whole” renders ‘sound’.

> 37 Chè cima di giudizio non s' avvalla,  
> 38 perchè foco d' amor compia in un punto  
> 39 ciò che dee satisfar chi qui s' astalla;  

For the height of justice is not abased because fire of love fulfils in one moment the satisfaction which he owes who here is lodged:

- *cima di giudizio* ‘summit of judgment’ → “the height of justice”: the article is supplied; the summit becomes its height, and “justice” replaces ‘judgment’.
- *non s' avvalla* ‘does not lower itself’ → “is not abased”: the reflexive becomes a passive.
- *compia* ‘may fulfil’ → “fulfils”: the Italian subjunctive after *perchè* becomes indicative.
- *in un punto* ‘in one point’ → “in one moment”: the temporal sense is made explicit.
- *ciò che dee satisfar* ‘that which he must make good’ → “the satisfaction which he owes”: the act expressed by the infinitive becomes the noun “satisfaction”, while the verb of obligation becomes “owes”.
- *chi qui s' astalla* ‘he who here takes his lodging’ → “who here is lodged”: English puts “he” before “owes” and leaves the relative clause after it; the reflexive verb becomes a passive.

> 40 e là dov' io fermai cotesto punto,  
> 41 non si ammendava, per pregar, difetto,  
> 42 perchè il prego da Dio era disgiunto.  

and there where I affirmed that point, default could not be amended by prayer, because the prayer was severed from God.

- *fermai* ‘I fixed’ → “I affirmed”: the verb is rendered as an act of assertion.
- *non si ammendava, per pregar, difetto* ‘was not amended, by praying, a fault’ → “default could not be amended by prayer”: the delayed subject comes first, “could” makes inability explicit, and the phrase containing the infinitive moves to the end and takes a noun instead.
- *da Dio era disgiunto* ‘from God was disjoined’ → “was severed from God”: the prepositional phrase follows the participle rather than preceding the verb, and “severed” renders ‘disjoined’.

> 43 Veramente a così alto sospetto  
> 44 non ti fermar, se quella nol ti dice,  
> 45 che lume fia tra il vero e l' intelletto.  

But do not rest in so profound a doubt except she tell it thee, who shall be a light between truth and intellect.

- *Veramente* ‘truly’ → “But”: the adverb is rendered as an adversative conjunction.
- *a così alto sospetto non ti fermar* ‘at so high a doubt do not stop thyself’ → “do not rest in so profound a doubt”: the prohibition comes first; the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb with “in” rather than ‘at’, and the figure of height becomes one of depth.
- *se quella nol ti dice* ‘if that woman does not tell it to thee’ → “except she tell it thee”: the negative condition becomes an exception with a subjunctive; the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun and the objects follow the verb.
- *che lume fia* ‘who a light shall be’ → “who shall be a light”: the predicate noun follows the verb, and English supplies its article.
- *tra il vero e l' intelletto* ‘between the true and the intellect’ → “between truth and intellect”: the substantivized adjective becomes an abstract noun, and neither English noun has an article.

> 46 Non so se intendi: io dico di Beatrice;  

I know not if thou understand: I speak of Beatrice;

- *se intendi* ‘if thou understandest’ → “if thou understand”: the Italian indicative is rendered by an uninflected subjunctive.
- *io dico di Beatrice* ‘I say of Beatrice’ → “I speak of Beatrice”: the verb changes to the idiomatic English one for naming the person meant.

> 47 tu la vedrai di sopra, in su la vetta  
> 48 di questo monte, ridente e felice.”  

thou shalt see her above, on the summit of this mount, smiling and blessed.”

- *tu la vedrai* ‘thou her shalt see’ → “thou shalt see her”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *in su la vetta* ‘up on the summit’ → “on the summit”: the combined prepositions are reduced to one.
- *felice* ‘happy’ → “blessed”: the English adjective gives happiness the more specific sense of blessedness.

## Lines 49–81 — Sordello’s Embrace and Enslaved Italy

> 49 Ed io: “Signore, andiamo a maggior fretta;  

And I: “My Lord, go we with greater haste;

- *Signore* ‘Lord’ → “My Lord”: the possessive is supplied in the address.
- *andiamo a* ‘let us go at’ → “go we with”: the exhortation takes verb–subject order, and the preposition changes.

> 50 chè già non m' affatico come dianzi;  

for already I grow not weary as before,

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating conjunction replaces the subordinating one.
- *non m' affatico* ‘I do not tire myself’ → “I grow not weary”: the reflexive verb becomes a verb of becoming with an adjective, and the negative follows the verb.

> 51 e vedi omai che il poggio l' ombra getta.”  

and look, the hillside doth now a shadow cast.”

- *vedi omai che* ‘see now that’ → “look,” / “now”: the verb of seeing becomes an independent imperative, the clause is no longer introduced by ‘that’, and the adverb moves into the statement about the hillside.
- *il poggio l' ombra getta* ‘the hill the shadow casts’ → “the hillside doth now a shadow cast”: ‘hill’ becomes “hillside”, the shadow takes the indefinite article, and the auxiliary “doth” precedes the object while the main verb remains after it.

> 52 “Noi anderem con questo giorno innanzi,”  
> 53 rispose, “quanto più potremo omai;  

“We with this day will onward go,” answered he, “so far as yet we may;

- *Noi anderem con questo giorno innanzi* ‘we shall go with this day forward’ → “We with this day will onward go”: the phrase about the day comes before the future verb, and the adverb comes before the main verb rather than after it.
- *quanto più potremo omai* ‘as much more as we shall be able from now on’ → “so far as yet we may”: extent is expressed by “so far as”, without a separate comparative; the future of ability becomes “may”, and the final time adverb becomes “yet” before the subject.

> 54 ma il fatto è d' altra forma che non stanzi.  

but the fact is other than thou deemest.

- *d' altra forma* ‘of another form’ → “other”: the prepositional phrase is reduced to a predicate adjective.
- *che non stanzi* ‘than thou judgest’ → “than thou deemest”: the Italian comparative construction has an expletive negative with no English counterpart.

> 55 Prima che sii lassù, tornar vedrai  
> 56 colui che già si copre della costa,  
> 57 sì che i suoi raggi tu romper non fai.  

Ere thou art above, him shalt thou see return that now is being hidden by the slope, so that thou makest not his rays to break.

- *Prima che sii lassù* ‘before thou be up there’ → “Ere thou art above”: the Italian subjunctive becomes indicative, and the directional expression with ‘there’ becomes “above”.
- *tornar vedrai colui* ‘return thou shalt see him’ → “him shalt thou see return”: the object comes first and the infinitive last, with the subject between the future auxiliary and the main verb.
- *già si copre della costa* ‘already covers himself with the slope’ → “now is being hidden by the slope”: “now” replaces ‘already’, and the reflexive verb with a phrase of means becomes a progressive passive with the slope as agent.
- *i suoi raggi tu romper non fai* ‘his rays thou break not makest’ → “thou makest not his rays to break”: the subject and causative verb move before the object, the negative follows the finite verb, and the infinitive takes “to”.

> 58 Ma vedi là un' anima, che, posta  
> 59 sola soletta, verso noi riguarda;  

But see there a soul which, placed alone, solitary, looketh towards us;

- *sola soletta* ‘alone, quite alone’ → “alone, solitary”: the emphatic repetition with a diminutive form becomes a pair of synonyms.
- *verso noi riguarda* ‘towards us looks’ → “looketh towards us”: the verb moves before the phrase of direction.

> 60 quella ne insegnerà la via più tosta.”  

it will point out to us the quickest way.”

- *quella ne insegnerà* ‘that one to us will show’ → “it will point out to us”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, “point out” renders the simple verb, and the indirect object moves after it.
- *la via più tosta* ‘the way quickest’ → “the quickest way”: the superlative moves before the noun.

> 61 Venimmo a lei. O anima Lombarda,  
> 62 come ti stavi altera e disdegnosa,  
> 63 e nel mover degli occhi onesta e tarda!  

We came to it: O Lombard soul, how wast thou haughty and disdainful, and in the movement of thine eyes majestic and slow!

- *a lei* ‘to her’ → “to it”: the feminine pronoun referring to the soul becomes neuter.
- *anima Lombarda* ‘soul Lombard’ → “Lombard soul”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *come ti stavi* ‘how thou didst hold thyself’ → “how wast thou”: a reflexive verb of bearing becomes the simple copula, with the subject after it.
- *nel mover degli occhi* ‘in the moving of the eyes’ → “in the movement of thine eyes”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *onesta* ‘dignified’ → “majestic”: the English adjective gives a stronger sense of grandeur.

> 64 Ella non ci diceva alcuna cosa;  

Naught it said to us,

- *Ella non ci diceva alcuna cosa* ‘she was not saying to us anything’ → “Naught it said to us”: the negative and its object become a single negative pronoun placed first; “it” replaces the feminine subject, the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the indirect object follows the verb.

> 65 ma lasciavane gir, solo sguardando  
> 66 a guisa di leon quando si posa.  

but allowed us to go on, watching only after the fashion of a lion when he couches.

- *lasciavane gir* ‘let us go’ → “allowed us to go on”: the imperfect takes a simple past, ‘let’ with a bare infinitive becomes “allowed” with a to-infinitive, and “on” makes continuation explicit.
- *solo sguardando* ‘only watching’ → “watching only”: the adverb follows the participle.
- *a guisa di leon* ‘in the manner of lion’ → “after the fashion of a lion”: the idiom changes and the generic singular takes an indefinite article.
- *si posa* ‘sets himself down’ → “couches”: a single intransitive verb replaces the reflexive construction.

> 67 Pur Virgilio si trasse a lei, pregando  
> 68 che ne mostrasse la miglior salita;  

Yet did Virgil draw on towards it, praying that it would show to us the best ascent;

- *Virgilio si trasse a lei* ‘Virgil drew himself towards her’ → “did Virgil draw on towards it”: the auxiliary precedes the subject; “draw on” replaces the reflexive, and the feminine pronoun becomes neuter.
- *ne mostrasse* ‘to us it might show’ → “it would show to us”: the imperfect subjunctive after the verb of entreaty is rendered with “would”, and the indirect object follows the verb.

> 69 e quella non rispose al suo domando;  

and that spirit answered not his demand,

- *quella* ‘that one’ → “that spirit”: a noun is supplied for the demonstrative to qualify.
- *non rispose al suo domando* ‘did not answer to his question’ → “answered not his demand”: the negative follows the verb, the prepositional complement becomes a direct object, and ‘question’ is rendered by “demand”.

> 70 ma di nostro paese e della vita  
> 71 c' inchiese. E il dolce duca incominciava:  

but of our country and of our life did ask us. And the sweet Leader began:

- *della vita* ‘of the life’ → “of our life”: the possessive is repeated where Italian has only the article.
- *c' inchiese* ‘us asked’ → “did ask us”: the object follows the verb and the simple past is expressed with an auxiliary.
- *duca* ‘guide’ → “Leader”: the title is rendered by a term for one who leads.
- *incominciava* ‘was beginning’ → “began”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.

> 72 “Mantova,” . . . e l' ombra, tutta in sè romita,  
> 73 surse ver lui del loco ove pria stava,  

“Mantua,” . . . and the shade, all rapt in self, leapt towards him from the place where first it was,

- *in sè romita* ‘within herself secluded’ → “rapt in self”: inward seclusion is rendered as absorption; the adjective moves before its complement, and the reflexive becomes the noun “self”.
- *surse* ‘rose’ → “leapt”: the English verb gives the movement greater force.
- *ove pria stava* ‘where she previously stood’ → “where first it was”: “first” renders the adverb of prior time, and simple being replaces the verb of remaining in place.

> 74 dicendo: “O Mantovano, io son Sordello  
> 75 della tua terra.” E l' un l' altro abbracciava.  

saying: “O Mantuan, I am Sordello of thy city.” And one embraced the other.

- *terra* ‘land’ → “city”: the narrower local sense of the noun is chosen.
- *l' un l' altro abbracciava* ‘the one the other was embracing’ → “one embraced the other”: the first article drops out, the object moves after the verb, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.

> 76 Ahi serva Italia, di dolore ostello,  
> 77 nave senza nocchiero in gran tempesta,  
> 78 non donna di provincie, ma bordello!  

Ah Italy, thou slave, hostel of woe, vessel without pilot in a mighty storm, no mistress of provinces, but a brothel!

- *serva Italia* ‘enslaved Italy’ → “Italy, thou slave”: the epithet preceding the name becomes a second address after it, with a pronoun supplied.
- *di dolore ostello* ‘of sorrow a lodging’ → “hostel of woe”: the noun comes before its complement, and ‘sorrow’ is rendered by “woe”.
- *nave* ‘ship’ → “vessel”: a broader nautical noun is used.
- *in gran tempesta* ‘in great storm’ → “in a mighty storm”: the indefinite article is supplied, and “mighty” renders the adjective of magnitude.
- *non donna* ‘not lady’ → “no mistress”: the negative becomes a determiner, and the noun is rendered in its sense of authority.
- *bordello* ‘brothel’ → “a brothel”: the predicate noun takes an indefinite article.

> 79 Quell' anima gentil fu così presta,  
> 80 sol per lo dolce suon della sua terra,  
> 81 di fare al cittadin suo quivi festa;  

That gentle spirit was thus quick, merely at the sweet name of his city, to give greeting there to his fellow-citizen;

- *anima gentil* ‘soul gentle’ → “gentle spirit”: “spirit” replaces ‘soul’, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *così* ‘so’ → “thus”: degree is rendered as manner.
- *sol per lo dolce suon* ‘only for the sweet sound’ → “merely at the sweet name”: the cause is introduced by “at”, and “name” identifies what was heard rather than rendering ‘sound’.
- *della sua terra* ‘of his land’ → “of his city”: the narrower local sense is again chosen.
- *di fare al cittadin suo quivi festa* ‘to make to the citizen of his there a welcome’ → “to give greeting there to his fellow-citizen”: the separated parts of the idiom for welcoming someone become the adjacent verb and noun “give greeting”; the adverb now precedes the recipient, the possessive comes before the noun, and “fellow-” makes shared citizenship explicit.

## Lines 82–114 — Italy at War and Rome Alone

> 82 ed ora in te non stanno senza guerra  
> 83 li vivi tuoi, e l' un l' altro si rode  
> 84 di quei che un muro ed una fossa serra.  

and now in thee thy living abide not without war, and one doth rend the other of those that one wall and one foss shuts in.

- *non stanno senza guerra li vivi tuoi* ‘abide not without war the living of thine’ → “thy living abide not without war”: the subject moves before the verb, and the possessive before the noun.
- *l' un l' altro si rode* ‘the one the other gnaws’ → “one doth rend the other”: the first article drops out, the object moves after the verb, and gnawing becomes rending, with the auxiliary “doth”.

> 85 Cerca, misera, intorno dalle prode  
> 86 le tue marine, e poi ti guarda in seno,  
> 87 se alcuna parte in te di pace gode.  

Search, wretched one, around thy seacoasts by the shores, and then gaze in thy bosom, if any part of thee enjoy peace.

- *misera* ‘wretched’ → “wretched one”: English supplies a noun for the adjective used in direct address.
- *intorno dalle prode le tue marine* ‘round about, from the shores, thy seacoasts’ → “around thy seacoasts by the shores”: the object comes directly after “around”, and “by” replaces ‘from’.
- *ti guarda in seno* ‘look for thyself in the bosom’ → “gaze in thy bosom”: the reflexive becomes a possessive on the noun, and ‘look’ is heightened to “gaze”.
- *alcuna parte in te* ‘any part in thee’ → “any part of thee”: a partitive phrase replaces the phrase of place.
- *di pace gode* ‘of peace enjoys’ → “enjoy peace”: the verb comes first and takes a direct object; English uses a subjunctive form for the Italian indicative.

> 88 Che val, perchè ti racconciasse il freno  
> 89 Giustiniano, se la sella è vota?  
> 90 Senz' esso fora la vergogna meno.  

What avails it that Justinian should refit thy bridle if the saddle is empty? But for that the shame were less.

- *Che val* ‘what avails’ → “What avails it”: “it” anticipates the following clause.
- *perchè ti racconciasse il freno Giustiniano* ‘because for thee should refit the bridle Justinian’ → “that Justinian should refit thy bridle”: the clause becomes the subject of ‘avails’, with “that” rather than a causal connective; Justinian moves before the verb, and the dative becomes a possessive.
- *Senz' esso* ‘without it’ → “But for that”: a demonstrative and an idiom of exception replace the personal pronoun and the preposition of absence.
- *fora la vergogna meno* ‘would be the shame less’ → “the shame were less”: the subject moves first, and a hypothetical “were” renders the conditional.

> 91 Ahi gente, che dovresti esser devota,  
> 92 e lasciar seder Cesar in la sella,  
> 93 se bene intendi ciò che Dio ti nota!  

Ah people, that shouldst be obedient, and let Cæsar sit in the saddle, if well thou understandest what God writeth to thee!

- *devota* ‘devout’ → “obedient”: Okey chooses the sense of submission rather than that of piety.
- *lasciar seder Cesar* ‘let sit Cæsar’ → “let Cæsar sit”: the subject of the infinitive comes before it in English.
- *ciò che* ‘that which’ → “what”: a single relative does the work of demonstrative and relative together.
- *Dio ti nota* ‘God for thee sets down’ → “God writeth to thee”: the verb of marking or setting down becomes “writeth”, and the dative follows it as a prepositional phrase.

> 94 guarda com' esta fiera è fatta fella,  
> 95 per non esser corretta dagli sproni,  
> 96 poi che ponesti mano alla predella.  

see how this beast hath grown vicious, for not being corrected by the spurs, since thou hast put thy hand to the bridle.

- *guarda* ‘look’ → “see”: the imperative names the perception rather than the act of looking.
- *è fatta fella* ‘is become fierce’ → “hath grown vicious”: “hath grown” renders the compound expressing a change of state, and “vicious” gives the adjective the sense of an animal’s bad temper.
- *ponesti mano* ‘thou didst put hand’ → “thou hast put thy hand”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, and the hand acquires an explicit possessive.
- *alla predella* ‘to the rein’ → “to the bridle”: Okey names the whole bridle rather than the part held in the hand.

> 97 O Alberto Tedesco, che abbandoni  
> 98 costei ch' è fatta indomita e selvaggia,  
> 99 e dovresti inforcar li suoi arcioni,  

O German Albert, that dost forsake her who is become wanton and savage, and that oughtest to bestride her saddle-bow,

- *Alberto Tedesco* ‘Albert German’ → “German Albert”: the adjective precedes the name.
- *abbandoni costei* ‘abandon this one’ → “dost forsake her”: a personal pronoun replaces the feminine demonstrative, and the verb is rendered with “dost”.
- *indomita* ‘untamed’ → “wanton”: lack of taming becomes unruliness.
- *e dovresti* ‘and oughtest’ → “and that oughtest”: the relative is repeated in English.
- *inforcar li suoi arcioni* ‘to straddle her saddle-bows’ → “to bestride her saddle-bow”: the plural object becomes singular.

> 100 giusto giudizio dalle stelle caggia  
> 101 sopra il tuo sangue, e sia nuovo ed aperto,  
> 102 tal che il tuo successor temenza n' aggia:  

may just judgment fall from the stars upon thy blood, and be it strange and manifest, so that thy successor may have fear thereof:

- *giusto giudizio dalle stelle caggia* ‘just judgment from the stars fall’ → “may just judgment fall from the stars”: “may” marks the wish expressed by the subjunctive, and the phrase of origin moves after the verb.
- *e sia nuovo ed aperto* ‘and be it new and open’ → “and be it strange and manifest”: novelty is taken as strangeness, and openness as plainness to view.
- *tal che* ‘such that’ → “so that”: an adverb replaces the adjective introducing the result.
- *temenza n' aggia* ‘fear thereof have’ → “may have fear thereof”: the verb moves before its objects, and “may” expresses the subjunctive.

> 103 chè avete tu e il tuo padre sofferto,  
> 104 per cupidigia di costà distretti,  
> 105 che il giardin dell' imperio sia diserto.  

for thou and thy father, held back yonder by covetousness, have suffered that the garden of the empire be laid waste.

- *chè avete tu e il tuo padre sofferto* ‘for have thou and thy father suffered’ → “for thou and thy father” … “have suffered”: the subject no longer separates auxiliary and participle; the English also delays the verb until after the participial phrase of line 104.
- *per cupidigia di costà distretti* ‘by covetousness yonder constrained’ → “held back yonder by covetousness”: the participle moves first and is rendered by a verb with “back”; the phrase of cause moves last.
- *sia diserto* ‘be deserted’ → “be laid waste”: Okey takes the participle in the sense of devastation rather than abandonment.

> 106 Vieni a veder Montecchi e Cappelletti,  
> 107 Monaldi e Filippeschi, uom senza cura:  
> 108 color già tristi, e costor con sospetti.  

Come and see Montagues and Capulets, Monaldi and Filippeschi, thou man without care: those already sad, and these in dread.

- *Vieni a veder* ‘come to see’ → “Come and see”: a coordinated imperative replaces the infinitive.
- *uom senza cura* ‘man without care’ → “thou man without care”: “thou” is added to the address.
- *con sospetti* ‘with apprehensions’ → “in dread”: a singular state replaces the plural noun, and the preposition changes.

> 109 Vien crudel, vieni, e vedi la pressura  
> 110 de' tuoi gentili, e cura lor magagne,  
> 111 e vedrai Santafior com' è sicura.  

Come, cruel one, come, and see the oppression of thy nobles and tend their sores, and thou shalt see Santafior how secure it is.

- *crudel* ‘cruel’ → “cruel one”: “one” supports the adjective used in address.
- *cura lor magagne* ‘cure their blemishes’ → “tend their sores”: Okey renders the verb as care rather than cure and makes the injuries specifically “sores”.
- *com' è sicura* ‘how it is secure’ → “how secure it is”: the adjective moves up beside “how”.

> 112 Vieni a veder la tua Roma che piagne,  
> 113 vedova e sola, e dì e notte chiama:  
> 114 “Cesare mio, perchè non m' accompagne?”  

Come and see thy Rome that weepeth widowed and alone, and day and night doth cry: “Cæsar mine, wherefore dost thou not companion me?”

- *Vieni a veder* ‘come to see’ → “Come and see”: the infinitive becomes a second imperative.
- *vedova* ‘a widow’ → “widowed”: the English describes the condition by a participial adjective.
- *e dì e notte chiama* ‘both day and night calls’ → “and day and night doth cry”: the first conjunction is attached to the verb clause rather than paired with the second before the two time words; “doth cry” renders the simple verb.
- *perchè non m' accompagne* ‘wherefore not me dost accompany’ → “wherefore dost thou not companion me”: English builds the question with “dost”, puts the object after the verb, and uses “companion” as the verb.

## Lines 115–147 — Italy’s Tyrants and Florence’s Wisdom

> 115 Vieni a veder la gente quanto s' ama;  

Come and see how thy people love one another;

- *Vieni a veder* ‘come to see’ → “Come and see”: the infinitive after the verb of motion becomes a second imperative joined by “and”.
- *la gente quanto s' ama* ‘the people, how much it loves itself’ → “how thy people love one another”: “how” comes before the subject, with no separate word for ‘much’; “thy” replaces the article. The collective singular takes a plural verb in English, and the reflexive is made explicitly reciprocal.

> 116 e se nulla di noi pietà ti move,  
> 117 a vergognar ti vien della tua fama.  

and if no pity for us move thee, come and shame thee for thy fame.

- *nulla di noi pietà* ‘any for us pity’ → “no pity for us”: “for us” follows the noun instead of separating it from its determiner. Okey takes *nulla* in the negative sense ‘no’ rather than in the sense ‘any’ possible after *se*.
- *ti move* ‘thee moves’ → “move thee”: the object follows the verb, and the Italian indicative becomes an English conditional-clause subjunctive.
- *a vergognar ti vien* ‘to shame thyself come’ → “come and shame thee”: the imperative comes first, the infinitive becomes a coordinated imperative, and the reflexive referring to the person addressed follows “shame”.
- *della tua fama* ‘of thy fame’ → “for thy fame”: “for” presents the fame as the grounds for shame.

> 118 E se licito m' è, o sommo Giove,  
> 119 che fosti in terra per noi crucifisso,  
> 120 son li giusti occhi tuoi rivolti altrove;  

And if it be permitted me, O highest Jove, who on earth for us wast crucified, are thy just eyes turned elsewhither;

- *licito m' è* ‘permissible to me it is’ → “it be permitted me”: the impersonal construction is reordered, the adjective becomes a passive participle, and “be” replaces the indicative.
- *che fosti in terra per noi crucifisso* ‘who wast on earth for us crucified’ → “who on earth for us wast crucified”: the phrases of place and beneficiary precede the whole passive verb instead of dividing it.

> 121 o è preparazion, che nell' abisso  
> 122 del tuo consiglio, fai, per alcun bene  
> 123 in tutto dall' accorger nostro scisso?  

or is it preparation which thou art making in the depths of thy counsel, for some good end wholly cut off from our vision?

- *è preparazion* ‘is there preparation’ → “is it preparation”: the English gives the question an identifying rather than an existential construction.
- *che nell' abisso del tuo consiglio, fai* ‘which in the abyss of thy counsel thou makest’ → “which thou art making in the depths of thy counsel”: the verb moves forward and becomes progressive; the singular ‘abyss’ becomes the plural “depths”.
- *per alcun bene* ‘for some good’ → “for some good end”: “end” is supplied, making ‘good’ adjectival rather than substantival.
- *in tutto dall' accorger nostro scisso* ‘entirely from our perceiving severed’ → “wholly cut off from our vision”: the participle precedes its complement; the substantivized infinitive becomes the noun “vision”, and its possessive moves before it.

> 124 Chè le città d' Italia tutte piene  
> 125 son di tiranni, ed un Marcel diventa  
> 126 ogni villan che parteggiando viene.  

For the cities of Italy are all full of tyrants, and every clown that comes to play the partizan becomes a Marcellus.

- *tutte piene son* ‘all full are’ → “are all full”: the copula moves before the predicate.
- *ed un Marcel diventa ogni villan* ‘and a Marcellus becomes every clown’ → “and every clown that comes to play the partizan becomes a Marcellus”: the Italian order is predicate, verb, subject; English puts the subject first, keeps its relative clause with it, and puts the predicate last.
- *che parteggiando viene* ‘that taking sides comes’ → “that comes to play the partizan”: the gerund expressing an accompanying action becomes an infinitive after “comes”, suggestive of purpose; the single verb for taking sides becomes a phrase naming a role.

> 127 Fiorenza mia, ben puoi esser contenta  
> 128 di questa digression che non ti tocca,  
> 129 mercè del popol tuo che s' argomenta.  

O my Florence, thou indeed mayst rejoice at this digression which touches thee not, thanks to thy people that reasons so well.

- *Fiorenza mia* ‘Florence mine’ → “O my Florence”: the vocative particle is supplied and the possessive precedes the name.
- *ben puoi esser contenta di* ‘well mayst thou be content with’ → “thou indeed mayst rejoice at”: “indeed” gives *ben* emphatic force; the copula and adjective become an active verb with a different preposition.
- *che non ti tocca* ‘which not thee touches’ → “which touches thee not”: verb, object, and negative are reordered.
- *che s' argomenta* ‘that exerts itself’ → “that reasons so well”: Okey takes the reflexive verb in the sense of exercising one’s reasoning powers, drops the reflexive form, and supplies the evaluation “so well”.

> 130 Molti han giustizia in cor, ma tardi scocca,  
> 131 per non venir senza consiglio all' arco;  
> 132 ma il popol tuo l' ha in sommo della bocca.  

Many have justice in their hearts, but slowly it is let fly, for it comes not without counsel to the bow; but thy people hath it ever on its lips.

- *in cor* ‘in heart’ → “in their hearts”: the distributive singular becomes a plural with an explicit possessive.
- *tardi scocca* ‘late it shoots forth’ → “slowly it is let fly”: lateness becomes slowness, and the intransitive verb becomes passive.
- *per non venir* ‘so as not to come’ → “for it comes not”: a negative infinitive of purpose becomes a finite clause giving a reason.
- *l' ha in sommo della bocca* ‘it hath at the tip of the mouth’ → “hath it ever on its lips”: the object follows the verb; the phrase specifying a part of the mouth becomes “on its lips”, with a possessive and a plural noun. “ever” is supplied.

> 133 Molti rifiutan lo comune incarco;  

Many refuse the public burdens;

- *lo comune incarco* ‘the public burden’ → “the public burdens”: the singular becomes plural.

> 134 ma il popol tuo sollecito risponde  
> 135 senza chiamare, e grida: “Io mi sobbarco.”  

but thy people answers eagerly without call, and cries out: “I bend me to the charge.”

- *sollecito risponde* ‘eager it answers’ → “answers eagerly”: an adjective describing the subject becomes an adverb following the verb.
- *senza chiamare* ‘without calling’ → “without call”: the active infinitive becomes a noun, allowing the call to be understood as one that would be addressed to the people.
- *Io mi sobbarco* ‘I shoulder it’ → “I bend me to the charge”: the reflexive verb for taking a burden upon oneself is expanded into a verb of posture, a reflexive object after it, and an explicit complement for the burden.

> 136 Or ti fa lieta, chè tu hai ben onde:  
> 137 tu ricca, tu con pace, tu con senno.  

Now make thee glad, for thou hast good reason: thou rich, thou at peace, thou so wise.

- *ti fa lieta* ‘thyself make glad’ → “make thee glad”: the reflexive object follows the imperative.
- *tu hai ben onde* ‘thou hast indeed wherewith’ → “thou hast good reason”: the emphatic adverb and the relative adverb indicating grounds are recast as adjective and noun.
- *tu con pace* ‘thou with peace’ → “thou at peace”: the preposition changes to the English idiom.
- *tu con senno* ‘thou with good sense’ → “thou so wise”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adjective, and “so” is supplied.

> 138 S' io dico 'l ver, l' effetto nol nasconde.  

If I speak sooth, the facts do not conceal it.

- *dico 'l ver* ‘I say the true’ → “I speak sooth”: the substantivized adjective with its article becomes the articleless noun “sooth” in an English idiom.
- *l' effetto* ‘the result’ → “the facts”: a singular outcome becomes plural facts.
- *nol nasconde* ‘not it conceals’ → “do not conceal it”: negation takes an auxiliary, and the object follows the verb.

> 139 Atene e Lacedemone, che fenno  
> 140 l' antiche leggi e furon sì civili,  
> 141 fecero al viver bene un picciol cenno  

Athens and Lacedemon, that framed the laws of old and were so grown in civil arts, gave a mere hint at well living

- *fenno l' antiche leggi* ‘made the ancient laws’ → “framed the laws of old”: “framed” specifies legislative activity, and a postposed phrase replaces the attributive adjective.
- *furon sì civili* ‘were so civil’ → “were so grown in civil arts”: the adjective is expanded into a phrase expressing development in a field of activity.
- *fecero al viver bene un picciol cenno* ‘made at living well a small sign’ → “gave a mere hint at well living”: “gave” replaces ‘made’, and its direct object precedes the phrase specifying what the hint concerns. Smallness becomes limitation in “mere”, ‘sign’ is narrowed to “hint”, and “well” moves before “living”.

> 142 verso di te, che fai tanto sottili  
> 143 provvedimenti, che a mezzo novembre  
> 144 non giunge quel che tu d' ottobre fili.  

beside thee, who dost make such subtle provision, that to mid-November reaches not what thou in October spinnest.

- *verso di te* ‘in relation to thee’ → “beside thee”: a single comparative preposition replaces the Italian phrase.
- *fai tanto sottili provvedimenti* ‘makest so subtle provisions’ → “dost make such subtle provision”: the simple verb takes an auxiliary, “such” qualifies the noun phrase rather than ‘so’ qualifying the adjective, and plural measures become collective singular “provision”.
- *non giunge* ‘not reaches’ → “reaches not”: the negative follows the verb.
- *quel che tu* ‘that which thou’ → “what thou”: a fused relative replaces demonstrative and relative pronoun.
- *d' ottobre* ‘of October’ → “in October”: the Italian temporal use of *di* is rendered by “in”.

> 145 Quante volte del tempo che rimembre,  
> 146 legge, moneta, offizio e costume  
> 147 hai tu mutato, e rinnovato membre!  

How often in the time which thou rememberest, laws, coinage, offices, and customs hast thou changed, and renewed thy members!

- *Quante volte* ‘how many times’ → “How often”: an interrogative adjective and plural noun become an adverb of frequency.
- *del tempo* ‘of the time’ → “in the time”: the period is expressed with a preposition of time within which.
- *legge, moneta, offizio e costume* ‘law, coin, office and custom’ → “laws, coinage, offices, and customs”: three singulars become plurals, while “coinage” gives *moneta* collective force.
- *e rinnovato membre* ‘and renewed members’ → “and renewed thy members”: the possessive is supplied.

## Lines 148–151 — A Sick Woman Turning for Rest

> 148 E se ben ti ricordi e vedi lume,  

And if thou well bethink thee, and see clear,

- *ti ricordi* ‘thyself thou rememberest’ → “thou well bethink thee”: the reflexive verb is rendered by ‘bethink oneself’, and the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.
- *vedi lume* ‘seest light’ → “see clear”: the noun naming what is seen becomes a word describing how one sees.

> 149 vedrai te simigliante a quella inferma,  

thou shalt behold thee like unto that sick one,

- *vedrai* ‘thou wilt see’ → “thou shalt behold”: the simple future is rendered with “shalt”, and ‘see’ becomes “behold”.
- *quella inferma* ‘that sick woman’ → “that sick one”: the adjective used as a noun is given the supporting “one”, which does not itself mark the feminine.

> 150 che non può trovar posa in su le piume,  

who can find no rest upon the down,

- *non può trovar posa* ‘cannot find rest’ → “can find no rest”: the negative is transferred from the verb to the noun.
- *in su* ‘on top of’ → “upon”: the two prepositions are rendered by one.
- *le piume* ‘the feathers’ → “the down”: a plural noun becomes a collective singular.

> 151 ma con dar volta suo dolore scherma.  

but by turning about shuns her pain.

- *con dar volta* ‘with giving a turn’ → “by turning about”: the preposition and infinitive expressing means become “by” with a gerund; the turn named by the noun is expressed by the verb.
- *suo dolore scherma* ‘her pain she wards off’ → “shuns her pain”: the verb moves before its object, and warding off is rendered as avoidance.

## Summary — Close in Image, Freer in Syntax

Okey often changes the order of a phrase without changing its terms. In *se ne va tutta la gente*, “all the folk” comes before “go away” rather than after it; *turba spessa* becomes “dense throng”, with the adjective first. Yet he does not consistently smooth away inversion: in “Pierre de la Brosse I mean” he keeps the object before the verb, as in *Pier dalla Broccia dico*. He also stays close to Dante’s phrasing where repetition gives a passage its shape, preserving the recurrent *Vieni a veder* as “Come and see”.

Changes of construction often do more than changes of vocabulary. The adjective *tristo* becomes the adverb “sadly”; the gerund *correndo* becomes the finite clause “as he ran”, whereas *promettendo* becomes “by promising”, making its instrumental force explicit. The pronominal *non s’ avvalla* is rendered by the passive “is not abased”. Elsewhere Okey keeps both the construction and the image: *nave senza nocchiero* remains a noun followed by a phrase of privation, “vessel without pilot”.

Okey sometimes generalizes, rendering the particular game *zara* by “dice”, and sometimes specifies, rendering *terra* by “city” or *le volte* by “the throws”. He drops the indirect object *mi* in *tu mi neghi*, but supplies “fellow-” in “his fellow-citizen” to make the shared citizenship explicit. Two more routine changes run throughout the canto: English supplies subjects already indicated by Italian verb endings, as in *Vidi* and “I saw”, and omits definite articles before possessives, as in *La mia scrittura* and “My writing”.
