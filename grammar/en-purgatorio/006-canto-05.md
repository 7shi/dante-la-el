# Purgatorio 5

## Lines 1–21 — Follow Me and Let the People Talk

> 1 Io era già da quell' ombre partito,  
> 2 e seguitava l' orme del mio duca,  

I was already parted from those shades, and was following my leader’s footsteps,

- *Io era già da quell' ombre partito* ‘I had already from those shades departed’ → “I was already parted from those shades”: the Italian pluperfect of departure becomes an expression of the resulting state; the phrase beginning “from” follows the participle.
- *seguitava* ‘followed’ → “was following”: the English progressive brings out the continuing action of the imperfect.
- *l' orme del mio duca* ‘the footsteps of my leader’ → “my leader’s footsteps”: the possessive precedes the noun in place of the following *di*-phrase.

> 3 quando di retro a me drizzando il dito,  
> 4 una gridò: “Ve' che non par che luca  
> 5 lo raggio da sinistra a quel di sotto,  
> 6 e come vivo par che si conduca.”  

when behind me, pointing his finger, one cried: “See, it seemeth not that the light shines on the left of him below, and he appears to demean himself like one alive.”

- *drizzando il dito* ‘directing the finger’ → “pointing his finger”: English uses the specific verb for the gesture and a possessive in place of the definite article.
- *una* ‘one [shade]’ → “one”: the English pronoun does not carry the feminine agreement of the Italian.
- *Ve' che* ‘see that’ → “See”: the clause introduced by *che* follows as an independent statement.
- *non par che luca lo raggio* ‘it does not appear that shines the ray’ → “it seemeth not that the light shines”: the negative follows the English verb; “light” replaces ‘ray’ and precedes its verb. The Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *da sinistra a quel di sotto* ‘from the left to that one below’ → “on the left of him below”: “on” expresses position rather than direction, and the Italian dative becomes an “of” phrase dependent on “the left.”
- *come vivo par che si conduca* ‘like one alive it appears that he conducts himself’ → “he appears to demean himself like one alive”: the subject of the Italian subjunctive becomes the subject of “appears,” the finite clause becomes an infinitive, and the comparison moves to the end.

> 7 Gli occhi rivolsi al suon di questo motto,  
> 8 e vidile guardar per maraviglia  
> 9 pur me, pur me, e il lume ch' era rotto.  

Mine eyes I turned at sound of these words, and saw them gazing in astonishment at me alone, me alone, and at the light that was broken.

- *Gli occhi* ‘the eyes’ → “Mine eyes”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *al suon* ‘at the sound’ → “at sound”: the article is omitted.
- *di questo motto* ‘of this utterance’ → “of these words”: the singular expression and its demonstrative become plural.
- *vidile guardar* ‘I saw-them gaze’ → “saw them gazing”: the object attached to the finite verb becomes a separate pronoun, the feminine gender is unmarked, and the infinitive after the verb of perception becomes a participle.
- *per maraviglia* ‘from wonder’ → “in astonishment”: a phrase of cause becomes one expressing the gazers’ state.
- *pur me, pur me, e il lume* ‘only me, only me, and the light’ → “at me alone, me alone, and at the light”: “alone” follows each pronoun, and “gazing” takes “at” where the Italian has direct objects.

> 10 “Perchè l' animo tuo tanto s' impiglia,”  
> 11 disse il maestro, “che l' andare allenti?  

“Why is thy mind so entangled,” said the Master, “that thou slackenest thy pace?

- *l' animo tuo tanto s' impiglia* ‘thy mind so entangles itself’ → “is thy mind so entangled”: the possessive moves from after the noun to before it, and the reflexive becomes a construction with “is” and a participle, with “is” preceding the subject in the question.
- *l' andare allenti* ‘the going thou slackenest’ → “thou slackenest thy pace”: the nominal infinitive becomes the noun “pace,” with a possessive in place of the article; the object follows the verb.

> 12 che ti fa ciò che quivi si pispiglia?  

what matters it to thee what they whisper here?

- *che ti fa* ‘what does it do to thee’ → “what matters it to thee”: the idiom with ‘do’ is rendered by “matters,” and the indirect object follows the verb.
- *ciò che quivi si pispiglia* ‘that which here is whispered’ → “what they whisper here”: “what” combines antecedent and relative; the impersonal construction becomes active with an indefinite “they,” and the adverb moves to the end.

> 13 Vien retro a me, e lascia dir le genti;  

Follow me and let the people talk;

- *Vien retro a me* ‘come behind me’ → “Follow me”: a single transitive verb replaces the verb of motion and its prepositional phrase.
- *lascia dir le genti* ‘let speak the people’ → “let the people talk”: the subject of the infinitive precedes it in English, and *dir* is rendered in its intransitive sense.

> 14 sta come torre ferma, che non crolla  
> 15 giammai la cima per soffiar de' venti:  

stand thou as a firm tower which never shakes its summit for blast of winds:

- *sta come torre ferma* ‘stand like tower firm’ → “stand thou as a firm tower”: English supplies an article in the comparison and places the adjective before the noun.
- *non crolla giammai la cima* ‘does not shake ever the summit’ → “never shakes its summit”: “never” gathers the two parts of the negative before the verb, and a possessive replaces the article.
- *per soffiar de' venti* ‘for blowing of the winds’ → “for blast of winds”: the nominal infinitive becomes the noun “blast,” and the article before “winds” is omitted.

> 16 chè sempre l' uomo, in cui pensier rampolla  
> 17 sopra pensier, da sè dilunga il segno,  
> 18 perchè la foga l' un dell' altro insolla.”  

for ever the man in whom thought wells up on thought, sets back his mark, because the one saps the force of the other.”

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a coordinating conjunction replaces the subordinating one.
- *da sè dilunga il segno* ‘from himself puts at a distance the mark’ → “sets back his mark”: “back” replaces the explicit distance from oneself, and the mark is associated with the man by a possessive rather than named with the definite article.
- *la foga l' un dell' altro insolla* ‘the impetus the one of the other weakens’ → “the one saps the force of the other”: English puts the subject first, follows it with the verb, and brings the object and its dependent “of” phrase together; “force” renders the idea of impetus, and “saps” that of weakening.

> 19 Che poteva io ridir, se non: “Io vegno?”  

What could I answer, save: “I come”?

- *ridir* ‘say in return’ → “answer”: the sense of speaking in reply is expressed by a single verb without a counterpart to the prefix.
- *se non* ‘if not’ → “save”: a single word of exception replaces the negative conditional phrase.

> 20 Dissilo, alquanto del color consperso  
> 21 che fa l' uom di perdon tal volta degno.  

This I said, suffused somewhat with that colour which ofttimes makes a man worthy of pardon.

- *Dissilo* ‘I said it’ → “This I said”: the attached object pronoun becomes a demonstrative placed first.
- *alquanto del color consperso* ‘somewhat with the colour besprinkled’ → “suffused somewhat with that colour”: the participle moves before its modifiers; “suffused” replaces the image of sprinkling, and “that” replaces the article.
- *che fa l' uom di perdon tal volta degno* ‘which makes the man of pardon sometimes worthy’ → “which ofttimes makes a man worthy of pardon”: the generic definite article becomes indefinite; the frequency phrase moves before the verb and becomes “ofttimes” rather than ‘sometimes’; “of pardon” follows the adjective it completes.

## Lines 22–54 — The Penitents Entreat the Living Pilgrim

> 22 E intanto per la costa di traverso  
> 23 venivan genti innanzi a noi un poco,  
> 24 cantando *Miserere* a verso a verso.  

And meanwhile across the mountain slope came people a little in front of us, chanting the *Miserere* verse by verse alterne.

- *per la costa di traverso* ‘along the slope crosswise’ → “across the mountain slope”: the adverbial phrase becomes the preposition “across”; “mountain” is supplied.
- *venivan* ‘were coming’ → “came”: the imperfect is rendered by a simple past.
- *innanzi a noi un poco* ‘in front of us a little’ → “a little in front of us”: the expression of distance comes first.
- *cantando Miserere* ‘singing Miserere’ → “chanting the *Miserere*”: the verb is more specific, and the article is supplied.
- *a verso a verso* ‘verse by verse’ → “verse by verse alterne”: “alterne” makes the alternation explicit.

> 25 Quando s' accorser ch' io non dava loco,  
> 26 per lo mio corpo, al trapassar de' raggi,  
> 27 mutar lor canto in un ‘oh’ lungo e roco;  

When they perceived that I gave no place, because of my body, to the passage of the rays, they changed their chant to an Oh! long and hoarse;

- *s' accorser* ‘became aware’ → “they perceived”: the pronominal verb becomes a simple transitive verb.
- *non dava loco* ‘did not give place’ → “gave no place”: the negative modifies the noun rather than the verb; the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *per lo mio corpo* ‘on account of my body’ → “because of my body”: the causal sense of the preposition is given by a phrase.
- *al trapassar de' raggi* ‘to the passing of the rays’ → “to the passage of the rays”: a noun replaces the substantivized infinitive.
- *lor canto in* ‘their song into’ → “their chant to”: the noun follows the earlier choice of verb, and the change of state is expressed with “to”.

> 28 e due di loro in forma di messaggi  
> 29 corsero incontro a noi, e domandarne:  
> 30 “Di vostra condizion fatene saggi.”  

and two of them in the guise of messengers ran to meet us, and asked of us: “Make us to know of your condition.”

- *in forma di messaggi* ‘in form of messengers’ → “in the guise of messengers”: “guise” replaces ‘form’, and an article is supplied.
- *incontro a noi* ‘towards us’ → “to meet us”: an infinitive replaces the directional phrase.
- *domandarne* ‘asked us’ → “asked of us”: the enclitic personal pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *Di vostra condizion fatene saggi* ‘of your condition make us knowledgeable’ → “Make us to know of your condition”: the imperative and its object come first; an infinitive replaces the predicative adjective.

> 31 E il mio maestro: “Voi potete andarne,  
> 32 e ritrarre a color che vi mandaro,  
> 33 che il corpo di costui è vera carne.  

And my Master: “Ye may go hence and bear back to those who sent you that the body of this man is very flesh.

- *potete andarne* ‘can go away’ → “may go hence”: ability is phrased as permission, and “hence” gives the force of the enclitic of departure.
- *ritrarre* ‘report’ → “bear back”: the single verb becomes a verb and adverb.
- *costui* ‘this one’ → “this man”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a demonstrative and noun.
- *vera carne* ‘real flesh’ → “very flesh”: “very” is used adjectivally in the sense of ‘actual’.

> 34 Se per veder la sua ombra restaro,  
> 35 com' io avviso, assai è lor risposto;  
> 36 facciangli onore, ed esser può lor caro.”  

If they stayed for seeing his shadow, as I opine, enough is answered: let them do him honour and he may be precious to them.”

- *per veder la sua ombra restaro* ‘on seeing his shadow they stopped’ → “they stayed for seeing his shadow”: the verb comes before its cause; “stayed” renders the act of stopping, and a gerund replaces the infinitive after the preposition.
- *assai è lor risposto* ‘enough is answered to them’ → “enough is answered”: the indirect object is omitted.
- *facciangli onore* ‘may they do him honour’ → “let them do him honour”: “let” expresses the force of the third-person jussive subjunctive.
- *esser può lor caro* ‘be he can to them dear’ → “he may be precious to them”: the modal precedes the infinitive, “precious” renders ‘dear’, and the indirect object follows the adjective.

> 37 Vapori accesi non vid' io sì tosto  
> 38 di prima notte mai fender sereno,  
> 39 nè, sol calando, nuvole d' agosto,  
> 40 che color non tornasser suso in meno;  

Ne’er saw I flaming vapours so swiftly cleave the bright sky at early night, or August clouds at setting sun, but that they returned upward in less,

- *Vapori accesi non vid' io … mai* ‘vapours kindled I did not ever see’ → “Ne’er saw I flaming vapours”: the separated negatives become one initial adverb; the object follows the verb and subject, and “flaming” precedes its noun.
- *sì tosto … di prima notte … fender sereno* ‘so swiftly at early night cleave clear sky’ → “so swiftly cleave the bright sky at early night”: the time phrase moves after the object; the substantivized adjective becomes an article, adjective and noun, with ‘clear’ rendered as “bright”.
- *nè, sol calando, nuvole d' agosto* ‘nor, the sun descending, clouds of August’ → “or August clouds at setting sun”: “or” continues the scope of the opening negative; the absolute construction becomes a time phrase following the object, and the month becomes an attributive noun.
- *che color non tornasser suso in meno* ‘that those did not return upward in less’ → “but that they returned upward in less”: “but that” absorbs the negative of the comparative construction, the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the imperfect subjunctive becomes a simple past.

> 41 e, giunti là, con gli altri a noi dier volta,  
> 42 come schiera che corre senza freno.  

and, arrived there, with the others wheeled round to us, like a troop that hastes with loosened rein.

- *a noi dier volta* ‘towards us they made a turn’ → “wheeled round to us”: a verb and adverb replace the verb and noun; the direction comes last.
- *come schiera* ‘like troop’ → “like a troop”: the indefinite article is supplied.
- *corre senza freno* ‘runs without a bridle’ → “hastes with loosened rein”: “hastes” stresses speed rather than the manner of movement; absence of a bridle is rendered as the loosening of a rein.

> 43 “Questa gente che preme a noi è molta,  
> 44 e vengonti a pregar,” disse il poeta;  

“This people that presses on to us is many, and they come to entreat thee,” said the poet;

- *preme a noi* ‘presses towards us’ → “presses on to us”: “on” makes the forward movement explicit.
- *è molta* ‘is numerous’ → “is many”: “many”, ordinarily used of a plural, is predicated of the singular collective.
- *vengonti a pregar* ‘they come thee to entreat’ → “they come to entreat thee”: the pronoun attached to the finite verb in Italian follows the infinitive of which it is the object.

> 45 “però pur va, ed in andando ascolta.”  

“but go thou ever on and, while going, listen.”

- *però* ‘therefore’ → “but”: an inference is rendered as a contrast.
- *pur va* ‘just keep going’ → “go thou ever on”: the force of the preverbal particle is given by “ever on” after the imperative and its expressed subject.
- *in andando* ‘in going’ → “while going”: a temporal conjunction replaces the preposition.

> 46 “O anima, che vai per esser lieta  
> 47 con quelle membra, con le quai nascesti,”  
> 48 venian gridando, “un poco il passo queta.  

“O soul, that goest to be glad with those members which thou wast born with,” they came crying, “arrest a while thy step.

- *con le quai nascesti* ‘with which thou wast born’ → “which thou wast born with”: the preposition moves to the end of the relative clause.
- *venian gridando* ‘were coming crying’ → “they came crying”: the imperfect of the finite verb becomes a simple past.
- *un poco il passo queta* ‘a little the step quiet’ → “arrest a while thy step”: the imperative moves first and denotes stopping rather than quieting; the qualification becomes one of duration, and a possessive replaces the article.

> 49 Guarda se alcun di noi unque vedesti,  
> 50 sì che di lui di là novelle porti:  

Look if e’er thou sawest any one of us, so that thou mayst bear tidings of him yonder:

- *alcun di noi unque vedesti* ‘any one of us ever thou sawest’ → “e’er thou sawest any one of us”: the adverb comes first and the object follows the verb.
- *sì che di lui di là novelle porti* ‘so that of him yonder tidings thou bear’ → “so that thou mayst bear tidings of him yonder”: “mayst” conveys the subjunctive of purpose; the verb precedes its object, “of him” follows “tidings”, and the adverb of place comes last.

> 51 deh, perchè vai? deh, perchè non t' arresti?  

ah, wherefore goest thou? ah, wherefore stayest thou not?

- *non t' arresti* ‘dost thou not stop thyself’ → “stayest thou not”: an intransitive verb replaces the reflexive, with the negative after the subject.

> 52 Noi fummo già tutti per forza morti,  
> 53 e peccatori infino all' ultim' ora:  
> 54 quivi lume del ciel ne fece accorti  

We were all slain by violence and sinners up to the last hour: then light from heaven made us ware

- *già* ‘once’ → “were”: the tense is retained, but the adverb has no separate equivalent.
- *per forza morti* ‘by force killed’ → “slain by violence”: the participle comes first, and “violence” specifies the sense of ‘force’.
- *infino all' ultim' ora* ‘until the last hour’ → “up to the last hour”: a prepositional phrase expresses the limit.
- *quivi* ‘there’ → “then”: the adverb is taken temporally rather than spatially.
- *lume del ciel* ‘light of heaven’ → “light from heaven”: the relation is expressed as one of origin.
- *ne fece accorti* ‘us made aware’ → “made us ware”: the object follows the verb, and the adjective is given in its older English form.

## Lines 55–87 — A Promise of Help and a Plea for Prayers

> 55 sì che, pentendo e perdonando, fuora  
> 56 di vita uscimmo a Dio pacificati,  

so that, repenting and pardoning, we came forth from life reconciled with God,

- *fuora di vita uscimmo* ‘out of life we came’ → “we came forth from life”: the verb moves before the phrase of departure, and “forth” goes with “came”.
- *a Dio pacificati* ‘with God made at peace’ → “reconciled with God”: the participle moves before its complement; being made at peace is expressed as reconciliation.

> 57 che del disio di sè veder n' accora.”  

who penetrates us with desire to behold him.”

- *del disio di sè veder n' accora* ‘with the desire himself to see pierces our hearts’ → “penetrates us with desire to behold him”: the verb and its object move before the desire-phrase. The image of the heart is not expressed; the definite article disappears, and the reflexive becomes “him” after the infinitive.

> 58 Ed io: “Perchè ne' vostri visi guati,  
> 59 non riconosco alcun; ma, se a voi piace  

And I: “How much soever I gaze in your faces, I recognise none;

- *Perchè ne' vostri visi guati* ‘however much in your faces I may gaze’ → “How much soever I gaze in your faces”: the concessive subjunctive becomes an indicative with “soever”, and the verb moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *non riconosco alcun* ‘I do not recognise anyone’ → “I recognise none”: the negation passes from the verb to the object.

> 60 cosa ch' io possa, spiriti ben nati,  
> 61 voi dite; ed io farò per quella pace,  

but if aught I can do may please you, ye spirits born for bliss, speak ye; and I will do it for the sake of that peace,

- *ma, se a voi piace cosa ch' io possa* ‘but if to you is pleasing a thing that I can’ → “but if aught I can do may please you”: the postponed subject moves forward; “aught” replaces the noun, the relative pronoun drops out, and “do” completes the modal. The person pleased becomes a direct object, and “may” is supplied.
- *spiriti ben nati* ‘spirits well born’ → “ye spirits born for bliss”: “ye” marks the address, and the adverb becomes a phrase expressing what the spirits were born for.
- *voi dite* ‘you speak’ → “speak ye”: the emphatic pronoun follows the imperative.
- *farò* ‘I shall do’ → “I will do it”: the object understood in Italian is expressed.
- *per* ‘for’ → “for the sake of”: the single preposition is expanded.

> 62 che, retro ai piedi di sì fatta guida,  
> 63 di mondo in mondo cercar mi si face.”  

which, following the steps of such a guide, makes me pursue it from world to world.”

- *che ... cercar mi si face* ‘which ... makes me seek it’ → “which” ... “makes me pursue it”: the infinitive moves from before the causative verb to after it and its object. “Pursue” replaces ‘seek’; “it” represents the peace itself, expressed reflexively in Italian.
- *retro ai piedi di sì fatta guida* ‘behind the feet of a guide so fashioned’ → “following the steps of such a guide”: the preposition becomes a participle, feet become steps, and “such” condenses the description of the guide.
- *di mondo in mondo* ‘from world into world’ → “from world to world”: the destination takes “to”, and the phrase moves from before the infinitive to after its object.

> 64 Ed uno incominciò: “Ciascun si fida  
> 65 del beneficio tuo senza giurarlo,  

And one began: “Each of us trusts in thy good offices without thine oath,

- *Ciascun* ‘each’ → “Each of us”: the group is made explicit.
- *si fida del beneficio tuo* ‘relies on the benefit of yours’ → “trusts in thy good offices”: the pronominal verb becomes a simple verb with “in”; a singular noun becomes a plural idiom, and the possessive moves before it.
- *senza giurarlo* ‘without swearing it’ → “without thine oath”: the infinitive and its object become a noun with a possessive identifying who would swear.

> 66 pur che il voler nonpossa non ricida.  

if only want of power cut not off the will.

- *il voler nonpossa non ricida* ‘the will inability cut not off’ → “want of power cut not off the will”: “want of power” takes *nonpossa* as the noun meaning inability, distinct from the negation of the verb. The subject moves first and the object last; the nominal infinitive becomes “the will”.

> 67 Ond' io, che solo innanzi agli altri parlo,  

Wherefore I, who merely speak before the others,

- *solo innanzi agli altri parlo* ‘only before the others I speak’ → “merely speak before the others”: “merely” takes *solo* as qualifying the act, rather than as ‘alone’ qualifying the speaker; the verb moves before the phrase of precedence.

> 68 ti prego, se mai vedi quel paese  
> 69 che siede tra Romagna e quel di Carlo,  

pray thee, if e’er thou see that country which lies between Romagna and that of Charles,

- *ti prego* ‘thee I pray’ → “pray thee”: the object follows the verb.
- *siede* ‘sits’ → “lies”: the verb of posture is replaced by the English idiom for geographical position.

> 70 che tu mi sie de' tuoi preghi cortese  
> 71 in Fano sì che ben per me s' adori,  
> 72 perch' io possa purgar le gravi offese.  

that thou be gracious to me of thy prayers in Fano, so that holy orison be made for me, that I may purge away my heavy offences,

- *mi sie de' tuoi preghi cortese* ‘to me be of thy prayers gracious’ → “be gracious to me of thy prayers”: the adjective moves next to the verb, ahead of both complements.
- *ben per me s' adori* ‘well for me one may pray’ → “holy orison be made for me”: the impersonal subjunctive becomes a passive, the act of praying becomes the noun “orison”, and the adverb becomes the adjective “holy”. The beneficiary moves to the end.
- *purgar le gravi offese* ‘purge the grave offences’ → “purge away my heavy offences”: “away” is added, the article becomes a possessive, and “heavy” renders the weight implicit in *gravi*.

> 73 Quindi fu' io; ma li profondi fori,  
> 74 onde uscì il sangue in sul qual io sedea,  
> 75 fatti mi furo in grembo agli Antenori,  

Thence sprang I; but the deep wounds whence flowed the blood wherein my life was set, were dealt me in the bosom of the Antenori,

- *fu' io* ‘was I’ → “sprang I”: being from a place is expressed as springing from it.
- *li profondi fori* ‘the deep holes’ → “the deep wounds”: the openings are named by the injuries that made them.
- *uscì* ‘came out’ → “flowed”: the verb specifies the manner of emergence.
- *in sul qual io sedea* ‘upon which I sat’ → “wherein my life was set”: ‘upon’ becomes “in”; the speaker becomes “my life”, and an active imperfect becomes a passive expressing a state.
- *fatti mi furo* ‘made to me were’ → “were dealt me”: the auxiliary moves first, and wounds are “dealt” rather than made.
- *in grembo agli Antenori* ‘in the lap to the Antenori’ → “in the bosom of the Antenori”: “bosom” replaces ‘lap’, and the Italian dative becomes an English possessive construction.

> 76 là dov' io più sicuro esser credea.  

there where I thought to be most secure.

- *più sicuro esser credea* ‘more secure to be I thought’ → “I thought to be most secure”: the order of the governing verb, infinitive, and adjective is reversed; the comparative is understood as a superlative.

> 77 Quel da Esti il fe' far, che m' avea in ira  
> 78 assai più là che dritto non volea.  

He of Este had it done, who held me in wrath far beyond what justice would.

- *Quel da Esti* ‘that one from Este’ → “He of Este”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative, and origin is expressed by “of”.
- *il fe' far* ‘it he caused to do’ → “had it done”: the object moves after the causative verb, and the active infinitive becomes a past participle.
- *m' avea in ira* ‘me had in wrath’ → “held me in wrath”: “held” replaces ‘had’, and the object follows the verb.
- *assai più là che dritto non volea* ‘much farther than right willed’ → “far beyond what justice would”: the comparative with ‘than’ becomes “beyond what”; “justice” renders ‘right’, and “would” carries the sense of willing. The Italian comparative's expletive negative has no English counterpart.

> 79 Ma s' io fossi fuggito in ver La Mira,  
> 80 quando fui sopraggiunto ad Oriago,  
> 81 ancor sarei di là dove si spira.  

But if I had fled towards La Mira, when I was surprised at Oriaco, I should yet be yonder where men breathe.

- *fossi fuggito* ‘were fled’ → “had fled”: the Italian pluperfect subjunctive with the auxiliary ‘be’ becomes an English past perfect with “had”.
- *fui sopraggiunto* ‘I was overtaken’ → “I was surprised”: the rendering stresses being come upon unexpectedly rather than being caught up with.
- *ancor sarei* ‘still I should be’ → “I should yet be”: the adverb moves inside the verb phrase.
- *di là* ‘over there’ → “yonder”: one adverb replaces the phrase.
- *si spira* ‘one breathes’ → “men breathe”: the impersonal singular becomes a personal plural.

> 82 Corsi al palude, e le cannucce e il brago  
> 83 m' impigliar sì ch' io caddi, e lì vid' io  
> 84 delle mie vene farsi in terra lago.”  

I ran to the marshes, and the reeds and the mire entangled me so, that I fell; and there saw I a pool growing on the ground from my veins.”

- *al palude* ‘to the marsh’ → “to the marshes”: singular becomes plural.
- *m' impigliar* ‘me entangled’ → “entangled me”: the object moves after the verb.
- *delle mie vene farsi in terra lago* ‘from my veins form itself on the ground a lake’ → “a pool growing on the ground from my veins”: the source moves from first to last and the thing seen from last to first. “Pool” reduces the scale of ‘lake’; the reflexive infinitive after the verb of seeing becomes a participle describing growth.

> 85 Poi disse un altro: “Deh, se quel disio  
> 86 si compia che ti tragge all' alto monte,  

Then said another: “Prithee,—and so be that desire satisfied which draws thee up the lofty mount—

- *disse un altro* ‘said another one’ → “said another”: the indefinite expression is condensed into a pronoun.
- *Deh* ‘ah’ → “Prithee”: the interjection becomes a formula of entreaty.
- *se quel disio si compia* ‘if that desire be fulfilled’ → “and so be that desire satisfied”: the wish introduced by conditional *se* becomes an independent optative with “and so”; the auxiliary precedes the subject, and fulfilment becomes satisfaction.
- *ti tragge all' alto monte* ‘thee draws to the high mountain’ → “draws thee up the lofty mount”: the object moves after the verb, and “up” gives a direction of ascent rather than just a destination.

> 87 con buona pietate aiuta il mio.  

with kindly pity help my desire.

- *con buona pietate* ‘with good pity’ → “with kindly pity”: the adjective is rendered in the sense of benevolence.
- *il mio* ‘mine’ → “my desire”: the possessive standing alone becomes a possessive determiner with its understood noun restored.

## Lines 88–120 — Buonconte’s Death and the Storm

> 88 Io fui di Montefeltro, io son Buonconte;  

I was of Montefeltro, I am Buonconte;

- Literal.

> 89 Giovanna o altri non ha di me cura:  
> 90 per ch' io vo tra costor con bassa fronte.”  

Giovanna, or any other hath no care for me; wherefore I go among these, with downcast brow.”

- *o altri* ‘or another’ → “or any other”: “any” makes the pronoun explicitly indefinite.
- *non ha di me cura* ‘has not of me care’ → “hath no care for me”: the negative goes with the noun instead of the verb; the complement follows “care” and takes “for” instead of ‘of’.
- *con bassa fronte* ‘with low brow’ → “with downcast brow”: “downcast” describes the bearing rather than simply the position of the brow.

> 91 Ed io a lui: “Qual forza o qual ventura  
> 92 ti traviò sì fuor di Campaldino  
> 93 che non si seppe mai tua sepoltura?”  

And I to him: “What violence or what chance made thee stray so far from Campaldino, that thy burial place ne’er was known?”

- *forza* ‘force’ → “violence”: the translation gives the more specific sense of force exerted violently.
- *ti traviò* ‘thee led astray’ → “made thee stray”: a transitive verb with a preceding object becomes “made” with an object and an infinitive.
- *sì fuor di Campaldino* ‘so far outside Campaldino’ → “so far from Campaldino”: distance from a place replaces position outside it.
- *non si seppe mai tua sepoltura* ‘never did one know thy burial’ → “thy burial place ne’er was known”: the *si* construction becomes an explicit passive, with its subject first; “place” specifies the local sense of *sepoltura*.

> 94 “Oh,” rispos' egli, “a piè del Casentino  
> 95 traversa un' acqua che ha nome l' Archiano,  
> 96 che sopra l' Ermo nasce in Apennino.  

“Oh,” answered he, “at Casentino’s foot a stream crosses, which is named Archiano, and rises in the Apennines above the Hermitage.

- *a piè del Casentino* ‘at the foot of the Casentino’ → “at Casentino’s foot”: the place-name becomes a preceding possessive, without its article.
- *traversa un' acqua* ‘crosses a water’ → “a stream crosses”: the subject moves before the verb, and “stream” specifies what kind of water is meant.
- *che ha nome l' Archiano* ‘which has the name the Archiano’ → “which is named Archiano”: possession of a name becomes a passive naming construction; the name loses its article.
- *che sopra l' Ermo nasce in Apennino* ‘which above the Hermitage is born in the Apennine’ → “and rises in the Apennines above the Hermitage”: coordination replaces the second relative; “rises” is the English verb for a stream’s origin, the mountain name is plural, and the phrase beginning with ‘above’ moves to the end.

> 97 Dove il vocabol suo diventa vano  
> 98 arriva' io forato nella gola,  
> 99 fuggendo a piede e sanguinando il piano.  

There where its name is lost, did I arrive, pierced in the throat, flying on foot, and bloodying the plain.

- *Dove il vocabol suo diventa vano* ‘where the name of it becomes vain’ → “There where its name is lost”: “There” is supplied, the possessive precedes the noun, and a passive expression of loss replaces ‘becomes vain’.
- *arriva' io* ‘arrived I’ → “did I arrive”: the simple past is distributed between the auxiliary before the subject and the infinitive after it.
- *fuggendo* ‘fleeing’ → “flying”: the translation uses ‘fly’ in its sense of fleeing.

> 100 Quivi perdei la vista, e la parola  
> 101 nel nome di Maria finii; e quivi  
> 102 caddi, e rimase la mia carne sola.  

There lost I vision, and ended my words upon the name of Mary; and there fell I, and my flesh alone was left.

- *Quivi perdei la vista* ‘there I lost the sight’ → “There lost I vision”: the English inverts subject and verb and uses the articleless “vision” for the faculty of sight.
- *la parola nel nome di Maria finii* ‘speech in the name of Mary I ended’ → “ended my words upon the name of Mary”: the verb comes first, singular ‘speech’ becomes a possessive plural, and “upon” marks what the words ended on.
- *quivi caddi* ‘there I fell’ → “there fell I”: the subject follows the verb.
- *rimase la mia carne sola* ‘remained my flesh alone’ → “my flesh alone was left”: the subject and its modifier move before the verb, and a passive replaces ‘remained’.

> 103 Io dirò il vero, e tu il ridi' tra i vivi;  

I will speak sooth, and do thou respeak it among the living;

- *dirò il vero* ‘I will say the truth’ → “I will speak sooth”: “speak sooth” replaces the verb and its definite object with an articleless idiom.
- *tu il ridi'* ‘thou it say again’ → “do thou respeak it”: “do” introduces the imperative, the object moves after the verb, and “respeak” uses ‘speak’ rather than ‘say’ for the repeated act.

> 104 l' angel di Dio mi prese, e quel d' inferno  
> 105 gridava: ‘O tu del ciel, perchè mi privi?  

the angel of God took me, and one from Hell cried: ‘O thou from Heaven, wherefore robbest thou me?

- *l' angel di Dio mi prese* ‘the angel of God me took’ → “the angel of God took me”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *quel d' inferno* ‘the one of Hell’ → “one from Hell”: the definite demonstrative becomes indefinite, and “from” replaces ‘of’.
- *gridava* ‘was crying out’ → “cried”: the simple past does not mark the continuing action of the imperfect.
- *del ciel* ‘of Heaven’ → “from Heaven”: origin replaces affiliation.
- *perchè mi privi* ‘why me dost thou deprive’ → “wherefore robbest thou me”: ‘rob’ replaces ‘deprive’, and the object moves after the verb and subject.

> 106 Tu te ne porti di costui l' eterno  
> 107 per una lagrimetta che il mi toglie;  
> 108 ma io farò dell' altro altro governo.’  

Thou bearest hence the eternal part of this man, for one little tear that snatches him from me; but with the other will I deal in other fashion.’

- *Tu te ne porti* ‘thou carriest away with thee’ → “Thou bearest hence”: “hence” conveys removal, without separately rendering the reflexive *te*.
- *di costui l' eterno* ‘of this man the eternal’ → “the eternal part of this man”: “part” supplies a noun for the substantivized adjective, and the possessor moves after it.
- *una lagrimetta* ‘a little tear’ → “one little tear”: “one” stresses the numerical sense of *una*; the separate adjective renders the diminutive suffix.
- *che il mi toglie* ‘that him from me takes’ → “that snatches him from me”: “snatches” strengthens ‘takes’, and both pronouns move after the verb, the dative becoming a prepositional phrase.
- *ma io farò dell' altro altro governo* ‘but I shall make of the other other disposal’ → “but with the other will I deal in other fashion”: ‘make … disposal’ becomes ‘deal with’; “with the other” is fronted, subject and auxiliary are inverted, and “in other fashion” expresses the difference in treatment.

> 109 Ben sai come nell' aere si raccoglie  
> 110 quell' umido vapor, che in acqua riede  
> 111 tosto che sale dove il freddo il coglie.  

Thou knowest how in the air that damp vapour gathers, which turns again to water soon as it ascends where the cold condenses it.

- *Ben sai* ‘well thou knowest’ → “Thou knowest”: the intensifying adverb is omitted.
- *si raccoglie quell' umido vapor* ‘gathers itself that damp vapour’ → “that damp vapour gathers”: English uses an intransitive verb without a reflexive and puts the subject first.
- *in acqua riede* ‘to water returns’ → “turns again to water”: “turns again” renders the single verb, and the complement follows it.
- *tosto che* ‘as soon as’ → “soon as”: the initial ‘as’ of the full English conjunction is absent.
- *il freddo il coglie* ‘the cold it catches’ → “the cold condenses it”: “condenses” names the physical effect rather than rendering ‘catches’, and the object follows the verb.

> 112 Giunse quel mal voler, che pur mal chiede,  
> 113 con l' intelletto, e mosse il fummo e il vento  
> 114 per la virtù, che sua natura diede.  

He united that evil will, which seeks ill only, with intellect, and stirred the mist and wind by the power which his nature gave.

- *quel mal voler* ‘that evil willing’ → “that evil will”: the nominal infinitive is rendered by a noun.
- *che pur mal chiede* ‘which only ill asks for’ → “which seeks ill only”: “seeks” replaces ‘asks for’, the object follows the verb, and “only” comes last.
- *con l' intelletto* ‘with the intellect’ → “with intellect”: the abstract noun loses its article.
- *mosse il fummo e il vento* ‘moved the smoke and the wind’ → “stirred the mist and wind”: “stirred” specifies the movement, “mist” interprets the smoke-like vapour, and the two nouns share an article.
- *per la virtù* ‘through the virtue’ → “by the power”: “by” marks the means, and “power” gives the sense of effective capacity.

> 115 Indi la valle, come il dì fu spento,  
> 116 da Pratomagno al gran giogo coperse  
> 117 di nebbia, e il ciel di sopra fece intento  

Then when day was spent, he covered the valley from Pratomagno to the great mountain chain with mist, and the sky above made lowering

- *la valle, come il dì fu spento, … coperse* ‘the valley, when the day was extinguished, … he covered’ → “when day was spent, he covered the valley”: the time clause comes first, the object follows the verb, “day” loses its article, and “spent” replaces the image of extinguishing.
- *da Pratomagno al gran giogo* ‘from Pratomagno to the great ridge’ → “from Pratomagno to the great mountain chain”: the phrase moves from before the verb to after its object; “mountain chain” interprets the singular *giogo*.
- *di nebbia* ‘of mist’ → “with mist”: English ‘cover’ takes “with” for the covering material.
- *e il ciel di sopra fece intento* ‘and the sky above he made tense’ → “and the sky above made lowering”: the English carries over the earlier subject and interprets *intento* as the threatening appearance of the sky.

> 118 sì che il pregno aere in acqua si converse:  

so that the saturated air was turned to water:

- *il pregno aere* ‘the pregnant air’ → “the saturated air”: an explicit description of moisture replaces the metaphor.
- *in acqua si converse* ‘into water it turned itself’ → “was turned to water”: a passive replaces the pronominal verb, and the result follows the verb.

> 119 la pioggia cadde, ed ai fossati venne  
> 120 di lei ciò che la terra non sofferse;  

the rain fell, and to the water-rills came what of it the earth endured not;

- *ai fossati* ‘to the ditches’ → “to the water-rills”: “water-rills” interprets the channels as small watercourses.
- *di lei ciò che* ‘of it that which’ → “what of it”: “what” combines antecedent and relative, and the partitive phrase moves after it.

## Lines 121–136 — The Archian Takes the Body and Pia Asks for Remembrance

> 121 e come a' rivi grandi si convenne,  
> 122 ver lo fiume real tanto veloce  
> 123 si ruinò, che nulla la ritenne.  

and as it united into great torrents, so swiftly it rushed towards the royal stream, that naught held it back.

- *a' rivi grandi si convenne* ‘with the streams great it came together’ → “it united into great torrents”: the verb comes first; “into” makes the torrents the result of the gathering rather than the streams the water joins. The adjective moves before the noun, and “torrents” is stronger than ‘streams’.
- *ver lo fiume real tanto veloce si ruinò* ‘towards the river royal so fast it hurtled down’ → “so swiftly it rushed towards the royal stream”: the phrase of direction moves from first to last; “royal” precedes its noun, and ‘river’ becomes “stream”. The adjective used adverbially becomes the adverb “swiftly”; “rushed” loses the suggestion of downward motion.
- *la ritenne* ‘it held back’ → “held it back”: the object pronoun moves from before the verb to between the verb and its particle.

> 124 Lo corpo mio gelato in su la foce  
> 125 trovò l' Archian rubesto; e quel sospinse  
> 126 nell' Arno, e sciolse al mio petto la croce,  

My frozen body at its mouth the raging Archian found, and swept it into the Arno, and loosed the cross on my breast,

- *corpo mio gelato* ‘body of mine, frozen’ → “My frozen body”: both possessive and adjective move before the noun.
- *in su la foce* ‘upon the mouth’ → “at its mouth”: the compound preposition becomes “at”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *trovò l' Archian rubesto* ‘found the Archian fierce’ → “the raging Archian found”: the subject moves before the verb; the adjective moves before the name and becomes the participle “raging”.
- *quel sospinse* ‘that it thrust’ → “swept it”: the demonstrative object becomes a personal pronoun and follows the verb; “swept” gives the thrust the motion of a current.
- *al mio petto la croce* ‘at my breast the cross’ → “the cross on my breast”: the object moves before the phrase of place, and ‘at’ becomes “on”.

> 127 ch' io fei di me quando il dolor mi vinse;  

which I made of me when pain o’ercame me:

- *il dolor mi vinse* ‘the pain me overcame’ → “pain o’ercame me”: the article drops out, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 128 voltommi per le ripe e per lo fondo,  
> 129 poi di sua preda mi coperse e cinse.”  

it rolled me along its banks and over its bed, then covered and wrapped me with its spoils.”

- *per le ripe e per lo fondo* ‘along the banks and along the bottom’ → “along its banks and over its bed”: the repeated preposition is varied, both articles become possessives, and ‘bottom’ becomes the river-specific “bed”.
- *di sua preda mi coperse e cinse* ‘with its spoil me it covered and encircled’ → “covered and wrapped me with its spoils”: the phrase of means moves to the end, ‘spoil’ becomes plural, and the shared object follows both verbs. “wrapped” takes the place of ‘encircled’.

> 130 “Deh, quando tu sarai tornato al mondo,  
> 131 e riposato della lunga via,”  

“Pray, when thou shalt return to the world, and art rested from thy long journey,”

- *Deh* ‘Ah’ → “Pray”: an interjection becomes a verb of entreaty.
- *sarai tornato* ‘thou wilt have returned’ → “thou shalt return”: the future perfect becomes a simple future.
- *riposato della lunga via* ‘rested from the long way’ → “art rested from thy long journey”: where the Italian participle shares the preceding future auxiliary, English supplies a present one. The article becomes a possessive, and ‘way’ becomes “journey”.

> 132 seguitò il terzo spirito al secondo,  

followed the third spirit after the second,

- *al secondo* ‘to the second’ → “after the second”: the preposition is changed to express succession in English.

> 133 “ricorditi di me, che son la Pia;  

“Remember me, who am La Pia:

- *ricorditi di me* ‘may it come to mind to thee of me’ → “Remember me”: the Italian wish with a dative pronoun becomes a direct imperative; the object of the preposition becomes the object of the verb.

> 134 Siena mi fe', disfecemi Maremma:  

Siena made me, Maremma unmade me:

- *mi fe'* ‘me made’ → “made me”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *disfecemi Maremma* ‘unmade me Maremma’ → “Maremma unmade me”: the subject moves from last to first, making the two clauses parallel in English.

> 135 salsi colui che innanellata, pria  
> 136 disposando, m' avea con la sua gemma.”  

’tis known to him who, first plighting troth, had wedded me with his gem.”

- *salsi colui* ‘he himself knows it’ → “’tis known to him”: the active verb with its emphatic pronominal construction becomes passive; what is known becomes the subject, and the knower is introduced by “to”.
- *innanellata … m' avea* ‘ringed … me he had’ → “had wedded me”: the auxiliary precedes the participle, the object follows it, and the feminine agreement of the participle has no English counterpart. “wedded” names the act rather than the giving of the ring; the whole verb phrase is deferred until after the intervening participial phrase.
- *pria disposando* ‘first espousing’ → “first plighting troth”: the act of espousing is expressed as the pledging of a vow.

## Summary — Close Echoes in English Syntax

Okey often keeps Dante’s words while changing their order. Object pronouns move after their verbs, and possessives and adjectives move before their nouns: *corpo mio gelato* becomes “My frozen body”. A complement separated from its governing word is often brought next to it, as when *di perdon … degno* becomes “worthy of pardon”. Two routine adjustments generally go unmarked in the comparison: English supplies subject pronouns where the Italian verb ending identifies the subject, and omits the article before a possessive, as in *lo mio corpo*, “my body”.

A change of construction can preserve the sense without matching the forms. The infinitive after *vidile* becomes a participle in “saw them gazing”; the impersonal *si spira* acquires a personal subject in “men breathe”; and the subjunctive *facciangli onore* takes the form “let them do him honour”. Okey sometimes supplies a word to make an implication explicit: “part” gives *l’ eterno* a noun, and “my desire” restores the noun understood in *il mio*. Elsewhere he leaves a word unexpressed, as with the intensifier *Ben* in “Thou knowest”.

The choice of an English word can also change what the reader pictures. Where the cold *il coglie*, ‘catches it’, Okey writes “condenses it”, naming the physical effect; “saturated” likewise gives the sense of *pregno* without its image of pregnancy. Yet Okey does not consistently smooth the Italian into English idiom. He retains the doubled emphasis of *pur me, pur me* in “me alone, me alone”, and even the unusual “is many” for *è molta*. In the final contrast, *fe’* and *disfecemi* remain audibly paired in “made me” and “unmade me”: the order of the second clause changes, but the relation between the verbs survives intact.
