# Purgatorio 28

## Lines 1–12 — Entering the Divine Forest

> 1 Vago già di cercar dentro e dintorno  
> 2 la divina foresta spessa e viva,  

Now eager to search within and around the divine forest dense and verdant,

- *Vago già* ‘eager already’ → “Now eager”: the adverb is rendered by “Now” and placed before the adjective.
- *viva* ‘alive’ → “verdant”: a word for being alive is rendered by one for green growth.

> 3 ch' agli occhi temperava il nuovo giorno,  

which to mine eyes was tempering the new day,

- *agli occhi* ‘to the eyes’ → “to mine eyes”: English makes the possessor explicit.
- *temperava* ‘tempered’ → “was tempering”: the English progressive brings out the ongoing action of the imperfect.

> 4 senza più aspettar lasciai la riva,  

without waiting more I left the mountain-side,

- *senza più aspettar* ‘without further to wait’ → “without waiting more”: the infinitive becomes an *-ing* form after “without”, and the adverb follows it.
- *la riva* ‘the bank’ → “the mountain-side”: the translation takes the word in the sense of a slope and specifies whose slope it is.

> 5 prendendo la campagna lento lento  
> 6 su per lo suol che d' ogni parte oliva.  

crossing the plain with lingering step, over the ground which gives forth fragrance on every side.

- *prendendo la campagna* ‘taking to the open country’ → “crossing the plain”: taking one's way into the country is rendered as movement across it, and the more specific “plain” is used.
- *lento lento* ‘slow, slow’ → “with lingering step”: the repeated adjective describing the walker becomes a phrase describing his step; the noun is supplied.
- *su per* ‘along over’ → “over”: one preposition does the work of the Italian combination.
- *d' ogni parte oliva* ‘on every side was fragrant’ → “gives forth fragrance on every side”: the imperfect becomes a simple present, the verb of scent is expanded into a verb and noun, and the phrase of place follows instead of preceding it.

> 7 Un' aura dolce, senza mutamento  
> 8 avere in sè, mi feria per la fronte  
> 9 non di più colpo che soave vento,  

A sweet breeze, itself invariable, was striking on my brow with no greater force than a gentle wind,

- *Un' aura dolce* ‘a breeze sweet’ → “A sweet breeze”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *senza mutamento avere in sè* ‘without change having in itself’ → “itself invariable”: the negative phrase with an infinitive becomes an adjective, and the reflexive following a preposition becomes the emphatic “itself”.
- *mi feria per la fronte* ‘struck me upon the brow’ → “was striking on my brow”: the imperfect is rendered by the progressive, and the object pronoun becomes a possessive attached to the part of the body struck.
- *non di più colpo* ‘not with more of a blow’ → “with no greater force”: the negation is attached to the comparison of degree, and the concrete blow becomes its force.
- *soave vento* ‘gentle wind’ → “a gentle wind”: the indefinite article is supplied.

> 10 per cui le fronde, tremolando pronte,  
> 11 tutte e quante piegavano alla parte  
> 12 u' la prim' ombra gitta il santo monte;  

before which the branches, responsively trembling, were all bending toward that quarter, where the holy mount casts its first shadow;

- *per cui* ‘by which’ → “before which”: the Italian marks the breeze as cause; English expresses the branches' position in yielding to it.
- *le fronde* ‘the leafy boughs’ → “the branches”: the English noun does not convey the foliage.
- *tremolando pronte* ‘trembling, ready’ → “responsively trembling”: the adjective agreeing with the boughs becomes an adverb modifying the participle and is moved before it.
- *tutte e quante piegavano* ‘all and every one bent’ → “were all bending”: the emphatic double expression is reduced to “all”, placed within the progressive verb phrase that renders the imperfect.
- *alla parte* ‘to the side’ → “toward that quarter”: the preposition is made explicitly directional, the article becomes demonstrative, and “quarter” gives the directional sense of the noun.
- *la prim' ombra gitta il santo monte* ‘the first shadow casts the holy mount’ → “the holy mount casts its first shadow”: the Italian order of object, verb, and subject is reversed to subject, verb, and object; the shadow's definite article becomes a possessive.

## Lines 13–45 — Birdsong, a Stream, and a Solitary Lady

> 13 non però dal lor esser dritto sparte  
> 14 tanto, che gli augelletti per le cime  
> 15 lasciasser d' operare ogni lor arte;  

yet not so far bent aside from their erect state, that the little birds in the tops ceased to practise their every art;

- *non però* ‘not, however’ → “yet not”: the adversative comes before the negative.
- *dal lor esser dritto sparte tanto* ‘from their upright being turned aside so much’ → “so far bent aside from their erect state”: the degree expression moves before the participle, and the phrase naming the state from which they are bent moves after it; the substantivized infinitive becomes “state”.
- *per le cime* ‘among the tops’ → “in the tops”: “in” does not mark the distribution expressed by *per*.
- *lasciasser d’ operare* ‘should leave off practising’ → “ceased to practise”: the imperfect subjunctive in the result clause becomes a simple past; “ceased” takes an infinitive here.
- *ogni lor arte* ‘every art of theirs’ → “their every art”: the possessive comes before “every”.

> 16 ma con piena letizia l' ôre prime,  
> 17 cantando, ricevièno intra le foglie,  
> 18 che tenevan bordone alle sue rime:  

but, singing, with full gladness they welcomed the first breezes within the leaves, which were murmuring the burden to their songs;

- *con piena letizia l’ ôre prime, cantando, ricevièno* ‘with full gladness the breezes first, singing, they received’ → “singing, with full gladness they welcomed the first breezes”: the participle moves to the front, the object follows the verb, and “first” precedes its noun. “Welcomed” gives a warmer sense to ‘received’; “breezes” takes *ôre* in the sense of *aure*, rather than ‘hours’.
- *tenevan bordone* ‘held a drone’ → “were murmuring the burden”: the idiom for sustaining an accompaniment becomes a description of its sound, with a progressive verb and a definite article.
- *alle sue rime* ‘to their rhymes’ → “to their songs”: the verse term becomes a term for singing; the possessive is understood as referring to the birds.

> 19 tal qual di ramo in ramo si raccoglie  
> 20 per la pineta in sul lito di Chiassi,  
> 21 quand' Eolo Scirocco fuor discioglie.  

even such as from bough to bough is gathered through the pine wood on Chiassi’s shore, when Aeolus looses Sirocco forth.

- *tal qual* ‘such as’ → “even such as”: “even” adds emphasis to the comparison.
- *si raccoglie* ‘gathers itself’ → “is gathered”: an English passive replaces the pronominal construction.
- *in sul lito di Chiassi* ‘upon the shore of Chiassi’ → “on Chiassi’s shore”: the compounded preposition is reduced to “on”, and the place name becomes a preceding possessive.
- *Scirocco fuor discioglie* ‘Sirocco forth unbinds’ → “looses Sirocco forth”: the verb moves before its object and adverb.

> 22 Già m' avean trasportato i lenti passi  
> 23 dentro alla selva antica tanto, ch' io  
> 24 non potea rivedere ond' io m' entrassi;  

Already my slow steps had carried me on so far within the ancient wood, that I could not see whence I had entered;

- *m’ avean trasportato i lenti passi* ‘me had carried the slow steps’ → “my slow steps had carried me on”: the subject comes before the verb, the object after it; “my” replaces the definite article, and “on” makes the onward motion explicit.
- *dentro alla selva antica tanto* ‘within the wood ancient so far’ → “so far within the ancient wood”: the degree expression moves to the front and the adjective before its noun.
- *rivedere* ‘see again’ → “see”: the force of the prefix is lost.
- *ond’ io m’ entrassi* ‘whence I had made my way in’ → “whence I had entered”: the pronominal verb becomes plain “entered”, and the Italian subjunctive of the indirect question is rendered by an English past perfect.

> 25 ed ecco il più andar mi tolse un rio,  
> 26 che in ver sinistra con sue picciole onde  
> 27 piegava l' erba che in sua riva uscìo.  

and lo, a stream took from me further passage which, toward the left with its little waves, bent the grass which sprang forth on its bank.

- *il più andar mi tolse un rio* ‘the going farther from me took a stream’ → “a stream took from me further passage”: the final subject moves to the front, the personal pronoun follows the verb in a prepositional phrase, and the substantivized infinitive with its adverb becomes a noun with a modifier.
- *in ver sinistra* ‘toward left’ → “toward the left”: English adds the article.
- *in sua riva uscìo* ‘on its bank came out’ → “sprang forth on its bank”: the verb precedes the place phrase and describes growth rather than simply emergence.

> 28 Tutte l' acque che son di qua più monde  
> 29 parrieno avere in sè mistura alcuna,  
> 30 verso di quella che nulla nasconde;  

All the waters which here are purest, would seem to have some mixture in them, compared with that, which hideth nought;

- *son di qua più monde* ‘are on this side more pure’ → “here are purest”: the place phrase is compressed and moved before the verb; the comparative in this selection from all waters is rendered as a superlative.
- *avere in sè mistura alcuna* ‘to have in themselves mixture some’ → “to have some mixture in them”: the object precedes the place phrase, the indefinite modifier precedes the noun, and the reflexive becomes a plain pronoun.
- *verso di quella* ‘in relation to that’ → “compared with that”: a participle makes the comparison explicit.
- *nulla nasconde* ‘nothing hides’ → “hideth nought”: the object follows the verb.

> 31 avvegna che si mova bruna bruna  
> 32 sotto l' ombra perpetua, che mai  
> 33 raggiar non lascia sole ivi, nè luna.  

albeit full darkly it flows beneath the everlasting shade, which never lets sun, nor moon, beam there.

- *si mova bruna bruna* ‘it move dark, dark’ → “full darkly it flows”: the concessive subjunctive becomes indicative, movement is specified as flowing, and the repeated adjective describing the water becomes an intensified adverb placed before the verb.
- *l’ ombra perpetua* ‘the shade everlasting’ → “the everlasting shade”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *mai raggiar non lascia sole ivi, nè luna* ‘ever to beam does not let sun there, nor moon’ → “never lets sun, nor moon, beam there”: “never” gathers the two parts of the negative together; the two nouns are brought together after “lets”, followed by the infinitive and the adverb of place.

> 34 Coi piè ristetti e con gli occhi passai  
> 35 di là dal fiumicello, per mirare  
> 36 la gran variazion dei freschi mai;  

With feet I halted and with mine eyes did pass beyond the rivulet, to gaze upon the great diversity of the tender blossoms;

- *Coi piè ristetti* ‘with the feet I stopped’ → “With feet I halted”: the definite article is dropped.
- *con gli occhi passai* ‘with the eyes I passed’ → “with mine eyes did pass”: a possessive replaces the article, and the simple past is expanded with “did”.
- *di là dal fiumicello* ‘on the far side of the little stream’ → “beyond the rivulet”: a single preposition gives the spatial relation, and “rivulet” conveys the diminutive lexically.
- *la gran variazion* ‘the great variation’ → “the great diversity”: “diversity” expresses multiplicity of kinds.
- *dei freschi mai* ‘of the fresh flowering boughs’ → “of the tender blossoms”: “tender” substitutes for ‘fresh’, and the flowering growths are identified specifically as blossoms.

> 37 e là m' apparve, sì com' egli appare  
> 38 subitamente cosa che disvia  
> 39 per maraviglia tutt' altro pensare,  

and there to me appeared, even as on a sudden something appears which, through amazement, sets all other thought astray,

- *sì com’ egli appare subitamente cosa* ‘just as there appears suddenly a thing’ → “even as on a sudden something appears”: the adverb becomes a phrase and moves before the subject; the postposed noun becomes an indefinite pronoun before the verb, with no counterpart needed for impersonal *egli*.
- *disvia per maraviglia tutt’ altro pensare* ‘diverts through wonder all other thinking’ → “through amazement, sets all other thought astray”: the cause moves before the verb, the simple verb becomes a verb and result complement enclosing the object, and the substantivized infinitive becomes “thought”.

> 40 una donna soletta, che si gia  
> 41 cantando ed iscegliendo fior da fiore,  
> 42 ond' era pinta tutta la sua via.  

a lady solitary, who went along singing, and culling flower after flower, wherewith all her path was painted.

- *si gia* ‘went her way’ → “went along”: the pronominal turn is rendered with an adverb.
- *iscegliendo fior da fiore* ‘choosing flower from flower’ → “culling flower after flower”: “culling” suggests picking as well as choosing, and “after” expresses succession rather than selection.
- *era pinta tutta la sua via* ‘was painted all her path’ → “all her path was painted”: the subject moves before the passive verb.

> 43 “Deh, bella donna, ch' ai raggi d' amore  
> 44 ti scaldi, s' io vo' credere ai sembianti  
> 45 che soglion esser testimon del core  

“Pray, fair lady, who at love’s beams dost warm thee, if I may believe outward looks, which are wont to be a witness of the heart,

- *Deh* ‘ah’ → “Pray”: the interjection becomes a word of entreaty.
- *ai raggi d’ amore* ‘at the beams of love’ → “at love’s beams”: a preceding possessive replaces the following prepositional phrase.
- *ti scaldi* ‘thyself thou warmest’ → “dost warm thee”: the finite verb is expanded with “dost”, and the reflexive object follows the verb.
- *vo’ credere ai sembianti* ‘will give credence to the appearances’ → “may believe outward looks”: willingness becomes tentative permission; “believe” takes a direct object, and “outward” makes the external character of the signs explicit.
- *esser testimon* ‘to be witness’ → “to be a witness”: English supplies the indefinite article.

## Lines 46–78 — The Lady Draws Near the River

> 46 vegnati voglia di trarreti avanti,”  
> 47 diss' io a lei, “verso questa riviera,  
> 48 tanto ch' io possa intender che tu canti.  

may it please thee to draw forward,” said I to her, “towards this stream, so far that I may understand what thou singest.

- *vegnati voglia* ‘may desire come to thee’ → “may it please thee”: the wish is expressed by an impersonal verb instead of a noun as subject.
- *di trarreti avanti* ‘of drawing thyself forward’ → “to draw forward”: the infinitive now complements “please” rather than the noun for desire; the English verb is intransitive rather than reflexive.
- *riviera* ‘river’ → “stream”: a different noun for the watercourse.
- *tanto ch’ io possa intender* ‘so much that I may understand’ → “so far that I may understand”: the degree is expressed specifically as distance.

> 49 Tu mi fai rimembrar, dove e qual era  
> 50 Proserpina nel tempo che perdette  
> 51 la madre lei, ed ella primavera.”  

Thou makest me to remember, where and what Proserpine was in the time her mother lost her, and she lost the spring.”

- *Tu mi fai rimembrar* ‘thou me makest remember’ → “Thou makest me to remember”: the object follows the finite verb, and the infinitive takes “to”.
- *dove e qual era Proserpina* ‘where and of what sort was Proserpine’ → “where and what Proserpine was”: “what” renders a question of quality or condition; the subject precedes the verb in the English indirect question.
- *nel tempo che* ‘in the time that’ → “in the time”: the temporal clause follows its antecedent without an expressed connective.
- *perdette la madre lei, ed ella primavera* ‘lost the mother her, and she spring’ → “her mother lost her, and she lost the spring”: the first subject moves before its verb and takes a possessive instead of the definite article; the verb is repeated in the second clause, and “spring” receives an article.

> 52 Come si volge, con le piante strette  
> 53 a terra ed intra sè, donna che balli,  
> 54 e piede innanzi piede a pena mette,  

As a lady who is dancing turns her round with feet close to the ground and to each other, and hardly putteth foot before foot,

- *Come si volge … donna che balli* ‘as turns herself … a lady who should dance’ → “As a lady who is dancing turns her round”: the subject and its relative clause move ahead of the verb; the subjunctive describing a supposed instance becomes a progressive indicative. “Turns her round” uses “her” reflexively and makes the turning motion explicit.
- *con le piante strette a terra ed intra sè* ‘with the soles pressed to earth and among themselves’ → “with feet close to the ground and to each other”: the whole feet stand for their soles, the article on the body part disappears, and “close” expresses position rather than pressure. The ground takes an article, and the second relation is expressed reciprocally.
- *e piede innanzi piede a pena mette* ‘and foot before foot hardly puts’ → “and hardly putteth foot before foot”: the adverb and verb move ahead of the object.

> 55 volsesi in sui vermigli ed in sui gialli  
> 56 fioretti verso me, non altrimenti  
> 57 che vergine che gli occhi onesti avvalli;  

she turned toward me upon the red and upon the yellow flowerets, not otherwise than a virgin that droppeth her modest eyes;

- *volsesi* ‘turned herself’ → “she turned”: the verb becomes intransitive.
- *in sui vermigli ed in sui gialli fioretti verso me* ‘upon the red and upon the yellow flowerets toward me’ → “toward me upon the red and upon the yellow flowerets”: the direction moves immediately after the verb, ahead of the phrase describing the footing.
- *vergine* ‘maiden’ → “a virgin”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *gli occhi onesti avvalli* ‘the eyes modest should lower’ → “droppeth her modest eyes”: the subjunctive of the comparison becomes indicative, and the verb precedes its object; the eyes take a possessive instead of an article, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 58 e fece i preghi miei esser contenti,  
> 59 sì appressando sè, che il dolce suono  
> 60 veniva a me co' suoi intendimenti.  

and made my prayers satisfied, drawing so near that the sweet sound reached me with its meaning.

- *i preghi miei* ‘the prayers mine’ → “my prayers”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *esser contenti* ‘be satisfied’ → “satisfied”: the adjective serves directly as object complement, without the infinitive.
- *sì appressando sè* ‘so bringing herself near’ → “drawing so near”: the gerund becomes intransitive, and the degree word moves next to “near”.
- *veniva a me* ‘was coming to me’ → “reached me”: the imperfect of motion toward someone becomes a simple past of arrival, with a direct object.
- *co’ suoi intendimenti* ‘with its meanings’ → “with its meaning”: the plural becomes singular.

> 61 Tosto che fu là dove l' erbe sono  
> 62 bagnate già dall' onde del bel fiume,  
> 63 di levar gli occhi suoi mi fece dono.  

Soon as she was there, where the grass is already bathed by the waves of the fair river, she vouchsafed to raise her eyes to me.

- *l’ erbe sono bagnate già* ‘the grasses are bathed already’ → “the grass is already bathed”: a collective singular replaces the plural, and the adverb moves before the participle.
- *di levar gli occhi suoi mi fece dono* ‘of raising the eyes hers she made me a gift’ → “she vouchsafed to raise her eyes to me”: the gift construction becomes a verb of gracious concession followed by an infinitive. The possessive comes before “eyes”, and the recipient of the gift becomes the person toward whom the eyes are raised.

> 64 Non credo che splendesse tanto lume  
> 65 sotto le ciglia a Venere trafitta  
> 66 dal figlio, fuor di tutto suo costume.  

I do not believe that so bright a light shone forth under the eyelids of Venus, pierced by her son, against all his wont.

- *Non credo* ‘I believe not’ → “I do not believe”: negation is carried by the auxiliary.
- *splendesse tanto lume* ‘there shone so much light’ → “so bright a light shone forth”: the subjunctive after the negated belief becomes a simple past; the subject moves before the verb, degree of brightness replaces quantity of light, and “forth” is supplied.
- *le ciglia a Venere* ‘the lashes to Venus’ → “the eyelids of Venus”: “eyelids” interprets the anatomical noun, and the possessor is expressed with “of”.
- *dal figlio* ‘by the son’ → “by her son”: a possessive replaces the article.
- *fuor di tutto suo costume* ‘outside all his custom’ → “against all his wont”: departure from custom is expressed as contrariety to it.

> 67 Ella ridea dall' altra riva dritta,  
> 68 traendo più color con le sue mani,  
> 69 che l' alta terra senza seme gitta.  

She smiled from the right bank opposite, gathering more flowers with her hands, which the high land bears without seed.

- *ridea* ‘was laughing’ → “smiled”: the verb is understood as smiling, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *dall’ altra riva dritta* ‘from the other bank, upright’ → “from the right bank opposite”: Okey takes *dritta* with the bank rather than as the woman’s posture; “right” precedes “bank”, while “opposite”, following it, renders the relation expressed by *altra*.
- *traendo più color* ‘drawing more colours’ → “gathering more flowers”: the action is specified as gathering, and the concrete things denoted by the colours replace the colours themselves.
- *senza seme gitta* ‘without seed casts forth’ → “bears without seed”: the verb is rendered in the sense of producing and moves before the modifying phrase.

> 70 Tre passi ci facea il fiume lontani;  

Three paces the river kept us distant;

- *ci facea il fiume lontani* ‘us made the river distant’ → “the river kept us distant”: the imperfect causative becomes a simple past expressing maintained distance; the subject precedes the verb and the object follows it.

> 71 ma Ellesponto, dove passò Xerse,  
> 72 ancora freno a tutti orgogli umani,  
> 73 più odio da Leandro non sofferse,  
> 74 per mareggiare intra Sesto ed Abido,  

but Hellespont, where Xerxes crossed, to this day a curb to all human pride, endured not more hatred from Leander for its turbulent waves ’twixt Sestos and Abydos,

- *dove passò Xerse* ‘where crossed Xerxes’ → “where Xerxes crossed”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *ancora* ‘still’ → “to this day”: the adverb is expanded into a temporal phrase.
- *freno* ‘curb’ → “a curb”: an article is supplied to the noun in apposition.
- *a tutti orgogli umani* ‘to all prides human’ → “to all human pride”: the plural becomes a collective singular, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *più odio da Leandro non sofferse* ‘more hatred from Leander it did not endure’ → “endured not more hatred from Leander”: the verb moves ahead of its object and the source phrase, and the negative follows the verb.
- *per mareggiare* ‘for surging like the sea’ → “for its turbulent waves”: the verbal action becomes a noun phrase, with a possessive and an adjective expressing the movement.

> 75 che quel da me, perchè allor non s' aperse.  

than that did from me, because it opened not then.

- *che quel da me* ‘than that from me’ → “than that did from me”: “did” stands for the verb left understood in Italian.
- *allor non s’ aperse* ‘then did not open itself’ → “it opened not then”: the reflexive becomes intransitive, the negative follows the verb, and the temporal adverb moves to the end.

> 76 “Voi siete nuovi, e forse perch' io rido,”  
> 77 cominciò ella, “in questo loco eletto  
> 78 all' umana natura per suo nido,  

“New-comers are ye,” she began, “and perchance, because I am smiling in this place, chosen for nest of the human race,

- *Voi siete nuovi* ‘you are new’ → “New-comers are ye”: the predicative adjective becomes a noun and moves to the front, with the subject after the verb.
- *e forse perch’ io rido … cominciò ella* ‘and perhaps because I smile … began she’ → “she began, “and perchance, because I am smiling”: the reporting clause moves before the conjunction and takes subject–verb order; the simple present becomes progressive.
- *in questo loco eletto all’ umana natura per suo nido* ‘in this place chosen for human nature as its nest’ → “in this place, chosen for nest of the human race”: “nest” is brought forward, and the phrase designating those for whom the place was chosen becomes dependent on “nest” with “of”, replacing the explicit possessive. “Human race” interprets ‘human nature’ collectively.

## Lines 79–111 — The Water and the Music of the Forest

> 79 meravigliando tienvi alcun sospetto;  

some doubt doth hold you marvelling;

- *meravigliando tienvi alcun sospetto* ‘marvelling holds you some doubt’ → “some doubt doth hold you marvelling”: the subject moves from last to first, the verb takes the auxiliary “doth”, and the participle moves after “you”.

> 80 ma luce rende il salmo *Delectasti,*  
> 81 che puote disnebbiar vostro intelletto.  

but the psalm *Delectasti* giveth light which may clear the mist from your understanding.

- *luce rende il salmo Delectasti* ‘light gives the psalm Delectasti’ → “the psalm *Delectasti* giveth light”: object–verb–subject becomes subject–verb–object.
- *puote disnebbiar vostro intelletto* ‘can uncloud your understanding’ → “may clear the mist from your understanding”: ability is expressed by “may”; the mist implicit in the Italian verb becomes the object of “clear”, and the understanding becomes the object of “from”.

> 82 E tu, che sei dinanzi, e mi pregasti,  
> 83 di' s' altro vuoi udir: ch' io venni presta  

And thou, who art in front, and didst entreat me, say if aught else thou wouldst hear:

- *mi pregasti* ‘me entreatedst’ → “didst entreat me”: the simple past is rendered with an auxiliary, and the object follows the verb.
- *altro vuoi udir* ‘anything else thou willest to hear’ → “aught else thou wouldst hear”: the present verb of wishing becomes the modal “wouldst” with a bare infinitive.

> 84 ad ogni tua question, tanto che basti.”  

for I came ready to all thy questioning till thou be satisfied.”

- *ch' io venni presta ad ogni tua question* ‘for I came ready for each question of thine’ → “for I came ready to all thy questioning”: the causal conjunction and verb phrase are retained, but the English carries over the opening words from line 83; “to” introduces the complement of “ready”, and the individual questions become the collective activity “all thy questioning”.
- *tanto che basti* ‘so much that it suffice’ → “till thou be satisfied”: sufficiency is expressed as an endpoint, with the questioner as subject of a passive construction.

> 85 “L' acqua,” diss' io, “e il suon della foresta,  
> 86 impugnan dentro a me novella fede  
> 87 di cosa, ch' io udi' contraria a questa.”  

“The water,” said I, “and the music of the forest, are combatting within me a new belief in a thing which I have heard contrary to this.”

- *il suon* ‘the sound’ → “the music”: a more specific word for the sound is chosen.
- *impugnan* ‘oppose’ → “are combatting”: the simple present becomes progressive.
- *novella fede di cosa* ‘new belief of a thing’ → “a new belief in a thing”: an article is supplied before “new belief”, and “in” replaces the genitive preposition.
- *udi’* ‘I heard’ → “I have heard”: the simple past becomes a present perfect.

> 88 Ond' ella: “Io dicerò come procede  
> 89 per sua cagion ciò ch' ammirar ti face,  
> 90 e purgherò la nebbia che ti fiede.  

Wherefore she: “I will tell from what cause that arises which makes thee marvel, and I will purge away the mist that offends thee.

- *come procede per sua cagion* ‘how it proceeds through its cause’ → “from what cause that arises”: the question of manner becomes one of cause; “what” replaces the possessive, and the subject of “arises” precedes the verb rather than following it.
- *ciò ch' ammirar ti face* ‘that which marvel thee makes’ → “that arises which makes thee marvel”: the English separates the demonstrative from its relative by the main verb and puts “makes” before its object and infinitive.
- *purgherò* ‘I shall purge’ → “I will purge away”: the particle makes removal explicit.
- *ti fiede* ‘thee wounds’ → “offends thee”: a less physical verb is used, with the object after it.

> 91 Lo sommo Ben, che solo esso a sè piace,  
> 92 fece l' uom buono, e a bene, e questo loco  
> 93 diede per arra a lui d' eterna pace.  

The highest Good, who himself alone doth please, made man good and for goodness, and gave this place to him as an earnest of eternal peace.

- *che solo esso a sè piace* ‘who alone he unto himself is pleasing’ → “who himself alone doth please”: the emphatic subject and reflexive complement are compressed; “himself” comes before “alone”, and the construction with a dative becomes one with the active verb “please”.
- *l' uom* ‘the man’ → “man”: the generic noun loses its article.
- *a bene* ‘to good’ → “for goodness”: the end for which man was made is expressed with “for” and an abstract noun.
- *questo loco diede per arra a lui d' eterna pace* ‘this place gave as a pledge to him of eternal peace’ → “gave this place to him as an earnest of eternal peace”: the verb precedes its object, and the recipient moves before “as an earnest”, so that “of eternal peace” follows “earnest” directly.

> 94 Per sua diffalta qui dimorò poco;  

Through his default, small time he sojourned here;

- *qui dimorò poco* ‘here he dwelt little’ → “small time he sojourned here”: the adverb of duration becomes a noun phrase and moves to the front; the adverb of place moves to the end.

> 95 per sua diffalta in pianto ed in affanno  
> 96 cambiò onesto riso e dolce gioco.  

through his default, for tears and sweat he exchanged honest laughter and sweet play.

- *in pianto ed in affanno cambiò* ‘into weeping and into distress he changed’ → “for tears and sweat he exchanged”: transformation into something becomes exchange for something, with the preposition used only once; “tears” stands for weeping, and “sweat” gives a physical expression to toil or distress.

> 97 Perchè il turbar, che sotto da sè fanno  
> 98 l' esalazion dell' acqua e della terra,  
> 99 che, quanto posson, retro al calor vanno,  

In order that the storms, which the exhalations of the water and of the earth cause below it, and which follow so far as they can after the heat,

- *il turbar* ‘the troubling’ → “the storms”: a singular substantivized infinitive becomes a plural noun for particular disturbances.
- *che sotto da sè fanno l' esalazion dell' acqua e della terra* ‘which below of themselves produce the exhalations of the water and of the earth’ → “which the exhalations of the water and of the earth cause below it”: the subject moves before the verb; ‘of themselves’ is omitted, and “below” acquires the explicit reference “it”.
- *che, quanto posson, retro al calor vanno* ‘which, as far as they can, after the heat go’ → “and which follow so far as they can after the heat”: “and” is supplied, making the relative appear coordinate with the preceding one rather than directly modifying the exhalations; “follow” replaces ‘go’ and moves ahead of the qualification of extent.

> 100 all' uomo non facesse alcuna guerra,  

should do no hurt to man,

- *all' uomo non facesse alcuna guerra* ‘to man should not make any war’ → “should do no hurt to man”: the recipient moves to the end; the singular subjunctive becomes a modal construction with the plural English subject, and ‘not … any war’ becomes “no hurt”.

> 101 questo monte salìo verso 'l ciel tanto;  
> 102 e libero n' è d' indi, ove si serra.  

this mount rose thus far towards heaven, and stands clear of them from where it is locked.

- *verso 'l ciel tanto* ‘towards heaven so much’ → “thus far towards heaven”: the degree of ascent is expressed as distance and placed first.
- *libero n' è* ‘free of them it is’ → “stands clear of them”: the predicate adjective follows the verb, and “stands” replaces the simple copula.
- *d' indi, ove si serra* ‘from there, where it closes itself’ → “from where it is locked”: the separate indication of place is absorbed into “from where”, and the reflexive form is read as passive.

> 103 Or, perchè in circuito tutto e quanto  
> 104 l' aer si volge con la prima volta,  
> 105 se non gli è rotto il cerchio d' alcun canto,  

Now since the whole of the air revolves in a circle with the primal motion, unless its circuit is broken in some direction,

- *in circuito tutto e quanto l' aer si volge* ‘in a circle all and entire the air turns itself’ → “the whole of the air revolves in a circle”: the doubled expression of totality becomes one partitive phrase, the reflexive becomes an intransitive verb, and the initial phrase of motion moves after it.
- *la prima volta* ‘the first revolution’ → “the primal motion”: the ordinal becomes “primal”, and the more general “motion” replaces ‘revolution’.
- *se non gli è rotto il cerchio* ‘if its circle is not broken’ → “unless its circuit is broken”: “unless” absorbs the negative; the possessive dative becomes “its”, and the subject moves before the passive verb.
- *d' alcun canto* ‘on some side’ → “in some direction”: direction is substituted for side.

> 106 in questa altezza, che in tutto è disciolta  
> 107 nell' aer vivo, tal moto percote,  
> 108 e fa suonar la selva perch' è folta;  

such motion strikes on this eminence, which is all free in the pure air, and makes the wood to sound because it is dense;

- *in questa altezza … tal moto percote* ‘on this height … such motion strikes’ → “such motion strikes on this eminence”: the subject and verb move ahead of the phrase of place and its relative clause; “eminence” renders ‘height’ as a place.
- *in tutto è disciolta* ‘entirely is unbound’ → “is all free”: the qualification of degree follows the copula, and an adjective replaces the participle.
- *nell' aer vivo* ‘in the living air’ → “in the pure air”: “pure” interprets rather than reproduces the adjective.
- *fa suonar la selva* ‘makes sound the wood’ → “makes the wood to sound”: the wood comes before the infinitive, which is introduced by “to”.

> 109 e la percossa pianta tanto puote,  
> 110 che della sua virtute l' aura impregna,  
> 111 e quella poi girando intorno scote;  

and the smitten plant has such power that with its virtue it impregnates the air, and that in its revolution then scatters it abroad:

- *tanto puote* ‘so much can it do’ → “has such power”: the verb of ability and its adverb of degree become “has” with a qualified noun.
- *della sua virtute l' aura impregna* ‘with its virtue the breeze it impregnates’ → “with its virtue it impregnates the air”: “air” replaces ‘breeze’, and the object moves after the verb.
- *e quella poi girando intorno scote* ‘and that then turning round shakes [it] off’ → “and that in its revolution then scatters it abroad”: the gerund of circular movement becomes a prepositional phrase placed before “then”; the shaking is rendered by its dispersive result, with an explicit object and the added adverb “abroad”.

## Lines 112–144 — The Seeds, the Waters, and the Golden Age

> 112 e l' altra terra, secondo ch' è degna  
> 113 per sè e per suo ciel, concepe e figlia  
> 114 di diverse virtù diverse legna.  

and the other land, according as it is worthy of itself and of its climate, conceives and brings forth divers trees of divers virtues.

- *per sè e per suo ciel* ‘through itself and through its heaven’ → “of itself and of its climate”: the two phrases giving the grounds of the land’s worth are attached to “worthy” with “of”; the sky is rendered by its climatic influence.
- *figlia* ‘gives birth’ → “brings forth”: the verb is rendered by a phrase that can take “trees” as its object.
- *di diverse virtù diverse legna* ‘of divers virtues divers woods’ → “divers trees of divers virtues”: the quality follows the things qualified, and the woods are rendered as living trees.

> 115 Non parrebbe di là poi maraviglia,  
> 116 udito questo, quando alcuna pianta  
> 117 senza seme palese vi s' appiglia.  

Were this understood, it would not then seem a marvel yonder when some plant takes root there without manifest seed.

- *Non parrebbe di là poi maraviglia* ‘would not seem yonder then a marvel’ → “it would not then seem a marvel yonder”: the main clause is put after the condition, “then” precedes the main verb, and the indication of place comes last.
- *udito questo* ‘this having been heard’ → “Were this understood”: the absolute participial phrase becomes an inverted conditional clause, and hearing becomes understanding.
- *senza seme palese vi s’ appiglia* ‘without seed manifest there attaches itself’ → “takes root there without manifest seed”: the reflexive verb becomes an idiom for a plant’s establishing itself; the place follows the verb, the phrase about seed comes last, and its adjective precedes the noun.

> 118 E saper dei che la campagna santa,  
> 119 ove tu sei, d' ogni semenza è piena,  
> 120 e frutto ha in sè che di là non si schianta.  

And thou must know that the holy plain where thou art, is full of every seed, and bears fruit in it which yonder is not plucked.

- *saper dei* ‘know thou must’ → “thou must know”: the modal precedes the infinitive.
- *la campagna santa* ‘the plain holy’ → “the holy plain”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *d’ ogni semenza è piena* ‘of every seed is full’ → “is full of every seed”: the complement follows the adjective.
- *frutto ha in sè* ‘fruit has in itself’ → “bears fruit in it”: possession becomes bearing, the object follows the verb, and a personal pronoun replaces the reflexive.
- *non si schianta* ‘is not broken off’ → “is not plucked”: the passive formed with *si* becomes an English passive, with a verb specific to gathering fruit.

> 121 L' acqua che vedi non surge di vena  
> 122 che ristori vapor che giel converta,  
> 123 come fiume ch' acquista e perde lena;  

The water which thou seest wells not from a spring that is fed by moisture which cold condenses, like a river that gains and loses volume,

- *non surge di vena* ‘rises not from a vein’ → “wells not from a spring”: both the verb and the image of a vein are rendered in the vocabulary of flowing water.
- *che ristori vapor* ‘which vapour may replenish’ → “that is fed by moisture”: the relative object becomes the passive subject, the postposed subject becomes the agent, and the subjunctive becomes indicative; replenishment is expressed as feeding, vapour as moisture.
- *che giel converta* ‘which cold may transform’ → “which cold condenses”: the kind of transformation is specified, and the subjunctive becomes indicative.
- *come fiume* ‘like river’ → “like a river”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *acquista e perde lena* ‘gains and loses vigour’ → “gains and loses volume”: the figurative vigour is rendered as a quantity of water.

> 124 ma esce di fontana salda e certa,  
> 125 che tanto dal voler di Dio riprende,  
> 126 quant' ella versa da due parti aperta.  

but issues from a fount, constant and sure, which regains by God’s will, so much as it pours forth freely on either side.

- *esce di fontana* ‘comes out of fount’ → “issues from a fount”: the motion outward is expressed by a single verb, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *salda* ‘firm’ → “constant”: firmness is rendered as constancy.
- *tanto dal voler di Dio riprende* ‘so much from the will of God takes back’ → “regains by God’s will, so much”: the verb comes first and the quantity last; source becomes agency, and the possessive precedes “will”.
- *da due parti aperta* ‘open on two sides’ → “freely on either side”: the adjective describing the fount becomes an adverb modifying the pouring; the two sides are expressed distributively.

> 127 Da questa parte con virtù discende,  
> 128 che toglie altrui memoria del peccato;  
> 129 dall' altra, d' ogni ben fatto la rende.  

On this side it descends with a virtue which takes from men the memory of sin; on the other it restores the memory of every good deed.

- *con virtù discende* ‘with virtue descends’ → “it descends with a virtue”: the verb precedes the phrase, and the power is individuated by an article.
- *altrui memoria* ‘from one memory’ → “from men the memory”: the indefinite personal pronoun becomes a plural noun, and the object takes a definite article.
- *dall’ altra, d’ ogni ben fatto la rende* ‘on the other, of every good done it gives back’ → “on the other it restores the memory of every good deed”: the verb precedes its object; the object pronoun is replaced by its antecedent, and its complement follows it. The participial expression becomes an adjective and noun.

> 130 Quinci Letè, così dall' altro lato  
> 131 Eunoè si chiama, e non adopra,  
> 132 se quinci e quindi pria non è gustato.  

On this side Lethe, as on the other Eunoe ’tis called, and works not except first it is tasted on this side and on that.

- *Quinci* ‘hence’ → “On this side”: the directional adverb is taken as marking a location.
- *così dall’ altro lato* ‘so on the other side’ → “as on the other”: “as” links the parallel names, and the noun for the side is left understood.
- *si chiama* ‘calls itself’ → “’tis called”: the pronominal naming construction becomes a passive.
- *se quinci e quindi pria non è gustato* ‘if on this side and on that first it is not tasted’ → “except first it is tasted on this side and on that”: the negative condition becomes an affirmative exception; the expressions of place follow the passive instead of preceding it.

> 133 A tutt' altri sapori esto è di sopra;  

This exceedeth all other savours;

- *A tutt’ altri sapori esto è di sopra* ‘to all other savours this is superior’ → “This exceedeth all other savours”: the subject comes first, and a transitive verb replaces the copula and expression of superiority, making the term of comparison a direct object.

> 134 ed avvegna ch' assai possa esser sazia  
> 135 la sete tua, perch' io più non ti scopra,  
> 136 darotti un corollario ancor per grazia;  

and albeit thy thirst may be full sated, even tho’ I reveal no more to thee, I will give thee yet a corollary as a grace;

- *assai possa esser sazia la sete tua* ‘sufficiently may be sated the thirst of thine’ → “thy thirst may be full sated”: the subject is moved before the modal, the possessive before its noun, and the adverb of sufficiency directly before “sated”, with the force of completeness.
- *perch’ io più non ti scopra* ‘though I more reveal not to thee’ → “even tho’ I reveal no more to thee”: the conjunction is taken concessively and strengthened by “even”; English puts the negation and quantity together after the verb, moves the recipient after them, and does not mark the subjunctive.
- *darotti un corollario ancor* ‘I shall give thee a corollary in addition’ → “I will give thee yet a corollary”: the inflected future becomes an auxiliary construction, the attached pronoun becomes a separate word, and the additive adverb precedes the object.
- *per grazia* ‘by favour’ → “as a grace”: the phrase is recast to describe what the gift counts as.

> 137 nè credo che il mio dir ti sia men caro,  
> 138 se oltre promission teco si spazia.  

nor do I think that my words will be less precious to thee if they extend beyond my promise to thee.

- *che il mio dir ti sia men caro* ‘that my speaking to thee be less dear’ → “that my words will be less precious to thee”: the nominal infinitive becomes a plural noun, the subjunctive becomes a future, and the recipient follows the adjective; dearness is expressed as preciousness.
- *se oltre promission teco si spazia* ‘if beyond promise with thee it ranges’ → “if they extend beyond my promise to thee”: the pronominal verb becomes an intransitive plural verb agreeing with “words” and moves before the limit of its range; “my” is supplied, and the companion in the speaking becomes the recipient of the promise.

> 139 Quelli che anticamente poetaro  
> 140 l' età dell' oro e suo stato felice,  
> 141 forse in Parnaso esto loco sognaro.  

They who in olden times sang of the golden age and its happy state, perchance dreamed in Parnassus of this place.

- *anticamente* ‘anciently’ → “in olden times”: an adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *poetaro l’ età dell’ oro* ‘celebrated in verse the age of gold’ → “sang of the golden age”: making poetry becomes singing, the direct object becomes the complement of “of”, and the phrase for gold becomes a preceding adjective.
- *suo stato felice* ‘its state happy’ → “its happy state”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *in Parnaso esto loco sognaro* ‘in Parnassus this place dreamed’ → “dreamed in Parnassus of this place”: the verb moves before both complements, and the direct object becomes the complement of “of”.

> 142 Qui fu innocente l' umana radice;  

Here the root of man’s race was innocent;

- *fu innocente l’ umana radice* ‘was innocent the human root’ → “the root of man’s race was innocent”: the subject precedes the predicate, and the adjective is expanded into a phrase specifying whose root it is.

> 143 qui primavera è sempre, ed ogni frutto;  

here spring is everlasting, and every kind of fruit;

- *è sempre* ‘is always’ → “is everlasting”: the adverb of time becomes a predicate adjective.
- *ogni frutto* ‘every fruit’ → “every kind of fruit”: “kind of” makes the reference to varieties explicit.

> 144 nettare è questo di che ciascun dice.”  

this is the nectar whereof each one tells.”

- *nettare è questo* ‘nectar is this’ → “this is the nectar”: the demonstrative subject precedes the copula, and the predicate noun takes a definite article.

## Lines 145–148 — The Poets’ Smiles

> 145 Io mi volsi di retro allora tutto  
> 146 a' miei poeti, e vidi che con riso  
> 147 udito avevan l' ultimo costrutto;  

Then did I turn me right back to my poets, and saw that with smiles they had heard the last interpretation;

- *Io mi volsi di retro allora tutto* ‘I myself turned back then wholly’ → “Then did I turn me right back”: the time adverb comes first, the simple past is recast with auxiliary “did” before the subject, and the reflexive pronoun moves after the main verb. “right” places the emphasis of *tutto* directly on “back” rather than describing how completely the speaker turned.
- *con riso* ‘with a smile’ → “with smiles”: the singular becomes plural.
- *udito avevan* ‘heard had’ → “they had heard”: the auxiliary precedes the participle.
- *l' ultimo costrutto* ‘the last statement’ → “the last interpretation”: the broader noun for what was said is rendered more specifically as an explanation.

> 148 poi alla bella donna tornai il viso.  

then to the fair Lady I turned my face.

- *il viso* ‘the face’ → “my face”: English uses a possessive where Italian uses the definite article with a part of the body.

## Summary — Order, Emphasis, and Idiom

Okey often keeps Dante’s words but changes their order. In *la prim’ ombra gitta il santo monte*, the shadow comes first and the mount last; in “the holy mount casts its first shadow”, the order is subject, verb, object. Elsewhere the translation stays close even to a marked arrangement: “Three paces” stands first, as does *Tre passi*, and “foot before foot” keeps both the order and the repetition of *piede innanzi piede*.

A change of construction can shift what a phrase describes. *Lento lento* ‘slow, slow’ describes the walker; “with lingering step” describes his step instead, supplying a noun in place of the repetition. The imperfect *mi feria* becomes the progressive “was striking”, while *di levar gli occhi suoi mi fece dono* ‘she made me a gift of raising her eyes’ becomes “she vouchsafed to raise her eyes to me”: the courtesy expressed by the noun for a gift passes into the verb.

Okey’s choice of words sometimes singles out a quality that the Italian leaves open: *viva* ‘alive’ becomes “verdant”, and *il suon* ‘the sound’ becomes “the music”. At other points a detail drops out, as when *rivedere* ‘see again’ becomes simply “see”. Yet much of the diction is closely matched: “honest laughter and sweet play” retains the paired nouns and their adjectives in *onesto riso e dolce gioco*.

Two routine differences generally pass unremarked in the comparison. English supplies subject pronouns where the Italian verb ending identifies the subject without a separate word, as in *Non credo*, “I do not believe”. Conversely, English leaves out the Italian article before a possessive: *a’ miei poeti* becomes “to my poets”, with the possessive alone introducing the noun.
