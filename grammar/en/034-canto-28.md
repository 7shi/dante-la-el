# Inferno 28

## Lines 1–6 — The Limits of Telling

> 1 Chi poría mai, pur con parole sciolte,  
> 2 Dicer del sangue e delle piaghe appieno,  
> 3 Ch’ i’ ora vidi, per narrar più volte?  

Who, even with words set free, could ever fully tell, by oft relating, the blood and the wounds that I now saw?

- *pur con parole sciolte* ‘even with words unbound’ → “even with words set free”: the phrase moves before “could ever”; “words set free” preserves the literal image rather than explicitly rendering the idiomatic sense ‘in prose’.
- *Dicer del sangue e delle piaghe appieno* ‘tell of the blood and of the wounds fully’ → “fully tell”, “the blood and the wounds”: the adverb moves before the verb, and the prepositional complements become direct objects, with both occurrences of ‘of’ omitted.
- *per narrar più volte* ‘by recounting several times’ → “by oft relating”: the infinitive becomes an English gerund, and ‘several times’ becomes the adverb “oft”, placed before it. The phrase moves from the end of the question to between “tell” and its objects.

> 4 Ogni lingua per certo verria meno  
> 5 Per lo nostro sermone e per la mente,  
> 6 Ch’ hanno a tanto comprender poco seno.  

Every tongue assuredly would fail, because of our speech and our memory that have small capacity to comprehend so much.

- *per certo* ‘for certain’ → “assuredly”: a prepositional phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *verria meno* ‘would come less’ → “would fail”: the Italian idiom is replaced by its English sense.
- *e per la mente* ‘and because of the mind’ → “and our memory”: the repeated preposition is omitted, leaving “because of” to govern both nouns; the definite article becomes the possessive “our”. “Memory” selects one sense of *mente* rather than the broader ‘mind’.
- *a tanto comprender* ‘for so much to comprehend’ → “to comprehend so much”: the infinitival phrase moves after “small capacity”, and its object moves after the verb; the relation expressed by *a* is conveyed by the English infinitive following “capacity”.
- *poco seno* ‘little bosom’ → “small capacity”: the concrete image of a bosom or containing space is rendered as the abstract capacity it signifies.

## Lines 7–18 — A Gathering of War’s Victims

> 7 Se s’ adunasse ancor tutta la gente,  
> 8 Che già in su la fortunata terra  
> 9 Di Puglia fu del suo sangue dolente  

If all the people too were gathered, who of old upon Apulia’s fateful land wailed for their blood,

- *s’ adunasse ancor tutta la gente* ‘should assemble again all the people’ → “all the people too were gathered”: the subject moves before the verb, and the reflexive construction becomes an English passive. The singular collective *gente* takes plural agreement in English; *ancor* is read additively as “too”, rather than temporally as ‘again’.
- *già* ‘already’ → “of old”: the adverb is interpreted as referring to the distant past.
- *in su la fortunata terra Di Puglia* ‘upon the fortunate land of Apulia’ → “upon Apulia’s fateful land”: the geographical modifier becomes a possessive placed before the noun; *fortunata* is interpreted as ‘subject to fate’, rather than ‘fortunate’.
- *fu del suo sangue dolente* ‘was sorrowful for its blood’ → “wailed for their blood”: the construction with ‘was’ and an adjective becomes a verb expressing an audible lament. The complement, placed between verb and adjective in Italian, follows the English verb; the singular possessive referring to the collective *gente* becomes plural “their”.

> 10 Per li Romani, e per la lunga guerra  
> 11 Che dell’ anella fe’ sì alte spoglie,  
> 12 Come Livio scrive, che non erra;  

shed by the Romans; and in that long war which made so vast a spoil of rings, as Livy writes, who errs not;

- *Per li Romani* ‘because of the Romans’ → “shed by the Romans”: “shed” is supplied, making the phrase modify ‘blood’ directly; the causal expression becomes the agent of a passive participle.
- *e per la lunga guerra* ‘and because of the long war’ → “and in that long war”: the causal preposition becomes one expressing the circumstances in which the blood was shed, and the definite article becomes demonstrative “that”.
- *Che dell’ anella fe’ sì alte spoglie* ‘which of the rings made so high spoils’ → “which made so vast a spoil of rings”: the phrase modifying ‘spoils’ moves after its noun and loses its definite article. *Alte* is interpreted as indicating magnitude, “vast”; plural *spoglie* becomes singular “a spoil”.

> 13 Con quella, che sentío di colpi doglie  
> 14 Per contrastare a Ruberto Guiscardo;  

with those who, by withstanding Robert Guiscard, felt the pains of blows;

- *Con quella* ‘with that [people]’ → “with those”: the singular demonstrative referring to the collective *gente* becomes a plural pronoun referring to individuals.
- *sentío di colpi doglie* ‘felt of blows pains’ → “felt the pains of blows”: the modifying phrase moves after ‘pains’, and a definite article is supplied.
- *Per contrastare a Ruberto Guiscardo* ‘for resisting Robert Guiscard’ → “by withstanding Robert Guiscard”: the causal expression becomes an English construction with “by” and a gerund. *Contrastare a* is rendered by the directly transitive “withstanding”; the whole phrase moves before the verb it modifies.

> 15 E l’ altra, il cui ossame ancor s’ accoglie  
> 16 A Ceperan, là dove fu bugiardo  
> 17 Ciascun Pugliese; e là da Tagliacozzo,  
> 18 Ove senz’ arme vinse il vecchio Alardo:  

and the rest whose bones are gathered still at Ceperano, where each Apulian proved false; and there at Tagliacozzo, where old Alardo conquered without weapons:

- *E l’ altra* ‘and the other [people]’ → “and the rest”: the singular expression designating another collective group becomes an expression designating those remaining.
- *il cui ossame ancor s’ accoglie* ‘whose collection of bones still is gathered’ → “whose bones are gathered still”: the singular collective *ossame* becomes plural “bones”, with corresponding plural agreement; the adverb moves after the verb.
- *là dove fu bugiardo Ciascun Pugliese* ‘there where was false each Apulian’ → “where each Apulian proved false”: ‘there’ is omitted, the subject moves before the verb, and ‘was’ becomes “proved”, presenting the falseness as something demonstrated.
- *Ove senz’ arme vinse il vecchio Alardo* ‘where without weapons conquered the old Alardo’ → “where old Alardo conquered without weapons”: the subject moves before the verb, and the phrase ‘without weapons’ moves after it. The article before the name and its descriptive adjective is omitted.

## Lines 19–31 — The Mangling of Mahomet

> 19 E qual forato suo membro, e qual mozzo  
> 20 Mostrasse, d’ agguagliar sarebbe nulla  
> 21 Il modo della nona bolgia sozzo.  

and one should shew his limbs transpierced, and another his cut off; it were nought to equal the hideous mode of the ninth chasm.

- *E qual forato suo membro, e qual mozzo Mostrasse* ‘and one a pierced member of his, and another a severed one, should show’ → “and one should shew his limbs transpierced, and another his cut off”: the shared verb is moved before the first object; singular *membro* becomes plural “limbs”; and the participles follow their objects instead of preceding them.
- *d’ agguagliar sarebbe nulla* ‘for equalling it would be nothing’ → “it were nought to equal”: the infinitive moves after the main clause, and the conditional becomes the hypothetical “were”.
- *Il modo della nona bolgia sozzo* ‘the foul manner of the ninth ditch’ → “the hideous mode of the ninth chasm”: the adjective moves from the end of the phrase to before its noun; “hideous” renders foulness as repulsiveness, and “chasm” replaces ‘ditch’.

> 22 Già veggia, per mezzul perdere o lulla,  
> 23 Com’ io vidi un, così non si pertugia,  
> 24 Rotto dal mento insin dove si trulla.  

Even a cask, through loss of middle-piece or cant, yawns not so wide as one I saw, ripped from the chin down to the part that utters vilest sound.

- *Già* ‘indeed’ → “Even”: the emphatic affirmation becomes an expression emphasizing the unexpected comparison.
- *per mezzul perdere o lulla* ‘through losing a middle-piece or side-piece’ → “through loss of middle-piece or cant”: the infinitive becomes the noun “loss”, followed by “of”; the objects, which surround the Italian infinitive, both follow the English noun. “Cant” supplies a technical term for the side-piece.
- *Com’ io vidi un, così non si pertugia* ‘as I saw one, so it does not become holed’ → “yawns not so wide as one I saw”: the comparison is reordered into the English construction “so wide as”; “wide” is supplied, and becoming holed is rendered as yawning open. The object of ‘I saw’ becomes the antecedent of an implicit relative clause.
- *Rotto* ‘broken’ → “ripped”: the translation specifies tearing rather than breaking.
- *insin dove si trulla* ‘as far as where one farts’ → “down to the part that utters vilest sound”: “down” makes the direction explicit, and the location expressed by ‘where’ becomes “the part”. The impersonal verb becomes a relative clause with that part as its subject; the blunt verb is replaced by a euphemistic description with the added judgment “vilest”.

> 25 Tra le gambe pendevan le minugia;  
> 26 La corata pareva, e il tristo sacco  
> 27 Che merda fa di quel che si trangugia.  

Between his legs the entrails hung; the pluck appeared, and the wretched sack that makes excrement of what it swallowed.

- *Tra le gambe pendevan le minugia* ‘between the legs hung the entrails’ → “Between his legs the entrails hung”: the definite article becomes the possessive “his”, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *Che merda fa* ‘that shit makes’ → “that makes excrement”: the object moves after the verb, and the coarse noun becomes the more formal “excrement”.
- *di quel che si trangugia* ‘of that which one gulps down’ → “of what it swallowed”: the demonstrative and relative become “what”; the impersonal construction becomes a personal one referring to the sack, and the present becomes past. “Swallowed” also loses the suggestion of gulping or devouring.

> 28 Mentre che tutto in lui veder m’ attacco,  
> 29 Guardommi, e con le man s’ aperse il petto,  

Whilst I stood all occupied in seeing him, he looked at me, and with his hands opened his breast,

- *tutto in lui veder m’ attacco* ‘I apply myself to seeing everything in him’ → “I stood all occupied in seeing him”: Carlyle takes *tutto* as expressing complete absorption rather than as the object of seeing. The reflexive expression of applying oneself becomes a description of being occupied, “stood” is supplied, and the present becomes past; ‘in him’ becomes the direct object “him”.
- *Guardommi* ‘he regarded me’ → “he looked at me”: the Italian direct object becomes the object of the English preposition “at”.
- *con le man* ‘with the hands’ → “with his hands”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *s’ aperse il petto* ‘opened for himself the breast’ → “opened his breast”: the reflexive pronoun is represented by the possessive “his”, rather than by a separate pronoun.

> 30 Dicendo: Or vedi come io mi dilacco:  

saying: “Now see how I dilacerate myself!

- *io mi dilacco* ‘I myself tear apart’ → “I dilacerate myself”: the reflexive object moves from before the verb to after it.

> 31 Vedi come storpiato è Maometto.  

See how Mahomet is mangled!

- *storpiato è Maometto* ‘maimed is Mahomet’ → “Mahomet is mangled”: the predicate-first order becomes subject–verb–predicate, and maiming is rendered as mangling.

## Lines 32–47 — The Cleft Sowers of Schism

> 32 Dinanzi a me sen va piangendo Alì  
> 33 Fesso nel volto dal mento al ciuffetto.  

Before me Ali weeping goes, cleft in the face from chin to forelock.

- *sen va piangendo Alì* ‘goes away weeping Ali’ → “Ali weeping goes”: the subject moves before the verb, and the participle also precedes it; the pronominal expression for going away or onwards is reduced to “goes”.
- *ciuffetto* ‘little tuft’ → “forelock”: the translation specifies the tuft’s position, while leaving the diminutive unexpressed.

> 34 E tutti gli altri, che tu vedi qui,  
> 35 Seminator di scandalo e di scisma  
> 36 Fur vivi; e però son fessi così.  

And all the others, whom thou seest here, were in their lifetime sowers of scandal and of schism; and therefore are they thus cleft.

- *Fur vivi* ‘were alive’ → “were in their lifetime”: the adjective becomes a temporal prepositional phrase, with “their” supplied; the verb and its temporal qualification move before “sowers of scandal and of schism”.
- *son fessi così* ‘are cleft thus’ → “are they thus cleft”: “thus” moves before “cleft”, and the supplied subject follows the verb.

> 37 Un Diavolo è qua dietro, che n’ accisma  
> 38 Sì crudelmente, al taglio della spada  
> 39 Rimettendo ciascun di questa risma,  

A Devil is here behind, who splits us thus cruelly, reapplying each of this class to his sword’s edge,

- *n’ accisma* ‘dresses us up’ → “splits us”: the ironic expression for dressing or trimming is replaced by an explicit description of cutting; the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *Sì crudelmente* ‘so cruelly’ → “thus cruelly”: the expression of degree becomes an expression of manner.
- *al taglio della spada* ‘to the edge of the sword’ → “to his sword’s edge”: the phrase moves after the participle and its object; the noun complement becomes a possessive construction, and “his” replaces the definite article.
- *Rimettendo* ‘putting back’ → “reapplying”: the general verb of putting back is replaced by a verb expressing renewed application.
- *di questa risma* ‘of this ream’ → “of this class”: the figurative description of a group as a ream is replaced by its nonfigurative meaning.

> 40 Quando avem volta la dolente strada;  
> 41 Perocchè le ferite son richiuse  
> 42 Prima ch’ altri dinanzi li rivada.  

when we have wandered round the doleful road; for the wounds heal up ere any goes again before him.

- *avem volta* ‘we have gone round’ → “we have wandered round”: “wandered” adds a characterization of the movement not explicit in the Italian verb.
- *la dolente strada* ‘the road doleful’ → “the doleful road”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Perocchè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *le ferite son richiuse* ‘the wounds are closed again’ → “the wounds heal up”: the passive or resultative construction becomes an active expression of healing; renewed closure is conveyed by “heal up” rather than explicitly by ‘again’.
- *altri dinanzi li rivada* ‘anyone before him goes again’ → “any goes again before him”: the phrase meaning ‘before him’ moves after the verb, and the verb’s prefix expressing repetition becomes the separate adverb “again”.

> 43 Ma tu chi sei, che in su lo scoglio muse,  
> 44 Forse per indugiar d’ ire alla pena,  
> 45 Ch’ è giudicata in su le tue accuse?  

But who art thou, that musest on the cliff, perhaps in order to delay thy going to the punishment, adjudged upon thy accusations?”

- *tu chi sei* ‘thou who art’ → “who art thou”: the interrogative comes first, followed by the verb and then the subject.
- *in su lo scoglio muse* ‘upon the cliff musest’ → “musest on the cliff”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase, and the compound Italian preposition becomes “on”.
- *per* ‘to’ → “in order to”: the purpose construction is rendered more explicitly.
- *indugiar d’ ire* ‘delay to go’ → “delay thy going”: the dependent infinitive becomes a possessive gerund construction, with “thy” explicitly identifying the person going.
- *Ch’ è giudicata* ‘which is adjudged’ → “adjudged”: the relative pronoun and finite auxiliary are omitted, leaving a reduced passive relative clause.

> 46 Nè morte il giunse ancor, nè colpa il mena,  
> 47 Rispose il mio Maestro, a tormentarlo;  

“Not yet has death come to him; nor does guilt lead him,” replied my Master, “to torment him.

- *Nè morte il giunse ancor* ‘neither death reached him yet’ → “Not yet has death come to him”: the first negative becomes “Not”, and “yet” moves to the opening; the simple past becomes a present perfect, with auxiliary–subject inversion. The transitive verb ‘reached’ becomes “come to”, turning the direct object into a prepositional complement.
- *nè colpa il mena* ‘nor guilt him leads’ → “nor does guilt lead him”: English introduces the auxiliary “does” and places it before the subject after “nor”; the object pronoun moves after the main verb.

## Lines 48–54 — Full Experience and Forgotten Torment

> 48 Ma per dar lui esperienza piena,  
> 49 A me, che morto son, convien menarlo  
> 50 Per lo Inferno quaggiù di giro in giro:  

But to give him full experience, it behooves me, who am dead, to lead him through the Hell down here, from round to round:

- *esperienza piena* ‘experience full’ → “full experience”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *A me … convien* ‘to me … it is fitting’ → “it behooves me”: the Italian impersonal construction with a dative becomes an English construction with “me” as the object; the governing verb moves before the relative clause.
- *che morto son* ‘who dead am’ → “who am dead”: the predicate adjective moves after the verb.
- *di giro in giro* ‘from round into round’ → “from round to round”: “to” replaces the literal ‘into’ in the English expression of progression.

> 51 E questo è ver così, com’ io ti parlo.  

and this is true as that I speak to thee.”

- *così, com’* ‘so, as’ → “as that”: the correlative construction is compressed, with no separate equivalent for *così*, and “that” is added to introduce the proposition used for comparison.
- *io ti parlo* ‘I to thee speak’ → “I speak to thee”: the preverbal indirect-object pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase after the verb.

> 52 Più fur di cento, che quando l’ udiro,  
> 53 S’ arrestaron nel fosso a riguardarmi,  
> 54 Per maraviglia obliando il martiro.  

More than a hundred, when they heard him, stopped in the fosse to look at me, through wonder forgetting their torment.

- *Più fur di cento, che* ‘more were than a hundred, who’ → “More than a hundred”: the introductory construction and following relative clause become a single main clause. *fur* and *che* have no separate equivalents, and the numerical phrase becomes the subject of “stopped”.
- *quando l’ udiro* ‘when him they heard’ → “when they heard him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *S’ arrestaron* ‘they halted themselves’ → “stopped”: the Italian pronominal verb is rendered by an English intransitive verb without a reflexive pronoun.
- *il martiro* ‘the torment’ → “their torment”: the definite article is replaced by a possessive.

## Lines 55–72 — A Warning And Another Speaker

> 55 Or di’ a Fra Dolcin dunque che s’ armi,  
> 56 Tu che forse vedrai il Sole in breve,  
> 57 S’ egli non vuol qui tosto seguitarmi,  
> 58 Sì di vivanda, che stretta di neve  

“Well, then, thou who perhaps shalt see the Sun ere long, tell Fra Dolcino, if he wish not speedily to follow me down here, so to arm himself with victuals, that stress of snow

- *Or ... dunque* ‘Now ... then’ → “Well, then”: the two introductory words are brought together, and ‘now’ becomes the conversational “Well”.
- *in breve* ‘shortly’ → “ere long”: the Italian prepositional expression becomes an English phrase meaning ‘before long’.
- *di’ a Fra Dolcin ... che s’ armi, Tu che forse vedrai il Sole* ‘tell Fra Dolcino ... that he arm himself, thou who perhaps shalt see the Sun’ → “thou who perhaps shalt see the Sun”, “tell Fra Dolcino”, and “to arm himself”: the address is moved before the command to tell Fra Dolcino; the subordinate clause with a subjunctive becomes an infinitival command, and its instruction is placed after the conditional clause.
- *S’ egli non vuol* ‘if he does not wish’ → “if he wish not”: the indicative becomes an English subjunctive, and the negative follows the verb.
- *qui tosto seguitarmi* ‘here soon to follow me’ → “speedily to follow me down here”: the place adverb moves after the verb and its object, “down” is supplied, and ‘soon’ becomes “speedily”.
- *Sì di vivanda* ‘so with provision’ → “so” and “with victuals”: the two elements are separated by the infinitive they qualify, and the Italian singular collective noun becomes an English plural.
- *stretta di neve* ‘a grip of snow’ → “stress of snow”: the image of constriction is rendered as pressure or hardship.

> 59 Non rechi la vittoria al Noarese,  
> 60 Ch’ altrimenti acquistar non saria lieve.  

may not bring victory to the Novarese, which otherwise would not be easy to attain.”

- *la vittoria* ‘the victory’ → “victory”: the definite article is omitted.
- *Ch’ altrimenti acquistar non saria lieve* ‘which otherwise to acquire would not be light’ → “which otherwise would not be easy to attain”: the infinitive moves after the adjective; ‘acquire’ becomes “attain”, and figurative ‘light’ becomes “easy”.

> 61 Poi che l’ un piè per girsene sospese,  
> 62 Maometto mi disse esta parola;  
> 63 Indi a partirsi in terra lo distese.  

After lifting up one foot to go away, Mahomet said this to me; then on the ground he stretched it to depart.

- *Poi che ... sospese* ‘after ... he raised’ → “After lifting up”: a finite temporal clause becomes a participial construction, and the verb moves before its object and the purpose phrase.
- *l’ un piè* ‘the one foot’ → “one foot”: the definite article is omitted.
- *mi disse esta parola* ‘to me said this word’ → “said this to me”: the indirect object moves after the direct object, and ‘word’ is omitted, leaving “this” as a pronoun.
- *a partirsi in terra lo distese* ‘to depart on the ground he stretched it’ → “on the ground he stretched it to depart”: the purpose infinitive moves to the end.

> 64 Un altro, che forata avea la gola  
> 65 E tronco il naso infin sotto le ciglia,  
> 66 E non avea ma’ ch’ un’ orecchia sola,  

Another, who had his throat pierced through, and nose cut off up to the eyebrows, and had but one single ear,

- *che forata avea la gola* ‘who pierced had the throat’ → “who had his throat pierced through”: the participle moves after the noun, the definite article becomes a possessive, and “through” makes the extent of the piercing explicit.
- *E tronco il naso* ‘and cut off the nose’ → “and nose cut off”: the participle moves after the noun, and the Italian definite article has no separate English equivalent.
- *infin sotto le ciglia* ‘as far as under the eyebrows’ → “up to the eyebrows”: the explicit position ‘under’ is lost, while “up” supplies a direction.
- *E non avea ma’ ch’ un’ orecchia sola* ‘and did not have except one ear alone’ → “and had but one single ear”: the negative construction with an exception becomes an affirmative verb restricted by “but”; the postposed adjective becomes “single” before the noun.

> 67 Restato a riguardar per maraviglia  
> 68 Con gli altri, innanzi agli altri aprì la canna,  
> 69 Ch’ era di fuor d’ ogni parte vermiglia;  

standing to gaze in wonder with the rest, before the rest opened his weasand, which outwardly was red on every part,

- *Restato* ‘having stopped’ → “standing”: the past participle marking a completed stopping becomes a present participle describing a continuing posture.
- *per maraviglia* ‘because of wonder’ → “in wonder”: a phrase expressing cause becomes one expressing the speaker’s state.
- *Con gli altri, innanzi agli altri* ‘with the others, before the others’ → “with the rest, before the rest”: both occurrences of the plural ‘others’ become the collective singular “rest”.
- *aprì la canna* ‘opened the pipe’ → “opened his weasand”: the figurative ‘pipe’ becomes an explicit anatomical term for the throat, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *di fuor* ‘on the outside’ → “outwardly”: the prepositional expression becomes an adverb and moves before the verb.
- *d’ ogni parte vermiglia* ‘on every side vermilion’ → “red on every part”: the adjective moves before the prepositional phrase, the more specific colour becomes “red”, and ‘side’ becomes ‘part’.

> 70 E disse: O tu, cui colpa non condanna,  
> 71 E cui già vidi su in terra Latina,  
> 72 Se troppa simiglianza non m’ inganna,  

and said: “O thou! whom guilt condemns not, and whom I have seen above on Latian ground ere now, unless too much resemblance deceive me;

- *colpa non condanna* ‘guilt not condemns’ → “guilt condemns not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *già vidi* ‘already I saw’ → “I have seen” and “ere now”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, and the adverb becomes a phrase placed at the end of the relative clause.
- *in terra Latina* ‘in land Latin’ → “on Latian ground”: ‘in’ becomes “on”, ‘land’ becomes “ground”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Se troppa simiglianza non m’ inganna* ‘if too much resemblance does not deceive me’ → “unless too much resemblance deceive me”: ‘if ... not’ becomes “unless”, the indicative becomes an English subjunctive, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.

## Lines 73–81 — A Warning of Treachery

> 73 Rimembriti di Pier da Medicina,  

remember Pier da Medicina,

- *Rimembriti di* ‘bethink yourself of’ → “remember”: the reflexive construction with *di* becomes a transitive verb taking the person remembered as its direct object.

> 74 Se mai torni a veder lo dolce piano,  
> 75 Che da Vercello a Marcabò dichina;  

if ever thou return to see the gentle plain that from Vercelli slopes to Marcabò;

- *dolce* ‘sweet’ → “gentle”: the adjective is rendered in terms of the landscape’s mild character rather than literally as sweetness.
- *Che da Vercello a Marcabò dichina* ‘that from Vercello to Marcabò slopes’ → “that from Vercelli slopes to Marcabò”: the verb moves before the phrase naming the endpoint.

> 76 E fa saper a’ duo miglior di Fano,  
> 77 A messer Guido, e anche ad Angiolello,  

and make known to the worthiest two of Fano, to Messer Guido and to Angiolello likewise,

- *fa saper* ‘make know’ → “make known”: the Italian causative construction with an infinitive becomes an English construction with a past participle.
- *a’ duo miglior di Fano* ‘to the two best of Fano’ → “to the worthiest two of Fano”: “worthiest” gives a more specific sense to *miglior*, and the adjective precedes rather than follows the numeral.
- *e anche ad Angiolello* ‘and also to Angiolello’ → “and to Angiolello likewise”: *anche* becomes “likewise” and moves after the name.

> 78 Che, se l’ antiveder qui non è vano,  

that, unless our foresight here be vain,

- *se l’ antiveder qui non è vano* ‘if the foreseeing here is not vain’ → “unless our foresight here be vain”: the negative condition becomes an “unless” clause; the substantivized infinitive becomes the noun “foresight”; the definite article is replaced by “our”; and the indicative *è* becomes the English subjunctive “be”.

> 79 Gittati saran fuor di lor vasello,  
> 80 E mazzerati presso alla Cattolica,  
> 81 Per tradimento d’ un tiranno fello.  

they shall be cast out of their ship, and drowned near the Cattolica, by a fell tyrant’s treachery.

- *Gittati saran* ‘cast they will be’ → “they shall be cast”: the fronted participle moves after the subject and auxiliary verbs, giving ordinary English passive word order.
- *vasello* ‘little vessel’ → “ship”: the diminutive form is rendered without a separate indication of smallness.
- *mazzerati* ‘drowned with weights attached’ → “drowned”: the translation leaves out the specific manner of drowning expressed by the Italian verb.
- *Per tradimento d’ un tiranno fello* ‘by treachery of a tyrant fell’ → “by a fell tyrant’s treachery”: the *di* phrase becomes a possessive preceding “treachery”, and the adjective moves before “tyrant”.

## Lines 82–93 — Treachery and a Bitter Sight

> 82 Tra l’ isola di Cipri e di Maiolica  
> 83 Non vide mai sì gran fallo Nettuno,  
> 84 Non da Pirati, non da gente Argolica.  

Between the isles of Cyprus and Majorca, Neptune never saw so great a crime—not even with Pirates, not even with Argives.

- *l’ isola di Cipri e di Maiolica* ‘the island of Cyprus and of Majorca’ → “the isles of Cyprus and Majorca”: the singular becomes plural, and the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *Non vide mai sì gran fallo Nettuno* ‘not ever saw so great a fault Neptune’ → “Neptune never saw so great a crime”: the subject moves from the end to the beginning; the negative expression becomes “never”; and *fallo*, ‘fault’ or ‘wrongdoing’, is rendered more specifically as “crime”.
- *Non da Pirati, non da gente Argolica* ‘not by pirates, not by Argolic people’ → “not even with Pirates, not even with Argives”: “even” is supplied twice, “with” replaces ‘by’, and the noun with its adjective becomes the collective name “Argives”.

> 85 Quel traditor che vede pur con l’ uno,  
> 86 E tien la terra, che tal è qui meco  
> 87 Vorrebbe di vedere esser digiuno,  

That traitor who sees with but one eye, and holds the land which one who is here with me would wish that he had never seen,

- *che vede pur con l’ uno* ‘who sees only with the one’ → “who sees with but one eye”: “but” renders the restrictive *pur* and follows rather than precedes “with”; the article is omitted, and the understood noun “eye” is supplied.
- *tal è qui meco* ‘such a one is here with me’ → “one who is here with me”: the Italian parenthetical statement becomes a noun phrase qualified by a relative clause, with “who” supplied.
- *Vorrebbe di vedere esser digiuno* ‘would wish to be fasting from seeing’ → “would wish that he had never seen”: the figurative expression ‘to be fasting from seeing’ becomes a statement of never having seen. The infinitive construction becomes a finite clause with a past-perfect verb, making the seeing explicitly anterior to the wish.

> 88 Farà venirli a parlamento seco;  

will make them come to parley with him;

- *Farà venirli* ‘will make come them’ → “will make them come”: the object pronoun attached to the Italian infinitive moves before the English infinitive.
- *a parlamento* ‘to a conference’ → “to parley”: the noun phrase is rendered as a verbal expression of purpose.
- *seco* ‘with himself’ → “with him”: the Italian reflexive becomes an ordinary personal pronoun.

> 89 Poi farà sì, che al vento di Focara  
> 90 Non farà lor mestier voto nè preco.  

then act so, that they shall need no vow nor prayer for Focara’s wind.”

- *Poi farà sì* ‘then will do so’ → “then act so”: *fare* becomes “act”, and the repeated future auxiliary is left understood from the preceding clause.
- *al vento di Focara* ‘for the wind of Focara’ → “for Focara’s wind”: the phrase moves from immediately after the conjunction to the end of the clause, and the prepositional expression of possession becomes an English possessive.
- *Non farà lor mestier voto nè preco* ‘neither vow nor prayer will be necessary to them’ → “they shall need no vow nor prayer”: the construction expressing necessity becomes the active verb “need”. The indirect object *lor* becomes the subject “they”, while ‘vow’ and ‘prayer’ become objects; the verbal negation is expressed through “no”.

> 91 Ed io a lui: Dimostrami e dichiara,  

And I to him: “Shew me and explain,

- Literal.

> 92 Se vuoi ch’ io porti su di te novella,  

so thou wouldst have me carry tidings up of thee,

- *Se* ‘if’ → “so”: the conditional conjunction is rendered by the older conditional use of “so”.
- *vuoi ch’ io porti* ‘you wish that I carry’ → “thou wouldst have me carry”: the present indicative becomes a modal expression with “wouldst”; the finite subordinate clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction after “have”.
- *su di te novella* ‘up of you news’ → “tidings up of thee”: the noun moves before the adverb and prepositional phrase, and singular *novella* becomes plural “tidings”.

> 93 Chi è colui dalla veduta amara.  

who he is that rues that sight.”

- *Chi è colui* ‘who is that one’ → “who he is”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes “he”, and the subject precedes the verb in the English indirect question.
- *dalla veduta amara* ‘of the bitter sight’ → “that rues that sight”: the prepositional description becomes a finite relative clause. The adjective ‘bitter’ is recast as the verb “rues”, and the definite article becomes the demonstrative “that”.

## Lines 94–107 — Curio’s Silence and Mosca’s Cry

> 94 Allor pose la mano alla mascella  
> 95 D’ un suo compagno, e la bocca gli aperse,  
> 96 Gridando: Questi è desso, e non favella:  

Then he laid his hand upon the jaw of one of his companions; and opened the mouth of him, saying: “This is he, and he speaks not.

- *la mano* ‘the hand’ → “his hand”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *alla mascella* ‘to the jaw’ → “upon the jaw”: the preposition expresses contact rather than direction.
- *D’ un suo compagno* ‘of a companion of his’ → “of one of his companions”: the singular noun becomes a plural within a partitive construction.
- *e la bocca gli aperse* ‘and the mouth to him opened’ → “and opened the mouth of him”: the object moves after the verb, and the possessive indirect pronoun becomes an explicit possessive phrase after the noun.
- *Gridando* ‘shouting’ → “saying”: the participial construction remains, but the force of the utterance is omitted.
- *Questi è desso* ‘This is that very one’ → “This is he”: the emphatic identification in *desso* is reduced to a personal pronoun.

> 97 Questi scacciato il dubitar sommerse  
> 98 In Cesare, affermando che il fornito  
> 99 Sempre con danno l’ attender sofferse.  

This outcast quenched the doubt in Cæsar, affirming that to men prepared delay is always hurtful.”

- *Questi scacciato* ‘This one, driven out’ → “This outcast”: the participle describing the demonstrative becomes a noun qualified by it.
- *il dubitar sommerse* ‘the doubting submerged’ → “quenched the doubt”: the object moves after the verb, the substantival infinitive becomes a noun, and submerging becomes quenching.
- *il fornito Sempre con danno l’ attender sofferse* ‘the prepared one always with harm endured the waiting’ → “to men prepared delay is always hurtful”: the singular substantivized adjective becomes a plural noun with an adjective and is recast as a phrase beginning with “to”; the substantival infinitive becomes “delay” and takes over as subject. The past-tense verb of enduring becomes present-tense “is”, while the adverbial phrase ‘with harm’ becomes the predicate adjective “hurtful”.

> 100 O quanto mi pareva sbigottito,  
> 101 Con la lingua tagliata nella strozza,  
> 102 Curio, ch’ a dicer fu così ardito!  

Oh, how dejected, with tongue slit in his gorge, seemed Curio to me, who was so daring in his speech!

- *mi pareva … Curio* ‘to me seemed … Curio’ → “seemed Curio to me”: the experiencer moves from before the verb to after the subject.
- *sbigottito* ‘dismayed’ → “dejected”: alarm or bewilderment becomes despondency, and the adjective moves before the verb and the intervening description of the tongue.
- *la lingua tagliata* ‘the tongue cut’ → “tongue slit”: the article is omitted, and the general cutting is rendered as slitting.
- *nella strozza* ‘in the throat’ → “in his gorge”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *a dicer fu così ardito* ‘in speaking was so daring’ → “was so daring in his speech”: the infinitive becomes a noun with an added possessive, and its phrase moves after the predicate adjective.

> 103 Ed un, ch’ avea l’ una e l’ altra man mozza,  
> 104 Levando i moncherin per l’ aura fosca,  
> 105 Sì che il sangue facea la faccia sozza,  

And one who had both hands cut off, raising the stumps through the dim air so that their blood defiled his face,

- *l’ una e l’ altra man* ‘the one and the other hand’ → “both hands”: the paired singular expressions are compressed into a plural construction.
- *l’ aura fosca* ‘the air dark’ → “the dim air”: the adjective moves before the noun, and darkness is rendered as dimness.
- *il sangue* ‘the blood’ → “their blood”: the article becomes a possessive referring to the stumps.
- *facea … sozza* ‘made … filthy’ → “defiled”: the verb-plus-adjective construction becomes a single transitive verb.
- *la faccia* ‘the face’ → “his face”: the article becomes a possessive.

> 106 Gridò: Ricorderaiti anche del Mosca,  
> 107 Che dissi, lasso! Capo ha cosa fatta:  

said: “Thou wilt recollect the Mosca too, ah me! who said, ‘A thing done has an end!’

- *Gridò* ‘cried out’ → “said”: the force of the utterance is omitted.
- *Ricorderaiti … del Mosca* ‘You will remember yourself … of the Mosca’ → “Thou wilt recollect the Mosca”: the Italian pronominal verb with a complement introduced by *di* becomes a transitive English verb with a direct object.
- *anche* ‘also’ → “too”: the adverb moves from before the complement to after it.
- *lasso!* ‘wretched!’ → “ah me!”: the adjective used as a lament becomes an interjection and moves before the relative clause.
- *Capo ha cosa fatta* ‘End has thing done’ → “A thing done has an end!”: object–verb–subject order becomes subject–verb–object, and indefinite articles are supplied for both nouns.

## Lines 108–120 — Grief and a Headless Trunk

> 108 Che fu il mal seme della gente Tosca.  

which was the seed of evil to the Tuscan people.”

- *mal seme* ‘evil seed’ → “seed of evil”: the adjective becomes a noun introduced by “of”.
- *della gente Tosca* ‘of the people Tuscan’ → “to the Tuscan people”: “to” replaces ‘of’, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 109 Ed io v’ aggiunsi: E morte di tua schiatta;  

“And death to thy kindred!” I added here.

- *Ed io v’ aggiunsi* ‘And I there added’ → “I added here”: the introductory conjunction is omitted, the reporting clause moves after the quoted speech, and the locative *v’* is rendered as “here”.
- *di tua schiatta* ‘of thy lineage’ → “to thy kindred”: “to” replaces ‘of’, and “kindred” renders the collective noun for lineage or stock.

> 110 Per ch’ egli, accumulando duol con duolo,  
> 111 Sen gìo come persona trista e matta.  

Wherefore he, accumulating pain on pain, went away as one distressed and mad.

- *con* ‘with’ → “on”: the preposition changes to suit the English expression for accumulating one pain upon another.
- *Sen gìo* ‘took himself away’ → “went away”: the Italian pronominal verb of motion becomes a simple intransitive verb with “away”.
- *persona trista* ‘person sad’ → “one distressed”: the noun becomes the pronoun “one”, and ‘sad’ is rendered more strongly as “distressed”.

> 112 Ma io rimasi a riguardar lo stuolo,  
> 113 E vidi cosa ch’ io avrei paura,  
> 114 Senza più prova, di contarla solo;  

But I remained to view the troop, and saw a thing which I should be afraid even to relate, without more proof;

- *cosa* ‘thing’ → “a thing”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *io avrei paura* ‘I would have fear’ → “I should be afraid”: the construction with ‘have’ and a noun becomes one with “be” and an adjective; “should” expresses the first-person conditional.
- *Senza più prova, di contarla solo* ‘without more proof, of merely recounting it’ → “even to relate, without more proof”: the phrase ‘without more proof’ moves from before the infinitival complement to the end of the clause; the Italian preposition introducing the infinitive is replaced by English infinitival “to”; the resumptive object pronoun *la* is omitted because “which” already supplies the object; and *solo* is rendered as the intensifier “even” rather than ‘merely’.

> 115 Se non che conscienzia m’ assicura,  
> 116 La buona compagnia che l’ uom francheggia  
> 117 Sotto l’ osbergo del sentirsi pura.  

but that conscience reassures me, that good companion which fortifies a man beneath the hauberk of his self-felt purity.

- *Se non che* ‘except that’ → “but that”: the exception is expressed through an adversative conjunction.
- *m’ assicura* ‘me assures’ → “reassures me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and ‘assures’ becomes “reassures”.
- *La buona compagnia* ‘the good company’ → “that good companion”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative, and the abstract noun for company or companionship becomes a concrete “companion”.
- *l’ uom francheggia* ‘the man emboldens’ → “fortifies a man”: the object *l’ uom* moves after the verb, its generic definite article becomes indefinite, and ‘emboldens’ is rendered as “fortifies”.
- *del sentirsi pura* ‘of feeling itself pure’ → “of his self-felt purity”: the substantivized infinitive becomes the noun “purity”, while the reflexive act of feeling becomes the compound adjective “self-felt”. The feminine *pura* grammatically refers to conscience; “his” instead attaches the purity to the man.

> 118 Io vidi certo, ed ancor par ch’ io il veggia,  
> 119 Un busto senza capo andar, sì come  
> 120 Andavan gli altri della trista greggia.  

Certainly I saw, and still seem to see a trunk going without a head, as the others of that dismal herd were going.

- *Io vidi certo* ‘I saw certainly’ → “Certainly I saw”: the adverb moves from after the verb to the beginning of the sentence.
- *par ch’ io il veggia* ‘it seems that I see it’ → “seem to see”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal one sharing the preceding subject “I”; the finite subjunctive becomes an infinitive, and the anticipatory object pronoun *il* is omitted.
- *senza capo andar* ‘without head to go’ → “going without a head”: the infinitive governed by a verb of perception becomes a participle, moves before the accompanying phrase, and an indefinite article is supplied before “head”.
- *sì come* ‘just as’ → “as”: the reinforcing *sì* is not separately represented.
- *Andavan* ‘went’ → “were going”: the imperfect is rendered by the past progressive, and the verb moves from before its subject to after it.
- *della trista greggia* ‘of the dismal herd’ → “of that dismal herd”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative.

## Lines 121–135 — The Severed Head Speaks

> 121 E il capo tronco tenea per le chiome  
> 122 Pesol con mano, a guisa di lanterna;  

And it was holding by the hair the severed head, swinging in his hand like a lantern;

- *E il capo tronco tenea per le chiome* ‘And the head severed it held by the hair’ → “And it was holding by the hair the severed head”: the object moves after the verb and the phrase describing the grip; the adjective moves before its noun; the imperfect is rendered by the past progressive.
- *Pesol* ‘hanging’ → “swinging”: the translation adds movement to the description of the suspended head.
- *con mano* ‘with a hand’ → “in his hand”: the preposition changes, and a possessive is supplied.
- *a guisa di* ‘in the manner of’ → “like”: the phrase becomes a single preposition.

> 123 E quei mirava noi, e dicea: O me!  

and that looked at us, and said: “O me!”

- *quei* ‘that one’ → “that”: the masculine demonstrative pronoun becomes the less personal English demonstrative.

> 124 Di se faceva a se stesso lucerna;  

Of itself it made for itself a lamp:

- *lucerna* ‘lamp’ → “a lamp”: English supplies an indefinite article before the predicative noun.

> 125 Ed eran due in uno, ed uno in due:  

and they were two in one, and one in two.

- Literal.

> 126 Com’ esser può, quei sa che sì governa.  

How this can be, he knows who so ordains.

- *Com’ esser può* ‘How be can’ → “How this can be”: the modal precedes the infinitive, and the explicit demonstrative subject is supplied.
- *governa* ‘governs’ → “ordains”: governing is rendered as ordering or determining.

> 127 Quando diritto appiè del ponte fue,  
> 128 Levò il braccio alto con tutta la testa  
> 129 Per appressarne le parole sue,  

When it was just at the foot of our bridge, it raised its arm high up, with all the head, to bring near to us its words,

- *Quando diritto appiè del ponte fue* ‘When directly at the foot of the bridge it was’ → “When it was just at the foot of our bridge”: the verb moves before the expression of place; *diritto* becomes “just”; and “our” replaces the definite article.
- *il braccio* ‘the arm’ → “its arm”: a possessive replaces the definite article used with the body part.
- *alto* ‘high’ → “high up”: “up” is supplied.
- *le parole sue* ‘the words its’ → “its words”: the possessive moves before the noun, and the article is omitted.

> 130 Che furo: Or vedi la pena molesta  
> 131 Tu che, spirando, vai veggendo i morti:  
> 132 Vedi s’ alcuna è grande come questa.  

which were: “Now see the grievous penalty, thou, who breathing goest to view the dead; see if any be as great as this!

- *la pena molesta* ‘the penalty grievous’ → “the grievous penalty”: the adjective moves before its noun.
- *vai veggendo* ‘goest seeing’ → “goest to view”: the gerund construction becomes an infinitive of purpose after a verb of motion, rather than expressing ongoing seeing; ‘seeing’ is rendered as “view”.
- *s’ alcuna è* ‘if any is’ → “if any be”: the Italian indicative becomes an English subjunctive.
- *grande come questa* ‘great as this’ → “as great as this”: English supplies the first “as” in the comparison.

> 133 E perchè tu di me novella porti,  
> 134 Sappi ch’ io son Bertram dal Bornio, quelli  
> 135 Che al Re Giovane diedi i mal conforti.  

And that thou mayest carry tidings of me, know, that I am Bertram de Born, he who to the Young King gave the evil counsels.

- *E perchè tu di me novella porti* ‘And so that thou of me a report carry’ → “And that thou mayest carry tidings of me”: the purpose conjunction is rendered by “that”; the subjunctive becomes “mayest carry”; the verb moves before its object, and ‘of me’ moves after it; singular *novella* becomes plural “tidings”.
- *quelli Che* ‘that one who’ → “he who”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *al Re Giovane* ‘to the King Young’ → “to the Young King”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *i mal conforti* ‘the evil encouragements’ → “the evil counsels”: encouragement or prompting is rendered as advice.

## Lines 136–142 — Division and Retribution

> 136 Io feci il padre e il figlio in sè ribelli:  

I made the father and the son rebels to each other.

- *in sè ribelli* ‘rebellious in themselves’ → “rebels to each other”: the reflexive expression is rendered explicitly reciprocally and placed after “rebels”; the adjective *ribelli* becomes a noun.

> 137 Achitofel non fe’ più d’ Absalone  
> 138 E di David co’ malvagi pungelli.  

Ahithophel did not do more with Absalom and David by his malicious instigations.

- *d’ Absalone E di David* ‘of Absalom and of David’ → “with Absalom and David”: “with” replaces *di*, and the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *co’ malvagi pungelli* ‘with the wicked goads’ → “by his malicious instigations”: “by” replaces ‘with’, the definite article becomes the possessive “his”, and the figurative ‘goads’ are rendered explicitly as “instigations”.

> 139 Perch’ io partii così giunte persone,  
> 140 Partito porto il mio cerebro, lasso!  
> 141 Dal suo principio ch’ è in questo troncone.  

Because I parted persons thus united, I carry my brain, ah me! parted from its source which is in this trunk.

- *così giunte persone* ‘thus joined persons’ → “persons thus united”: the noun moves before its participial modifier, and ‘joined’ is rendered as “united”.
- *Partito porto il mio cerebro, lasso* ‘parted I carry my brain, wretched!’ → “I carry my brain, ah me! parted”: the participle moves from before the verb to after its object and the intervening exclamation; *lasso*, an adjective used exclamatorily, becomes the interjection “ah me!”.
- *suo principio* ‘its beginning’ → “its source”: “source” renders *principio* as a point of origin.
- *troncone* ‘stump’ → “trunk”: the English does not explicitly retain the Italian word’s sense of a truncated remainder.

> 142 Così s’ osserva in me lo contrappasso.  

Thus the law of retribution is observed in me.”

- *s’ osserva in me lo contrappasso* ‘is observed in me the counter-suffering’ → “the law of retribution is observed in me”: the subject moves before the verb; *contrappasso* is paraphrased as “law of retribution”, supplying “law of” rather than retaining the Italian compound’s formulation.

## Summary — Close Following with English Reshaping

Carlyle follows the Italian closely in the sequence of its statements, its repetitions, and many of its images, but regularly rearranges words into English order. Subjects and verbs move before objects or descriptive phrases that precede them in Italian; adjectives generally move before their nouns, and complements follow the words they qualify. Thus *Capo ha cosa fatta* becomes “A thing done has an end!”, while *Partito porto il mio cerebro* loses its initial emphasis on *Partito* when the translation places “parted” after “I carry my brain” and the intervening exclamation. Some Italian arrangements nevertheless remain conspicuous, as in “Before me Ali weeping goes” and “Of itself it made for itself a lamp”.

Changes of construction often accompany this reordering. Pronominal verbs become English intransitives or direct transitives: *S’ arrestaron* becomes “stopped”, and *Rimembriti di* becomes “remember”. Infinitives become gerunds or nouns, as in *per narrar più volte*, “by oft relating”, and *l’ antiveder*, “foresight”. Elsewhere a finite clause becomes an infinitive construction: *par ch’ io il veggia* is compressed into “seem to see”. Collective singulars frequently become plurals, notably *ossame*, “bones”, while articles attached to body parts become possessives, as in *la faccia*, “his face”. More generally, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits the articles that Italian uses before possessives.

The vocabulary ranges from close, sometimes unusual equivalents to explanatory paraphrase. “Words set free” retains the literal image of *parole sciolte* rather than explicitly giving ‘in prose’; “hauberk” likewise preserves *osbergo*. By contrast, *poco seno* becomes the abstract “small capacity”, *risma* becomes “class”, and *contrappasso* expands into “the law of retribution”. Some choices narrow or alter the Italian sense: *mente* becomes “memory”, *sbigottito* becomes “dejected”, and *Gridando* becomes the less forceful “saying”. Register also shifts, especially when *merda* becomes “excrement” and *dove si trulla* becomes “the part that utters vilest sound”.

Omissions and additions are generally local but sometimes affect the precision or emphasis. The specific weighted drowning expressed by *mazzerati* is reduced to “drowned”, and *infin sotto le ciglia* loses ‘under’ in “up to the eyebrows”. Conversely, “shed” makes explicit the relation between blood and the Romans, “eye” supplies the noun understood in *con l’ uno*, and “wandered” adds a characterization to the movement expressed by *avem volta*. Overall, the translation remains recognizably close to the Italian’s phrasing and imagery, while reshaping its syntax, making implicit relations explicit, and occasionally replacing concrete or forceful expressions with more abstract, explanatory, or restrained English.
