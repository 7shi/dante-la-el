# Inferno 4

## Lines 1–15 — Awakening Above the Abyss

> 1 Ruppemi l’ alto sonno nella testa  
> 2 Un greve tuono, sì ch’ io mi riscossi,  
> 3 Come persona che per forza è desta:  

A heavy thunder broke the deep sleep in my head; so that I started like one who is awaked by force.

- *Ruppemi l’ alto sonno nella testa Un greve tuono* ‘broke for me the high sleep in the head a heavy thunder’ → “A heavy thunder broke the deep sleep in my head”: the subject moves from after the object to before the verb; the personal reference in *-mi* becomes the possessive “my” before “head”; and *alto*, literally ‘high’, is rendered idiomatically as “deep”.
- *sì ch’ io mi riscossi* ‘so that I shook myself’ → “so that I started”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb expressing a sudden involuntary movement.
- *Come persona che per forza è desta* ‘like a person who by force is awakened’ → “like one who is awaked by force”: “one” replaces *persona*, and the phrase expressing force moves after the passive verb.

> 4 E l’ occhio riposato intorno mossi,  
> 5 Dritto levato, e fiso riguardai  
> 6 Per conoscer lo loco dov’ io fossi.  

And, having risen erect, I moved my rested eyes around, and looked stedfastly to know the place in which I was.

- *l’ occhio riposato intorno mossi* ‘the rested eye around I moved’ → “I moved my rested eyes around”: the object moves after the verb, and “around” follows the object; the singular *occhio* becomes plural “eyes”, with “my” replacing the definite article.
- *Dritto levato* ‘erect, risen’ → “having risen erect”: the participial phrase moves before the main clause; the simple participle becomes an explicitly perfect participial construction, and “erect” follows it.
- *e fiso riguardai* ‘and fixedly I looked’ → “and looked stedfastly”: the adverb moves from before the verb to after it.
- *dov’ io fossi* ‘where I was’ → “in which I was”: the relative adverb becomes a preposition and relative pronoun; the Italian imperfect subjunctive is represented by the English past indicative.

> 7 Vero è, che in su la proda mi trovai  
> 8 Della valle d’ abisso dolorosa,  
> 9 Che tuono accoglie d’ infiniti guai.  

True is it, that I found myself upon the brink of the dolorous Valley of the Abyss, which gathers thunder of endless wailings.

- *in su la proda mi trovai* ‘on upon the brink myself I found’ → “I found myself upon the brink”: the compound prepositional expression becomes simply “upon”; the place phrase moves after the verb, and the reflexive pronoun follows rather than precedes it.
- *Della valle d’ abisso dolorosa* ‘of the valley of abyss dolorous’ → “of the dolorous Valley of the Abyss”: *dolorosa* moves before the noun it qualifies, and an article is supplied before “Abyss”.
- *Che tuono accoglie d’ infiniti guai* ‘which thunder gathers of infinite woes’ → “which gathers thunder of endless wailings”: the verb moves before its object, bringing “thunder” directly beside its dependent phrase; “endless” renders *infiniti*, and “wailings” takes *guai* specifically as audible cries of distress rather than woes generally.

> 10 Oscura, profonda era, e nebulosa,  
> 11 Tanto che, per ficcar lo viso al fondo,  
> 12 Io non vi discernea veruna cosa.  

It was so dark, profound, and cloudy, that, with fixing my look upon the bottom, I there discerned nothing.

- *Oscura, profonda era, e nebulosa, Tanto che* ‘dark, profound it was, and misty, so much that’ → “It was so dark, profound, and cloudy, that”: the verb moves before all three adjectives; *tanto* becomes “so” before the adjectives instead of following them; and “cloudy” renders *nebulosa*.
- *per ficcar lo viso al fondo* ‘for thrusting the sight to the bottom’ → “with fixing my look upon the bottom”: *per* with the infinitive becomes “with” and a gerund, leaving the Italian’s concessive force—‘despite straining to look’—less explicit. The forceful *ficcar* becomes “fixing”; *viso* is understood as sight or gaze, and its article becomes “my”; “upon” replaces the directional ‘to’.
- *Io non vi discernea veruna cosa* ‘I did not there discern any thing’ → “I there discerned nothing”: the negative attached to the verb and the indefinite object are combined into the negative pronoun “nothing”, leaving the verb affirmative. The Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past.

> 13 Or discendiam quaggiù nel cieco mondo,  
> 14 Incominciò il Poeta tutto smorto:  

“Now let us descend into the blind world here below,” began the Poet all pale:

- *quaggiù nel cieco mondo* ‘down here into the blind world’ → “into the blind world here below”: the adverb of place moves after the destination phrase.
- *smorto* ‘deathly pale’ → “pale”: the translation does not separately express the stronger, deathlike quality of the Italian adjective.

> 15 Io sarò primo, e tu sarai secondo.  

“I will be first, and thou shalt be second.”

- Literal.

## Lines 16–30 — Pity and Sighs in the First Circle

> 16 Ed io, che del color mi fui accorto,  
> 17 Dissi: Come verrò, se tu paventi  
> 18 Che suoli al mio dubbiare esser conforto?  

And I, who had remarked his colour, said: “How shall I come, when thou fearest, who art wont to be my strength in doubt?”

- *del color mi fui accorto* ‘of the colour I had become aware’ → “had remarked his colour”: the reflexive construction with *di* becomes a transitive verb with a direct object, which follows rather than precedes the verb. The definite article becomes the possessive “his”, identifying whose colour is meant.
- *se* ‘if’ → “when”: the conditional conjunction becomes temporal or circumstantial.
- *al mio dubbiare esser conforto* ‘to my doubting to be comfort’ → “to be my strength in doubt”: “strength” replaces ‘comfort’; the substantivized infinitive ‘doubting’ becomes “doubt”. The possessive is transferred from the doubting to the strength, and the phrase describing the doubt follows rather than precedes the infinitive.

> 19 Ed egli a me: L’ angoscia delle genti,  
> 20 Che son quaggiù, nel viso mi dipinge  
> 21 Quella pietà, che tu per tema senti.  

And he to me: “The anguish of the people who are here below, on my face depaints that pity, which thou takest for fear.

- *nel viso mi* ‘in the face for me’ → “on my face”: “on” replaces ‘in’, and the personal pronoun marking the person affected becomes the possessive “my”.
- *che tu per tema senti* ‘which thou perceivest as fear’ → “which thou takest for fear”: perception is expressed through the idiom ‘take for’. The verb precedes rather than follows the phrase identifying the mistaken interpretation.

> 22 Andiam, chè la via lunga ne sospinge.  

Let us go; for the length of way impels us.”

- *Andiam* ‘we go’ → “Let us go”: the first-person plural verb, used exhortatively, becomes an explicit English exhortation with “Let us”.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes coordinating.
- *la via lunga* ‘the way long’ → “the length of way”: the adjective becomes the noun “length”, while the original noun becomes its dependent phrase, “of way”.
- *ne sospinge* ‘us drives onward’ → “impels us”: the object pronoun follows rather than precedes the verb; the sense of being driven onward is expressed by “impels”.

> 23 Così si mise, e così mi fe’ entrare  
> 24 Nel primo cerchio che l’ abisso cinge.  

Thus he entered, and made me enter, into the first circle that girds the abyss.

- *Così si mise* ‘thus he set out’ → “Thus he entered”: the reflexive expression for setting oneself in motion is interpreted specifically as entering and rendered by a non-reflexive verb.
- *e così mi fe’ entrare* ‘and thus me he made enter’ → “and made me enter”: the repeated *così* is omitted, and “me” follows rather than precedes the causative verb.
- *Nel* ‘in the’ → “into the”: the English preposition explicitly marks movement inward.
- *che l’ abisso cinge* ‘that the abyss girds’ → “that girds the abyss”: *l’ abisso*, the Italian object, follows rather than precedes the verb in English.

> 25 Quivi, secondo che per ascoltare,  
> 26 Non avea pianto, ma’ che di sospiri,  
> 27 Che l’ aura eterna facevan tremare:  

Here there was no plaint, that could be heard, except of sighs, which caused the eternal air to tremble.

- *secondo che per ascoltare* ‘according as by listening’ → “that could be heard”: the elliptical qualification concerning what could be discerned by listening becomes a passive relative clause modifying “plaint”. The modal “could” is supplied, and the qualification moves after the statement it qualifies.
- *Non avea pianto* ‘had no weeping’ → “there was no plaint”: impersonal *avere* becomes the English existential construction “there was”; “plaint” renders weeping as an audible lament.
- *l’ aura eterna facevan tremare* ‘the air eternal they made tremble’ → “caused the eternal air to tremble”: the object follows rather than precedes the causative verb, and the adjective precedes rather than follows its noun. The construction with *fare* and an infinitive becomes “caused” with an object and a *to*-infinitive.

> 28 E ciò avvenia di duol senza martiri,  
> 29 Ch’ avean le turbe, ch’ eran molte e grandi,  
> 30 E d’ infanti e di femmine e di viri.  

And this arose from the sadness, without torment, of the crowds that were many and great, both of children, and of women and men.

- *avvenia* ‘happened’ → “arose”: occurrence is expressed as arising from a cause.
- *di duol* ‘from grief’ → “from the sadness”: “sadness” replaces ‘grief’, and the definite article is supplied.
- *martiri* ‘torments’ → “torment”: the plural becomes a singular mass noun.
- *Ch’ avean le turbe* ‘which the crowds had’ → “of the crowds”: the relative clause expressing possession becomes a possessive prepositional phrase; the verb ‘had’ is omitted.
- *E d’ infanti e di femmine e di viri* ‘and of infants and of women and of men’ → “both of children, and of women and men”: the opening *e* becomes “both”, recasting the repeated conjunctions as an English correlative construction. “Children” is broader than ‘infants’, and the final repeated *di* is omitted.

## Lines 31–42 — Desire Without Hope

> 31 Lo buon Maestro a me: Tu non dimandi  
> 32 Che spiriti son questi, che tu vedi?  

The good Master to me: “Thou askest not what spirits are these thou seest?

- *non dimandi* ‘not askest’ → “askest not”: the negative follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *che tu vedi* ‘whom thou seest’ → “thou seest”: the object relative pronoun is omitted.

> 33 Or vo’ che sappi, innanzi che più andi,  
> 34 Ch’ ei non peccaro: e s’ egli hanno mercedi,  
> 35 Non basta, perch’ ei non ebber battesmo,  
> 36 Ch’ è porta della Fede che tu credi:  

I wish thee to know, before thou goest farther, that they sinned not. And though they have merit, it suffices not; for they had not Baptism, which is the portal of the Faith that thou believest.

- *Or vo’ che sappi* ‘Now I wish that thou know’ → “I wish thee to know”: *Or* is omitted; the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object pronoun followed by an infinitive.
- *innanzi che più andi* ‘before that farther thou go’ → “before thou goest farther”: the conjunction is reduced to “before”; the subjunctive becomes indicative, and the adverb moves after the verb.
- *non peccaro* ‘not sinned’ → “sinned not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *e s’ egli hanno mercedi* ‘and if they have merits’ → “And though they have merit”: the conditional conjunction is interpreted concessively, and the plural noun becomes a singular mass noun.
- *Non basta* ‘not suffices’ → “it suffices not”: the negative moves after the verb, and an explicit impersonal subject is supplied.
- *perch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *non ebber* ‘not had’ → “had not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *porta* ‘door’ → “the portal”: the noun is rendered by “portal”, and a definite article is supplied.

> 37 E se furon dinanzi al Cristianesmo,  
> 38 Non adoràr debitamente Dio:  

And seeing they were before Christianity, they worshipped not God aright.

- *E se* ‘And if’ → “And seeing”: the conditional wording is interpreted causally rather than hypothetically.
- *dinanzi al Cristianesmo* ‘before the Christianity’ → “before Christianity”: the definite article is omitted before the name of the religion.
- *Non adoràr debitamente Dio* ‘Not worshipped duly God’ → “they worshipped not God aright”: the negative moves after the verb, the adverb moves after the object, and ‘duly’ is rendered as “aright”.

> 39 E di questi cotai son io medesmo.  

And of these am I myself.

- *questi cotai* ‘these such ones’ → “these”: *cotai*, qualifying the group as being of that kind, has no separate equivalent.

> 40 Per tai difetti, e non per altro rio,  
> 41 Semo perduti, e sol di tanto offesi,  
> 42 Che senza speme vivemo in disio.  

For such defects, and for no other fault, are we lost; and only in so far afflicted, that without hope we live in desire.”

- *e non per altro rio* ‘and not for other evil’ → “and for no other fault”: the negative moves from before the preposition into the noun phrase as “no”; the substantive *rio*, ‘evil’, is rendered as “fault”.
- *sol di tanto* ‘only in so much’ → “only in so far”: the expression of extent is recast in the English idiom “in so far”.

## Lines 43–58 — Grief and Release From Limbo

> 43 Gran duol mi prese al cuor, quando lo intesi,  

Great sadness took me at the heart on hearing this;

- *Gran duol* ‘great grief’ → “Great sadness”: ‘grief’ is rendered by the less intense “sadness”.
- *quando lo intesi* ‘when I heard it’ → “on hearing this”: the finite temporal clause becomes a prepositional phrase with a gerund; “this” replaces the personal pronoun ‘it’.

> 44 Perocchè gente di molto valore  
> 45 Conobbi, che in quel limbo eran sospesi.  

because I knew men of much worth, who in that Limbo were suspense.

- *gente di molto valore Conobbi* ‘people of much worth I knew’ → “I knew men of much worth”: the object moves after the verb, and the collective noun ‘people’ becomes “men”.
- *eran sospesi* ‘were suspended’ → “were suspense”: the Italian participle is represented by the archaic English adjective “suspense”.

> 46 Dimmi, Maestro mio, dimmi, Signore,  
> 47 Cominciai io, per voler esser certo  
> 48 Di quella fede che vince ogni errore:  

“Tell me, Master; tell me, Sir,” I began, desiring to be assured of that Faith which conquers every error;

- *Maestro mio* ‘master my’ → “Master”: the possessive ‘my’ is omitted.
- *Signore* ‘Lord’ → “Sir”: the title is rendered as an English form of respectful address.
- *Cominciai io* ‘began I’ → “I began”: the expressed subject moves before the verb.
- *per voler esser certo* ‘from wishing to be certain’ → “desiring to be assured”: the prepositional construction expressing motive becomes a participial phrase; ‘be certain’ becomes “be assured”.

> 49 Uscinne mai alcuno, o per suo merto,  
> 50 O per altrui, che poi fosse beato?  

“did ever any, by his own merit, or by others’, go out from hence, that afterwards was blessed?”

- *Uscinne mai alcuno* ‘went out thence ever anyone’ → “did ever any” and “go out from hence”: the single finite verb becomes an interrogative auxiliary and infinitive, separated by the phrases concerning merit. The verb’s enclitic *ne* becomes “from hence”, placed after “go out”.
- *o per suo merto, O per altrui* ‘either by his merit, or by another’s’ → “by his own merit, or by others’”: the first member of the paired conjunction is omitted; “own” makes the possessive emphatic, and the indefinite possessive *altrui* is given a plural English form.
- *che poi fosse beato* ‘who afterwards might be blessed’ → “that afterwards was blessed”: the imperfect subjunctive becomes a past indicative.

> 51 E quei, che intese il mio parlar coverto,  
> 52 Rispose: Io era nuovo in questo stato,  
> 53 Quando ci vidi venire un Possente  
> 54 Con segno di vittoria incoronato.  

And he, understanding my covert speech, replied: “I was new in this condition, when I saw a Mighty One come to us, crowned with sign of victory.

- *E quei* ‘and that one’ → “And he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *che intese il mio parlar coverto* ‘who understood my speaking veiled’ → “understanding my covert speech”: the finite past-tense relative clause becomes a present-participial phrase. The substantival infinitive *parlar* becomes the noun “speech”, and the adjective moves before it.
- *stato* ‘state’ → “condition”: a different noun expresses the state in question.
- *ci vidi venire un Possente* ‘here I saw come a mighty one’ → “I saw a Mighty One come to us”: the subject of *venire*, placed after that infinitive in Italian, comes before “come” as the object of “saw”. The locative *ci* becomes the personal destination “to us” and moves to the end; “One” supplies an English support word for the substantival adjective.
- *Con segno di vittoria incoronato* ‘with sign of victory crowned’ → “crowned with sign of victory”: the participle moves before its accompanying prepositional phrase.

> 55 Trasseci l’ ombra del Primo Parente,  
> 56 D’ Abel suo figlio, e quella di Noè,  
> 57 Di Moisè Legista, e ubbidiente  
> 58 Abraam Patriarca, e David Re,  

He took away from us the shade of our First Parent, of Abel his son, and that of Noah; of Moses the Legislator, and obedient Abraham the Patriarch; David the King;

- *Trasseci* ‘drew out from here’ → “He took away from us”: ‘drew out’ becomes “took away”, and the locative enclitic *ci* is rendered personally as “from us”.
- *del Primo Parente* ‘of the first parent’ → “of our First Parent”: the definite article is replaced by the supplied possessive “our”.
- *Di Moisè Legista* ‘of Moses legislator’ → “of Moses the Legislator”: an article is supplied before the title.
- *e ubbidiente Abraam Patriarca* ‘and obedient Abraham patriarch’ → “and obedient Abraham the Patriarch”: the translation takes *ubbidiente* with *Abraam*, rather than with *Moisè*, and supplies an article before “Patriarch”.
- *e David Re* ‘and David king’ → “David the King”: the conjunction is omitted, and an article is supplied before the title.

## Lines 59–72 — Saved Souls and Honourable Spirits

> 59 Israel con suo padre, e co’ suoi nati,  
> 60 E con Rachele, per cui tanto fe’,  
> 61 Ed altri molti; e fecegli beati:  

Israel with his father and his children, and Rachel, for whom he did so much; and many others, and made them blessed.

- *co’ suoi nati, E con Rachele* ‘with his offspring, and with Rachel’ → “his children, and Rachel”: both repetitions of the preposition are omitted; the substantivized participle *nati*, literally ‘those born’, becomes “children”.
- *tanto fe’* ‘so much did’ → “he did so much”: the verb precedes, rather than follows, the expression of amount.

> 62 E vo’ che sappi che, dinanzi ad essi,  
> 63 Spiriti umani non eran salvati.  

And I wish thee to know, that, before these, no human souls were saved.”

- *vo’ che sappi* ‘I wish that thou know’ → “I wish thee to know”: a subordinate clause with a subjunctive verb becomes an object pronoun followed by an infinitive.
- *dinanzi ad essi* ‘before them’ → “before these”: the personal pronoun becomes a demonstrative.
- *Spiriti umani non eran salvati* ‘spirits human were not saved’ → “no human souls were saved”: “souls” replaces ‘spirits’; the adjective moves before the noun; and the verbal negation becomes “no” modifying the subject.

> 64 Non lasciavam l’ andar, perch’ ei dicessi,  
> 65 Ma passavam la selva tuttavia,  
> 66 La selva dico di spiriti spessi.  

We ceased not to go, though he was speaking; but passed the wood meanwhile, the wood, I say, of crowded spirits.

- *Non lasciavam l’ andar* ‘we did not leave off the going’ → “We ceased not to go”: leaving off an activity becomes ceasing to perform it; the substantivized infinitive becomes “to go”, and the negative follows the finite verb.
- *perch’ ei dicessi* ‘because he spoke’ → “though he was speaking”: the connective is given concessive rather than causal force, and the imperfect subjunctive becomes an indicative past progressive.
- *tuttavia* ‘still’ → “meanwhile”: continued movement is expressed instead as movement simultaneous with the speaking.
- *spiriti spessi* ‘spirits thick’ → “crowded spirits”: the adjective precedes the noun, and ‘thick’, in the sense of densely gathered, becomes “crowded”.

> 67 Non era lungi ancor la nostra via  
> 68 Di qua dal sommo, quand’ io vidi un foco,  
> 69 Ch’ emisperio di tenebre vincia.  

Our way was not yet far within the topmost part, when I saw a fire, which conquered a hemisphere of the darkness.

- *Non era lungi ancor la nostra via* ‘not was far yet our way’ → “Our way was not yet far”: the postponed subject moves to the beginning; the negative follows the verb; and “yet” precedes “far”.
- *Di qua dal sommo* ‘on this side of the summit’ → “within the topmost part”: a position relative to an upper boundary becomes a position within an upper region; “part” is supplied to express the substantivized *sommo*.
- *Ch’ emisperio di tenebre vincia* ‘which hemisphere of darkness conquered’ → “which conquered a hemisphere of the darkness”: the verb moves before its object, and the articles “a” and “the” are supplied.

> 70 Di lungi v’ eravamo ancora un poco,  
> 71 Ma non sì, ch’ io non discernessi in parte,  
> 72 Che orrevol gente possedea quel loco.  

We were still a little distant from it; yet not so distant, that I did not in part discern what honourable people occupied that place.

- *Di lungi v’ eravamo ancora un poco* ‘at a distance there we were still a little’ → “We were still a little distant from it”: the opening expression of distance moves after the verb and becomes adjectival; “still a little” precedes it; and the locative pronoun *v’*, ‘there’, becomes “from it”.
- *Ma* ‘but’ → “yet”: the adversative conjunction is replaced by another turn of phrase.
- *non sì* ‘not so’ → “not so distant”: “distant” is supplied from the preceding clause.
- *ch’ io non discernessi in parte* ‘that I did not discern in part’ → “that I did not in part discern”: “in part” moves before the main verb, and the Italian subjunctive is represented by an English indicative construction.
- *Che* ‘that’ → “what”: the translation reads this as an interrogative determiner introducing the people discerned, rather than as a conjunction introducing a statement.
- *gente possedea* ‘people possessed’ → “people occupied”: possession is rendered as occupancy, and the Italian singular collective noun becomes the English plural “people”.

## Lines 73–85 — Honour for the Great Poets

> 73 O tu, che onori ogni scienza ed arte,  

“O thou, that honourest every science and art;

- Literal.

> 74 Questi chi son, ch’ hanno cotanta orranza,  
> 75 Che dal modo degli altri li diparte?  

who are these, who have such honour, that it separates them from the manner of the rest?”

- *Questi chi son* ‘these who are’ → “who are these”: the demonstrative moves after the verb, following English interrogative word order.
- *cotanta orranza* ‘so much honour’ → “such honour”: an expression of degree becomes one of quality or degree.
- *dal modo degli altri li diparte* ‘from the manner of the others them separates’ → “it separates them from the manner of the rest”: the verb precedes its object and the prepositional phrase; ‘the others’ becomes “the rest”.

> 76 E quegli a me: L’ onrata nominanza,  
> 77 Che di lor suona su nella tua vita,  
> 78 Grazia acquista nel ciel che sì gli avanza.  

And he to me: “The honoured name, which sounds of them, up in that life of thine, gains favour in heaven which thus advances them.”

- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *nominanza* ‘renown’ → “name”: reputation is expressed through “name”.
- *Che di lor suona* ‘which of them sounds’ → “which sounds of them”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb.
- *nella tua vita* ‘in thy life’ → “in that life of thine”: the translation adds demonstrative force with “that” and replaces the possessive adjective with the postposed phrase “of thine”.
- *Grazia acquista* ‘favour gains’ → “gains favour”: the object moves after the verb.
- *che sì gli avanza* ‘which thus them advances’ → “which thus advances them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 79 Intanto voce fu per me udita:  

Meanwhile a voice was heard by me:

- *voce fu per me udita* ‘voice was by me heard’ → “a voice was heard by me”: an indefinite article is supplied, and the agent phrase moves after the participle.

> 80 Onorate l’ altissimo Poeta;  

“Honour the great Poet!

- *altissimo* ‘highest’ → “great”: the superlative is replaced by a positive adjective, reducing its explicit degree.

> 81 L’ ombra sua torna, ch’ era dipartita.  

His shade returns that was departed.”

- *L’ ombra sua* ‘the shade his’ → “His shade”: the possessive moves before the noun, and the Italian article is not retained.
- *ch’ era dipartita* ‘that had departed’ → “that was departed”: the Italian pluperfect is rendered with the archaic English perfect construction using ‘be’ rather than ‘have’.

> 82 Poichè la voce fu restata e queta,  
> 83 Vidi quattro grand’ ombre a noi venire:  
> 84 Sembianza avevan nè trista nè lieta.  

After the voice had paused, and was silent, I saw four great shadows come to us. They had an aspect neither sad nor joyful.

- *fu restata* ‘had ceased’ → “had paused”: cessation becomes a pause; the Italian past anterior is expressed by the English past perfect.
- *e queta* ‘and quiet’ → “and was silent”: a copula is supplied for the coordinated adjective, and ‘quiet’ is rendered more specifically as “silent”.
- *a noi venire* ‘to us come’ → “come to us”: the infinitive moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *Sembianza avevan* ‘aspect they had’ → “They had an aspect”: the object moves after the verb, and an indefinite article is supplied.

> 85 Lo buon Maestro cominciò a dire:  

The good Master began to speak:

- *a dire* ‘to say’ → “to speak”: the verb of saying is rendered as a verb of speaking.

## Lines 86–102 — Dante Joins the Great Poets

> 86 Mira colui con quella spada in mano,  
> 87 Che vien dinanzi a’ tre sì come sire.  

“Mark him with that sword in hand, who comes before the three as their lord.

- *colui* ‘that one’ → “him”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *sì come sire* ‘as lord’ → “as their lord”: “their” is supplied.

> 88 Quegli è Omero poeta sovrano:  

That is Homer, the sovereign Poet.

- *poeta sovrano* ‘poet sovereign’ → “the sovereign Poet”: the adjective moves before the noun, and the definite article is supplied.

> 89 L’ altro è Orazio satiro, che viene;  

The next who comes is Horace the satirist.

- *L’ altro è Orazio satiro, che viene* ‘The other is Horace, satirist, who comes’ → “The next who comes is Horace the satirist”: “The next” replaces ‘The other’, making the sequence explicit; the relative clause moves from the end to immediately after “The next”; and the definite article is supplied before “satirist”.

> 90 Ovidio è il terzo, e l’ ultimo è Lucano.  

Ovid is the third; and the last is Lucan.

- Literal.

> 91 Però che ciascun meco si conviene  
> 92 Nel nome, che sonò la voce sola;  
> 93 Fannomi onore, e di ciò fanno bene.  

Because each agrees with me in the name, which the one voice sounded, they do me honour: and therein they do well.”

- *meco si conviene* ‘with me agrees’ → “agrees with me”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb.
- *che sonò la voce sola* ‘which sounded the single voice’ → “which the one voice sounded”: the subject, *la voce sola*, moves before the verb; *sola* becomes the attributive “one”, placed before its noun.
- *Fannomi onore* ‘make me honour’ → “they do me honour”: the Italian construction with ‘make’ is rendered by the English idiom with “do”.
- *di ciò* ‘in respect of this’ → “therein”: a prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.

> 94 Così vidi adunar la bella scuola  
> 95 Di quel signor dell’ altissimo canto,  
> 96 Che sovra gli altri, com’ aquila, vola.  

Thus I saw assembled the goodly school of that lord of highest song, who, like an eagle, soars above the rest.

- *adunar* ‘assemble’ → “assembled”: the infinitive describing the school’s gathering becomes a past participle describing it as assembled.
- *bella* ‘beautiful’ → “goodly”: a different adjective renders the commendatory force.
- *dell’ altissimo canto* ‘of the highest song’ → “of highest song”: the definite article is omitted.
- *sovra gli altri* ‘above the others’ → “above the rest”: “the rest” replaces ‘the others’, and the phrase moves from before the comparison to after the verb.
- *com’ aquila* ‘like eagle’ → “like an eagle”: the indefinite article is supplied.
- *vola* ‘flies’ → “soars”: the English verb specifies a particular manner of flying.

> 97 Da ch’ ebber ragionato insieme alquanto,  
> 98 Volsersi a me con salutevol cenno:  

After they had talked a space together, they turned to me with sign of salutation;

- *Da ch’* ‘from when’ → “After”: the Italian temporal phrase becomes a simple conjunction.
- *insieme alquanto* ‘together somewhat’ → “a space together”: the order is reversed, and *alquanto* is rendered as a noun phrase expressing duration.
- *Volsersi* ‘turned themselves’ → “they turned”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *con salutevol cenno* ‘with saluting sign’ → “with sign of salutation”: the adjective becomes a noun introduced by “of”, following rather than preceding “sign”.

> 99 E il mio Maestro sorrise di tanto.  

and my Master smiled thereat.

- *di tanto* ‘at so much’ → “thereat”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb referring back to the preceding action, without retaining the expression of degree.

> 100 E più d’ onore ancora assai mi fenno,  
> 101 Ch’ essi mi fecer della loro schiera,  
> 102 Sì ch’ io fui sesto tra cotanto senno.  

And greatly more besides they honoured me; for they made me of their number, so that I was a sixth amid such intelligences.

- *più d’ onore ancora assai mi fenno* ‘more honour besides, much, they did me’ → “greatly more besides they honoured me”: the construction ‘did me honour’ becomes the verb “honoured” with a direct object; *assai* becomes “greatly” and moves before “more”, while “besides” follows that combination.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *mi fecer della loro schiera* ‘me they made of their band’ → “they made me of their number”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and ‘band’ becomes “number”, expressing membership rather than naming the group.
- *io fui sesto* ‘I was sixth’ → “I was a sixth”: the indefinite article is supplied before the ordinal.
- *cotanto senno* ‘so much wisdom’ → “such intelligences”: the singular abstract noun becomes a plural count noun referring to people, and the expression of quantity becomes one of quality.

## Lines 103–113 — Arrival at the Noble Castle

> 103 Così n’ andammo infino alla lumiera  
> 104 Parlando cose, che il tacere è bello,  
> 105 Sì com’ era il parlar colà dov’ era.  

Thus we went onwards to the light, speaking things which it is well to pass in silence, as it was well to speak there where I was.

- *n’ andammo* ‘we went away’ → “we went onwards”: the departure expressed by *ne* becomes continued forward movement.
- *infino alla* ‘as far as the’ → “to the”: the explicit emphasis on reaching a limit is reduced to a simple preposition.
- *che il tacere è bello* ‘which to keep silent is beautiful’ → “which it is well to pass in silence”: the nominal infinitive *il tacere* becomes an infinitive phrase following anticipatory “it”; *tacere* is expanded to “pass in silence,” and *bello* becomes “well” rather than ‘beautiful’.
- *Sì com’ era il parlar* ‘just as speaking was [beautiful]’ → “as it was well to speak”: *sì* is not separately represented; the nominal infinitive becomes “to speak,” placed after anticipatory “it,” and the understood predicate is supplied as “well”.
- *dov’ era* ‘where it was’ → “where I was”: Carlyle reads *era* as first-person, referring to the narrator, rather than third-person, referring back to the speaking.

> 106 Venimmo al piè d’ un nobile castello,  
> 107 Sette volte cerchiato d’ alte mura,  
> 108 Difeso intorno d’ un bel fiumicello.  

We came to the foot of a Noble Castle, seven times circled with lofty Walls, defended round by a fair Rivulet.

- *d’ alte mura* ‘of high walls’ → “with lofty Walls”: *di* becomes “with,” and *alte* is rendered “lofty” rather than ‘high’.
- *d’ un bel fiumicello* ‘by a beautiful little river’ → “by a fair Rivulet”: *bel* becomes “fair”; the diminutive force of *fiumicello* is conveyed by the separate English noun “Rivulet,” rather than by adding ‘little’ to ‘river’.

> 109 Questo passammo come terra dura;  

This we passed as solid land.

- *dura* ‘hard’ → “solid”: the adjective is rendered in terms of firmness or solidity rather than hardness.

> 110 Per sette porte intrai con questi savi;  

Through seven gates I entered with those sages.

- *questi* ‘these’ → “those”: the near demonstrative becomes a distant demonstrative.

> 111 Giugnemmo in prato di fresca verdura.  

We reached a meadow of fresh verdure.

- *Giugnemmo in prato* ‘we arrived in meadow’ → “We reached a meadow”: the intransitive verb and prepositional phrase become a transitive verb with a direct object; English also supplies the indefinite article before “meadow”.

> 112 Genti v’ eran con occhi tardi e gravi,  
> 113 Di grande autorità ne’ lor sembianti:  

On it were people with eyes slow and grave, of great authority in their appearance.

- *Genti v’ eran* ‘people there were’ → “On it were people”: the locative *v’* is expanded to “On it,” explicitly referring to the meadow; the locative expression comes first, and the subject moves after the verb.
- *sembianti* ‘appearances’ → “appearance”: the plural becomes singular.

## Lines 114–126 — The Great Spirits

> 114 Parlavan rado, con voci soavi.  

They spoke seldom, with mild voices.

- *voci soavi* ‘voices sweet’ → “mild voices”: “mild” replaces ‘sweet’, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 115 Traemmoci così dall’ un de’ canti  
> 116 In luogo aperto, luminoso, ed alto,  
> 117 Sì che veder si potean tutti quanti.  

Thus we retired on one of the sides; into a place open, luminous, and high, so that they could all be seen.

- *Traemmoci così* ‘we drew ourselves thus’ → “Thus we retired”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and the adverb moves before the subject and verb.
- *dall’ un de’ canti* ‘from one of the corners’ → “on one of the sides”: “on” replaces ‘from’, and *canti* is interpreted as ‘sides’ rather than ‘corners’.
- *In luogo* ‘in place’ → “into a place”: English supplies an indefinite article, and “into” makes the direction of movement explicit.
- *veder si potean tutti quanti* ‘be seen could all of them’ → “they could all be seen”: the passive infinitive moves after the modal verb; *tutti quanti*, the emphatic subject, becomes “they” with “all” placed after “could”.

> 118 Colà diritto, sopra il verde smalto,  
> 119 Mi fur mostrati gli spiriti magni,  
> 120 Che di vederli in me stesso m’ esalto.  

There direct, upon the green enamel, were shewn to me the great spirits whom I glory within myself in having seen.

- *diritto* ‘upright’ → “direct”: the translation takes the word adverbially, rather than as describing the speaker’s upright posture.
- *Mi fur mostrati* ‘to me were shown’ → “were shewn to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *gli spiriti magni* ‘the spirits great’ → “the great spirits”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Che di vederli in me stesso m’ esalto* ‘that, from seeing them, within myself I exalt myself’ → “whom I glory within myself in having seen”: the relative becomes the direct object “whom”, absorbing the resumptive ‘them’ in *vederli*. The reflexive *m’esalto* becomes the intransitive “I glory”; the verb moves before the phrases qualifying it, and the simple infinitive ‘seeing’ becomes the retrospective “having seen”.

> 121 Io vidi Elettra con molti compagni,  
> 122 Tra’ quai conobbi ed Ettore, ed Enea,  
> 123 Cesare armato con gli occhi grifagni.  

I saw Electra with many companions: amongst whom I knew both Hector and Æneas; Cæsar armed, with the falcon eyes.

- *conobbi* ‘I recognized’ → “I knew”: the verb is rendered as knowing rather than recognizing.
- *ed Ettore, ed Enea* ‘and Hector, and Æneas’ → “both Hector and Æneas”: the repeated conjunction becomes the English correlative construction “both” … “and”.
- *gli occhi grifagni* ‘the eyes hawk-like’ → “the falcon eyes”: the descriptive adjective becomes an attributive noun placed before “eyes”.

> 124 Vidi Cammilla e la Pentesilea.  

I saw Camilla and Penthesilea.

- *la Pentesilea* ‘the Penthesilea’ → “Penthesilea”: the definite article before the personal name is omitted.

> 125 Dall’ altra parte vidi il re Latino,  
> 126 Che con Lavinia sua figlia sedea.  

On the other hand I saw the Latian king, sitting with Lavinia his daughter.

- *Dall’ altra parte* ‘from the other side’ → “On the other hand”: the spatial phrase is recast with “On” rather than ‘from’ and “hand” rather than ‘side’.
- *il re Latino* ‘the king Latian’ → “the Latian king”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Che con Lavinia sua figlia sedea* ‘who with Lavinia his daughter was sitting’ → “sitting with Lavinia his daughter”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase, and the verb moves before the phrase of accompaniment.

## Lines 127–138 — Heroes and Philosophers in Limbo

> 127 Vidi quel Bruto, che cacciò Tarquino,  

I saw that Brutus who expelled the Tarquin;

- *Tarquino* ‘Tarquin’ → “the Tarquin”: a definite article is supplied.

> 128 Lucrezia, Julia, Marzia e Corniglia,  

Lucretia, Julia, Martia, and Cornelia.

- Literal.

> 129 E solo in parte vidi il Saladino.  

And by himself apart, I saw the Saladin.

- *solo* ‘alone’ → “by himself”: the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *in parte* ‘to one side’ → “apart”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.

> 130 Poi che innalzai un poco più le ciglia,  
> 131 Vidi il Maestro di color che sanno,  
> 132 Seder tra filosofica famiglia.  

When I raised my eyelids a little higher, I saw the Master of those that know, sitting amid a philosophic family.

- *Poi che* ‘after’ → “When”: the explicit relation of succession becomes a more general temporal connection.
- *innalzai un poco più le ciglia* ‘I raised a little more the eyebrows’ → “I raised my eyelids a little higher”: “eyelids” interprets *ciglia* rather than rendering it as ‘eyebrows’; the definite article becomes a possessive, and the degree phrase moves after the object, with *più* rendered as “higher”.
- *Seder* ‘sit’ → “sitting”: the infinitive dependent on the verb of seeing becomes a present participle.
- *tra filosofica famiglia* ‘amid philosophic family’ → “amid a philosophic family”: an indefinite article is supplied.

> 133 Tutti lo miran, tutti onor gli fanno.  

All regard him; all do him honour.

- *Tutti lo miran* ‘all him regard’ → “All regard him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *tutti onor gli fanno* ‘all honour to him do’ → “all do him honour”: the verb moves before its objects, and the recipient is expressed as an indirect object without a preposition.

> 134 Quivi vid’ io e Socrate e Platone,  
> 135 Che innanzi agli altri più presso gli stanno.  

Here I saw Socrates and Plato, who before the rest stand nearest to him;

- *vid’ io* ‘saw I’ → “I saw”: the explicit subject moves before the verb.
- *e Socrate e Platone* ‘and Socrates and Plato’ → “Socrates and Plato”: the first, correlative conjunction is omitted.
- *innanzi agli altri* ‘before the others’ → “before the rest”: a singular collective expression replaces the plural.
- *più presso gli stanno* ‘nearer to him stand’ → “stand nearest to him”: the verb moves before the expression of proximity, and the comparative is rendered as a superlative.

> 136 Democrito, che il mondo a caso pone,  

Democritus, who ascribes the world to chance;

- *che il mondo a caso pone* ‘who the world at random places’ → “who ascribes the world to chance”: the verb moves before its complements; “ascribes” interprets *pone* as assigning an origin, and “to chance” makes chance the cause rather than rendering *a caso* as an adverbial phrase.

> 137 Diogenes, Anassagora e Tale,  
> 138 Empedocles, Eraclito e Zenone:  

Diogenes, Anaxagoras, and Thales; Empedocles, Heraclítus, and Zeno.

- Literal.

## Lines 139–151 — The Sages and the Departure

> 139 E vidi il buono accoglitor del quale,  
> 140 Dioscoride dico: e vidi Orfeo,  
> 141 Tullio, e Livio, e Seneca morale:  

And I saw the good collector of the qualities, Dioscorides I mean; and saw Orpheus, Tully, Livy, and Seneca the moralist;

- *del quale* ‘of the quality’ → “of the qualities”: the substantivized singular *quale* becomes a plural noun.
- *Dioscoride dico* ‘Dioscorides I say’ → “Dioscorides I mean”: “mean” replaces ‘say’ in this clarification of the preceding description.
- *e Livio* ‘and Livy’ → “Livy”: the conjunction is omitted.
- *Seneca morale* ‘Seneca moral’ → “Seneca the moralist”: the adjective becomes a noun in apposition, with an article supplied.

> 142 Euclide geométra, e Tolommeo,  
> 143 Ippocrate, Avicenna e Galieno,  
> 144 Averrois che il gran comento feo.  

Euclid the geometer, and Ptolemæus; Hippocrates, Avicenna, and Galen; Averrhoës, who made the great comment.

- *Euclide geométra* ‘Euclid geometer’ → “Euclid the geometer”: an article is supplied before the appositional noun.
- *che il gran comento feo* ‘who the great commentary made’ → “who made the great comment”: the verb moves before its object; “comment” renders *comento* in the sense of a commentary.

> 145 Io non posso ritrar di tutti appieno;  

I may not paint them all in full;

- *Io non posso* ‘I cannot’ → “I may not”: “may” replaces ‘can’ as the modal expressing inability here.
- *ritrar di tutti* ‘give a portrayal of all’ → “paint them all”: the construction with *di* becomes a direct object, with “them” supplied.
- *appieno* ‘fully’ → “in full”: the single adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.

> 146 Però che sì mi caccia il lungo tema,  
> 147 Che molte volte al fatto il dir vien meno.  

for the long theme so chases me, that many times the word comes short of the reality.

- *Però che* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *sì mi caccia il lungo tema* ‘so me chases the long theme’ → “the long theme so chases me”: the subject moves before the verb, and the object pronoun follows it.
- *al fatto* ‘to the fact’ → “of the reality”: *fatto* is rendered as “reality”; the preposition changes to suit “comes short”, and the phrase moves from before the subject to the end of the clause.
- *il dir* ‘the saying’ → “the word”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun denoting verbal expression.
- *vien meno* ‘comes less’ → “comes short”: the Italian idiom is replaced by an English idiom for falling short.

> 148 La sesta compagnia in duo si scema:  

The company of six diminishes to two.

- *La sesta compagnia* ‘the sixth company’ → “The company of six”: the ordinal adjective is interpreted as indicating a company numbering six, not the sixth company in a sequence.
- *in duo si scema* ‘into two diminishes itself’ → “diminishes to two”: the reflexive construction becomes an English intransitive verb; the phrase indicating the resulting number follows the verb, with “to” replacing ‘into’.

> 149 Per altra via mi mena il savio Duca,  
> 150 Fuor della queta, nell’ aura che trema;  
> 151 E vengo in parte, ove non è che luca.  

By another road the sage guide leads me, out of the quiet, into the trembling air; and I come to a part where there is nought that shines.

- *mi mena il savio Duca* ‘me leads the sage guide’ → “the sage guide leads me”: the subject moves before the verb, and the object pronoun follows it.
- *nell’ aura che trema* ‘in the air that trembles’ → “into the trembling air”: the preposition explicitly expresses movement into; the relative clause becomes a participial modifier before the noun.
- *in parte* ‘in a place’ → “to a part”: the preposition expresses the destination, and *parte* is rendered as “part” rather than ‘place’; English supplies an indefinite article.
- *non è che luca* ‘there is not [anything] that may shine’ → “there is nought that shines”: the negative existential construction is expressed with “nought”; the subjunctive *luca* becomes the indicative “shines”.

## Summary — Close Following, English Reshaping

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s phrasing and sequence, often retaining its images, repetitions, and unusual expressions. “Without hope we live in desire” closely follows *senza speme vivemo in disio*, while “the green enamel” preserves *il verde smalto*. Yet he regularly rearranges words into more usual English prose order: subjects placed after verbs move before them, objects follow their verbs, and adjectives precede their nouns. Thus *Grazia acquista* becomes “gains favour”, and *gli spiriti magni* becomes “the great spirits”. Longer phrases also move: *Dritto levato* is brought forward as “having risen erect”.

Changes of construction frequently make the English less literal without greatly changing its sense. *Vo’ che sappi* becomes “I wish thee to know”, replacing a finite subordinate clause with an object and infinitive; *Ch’ avean le turbe* becomes “of the crowds”, reducing a relative clause to a possessive phrase. Reflexive verbs often become English intransitives, as *Volsersi* becomes “they turned”. Elsewhere, a quality becomes a noun: *la via lunga*, ‘the long way’, becomes “the length of way”. Italian subjunctives are generally represented by English indicatives, while negatives are redistributed, as in *non … veruna cosa*, rendered “nothing”.

Word choice ranges from close equivalents to interpretive substitutions. *Alto sonno* becomes the idiomatic “deep sleep”; *guai* becomes “wailings”, emphasizing sound; and *cotanto senno*, ‘so much wisdom’, becomes “such intelligences”, naming the people rather than their collective quality. Some choices reduce intensity: *smorto*, ‘deathly pale’, becomes “pale”, and *altissimo* becomes “great” in the greeting to the poet. Connectives can also sharpen an interpretation: *se* is rendered “though” or “seeing”, and *perch’ ei dicessi* becomes “though he was speaking”.

Small omissions and additions help shape the prose. Carlyle leaves out *Or*, the possessive in *Maestro mio*, and some repeated conjunctions and prepositions; he supplies such clarifications as “their” in “as their lord” and “distant” after “not so”. Definite articles marking personal association sometimes become possessives, as *l’ occhio* becomes “my … eyes” and *del Primo Parente* becomes “of our First Parent”. More generally, beyond the changes individually noted in the comparison, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and omits Italian articles before possessives, as *il mio Maestro* becomes “my Master”. These routine adjustments coexist with a deliberately close manner of translation, preserving much of the Italian’s verbal texture even where its grammar is reshaped.
