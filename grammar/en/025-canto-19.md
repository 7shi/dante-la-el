# Inferno 19

## Lines 1–9 — Denunciation Above the Third Chasm

> 1 O SIMON mago, o miseri seguaci,  
> 2 Che le cose di Dio, che di bontate  
> 3 Deono essere spose, e voi rapaci  
> 4 Per oro e per argento adulterate:  

O SIMON MAGUS! O wretched followers of his and robbers ye, who for gold and silver prostitute the things of God, that should be wedded unto righteousness!

- *miseri seguaci* ‘wretched followers’ → “wretched followers of his”: “of his” is supplied to make the relationship explicit.
- *che di bontate Deono essere spose* ‘that of goodness ought to be brides’ → “that should be wedded unto righteousness”: the noun *spose* becomes the participle “wedded”; *di* becomes “unto”; and *bontate* is interpreted as “righteousness” rather than simply ‘goodness’. The phrase expressing the relationship moves after the predicate.
- *e voi rapaci* ‘and you rapacious ones’ → “and robbers ye”: the adjective becomes the noun “robbers”, and its position relative to the pronoun is reversed. The whole phrase moves into the opening address, before “who”, rather than resuming the address within the relative construction.
- *Per oro e per argento* ‘for gold and for silver’ → “for gold and silver”: the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *adulterate* ‘you adulterate’ → “prostitute”: a different verb expresses the sexual metaphor. The verb moves before its object, “the things of God”, which in Italian precedes both the payment phrase and the verb; the object’s accompanying relative clause consequently moves with it.

> 5 Or convien che per voi suoni la tromba,  
> 6 Perocchè nella terza bolgia state.  

Now must the trumpet sound for you; for ye are in the third chasm.

- *Or convien che per voi suoni la tromba* ‘Now it is fitting that for you the trumpet should sound’ → “Now must the trumpet sound for you”: the impersonal construction *convien che*, followed by a subjunctive clause, becomes a main clause with the modal “must”. The subject precedes “sound” rather than following it, and “for you” moves to the end.
- *Perocchè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *nella terza bolgia* ‘in the third pouch’ → “in the third chasm”: the figurative *bolgia* is rendered by a word describing the physical hollow.
- *state* ‘you stand’ → “ye are”: the verb is rendered as simple location rather than standing or remaining, and moves before the locative phrase.

> 7 Già eravamo alla seguente tomba  
> 8 Montati, dello scoglio in quella parte  
> 9 Che appunto sovra mezzo il fosso piomba.  

Already we had mounted to the following grave, on that part of the cliff which hangs right over the middle of the foss.

- *Già eravamo … Montati* ‘Already we were … mounted’ → “Already we had mounted”: Italian forms this pluperfect with *essere*; English uses “had”. The destination phrase no longer intervenes between the auxiliary and the participle.
- *dello scoglio in quella parte* ‘of the rock in that part’ → “on that part of the cliff”: the preceding possessive phrase moves after “part”; *in* becomes “on”; and *scoglio* is rendered more specifically as “cliff”.
- *piomba* ‘plunges’ → “hangs”: the translation expresses overhanging position rather than a downward plunge. The verb moves from the end of the relative clause to immediately after “which”, before the adverb and locative phrase.

## Lines 10–18 — Divine Art and Round Holes

> 10 O Somma Sapïenza, quanta è l’ arte  
> 11 Che mostri in cielo, in terra e nel mal mondo,  
> 12 E quanto giusto tua Virtù comparte!  

O Wisdom Supreme, what art thou shewest in heaven, on earth and in the evil world, and how justly thy Goodness dispenses!

- *Somma Sapïenza* ‘Supreme Wisdom’ → “Wisdom Supreme”: the adjective is placed after the noun.
- *quanta è l’ arte Che mostri* ‘how great is the art that thou shewest’ → “what art thou shewest”: the copular exclamation and its relative clause become a single exclamatory construction; “what” replaces the explicit expression of greatness, and the copula and relative pronoun disappear.
- *in terra* ‘in earth’ → “on earth”: the preposition changes to the idiomatic English expression.
- *quanto giusto* ‘how just’ → “how justly”: the adjective used adverbially becomes an explicit English adverb.
- *Virtù* ‘power’ → “Goodness”: the translation gives the noun a moral sense rather than the sense of power or efficacy.

> 13 Io vidi, per le coste e per lo fondo,  
> 14 Piena la pietra livida di fori  
> 15 D’ un largo tutti, e ciascuno era tondo.  

I saw the livid stone, on the sides and on the bottom, full of holes, all of one breadth; and each was round.

- *per le coste e per lo fondo* ‘along the sides and along the bottom’ → “on the sides and on the bottom”: the prepositions express position rather than extension along a surface, and the phrase moves from before the object to after it.
- *Piena la pietra livida* ‘full the stone livid’ → “the livid stone, on the sides and on the bottom, full”: the predicative adjective moves from before the object to after both the object and the phrase specifying position; the adjective describing the stone moves before the noun.
- *D’ un largo tutti* ‘of one breadth all’ → “all of one breadth”: the quantifier moves before the phrase describing breadth.

> 16 Non mi parean meno ampi nè maggiori,  
> 17 Che quei che son nel mio bel San Giovanni  
> 18 Fatti per luogo de’ battezzatori;  

Not less wide they seemed to me, nor larger, than those that are in my beauteous San Giovanni made for stands to the baptizers;

- *Non mi parean meno ampi* ‘not to me seemed less wide’ → “Not less wide they seemed to me”: the comparison is brought forward, the subject implicit in the verb is supplied, and the indirect-object pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase after the verb.
- *per luogo* ‘for a place’ → “for stands”: the singular becomes plural, and the general noun for a place is rendered as designated standing places.
- *de’ battezzatori* ‘of the baptizers’ → “to the baptizers”: the possessive or relational construction with *di* becomes a phrase indicating those for whom the stands are intended.

## Lines 19–33 — Burning Soles and Writhing Feet

> 19 L’ un degli quali, ancor non è molt’ anni,  
> 20 Rupp’ io per un che dentro vi annegava:  

one of which, not many years ago, I broke to save one that was drowning in it:

- *L’ un degli quali* ‘the one of which’ → “one of which”: the definite article before ‘one’ is omitted.
- *ancor non è molt’ anni* ‘it is not yet many years’ → “not many years ago”: the finite temporal expression becomes an adverbial phrase; ‘yet’ is not separately rendered, and “ago” supplies the backward reference.
- *Rupp’ io* ‘broke I’ → “I broke”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *per un* ‘for one’ → “to save one”: the prepositional phrase becomes an infinitive of purpose, explicitly supplying the action of saving.
- *dentro vi* ‘inside there’ → “in it”: the two adverbs become a prepositional phrase referring to the object, and move after “was drowning”.

> 21 E questo sia suggel ch’ ogni uomo sganni.  

and be this a seal to undeceive all men.

- *questo sia suggel* ‘this be seal’ → “be this a seal”: the subject follows the verb, and an indefinite article is supplied before the predicate noun.
- *ch’ ogni uomo sganni* ‘that every man it may undeceive’ → “to undeceive all men”: the relative clause with a subjunctive verb becomes an infinitive expressing purpose; the object moves after the verb, and distributive singular ‘every man’ becomes plural “all men”.

> 22 Fuor della bocca a ciascun soperchiava  
> 23 D’ un peccator li piedi, e delle gambe  
> 24 Infino al grosso; e l’ altro dentro stava.  

From the mouth of each emerged a sinner’s feet, and legs up to the calf; and the rest remained within.

- *Fuor della bocca a ciascun* ‘out of the mouth for each’ → “From the mouth of each”: ‘out of’ becomes “From”, and the distributive phrase ‘for each’ becomes a possessive construction modifying “mouth”.
- *soperchiava* ‘projected’ → “emerged”: projection beyond an opening is expressed as emergence from it.
- *D’ un peccator li piedi* ‘of a sinner the feet’ → “a sinner’s feet”: the preceding possessive phrase becomes an English possessive determiner.
- *delle gambe* ‘of the legs’ → “legs”: the partitive construction is omitted, leaving the noun directly coordinated with “feet”.
- *al grosso* ‘to the thick part’ → “to the calf”: the substantivized adjective is rendered by the specific anatomical noun.
- *l’ altro* ‘the other’ → “the rest”: the substantivized adjective denoting the remaining portion becomes a collective expression.
- *dentro stava* ‘within stayed’ → “remained within”: the locative adverb moves after the verb.

> 25 Le piante erano accese a tutti intrambe;  
> 26 Perchè sì forte guizzavan le giunte,  
> 27 Che spezzate averian ritorte e strambe.  

The soles of all were both on fire; wherefore the joints quivered so strongly, that they would have snapped in pieces withes and grass-ropes.

- *erano accese a tutti intrambe* ‘were kindled for all both’ → “of all were both on fire”: the participle becomes a prepositional phrase describing a state; the distributive phrase ‘for all’ becomes a possessive modifier of “soles” and moves beside that noun; ‘both’ moves from the end of the clause to a position before “on fire”.
- *Perchè* ‘because’ → “wherefore”: the connective is interpreted consequentially, as ‘for which reason’, rather than as introducing a cause.
- *sì forte guizzavan le giunte* ‘so strongly quivered the joints’ → “the joints quivered so strongly”: the subject moves before the verb, and the adverbial phrase moves after it.
- *spezzate averian* ‘broken they would have’ → “they would have snapped in pieces”: the participle moves after the conditional auxiliary, and ‘broken’ is expanded into “snapped in pieces”.

> 28 Qual suole il fiammeggiar delle cose unte  
> 29 Muoversi pur su per l’ estrema buccia,  
> 30 Tal era lì da’ calcagni alle punte.  

As the flaming of things oiled moves only on their outer surface; so was it there, from the heels to the points.

- *suole … Muoversi* ‘is accustomed … to move’ → “moves”: the customary-action construction becomes a simple present verb; the auxiliary preceding the subject disappears, and the translated verb follows the subject.
- *pur su per l’ estrema buccia* ‘only up over the outermost skin’ → “only on their outer surface”: directional wording becomes the locative “on”; ‘outermost’ becomes “outer”, and ‘skin’ becomes “surface”; the definite article is replaced by the supplied possessive “their”.
- *Tal* ‘such’ → “so”: the demonstrative adjective is rendered by an adverb.

> 31 Chi è colui, Maestro, che si cruccia,  
> 32 Guizzando più che gli altri suoi consorti,  
> 33 Diss’ io, e cui più rossa fiamma succia?  

“Master! who is that who writhes himself, quivering more than all his fellows,” I said, “and sucked by ruddier flame?”

- *Chi è colui, Maestro* ‘Who is that, Master’ → “Master! who is that”: the address moves from within the question to its beginning.
- *si cruccia* ‘torments himself’ → “writhes himself”: suffering is rendered through its bodily manifestation, while the reflexive construction is retained.
- *gli altri* ‘the other’ → “all”: the distinction between this individual and the others becomes a statement encompassing all his fellows.
- *Diss’ io* ‘said I’ → “I said”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *e cui più rossa fiamma succia* ‘and whom redder flame sucks’ → “and sucked by ruddier flame”: the active relative clause becomes a passive participial phrase, without an expressed relative pronoun or auxiliary; “flame” changes from subject to agent introduced by “by”, and the analytic comparative becomes the inflected “ruddier”.

## Lines 34–45 — Descent to the Cleft

> 34 Ed egli a me: Se tu vuoi ch’ io ti porti  
> 35 Laggiù per quella ripa che più giace,  
> 36 Da lui saprai di sè e de’ suoi torti.  

And he to me: “If thou wilt have me carry thee down there, by that lower bank, thou shalt learn from him about himself and about his wrongs.”

- *Se tu vuoi ch’ io ti porti* ‘if thou wishest that I carry thee’ → “If thou wilt have me carry thee”: the subordinate clause with a subjunctive verb becomes a construction with “have”, an object, and a bare infinitive; *io* becomes “me”, and *ti* moves from before the verb to after it.
- *per quella ripa che più giace* ‘by that bank which lies lower’ → “by that lower bank”: the relative clause becomes an adjective before the noun; *giace*, ‘lies’, is no longer separately expressed.
- *Da lui saprai* ‘from him thou shalt know’ → “thou shalt learn from him”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb, and knowing is rendered as acquiring knowledge.
- *di sè e de’ suoi torti* ‘of himself and of his wrongs’ → “about himself and about his wrongs”: both occurrences of *di* are rendered as “about”, explicitly marking the subjects of what will be learned.

> 37 Ed io: Tanto m’ è bel, quanto a te piace:  

And I: “Whatever pleases thee, to me is grateful.

- *Tanto m’ è bel, quanto a te piace* ‘as much is pleasing to me as pleases thee’ → “Whatever pleases thee, to me is grateful”: the correlative construction *tanto … quanto*, expressing correspondence in degree, becomes a clause introduced by “Whatever”. The clause about what pleases the addressee comes first rather than last, and *bel*, literally ‘beautiful’ or here ‘pleasing’, becomes “grateful” in its sense of ‘agreeable’.

> 38 Tu se’ Signore, e sai ch’ io non mi parto  
> 39 Dal tuo volere, e sai quel che si tace.  

Thou art my Lord; and knowest that I depart not from thy will: also thou knowest what is not spoken.”

- *Tu se’ Signore* ‘thou art Lord’ → “Thou art my Lord”: “my” is supplied.
- *io non mi parto* ‘I do not withdraw myself’ → “I depart not”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes the English intransitive “depart”, without a reflexive pronoun; the negative follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *e* ‘and’ → “also”: the conjunction introducing the second statement of knowing becomes an additive adverb.
- *quel che si tace* ‘that which is kept silent’ → “what is not spoken”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun are condensed into “what”, and the affirmative verb of keeping silent becomes a negated verb of speaking.

> 40 Allor venimmo in su l’ argine quarto;  

Then we came upon the fourth bulwark.

- *l’ argine quarto* ‘the embankment fourth’ → “the fourth bulwark”: “bulwark” replaces ‘embankment’, and the ordinal moves before the noun.

> 41 Volgemmo, e discendemmo a mano stanca  
> 42 Laggiù nel fondo foracchiato ed arto.  

We turned and descended, on the left hand, down there into the perforated and narrow bottom.

- *a mano stanca* ‘at the weary hand’ → “on the left hand”: the idiomatic designation *stanca* is rendered as “left” rather than literally ‘weary’; the adjective moves before the noun, an article is supplied, and *a* becomes “on”.
- *nel* ‘in the’ → “into the”: the English preposition explicitly expresses movement into a place.
- *fondo foracchiato ed arto* ‘bottom perforated and narrow’ → “perforated and narrow bottom”: both adjectives move before the noun.

> 43 E il buon Maestro ancor dalla sua anca  
> 44 Non mi dipose, sin mi giunse al rotto  
> 45 Di quei che sì piangeva con la zanca.  

And the kind Master did not yet depose me from his side, till he brought me to the cleft of him who so lamented with his legs.

- *buon* ‘good’ → “kind”: general goodness is rendered specifically as kindness.
- *ancor … Non mi dipose* ‘still … did not set me down’ → “did not yet depose me”: *ancor* is placed within the negative construction as “not yet”, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *dalla sua anca* ‘from his hip’ → “from his side”: the specific body part becomes the broader “side”, and the phrase moves from before the verb to after it.
- *sin mi giunse* ‘until me he brought’ → “till he brought me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *al rotto* ‘to the hole’ → “to the cleft”: the opening is described as a “cleft” rather than a hole.
- *Di quei* ‘of that one’ → “of him”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *sì piangeva* ‘so was weeping’ → “so lamented”: weeping becomes the broader act of lamenting, and the Italian imperfect is rendered by a simple English past rather than an explicitly ongoing construction.
- *con la zanca* ‘with the leg’ → “with his legs”: the singular becomes plural, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

## Lines 46–57 — An Address and a Mistaken Identity

> 46 O qual che sei, che ’l di su tien di sotto,  
> 47 Anima trista, come pal commessa,  
> 48 Comincia’ io a dir, se puoi, fa motto.  

“O whoe’er thou be that hast thy upper part beneath, unhappy spirit, planted like a stake!” I began to say; “if thou art able, speak.”

- *qual che sei* ‘whoever thou art’ → “whoe’er thou be”: the Italian indicative becomes the English subjunctive “be”.
- *che ’l di su tien di sotto* ‘that the upper part holdest below’ → “that hast thy upper part beneath”: “hast” replaces ‘holdest’; the object moves after the verb; and the definite article becomes the possessive “thy”. “part” makes explicit the noun implied by the substantivized *di su*.
- *Anima trista* ‘soul sorrowful’ → “unhappy spirit”: “spirit” renders ‘soul’, “unhappy” renders ‘sorrowful’, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *come pal commessa* ‘like a stake driven in’ → “planted like a stake”: ‘driven in’ becomes “planted”, and the participle moves before the comparison.
- *Comincia’ io a dir* ‘began I to say’ → “I began to say”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *se puoi* ‘if thou canst’ → “if thou art able”: the modal verb becomes a construction with “art” and an adjective.
- *fa motto* ‘make an utterance’ → “speak”: the verb-and-noun expression becomes a single verb.

> 49 Io stava come il frate che confessa  
> 50 Lo perfido assassin che, poi ch’ è fitto,  
> 51 Richiama lui, perchè la morte cessa.  

I stood, like the friar who is confessing a treacherous assassin that, after being fixed, recalls him to delay the death.

- *Io stava* ‘I was standing’ → “I stood”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by an English simple past rather than an explicitly continuing construction.
- *che confessa* ‘who confesses’ → “who is confessing”: the simple present becomes a present progressive.
- *Lo perfido assassin* ‘the treacherous assassin’ → “a treacherous assassin”: the definite article becomes indefinite.
- *poi ch’ è fitto* ‘after he is fixed’ → “after being fixed”: the finite passive clause becomes a nonfinite passive construction.
- *perchè la morte cessa* ‘because he postpones the death’ → “to delay the death”: the finite clause is interpreted as expressing purpose and recast as an infinitive. The object *la morte*, which precedes *cessa*, follows “delay”.

> 52 Ed ei gridò: Sei tu già costì ritto,  
> 53 Sei tu già costì ritto, Bonifazio?  
> 54 Di parecchi anni mi mentì lo scritto.  

And he cried: “Art thou there already standing, Boniface? Art thou there already standing? By several years the writ has lied to me.

- *Sei tu già costì ritto, Sei tu già costì ritto, Bonifazio?* ‘Art thou already there upright, art thou already there upright, Boniface?’ → “Art thou there already standing, Boniface? Art thou there already standing?”: “there” precedes “already”, reversing the Italian adverbs’ order; “standing” renders ‘upright’; and the name moves from the second occurrence of the question to the first.
- *mi mentì lo scritto* ‘to me lied the writ’ → “the writ has lied to me”: the postposed subject moves before the verb, the indirect-object pronoun becomes a following prepositional phrase, and the simple past becomes a present perfect.

> 55 Sei tu sì tosto di quell’ aver sazio,  
> 56 Per lo qual non temesti torre a inganno  
> 57 La bella Donna, e di poi farne strazio?  

Art thou so quickly sated with that wealth, for which thou didst not fear to seize the comely Lady by deceit, and then make havoc of her?”

- *sì tosto di quell’ aver sazio* ‘so soon of that wealth sated’ → “so quickly sated with that wealth”: “quickly” renders the temporal ‘soon’; the complement moves after “sated”; and *di* becomes “with” rather than ‘of’.
- *Per lo qual* ‘for the which’ → “for which”: the article accompanying the Italian relative pronoun is omitted.
- *torre a inganno La bella Donna* ‘to take by deceit the beautiful Lady’ → “to seize the comely Lady by deceit”: “seize” gives ‘take’ a stronger sense, “comely” renders ‘beautiful’, and the phrase expressing means moves after the object.
- *farne strazio* ‘make of her havoc’ → “make havoc of her”: the attached pronoun *ne* becomes the separate phrase “of her”, placed after the noun.

## Lines 58–69 — Denial and a Spirit’s Reply

> 58 Tal mi fec’ io, quai son color che stanno,  
> 59 Per non intender ciò ch’ è lor risposto,  
> 60 Quasi scornati, e risponder non sanno.  

I became like those who stand as if bemocked, not comprehending what is answered to them, and unable to reply.

- *Tal mi fec’ io, quai son* ‘Such I made myself, as are’ → “I became like”: the reflexive construction becomes “became”; the correlative construction is compressed into “like”, with no separate rendering of *son*.
- *Per non intender* ‘through not understanding’ → “not comprehending”: the causal preposition is omitted, and the infinitive becomes a participle.
- *ciò ch’ è lor risposto* ‘that which is answered to them’ → “what is answered to them”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun are combined into “what”.
- *Quasi scornati* ‘as if put to shame’ → “as if bemocked”: being shamed or discomfited is rendered as being mocked; the phrase is moved before the clause about not understanding.
- *e risponder non sanno* ‘and how to reply they do not know’ → “and unable to reply”: the finite verb expressing lack of knowledge becomes an adjective expressing inability, and the infinitive moves after it.

> 61 Allor Virgilio disse: Dilli tosto,  
> 62 Non son colui, non son colui che credi.  

Then Virgil said: “Say to him quickly, ‘I am not he, I am not he whom thou thinkest.’”

- *colui … colui* ‘that one … that one’ → “he” … “he”: the demonstrative pronoun is rendered by a personal pronoun in both occurrences.

> 63 Ed io risposi come a me fu imposto:  
> 64 Perchè lo spirto tutti storse i piedi:  

And I replied as was enjoined me; whereat the spirit quite wrenched his feet.

- *come a me fu imposto* ‘as to me was enjoined’ → “as was enjoined me”: the indirect object moves after the verb, and its preposition is omitted.
- *Perchè* ‘for which reason’ → “whereat”: the explicit connection of consequence becomes one of reaction to the preceding words.
- *tutti* ‘all’ → “quite”: the adjective agreeing with *piedi* becomes an adverb modifying the action.
- *storse* ‘twisted’ → “wrenched”: the English verb gives the twisting a more forceful expression.
- *i piedi* ‘the feet’ → “his feet”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 65 Poi sospirando, e con voce di pianto,  
> 66 Mi disse: Dunque che a me richiedi?  

Thereafter, sighing and with voice of weeping, he said to me: “Then what askest thou of me?

- *Mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “he said to me”: the indirect object moves from before the verb to after it.
- *che a me richiedi* ‘what of me dost thou ask’ → “what askest thou of me”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb.

> 67 Se di saper ch’ io sia ti cal cotanto,  
> 68 Che tu abbi però la ripa scorsa,  
> 69 Sappi ch’ io fui vestito del gran manto:  

If to know who I am concerneth thee so much, that thou hast therefore passed the bank, learn that I was clothed with the Great Mantle.

- *di saper* ‘about knowing’ → “to know”: the preposition governing the infinitive disappears, and the infinitive becomes the subject of the English verb.
- *ch’ io sia* ‘who I be’ → “who I am”: the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *ti cal cotanto* ‘it matters to thee so much’ → “concerneth thee so much”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal verb whose subject is the preceding infinitive; the indirect object becomes a direct object.
- *Che tu abbi però la ripa scorsa* ‘that thou have therefore the bank traversed’ → “that thou hast therefore passed the bank”: the perfect subjunctive becomes a perfect indicative, and the object moves from between the auxiliary and participle to after the completed verb phrase.
- *Sappi* ‘Know’ → “learn”: the command to know is expressed as a command to acquire knowledge.

## Lines 70–84 — Greed and Successors in Punishment

> 70 E veramente fui figliuol dell’ Orsa,  
> 71 Cupido sì, per avanzar gli Orsatti,  
> 72 Che su l’ avere, e qui me misi in borsa.  

And verily I was a son of the She-bear, so eager to advance the Whelps, that I pursed wealth above, and here myself.

- *Cupido sì* ‘eager so’ → “so eager”: the degree adverb moves before the adjective.
- *gli Orsatti* ‘the little bears’ → “the Whelps”: the diminutive naming bears becomes a more general word for young animals.
- *Che su l’ avere, e qui me misi in borsa* ‘that above the wealth, and here myself, I put in a purse’ → “that I pursed wealth above, and here myself”: ‘put in a purse’ becomes the verb “pursed”; the verb moves before its objects, “above” follows “wealth,” and the article before ‘wealth’ is omitted.

> 73 Di sotto al capo mio son gli altri tratti,  
> 74 Che precedetter me simoneggiando,  
> 75 Per la fessura della pietra piatti.  

Beneath my head are dragged the others who preceded me in simony, cowering along the fissure of the stone.

- *Di sotto al capo mio* ‘beneath the head my’ → “Beneath my head”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *son gli altri tratti* ‘are the others dragged’ → “are dragged the others”: the participle moves before the subject.
- *simoneggiando* ‘practising simony’ → “in simony”: the verbal gerund becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *Per* ‘through’ → “along”: the preposition is interpreted as indicating extension along the fissure.
- *piatti* ‘flat’ → “cowering”: an adjective describing position becomes a participle suggesting an action or posture; it also moves before the phrase describing the fissure.

> 76 Laggiù cascherò io altresì, quando  
> 77 Verrà colui ch’ io credea che tu fossi,  
> 78 Allor ch’ io feci il subito dimando.  

I too shall fall down thither, when he comes for whom I took thee when I put the sudden question.

- *Laggiù* ‘down there’ → “down thither”: the English explicitly marks motion towards a place; the adverbial phrase moves after the verb.
- *cascherò io altresì* ‘shall fall I also’ → “I too shall fall”: the subject and additive adverb move before the verb.
- *Verrà colui ch’ io credea che tu fossi* ‘will come the one whom I believed that thou wast’ → “he comes for whom I took thee”: the future becomes a present tense in the temporal clause, and the subject moves before the verb. ‘Believed that thou wast’ is recast as “took thee” with “for whom,” replacing the subordinate clause with an object-and-complement construction.
- *Allor ch’* ‘then when’ → “when”: the explicit ‘then’ is omitted.
- *io feci* ‘I made’ → “I put”: English uses a different verb for asking the question.

> 79 Ma più è il tempo già che i piè mi cossi,  
> 80 E ch’ io son stato così sottosopra,  
> 81 Ch’ ei non starà piantato coi piè rossi:  

But longer is the time already, that I have baked my feet and stood inverted thus, than he shall stand planted with glowing feet.

- *più* ‘more’ → “longer”: the comparative is expressed specifically in terms of duration.
- *i piè mi cossi* ‘the feet for myself I baked’ → “I have baked my feet”: the reflexive dative becomes possessive “my,” the object moves after the verb, and the simple past becomes a present perfect.
- *E ch’ io son stato* ‘and that I have been’ → “and stood”: the repeated ‘that’ is omitted, ‘been’ becomes “stood,” and the subject and perfect auxiliary are shared with the preceding clause.
- *così sottosopra* ‘thus upside down’ → “inverted thus”: ‘upside down’ becomes the participial “inverted,” and the adverb moves after it.
- *Ch’ ei non* ‘than he not’ → “than he”: the comparative construction’s expletive negative is omitted.
- *starà* ‘will remain’ → “shall stand”: the verb is rendered as describing posture rather than simply remaining in a condition.
- *coi piè rossi* ‘with the feet red’ → “with glowing feet”: ‘red’ is interpreted as “glowing,” the adjective moves before the noun, and the definite article is omitted.

> 82 Chè dopo lui verrà, di più laid’ opra,  
> 83 Di vêr ponente un Pastor senza legge,  
> 84 Tal che convien che lui e me ricopra.  

For after him, from westward, there shall come a lawless Shepherd, of uglier deeds, fit to cover him and me.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *verrà* ‘shall come’ → “there shall come”: introductory “there” is supplied, and the verb moves after the phrase indicating direction.
- *di più laid’ opra* ‘of more foul work’ → “of uglier deeds”: singular ‘work’ becomes plural “deeds,” and the phrase moves after the noun it qualifies.
- *Di vêr ponente* ‘from towards the west’ → “from westward”: the directional expression is compressed into a preposition and directional adverb.
- *un Pastor senza legge* ‘a Shepherd without law’ → “a lawless Shepherd”: the postposed prepositional phrase becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *Tal che convien che lui e me ricopra* ‘such that it is necessary that him and me he cover’ → “fit to cover him and me”: necessity is recast as fitness; the finite subjunctive clause becomes an infinitive, and its objects move after the verb.

## Lines 85–96 — Apostolic Poverty Against Simony

> 85 Nuovo Iason sarà, di cui si legge  
> 86 Ne’ Maccabei: e come a quel fu molle  
> 87 Suo Re, così fia a lui chi Francia regge.  

A new Jason will it be, of whom we read in Maccabees: and as to that high priest his king was pliant; so to this shall be he who governs France.”

- *Nuovo Iason sarà* ‘new Jason he will be’ → “A new Jason will it be”: an indefinite article is supplied, and the subject is rendered as “it”, placed between the auxiliary and the main verb.
- *si legge* ‘one reads’ → “we read”: the impersonal construction becomes a first-person plural.
- *Ne’ Maccabei* ‘in the Maccabees’ → “in Maccabees”: the definite article is omitted.
- *a quel* ‘to that one’ → “to that high priest”: “high priest” supplies a noun identifying the demonstrative’s referent.
- *fu molle Suo Re* ‘was soft his king’ → “his king was pliant”: the subject moves before the verb, and “pliant” renders *molle* in its figurative sense.
- *fia a lui* ‘will be to him’ → “to this shall be”: the prepositional phrase moves before the verb, and the personal pronoun becomes a demonstrative.
- *chi Francia regge* ‘he who France governs’ → “he who governs France”: the object moves after the verb.

> 88 Io non so s’ io mi fui qui troppo folle,  
> 89 Ch’ io pur risposi lui a questo metro:  

I know not if here I was too hardy, for I answered him in this strain:

- *s’ io mi fui qui troppo folle* ‘if I was myself here too foolish’ → “if here I was too hardy”: the pleonastic *mi* has no separate equivalent; “here” moves before the subject; and “hardy” interprets *folle* as excessive boldness rather than foolishness.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *io pur risposi lui* ‘I indeed answered him’ → “I answered him”: the emphatic *pur* is omitted.
- *a questo metro* ‘to this measure’ → “in this strain”: the Italian expression is replaced by an English idiom for a manner of speaking.

> 90 Deh or mi di’ quanto tesoro volle  
> 91 Nostro Signore in prima da San Pietro,  
> 92 Che ponesse le chiavi in sua balìa?  

“Ah! Now tell me how much treasure our Lord required of St. Peter, before he put the keys into his keeping?

- *mi di’* ‘to me tell’ → “tell me”: the object pronoun follows the imperative instead of preceding it.
- *volle Nostro Signore* ‘wanted our Lord’ → “our Lord required”: the subject moves before the verb, and wanting is rendered as requiring.
- *in prima … Che ponesse* ‘first … that he should put’ → “before he put”: the separated Italian elements are joined into an English temporal clause, placed after “of St. Peter”; the imperfect subjunctive becomes a simple past indicative.
- *da San Pietro* ‘from St. Peter’ → “of St. Peter”: the preposition changes to suit the English construction with “required”.
- *in sua balìa* ‘in his power’ → “into his keeping”: “into” explicitly marks transfer, while “keeping” renders power or control as custody.

> 93 Certo non chiese se non, Viemmi dietro.  

Surely he demanded nought but ‘Follow me!’

- *non chiese se non* ‘did not ask except’ → “he demanded nought but”: the negative verb and exception are recast as an affirmative verb with “nought but”; “demanded” is stronger than ‘asked’.
- *Viemmi dietro* ‘come behind me’ → “Follow me!”: the construction with a verb of coming and an adverb becomes a transitive verb.

> 94 Nè Pier nè gli altri chiesero a Mattia  
> 95 Oro o argento, quando fu sortito  
> 96 Nel luogo, che perdè l’ anima ria.  

Nor did Peter, nor the others, ask of Matthias gold or silver, when he was chosen for the office which the guilty soul had lost.

- *Nè Pier nè gli altri chiesero* ‘neither Peter nor the others asked’ → “Nor did Peter, nor the others, ask”: the paired negative becomes “Nor … nor”, and initial “Nor” produces auxiliary–subject inversion with “did”.
- *a Mattia* ‘to Matthias’ → “of Matthias”: the preposition changes to suit the English construction with “ask”.
- *fu sortito* ‘was chosen by lot’ → “he was chosen”: the specific manner of choosing expressed by *sortito* is omitted.
- *Nel luogo* ‘in the place’ → “for the office”: “for” replaces the locative preposition, and “office” interprets ‘place’ as a position held.
- *che perdè l’ anima ria* ‘which lost the soul guilty’ → “which the guilty soul had lost”: the subject moves before the verb, the adjective moves before its noun, and the simple past becomes a past perfect.

## Lines 97–105 — Just Punishment and Destructive Avarice

> 97 Però ti sta, che tu se’ ben punito;  

Therefore stay thou here, for thou art justly punished:

- *ti sta* ‘stay’ → “stay thou here”: the pronominal imperative is rendered without a separate equivalent for *ti*, and “here” makes the implied location explicit.
- *che* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *ben* ‘well’ → “justly”: the translation makes the word express the justice of the punishment.

> 98 E guarda ben la mal tolta moneta  
> 99 Ch’ esser ti fece contra Carlo ardito.  

and keep well the ill-got money, which against Charles made thee be bold.

- *guarda* ‘guard’ → “keep”: guarding is expressed as keeping or retaining.
- *mal tolta moneta* ‘wrongly taken coin’ → “ill-got money”: “ill-got” expresses wrongful acquisition rather than literally wrongful taking; the collective singular *moneta* becomes the English mass noun “money”.
- *Ch’ esser ti fece contra Carlo ardito* ‘which to be thee made against Charles bold’ → “which against Charles made thee be bold”: “against Charles” moves before the causative verb; the Italian infinitive–pronoun–verb sequence becomes the English verb–pronoun–infinitive sequence “made thee be”.

> 100 E se non fosse ch’ ancor lo mi vieta  
> 101 La riverenza delle somme Chiavi,  
> 102 Che tu tenesti nella vita lieta,  
> 103 Io userei parole ancor più gravi;  

And were it not that reverence for the Great Keys thou heldest in the glad life yet hinders me, I should use still heavier words;

- *se non fosse* ‘if it were not’ → “were it not”: inversion replaces the explicit conditional conjunction.
- *ancor lo mi vieta* ‘still it to me forbids’ → “yet hinders me”: forbidding something to someone becomes hindering that person; the object *lo* is not separately expressed, and the indirect object *mi* becomes a direct object. The whole predicate moves after its subject and the relative clause modifying that subject.
- *La riverenza* ‘the reverence’ → “reverence”: the definite article is omitted before the abstract noun.
- *delle somme Chiavi* ‘of the highest Keys’ → “for the Great Keys”: “for” expresses the object of reverence in place of Italian *di*; “Great” does not retain the explicit superlative force of *somme*.
- *Che tu tenesti* ‘which thou heldest’ → “thou heldest”: the object relative pronoun is omitted.
- *nella vita lieta* ‘in the life glad’ → “in the glad life”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *parole ancor più gravi* ‘words still more heavy’ → “still heavier words”: the adjectival phrase moves before the noun, and the separate comparative marker *più* becomes the ending of “heavier”.

> 104 Chè la vostra avarizia il mondo attrista,  
> 105 Calcando i buoni e sollevando i pravi.  

for your avarice grieves the world, trampling on the good, and raising up the wicked.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *la vostra avarizia il mondo attrista* ‘your avarice the world saddens’ → “your avarice grieves the world”: the object moves after the verb, replacing Italian subject–object–verb order with English subject–verb–object order.
- *Calcando i buoni* ‘trampling the good’ → “trampling on the good”: the Italian direct object becomes the complement of “on”.

## Lines 106–114 — Corrupt Shepherds and Their Idols

> 106 Di voi pastor s’ accorse il Vangelista,  
> 107 Quando colei, che siede sovra l’ acque,  
> 108 Puttaneggiar co’ regi a lui fu vista:  

Shepherds such as ye the Evangelist perceived, when she, that sitteth on the waters, was seen by him committing fornication with the kings;

- *Di voi pastor* ‘of you shepherds’ → “Shepherds such as ye”: the prepositional phrase becomes the direct object of “perceived”; “such as” changes direct reference to the shepherds addressed into reference to shepherds like them.
- *s’accorse il Vangelista* ‘became aware the Evangelist’ → “the Evangelist perceived”: the pronominal verb becomes a transitive verb, and the subject moves before it.
- *colei* ‘that woman’ → “she”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *Puttaneggiar* ‘to play the whore’ → “committing fornication”: the single verb becomes a verb-and-noun expression, and the infinitive becomes a participle placed after “was seen by him”.
- *a lui fu vista* ‘by him was seen’ → “was seen by him”: the agent moves after the passive verb.

> 109 Quella che con le sette teste nacque,  
> 110 E dalle diece corna ebbe argomento,  
> 111 Fin che virtute al suo marito piacque.  

she that was born with seven heads, and in her ten horns had a witness so long as virtue pleased her spouse.

- *Quella* ‘that woman’ → “she”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *che con le sette teste nacque* ‘that with the seven heads was born’ → “that was born with seven heads”: the phrase describing the heads moves after the verb, and its definite article is omitted.
- *dalle diece corna* ‘from the ten horns’ → “in her ten horns”: “in” replaces ‘from’, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *argomento* ‘support’ → “a witness”: the translation takes the noun in the sense of evidence or testimony rather than support or strength, and supplies an indefinite article.
- *virtute al suo marito piacque* ‘virtue to her husband was pleasing’ → “virtue pleased her spouse”: the verb moves before its complement, and the Italian indirect object becomes an English direct object; ‘husband’ becomes the gender-neutral “spouse”.

> 112 Fatto v’ avete Dio d’ oro e d’ argento:  

Ye have made you a god of gold and silver;

- *Fatto v’avete* ‘made for yourselves you have’ → “Ye have made you”: the participle moves after the auxiliary, and the reflexive pronoun follows the completed verb phrase; “you” retains the sense ‘for yourselves’.
- *Dio* ‘god’ → “a god”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *d’oro e d’argento* ‘of gold and of silver’ → “of gold and silver”: the second preposition is omitted.

> 113 E che altro è da voi all’ idolatre,  
> 114 Se non ch’ egli uno, e voi n’ orate cento?  

and wherein do ye differ from the idolater, save that he worships one, and ye a hundred?

- *E che altro è da voi all’idolatre* ‘and what difference is there from you to the idolater’ → “and wherein do ye differ from the idolater”: the construction with ‘is’ becomes a question with “do ye differ”; ‘you’ becomes the subject, and the comparison expressed by ‘from you to the idolater’ is recast as differing “from the idolater”.
- *egli uno, e voi n’orate cento* ‘he one, and you pray to a hundred of them’ → “he worships one, and ye a hundred”: the verb, implicit in the first Italian clause and explicit in the second, becomes explicit in the first English clause and implicit in the second. ‘Pray to’ becomes “worships”, and *n’*, ‘of them’, is not separately rendered.

## Lines 115–124 — Constantine’s Dower and the Guide’s Approval

> 115 Ahi Costantin, di quanto mal fu matre,  
> 116 Non la tua conversion, ma quella dote  
> 117 Che da te prese il primo ricco patre!  

Ah Constantine! to how much ill gave birth, not thy conversion, but that dower which the first rich Father took from thee!”

- *di quanto mal fu matre* ‘of how much evil was mother’ → “to how much ill gave birth”: the predicate noun *matre* becomes the verbal expression “gave birth”, with “to” replacing *di*.
- *Che da te prese il primo ricco patre* ‘which from thee took the first rich father’ → “which the first rich Father took from thee”: the subject moves before the verb, and “from thee” moves after it.

> 118 E mentre io gli cantava cotai note,  
> 119 O ira o coscïenza che il mordesse,  
> 120 Forte spingava con ambo le piote.  

And whilst I sung these notes to him, whether it was rage or conscience gnawed him, he violently sprawled with both his feet.

- *io gli cantava cotai note* ‘I to him sang such notes’ → “I sung these notes to him”: the indirect-object pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase following the direct object; *cotai*, ‘such’, becomes “these”.
- *O ira o coscïenza che il mordesse* ‘either anger or conscience that might bite him’ → “whether it was rage or conscience gnawed him”: the alternative construction becomes a clause introduced by “whether”, with “it was” supplied. The relative *che* has no expressed English counterpart; the imperfect subjunctive *mordesse* becomes the indicative “gnawed”, replacing biting with gnawing, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Forte spingava* ‘strongly he kicked’ → “he violently sprawled”: *forte* becomes “violently”, and the specific action of kicking becomes “sprawled”.
- *con ambo le piote* ‘with both the soles’ → “with both his feet”: “feet” replaces ‘soles’, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 121 Io credo ben ch’ al mio Duca piacesse,  
> 122 Con sì contenta labbia sempre attese  
> 123 Lo suon delle parole vere espresse.  

And indeed I think it pleased my Guide, with so satisfied a look did he keep listening to the sound of the true words uttered.

- *Io credo ben* ‘I believe indeed’ → “And indeed I think”: “And” is supplied, *ben* moves before the subject as “indeed”, and “think” replaces ‘believe’.
- *ch’ al mio Duca piacesse* ‘that to my leader it was pleasing’ → “it pleased my Guide”: the conjunction *che* is omitted; the Italian construction with *a* becomes an English direct object. The imperfect subjunctive becomes the indicative “pleased”, and *Duca*, ‘leader’, is rendered “Guide”.
- *Con sì contenta labbia* ‘with so contented countenance’ → “with so satisfied a look”: “satisfied” replaces ‘contented’, and *labbia*, ‘countenance’, becomes “look”, emphasizing the expression. English supplies the indefinite article after the adjective.
- *sempre attese* ‘always he attended to’ → “did he keep listening to”: the adverb becomes the continuative construction “keep listening”; attending is rendered specifically as listening. The auxiliary “did” is supplied and placed before the subject after the preceding descriptive phrase.
- *delle parole vere espresse* ‘of the words true expressed’ → “of the true words uttered”: *vere* moves before the noun, while *espresse*, ‘expressed’, becomes “uttered”.

> 124 Però con ambo le braccia mi prese,  

Therefore with both his arms he took me;

- *con ambo le braccia* ‘with both the arms’ → “with both his arms”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *mi prese* ‘me he took’ → “he took me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

## Lines 125–133 — Carried to the Next Valley

> 125 E poi che tutto su mi s’ ebbe al petto,  
> 126 Rimontò per la via onde discese;  

and, when he had me quite upon his breast, remounted by the path where he had descended.

- *E poi che* ‘and after’ → “and, when”: the explicit succession expressed by *poi che* becomes a more general temporal connection.
- *tutto su mi s’ ebbe al petto* ‘wholly up he had me to himself at the breast’ → “he had me quite upon his breast”: the verb and object move before the expression of completeness; *su* and *al* are combined in “upon”; the reflexive is not separately rendered, and the definite article becomes the possessive “his”.
- *onde* ‘whence’ → “where”: the relative expression loses its explicit indication of movement from a place.
- *discese* ‘he descended’ → “he had descended”: the simple past becomes a past perfect, marking the descent as earlier than the return.

> 127 Nè si stancò d’ avermi a sè ristretto,  
> 128 Sì men portò sovra il colmo dell’ arco,  
> 129 Che dal quarto al quinto argine è tragetto.  

Nor did he weary in holding me clasped to him, till he bore me away to the summit of the arch which is a crossway from the fourth to the fifth rampart.

- *Nè si stancò* ‘nor he grew weary’ → “Nor did he weary”: the reflexive construction becomes an English intransitive verb, with auxiliary “did” and inversion after “Nor”.
- *d’ avermi a sè ristretto* ‘of having me to himself clasped’ → “in holding me clasped to him”: “in holding” replaces ‘of having’; the phrase expressing proximity follows the participle, and the reflexive ‘himself’ becomes “him”.
- *Sì* ‘but rather’ → “till”: the affirmative or adversative continuation is interpreted as a temporal limit.
- *men portò* ‘me away he bore’ → “he bore me away”: the object and the adverb represented by the attached pronouns follow the verb.
- *sovra il colmo* ‘upon the summit’ → “to the summit”: the translation explicitly presents the summit as the destination.
- *Che dal quarto al quinto argine è tragetto* ‘which from the fourth to the fifth rampart is a crossing’ → “which is a crossway from the fourth to the fifth rampart”: the verb and its complement move before the phrase describing the crossing’s extent.

> 130 Quivi soavemente spose il carco,  
> 131 Soave per lo scoglio sconcio ed erto,  
> 132 Che sarebbe alle capre duro varco.  

Here he placidly set down the burden, pleasing to him on the rough steep cliff, which to the goats would be a painful passage.

- *Quivi* ‘there’ → “Here”: the place is presented with a proximal rather than a distal adverb.
- *soavemente* ‘gently’ → “placidly”: gentleness of action is expressed instead as calmness.
- *Soave* ‘sweet’ → “pleasing to him”: the adjective is interpreted as describing the burden as welcome or agreeable; “to him” makes its implied experiencer explicit.
- *per* ‘over’ → “on”: the preposition is rendered as location rather than passage over the terrain.
- *lo scoglio sconcio ed erto* ‘the crag misshapen and steep’ → “the rough steep cliff”: the adjectives precede the noun, ‘misshapen’ becomes “rough”, and the conjunction is omitted.
- *Che sarebbe alle capre duro varco* ‘which would be to the goats hard passage’ → “which to the goats would be a painful passage”: the phrase referring to the goats moves before the verb; “a” is supplied, and ‘hard’ is rendered as “painful”.

> 133 Indi un altro vallon mi fu scoverto.  

Thence another valley was discovered to me.

- *mi fu scoverto* ‘to me was uncovered’ → “was discovered to me”: the recipient phrase moves after the passive verb, and ‘uncovered’ is expressed as “discovered”.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Recasting

Carlyle remains close to the Italian’s vocabulary, imagery, and rhetorical emphasis, while repeatedly rearranging its syntax. Objects placed before verbs generally move after them, postposed subjects move forward, and descriptive adjectives usually precede their nouns. Thus *il mondo attrista* becomes “grieves the world”, and *parole ancor più gravi* becomes “still heavier words”. Yet the translation also preserves conspicuous inversions, as in “Not less wide they seemed to me”, and closely echoes unusual expressions such as “I pursed wealth above, and here myself”. Its archaic pronouns and verb forms help retain the Italian’s elevated register without reproducing every grammatical feature.

Changes of construction often make the English more direct or compact. *Convien che* becomes “must”; *che più giace* is compressed into “lower”; and *fa motto* becomes simply “speak”. Elsewhere, a finite clause becomes an infinitive or participial phrase, an impersonal construction acquires a personal subject, or an active clause becomes passive: *e cui più rossa fiamma succia* is rendered “and sucked by ruddier flame”. Italian pronominal verbs frequently become ordinary English intransitives, while indirect objects sometimes become direct objects. Tenses and moods are also adjusted, with subjunctives rendered as indicatives and some simple pasts recast as perfect tenses.

Word choice ranges from close correspondence to interpretive substitution. *Bontate* becomes “righteousness”, *Virtù* becomes “Goodness”, and *bolgia* becomes “chasm”; these choices emphasize particular senses rather than preserve the Italian words’ full range. More concrete or explanatory wording appears when *al grosso*, ‘to the thick part’, becomes “to the calf”, or *per un*, ‘for one’, becomes “to save one”. Conversely, some distinctions disappear: *sortito* becomes “chosen” without specifying selection by lot, *suole … Muoversi* becomes “moves” without a separate expression of customary action, and repeated prepositions or emphatic particles are sometimes omitted.

Small additions and omissions also help establish English grammatical relationships. Possessives replace Italian definite articles with body parts, while explanatory additions such as “of his” and “high priest” identify connections or referents left implicit in the Italian. More generally, beyond the changes individually recorded in the comparison, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and normally omits the definite article before possessives. These routine adjustments accompany a translation that retains much of the original’s verbal texture while making its clause structure more recognizably English.
