# Inferno 15

## Lines 1–12 — Along the Sheltered Banks

> 1 Ora cen porta l’ un de’ duri margini,  
> 2 E il fummo del ruscel di sopra aduggia,  
> 3 Sì che dal fuoco salva l’ acqua e gli argini.  

Now one of the hard margins bears us on, and the smoke of the rivulet makes shade above, so that from the fire it shelters the water and the banks.

- *cen porta* ‘carries us onward’ → “bears us on”: “bears” replaces ‘carries’; the object pronoun and the expression of onward movement follow the verb rather than precede it.
- *l’un de’ duri margini* ‘the one of the hard margins’ → “one of the hard margins”: the article before ‘one’ is omitted, and the subject moves from after the verb to before it.
- *di sopra aduggia* ‘above overshadows’ → “makes shade above”: the single verb becomes a verb-and-noun phrase, and the adverbial expression moves after it.
- *salva* ‘saves’ → “it shelters”: saving is expressed as sheltering; the subject, understood in Italian, is explicitly supplied.

> 4 Quale i Fiamminghi, tra Guzzante e Bruggia,  
> 5 Temendo il fiotto che in vêr lor s’ avventa,  
> 6 Fanno lo schermo, perchè il mar si fuggia;  

As the Flemings between Bruges and Cadsand, dreading the flood that rushes towards them, make their bulwark to repel the sea;

- *tra Guzzante e Bruggia* ‘between Cadsand and Bruges’ → “between Bruges and Cadsand”: the order of the two places is reversed.
- *Temendo* ‘fearing’ → “dreading”: the translation uses a stronger expression of fear.
- *il fiotto* ‘the surge’ → “the flood”: the word for a surge or rushing wave becomes the broader “flood”.
- *che in vêr lor s’avventa* ‘that towards them hurls itself’ → “that rushes towards them”: the reflexive verb becomes an intransitive verb, and the expression of direction moves after it.
- *lo schermo* ‘the barrier’ → “their bulwark”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the general term for a protective barrier becomes “bulwark”.
- *perchè il mar si fuggia* ‘so that the sea may flee’ → “to repel the sea”: a finite purpose clause with a subjunctive becomes an infinitive phrase. The sea changes from the subject of fleeing to the object of repelling.

> 7 E quale i Padovan, lungo la Brenta,  
> 8 Per difender lor ville e lor castelli,  
> 9 Anzi che Chiarentana il caldo senta;  

and as the Paduans, along the Brenta, to defend their towns and villages, ere Chiarentana feels the heat:

- *lor ville e lor castelli* ‘their villages and their castles’ → “their towns and villages”: ‘villages’ becomes “towns”, while “villages” interprets the castles as settlements rather than individual fortifications. The repeated possessive is expressed only once.
- *il caldo senta* ‘the heat may feel’ → “feels the heat”: the verb moves before its object, and the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.

> 10 A tale imagine eran fatti quelli,  
> 11 Tutto che nè sì alti nè sì grossi,  
> 12 Qual che si fosse, lo maestro felli.  

in like fashion those banks were formed, though not so high nor so large, the master, whoever it might be, made them.

- *A tale imagine* ‘according to such an image’ → “in like fashion”: the expression of resemblance is recast as one of manner.
- *eran fatti quelli* ‘were made those’ → “those banks were formed”: the subject moves before the verb; the demonstrative is expanded with the supplied noun “banks”; and ‘made’ becomes “formed”.
- *nè sì alti nè sì grossi* ‘neither so high nor so thick’ → “not so high nor so large”: the first member of the paired negative becomes “not”, and thickness is rendered as general size.
- *Qual che si fosse, lo maestro* ‘whoever he might be, the master’ → “the master, whoever it might be”: the qualifying clause moves after “the master”, becoming parenthetical, and the understood personal subject is expressed by the identifying “it”.

## Lines 13–24 — An Encounter With Spirits

> 13 Già eravam dalla selva rimossi  
> 14 Tanto, ch’ io non avrei visto dov’ era,  
> 15 Perch’ io indietro rivolto mi fossi,  

Already we were so far removed from the wood, that I should not have seen where it was, had I turned back,

- *dalla selva rimossi Tanto* ‘from the wood removed so much’ → “so far removed from the wood”: the degree expression moves before the participle and the prepositional phrase after it; “far” makes the spatial meaning explicit.
- *Perch’ io indietro rivolto mi fossi* ‘even if I had turned myself backwards’ → “had I turned back”: the concessive condition becomes a plain condition expressed by inversion, without a conjunction. The Italian reflexive becomes an intransitive verb, and the auxiliary precedes the participle.

> 16 Quando incontrammo d’ anime una schiera,  
> 17 Che venìa lungo l’ argine, e ciascuna  
> 18 Ci riguardava, come suol da sera  
> 19 Guardar l’ un l’ altro sotto nuova luna;  

when we met a troop of spirits, who were coming alongside the bank; and each looked at us, as in the evening men are wont to look at one another under a new moon;

- *d’ anime una schiera* ‘of souls a troop’ → “a troop of spirits”: the noun precedes its dependent phrase, reversing the Italian order; “spirits” replaces ‘souls’.
- *Che venìa* ‘which was coming’ → “who were coming”: the singular agreement with *schiera* becomes plural agreement with “spirits”; the relative pronoun likewise refers to the individuals rather than the troop.
- *lungo* ‘along’ → “alongside”: movement along the bank is expressed as movement beside it.
- *ciascuna Ci riguardava* ‘each us was looking at’ → “each looked at us”: the object follows the verb, and the imperfect is rendered by the simple past rather than a progressive construction.
- *come suol da sera Guardar l’ un l’ altro* ‘as is wont at evening to look the one at the other’ → “as in the evening men are wont to look at one another”: the time phrase moves before the verb; “men” is supplied as the subject, singular *suol* becomes plural “are wont”, and the subject–object pairing ‘the one … the other’ becomes the reciprocal “one another”.
- *sotto nuova luna* ‘under new moon’ → “under a new moon”: the indefinite article is supplied.

> 20 E sì vêr noi aguzzavan le ciglia,  
> 21 Come vecchio sartor fa nella cruna.  

and towards us sharpened their vision, as an aged tailor does at the eye of his needle.

- *E sì* ‘and so’ → “and”: *sì*, expressing degree, is omitted.
- *vêr noi aguzzavan le ciglia* ‘towards us they were sharpening the eyebrows’ → “towards us sharpened their vision”: the bodily image of sharpening the eyebrows is rendered through its figurative meaning, sharpening sight. The definite article becomes a possessive, and the imperfect is rendered by the simple past.
- *vecchio sartor* ‘old tailor’ → “an aged tailor”: the indefinite article is supplied, and “aged” replaces ‘old’.
- *nella cruna* ‘in the needle’s eye’ → “at the eye of his needle”: “at” replaces ‘in’; the single noun *cruna* is expanded into “eye of his needle”, with possession explicitly assigned to the tailor.

> 22 Così adocchiato da cotal famiglia,  
> 23 Fui conosciuto da un, che mi prese  
> 24 Per lo lembo, e gridò: Qual maraviglia!  

Thus eyed by that family, I was recognized by one who took me by the skirt, and said: “What a wonder!”

- *cotal* ‘such a’ → “that”: a qualitative expression becomes a demonstrative.
- *che mi prese* ‘who me took’ → “who took me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Per lo lembo* ‘by the hem’ → “by the skirt”: the garment’s edge is rendered as its lower portion.
- *e gridò* ‘and cried out’ → “and said”: the translation removes the verb’s indication of a raised voice.
- *Qual maraviglia* ‘what wonder’ → “What a wonder!”: the indefinite article is supplied.

## Lines 25–30 — Recognizing Ser Brunetto

> 25 Ed io, quando il suo braccio a me distese,  
> 26 Ficcai gli occhi per lo cotto aspetto  

And I, when he stretched out his arm to me, fixed my eyes on his baked aspect,

- *il suo braccio a me distese* ‘his arm to me he stretched out’ → “he stretched out his arm to me”: the verb precedes its object and the phrase indicating the recipient, rather than following both.
- *Ficcai gli occhi* ‘I thrust the eyes’ → “fixed my eyes”: the forceful verb is rendered by the English idiom for directing one’s gaze; the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *per lo cotto aspetto* ‘through the cooked aspect’ → “on his baked aspect”: “on” replaces the preposition expressing penetration; the definite article becomes a possessive, and “baked” gives a more specific sense to *cotto*.

> 27 Sì, che il viso abbruciato non difese  
> 28 La conoscenza sua al mio intelletto;  

so that the scorching of his visage hindered not my mind from knowing him.

- *il viso abbruciato* ‘the burned face’ → “the scorching of his visage”: the noun and its participial adjective become an abstract noun followed by an *of*-phrase; the face is no longer itself the grammatical subject, and possession is made explicit.
- *non difese La conoscenza sua al mio intelletto* ‘did not withhold recognition of him from my intellect’ → “hindered not my mind from knowing him”: the construction changes from withholding recognition to hindering the mind. The intellect becomes the direct object, while the noun denoting recognition becomes a gerund and its possessive becomes the object “him”; *intelletto* is rendered by the broader “mind”.

> 29 E chinando la mia alla sua faccia,  
> 30 Risposi: Siete voi qui, Ser Brunetto?  

And bending my face to his, I answered: “Are you here, Ser Brunetto?”

- *la mia alla sua faccia* ‘mine to his face’ → “my face to his”: the noun understood after the first possessive in Italian is supplied there in English, while the noun expressed after the second possessive is left understood.

## Lines 31–45 — Brunetto’s Request and Dante’s Reverence

> 31 E quegli: O figliuol mio, non ti dispiaccia,  
> 32 Se Brunetto Latini un poco teco  
> 33 Ritorna indietro, e lascia andar la traccia.  

And he: “O my son! let it not displease thee, if Brunetto Latini turn back with thee a little, and let go his train.”

- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *non ti dispiaccia* ‘may it not displease thee’ → “let it not displease thee”: the optative subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”; the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *un poco teco Ritorna indietro* ‘a little with thee turns back’ → “turn back with thee a little”: the verb precedes both adverbial phrases, whose relative order is reversed; the Italian indicative becomes an English subjunctive in the conditional clause.
- *e lascia andar la traccia* ‘and lets the company go’ → “and let go his train”: the construction becomes “let go” followed by its object; the definite article becomes a possessive, and *traccia*, the moving company or file, is rendered as “train”.

> 34 Io dissi lui: Quanto posso ven preco;  

I said: “With all my power I do beseech it of you.

- *Io dissi lui* ‘I said to him’ → “I said”: the indirect object is omitted.
- *Quanto posso* ‘as much as I can’ → “With all my power”: a clause expressing the extent of the speaker’s ability becomes a prepositional phrase with a noun.
- *ven preco* ‘I beg you for it’ → “I do beseech it of you”: the person addressed becomes the complement of “of”, while the thing requested becomes the direct object; emphatic “do” is supplied.

> 35 E se volete che con voi m’ asseggia,  
> 36 Faròl, se piace a costui, chè vo seco.  

And if you wish me to sit down with you, I will do so, if it pleases him there, for I go with him.”

- *volete che con voi m’ asseggia* ‘wish that with you I seat myself’ → “wish me to sit down with you”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction; the reflexive verb becomes intransitive “sit down”, and the phrase expressing company moves after it.
- *Faròl* ‘I will do it’ → “I will do so”: the object pronoun becomes the pro-form “so”.
- *a costui* ‘to this man’ → “him there”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun qualified by “there”; the Italian indirect object becomes the direct object required by English “pleases”.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.

> 37 O figliuol, disse, qual di questa greggia  
> 38 S’ arresta punto, giace poi cent’ anni  
> 39 Senza arrostarsi quando il fuoco il feggia.  

“O my son,” he said, “whoever of this flock stops one instant, lies a hundred years thereafter, without fanning himself when the fire strikes him.

- *O figliuol* ‘O son’ → “O my son”: the possessive is supplied.
- *giace poi cent’ anni* ‘lies then a hundred years’ → “lies a hundred years thereafter”: the temporal adverb moves after the expression of duration and is rendered by the more explicitly retrospective “thereafter”.
- *arrostarsi* ‘ward off [the flames] from oneself’ → “fanning himself”: the translation describes the fanning action rather than explicitly expressing its defensive purpose.
- *il feggia* ‘him strike’ → “strikes him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the Italian subjunctive is rendered by an English indicative.

> 40 Però va oltre: io ti verrò a’ panni,  
> 41 E poi rigiugnerò la mia masnada,  
> 42 Che va piangendo i suoi eterni danni.  

Therefore go on: I will follow at thy skirts; and then will I rejoin my band, that go lamenting their eternal losses.”

- *va oltre* ‘go further’ → “go on”: the spatial adverb becomes the particle of an English expression for continuing.
- *io ti verrò a’ panni* ‘I will come to thy clothes’ → “I will follow at thy skirts”: coming becomes following, “at” replaces the directional preposition, and the general word for clothing becomes “skirts”; the possessive dative *ti* is rendered by “thy”.
- *E poi rigiugnerò* ‘and then I will rejoin’ → “and then will I rejoin”: the supplied subject is placed after the auxiliary, producing inversion.
- *Che va piangendo* ‘which goes weeping’ → “that go lamenting”: the singular verb agreeing with *masnada* becomes plural by agreement with the band’s members; weeping is rendered as lamenting.
- *i suoi eterni danni* ‘its eternal losses’ → “their eternal losses”: the possessive likewise refers to the members individually rather than to the singular collective noun.

> 43 Io non osava scender della strada  
> 44 Per andar par di lui; ma il capo chino  
> 45 Tenea, com’ uom che riverente vada.  

I durst not descend from the road to go level with him; but kept my head bent down, like one who walks in reverence.

- *Per* ‘for’ → “to”: the Italian preposition introducing purpose is absorbed into the English infinitive marker.
- *andar par di lui* ‘go equal to him’ → “go level with him”: the expression of equality becomes explicitly spatial, and its complement is introduced by “with”.
- *ma il capo chino Tenea* ‘but the head bent I kept’ → “but kept my head bent down”: the verb moves before its object, the definite article becomes a possessive, and “down” makes the direction of the bending explicit.
- *com’ uom* ‘like a man’ → “like one”: the generic noun becomes a pronoun.
- *che riverente vada* ‘who reverent may walk’ → “who walks in reverence”: the adjective becomes an adverbial prepositional phrase and moves after the verb; the subjunctive becomes an indicative.

## Lines 46–57 — The Way Home and the Guiding Star

> 46 Ei cominciò: Qual fortuna, o destino,  
> 47 Anzi l’ ultimo dì quaggiù ti mena?  

He began: “What chance, or destiny, brings thee, ere thy last day, down here?

- *fortuna* ‘fortune’ → “chance”: the noun is rendered as chance rather than fortune.
- *Anzi l’ ultimo dì quaggiù ti mena* ‘before the last day down here thee brings’ → “brings thee, ere thy last day, down here”: the verb precedes its object and both adverbial phrases; ‘the last day’ becomes “thy last day”, making the personal reference explicit.

> 48 E chi è questi, che mostra il cammino?  

And who is this that shows the way?”

- Literal.

> 49 Lassù di sopra in la vita serena,  
> 50 Rispos’ io lui, mi smarri’ in una valle,  
> 51 Avanti che l’ età mia fosse piena.  

“There above, up in the clear life, I lost myself,” replied I, “in a valley, before my age was full.

- *Lassù di sopra in la vita serena* ‘up there above in the life serene’ → “There above, up in the clear life”: “up” is placed after “above”, adjoining the following prepositional phrase; ‘serene’ becomes “clear”, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *Rispos’ io lui* ‘replied I to him’ → “replied I”: the indirect object ‘to him’ is omitted, and the reporting clause is moved after “I lost myself”.
- *mi smarri’* ‘myself I lost’ → “I lost myself”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *Avanti che l’ età mia fosse piena* ‘before that my age were full’ → “before my age was full”: the conjunction *Avanti che* becomes the single word “before”; the possessive precedes rather than follows its noun, and the Italian imperfect subjunctive becomes an English past indicative.

> 52 Pur ier mattina le volsi le spalle:  

Only yester morn I turned my back to it.

- *le volsi le spalle* ‘to it I turned the shoulders’ → “I turned my back to it”: the indirect-object pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase following the object; plural ‘shoulders’ becomes singular “back”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 53 Questi m’ apparve, ritornando in quella,  
> 54 E riducemi a ca’ per questo calle.  

He appeared to me, as I was returning into it, and guides me home again by this path.”

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “He”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *m’ apparve* ‘to me appeared’ → “appeared to me”: the indirect object follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *ritornando in quella* ‘returning into that’ → “as I was returning into it”: the gerund becomes a finite temporal clause with an explicitly identified subject and a past-progressive verb; the demonstrative becomes the personal pronoun “it”.
- *riducemi a ca’* ‘brings me back to home’ → “guides me home again”: bringing back is expressed as guiding “again”; the prepositional phrase becomes the directional adverb “home”.

> 55 Ed egli a me: Se tu segui tua stella,  
> 56 Non puoi fallire a glorioso porto,  
> 57 Se ben m’ accorsi nella vita bella.  

And he to me: “If thou follow thy star, thou canst not fail of glorious haven, if I discerned rightly in the fair life.

- *tu segui* ‘thou followest’ → “thou follow”: the Italian present indicative is rendered by an English present subjunctive in the conditional clause.
- *Non puoi* ‘not canst’ → “thou canst not”: the negative follows rather than precedes the modal verb.
- *fallire a glorioso porto* ‘fail at glorious port’ → “fail of glorious haven”: the Italian construction is rendered by English “fail of”, meaning fail to attain; ‘port’ becomes “haven”.
- *ben m’ accorsi* ‘well I became aware’ → “I discerned rightly”: the pronominal verb of becoming aware is rendered by “discerned”; *ben* is interpreted as ‘correctly’ and placed after the verb.
- *nella vita bella* ‘in the life fair’ → “in the fair life”: the adjective precedes rather than follows its noun.

## Lines 58–68 — Encouragement and Hostility

> 58 E s’ io non fossi sì per tempo morto,  
> 59 Veggendo il cielo a te così benigno,  
> 60 Dato t’ avrei all’ opera conforto.  

And if I had not died so early, seeing heaven so kind to thee, I would have cheered thee in the work.

- *s’ io non fossi sì per tempo morto* ‘if I had not so early died’ → “if I had not died so early”: the expression of time moves after the participle.
- *Veggendo il cielo a te così benigno* ‘seeing the heaven to thee so benign’ → “seeing heaven so kind to thee”: the article before ‘heaven’ is omitted; ‘benign’ becomes “kind”; and “to thee” moves after the adjective it qualifies.
- *Dato t’ avrei all’ opera conforto* ‘given thee I would have to the work comfort’ → “I would have cheered thee in the work”: the construction ‘give comfort’ becomes the verb “cheered”, with “thee” as its direct object. The auxiliary precedes the participle, and ‘to the work’ becomes “in the work”, placed after the object.

> 61 Ma quell’ ingrato popolo maligno,  
> 62 Che discese di Fiesole ab antico,  
> 63 E tiene ancor del monte e del macigno,  
> 64 Ti si farà, per tuo ben far, nimico:  

But that ungrateful, malignant people, who of old came down from Fiesole, and still savours of the mountain and the rock, will make itself an enemy to thee for thy good deeds.

- *quell’ ingrato popolo maligno* ‘that ungrateful people malignant’ → “that ungrateful, malignant people”: the second adjective moves before the noun.
- *Che discese di Fiesole ab antico* ‘who came down from Fiesole of old’ → “who of old came down from Fiesole”: the expression of time moves before the verb.
- *E tiene ancor del monte e del macigno* ‘and retains still something of the mountain and of the rock’ → “and still savours of the mountain and the rock”: the idiomatic construction expressing retention of something’s qualities becomes “savours of”. “Still” moves before the verb, and the preposition is not repeated before “the rock”.
- *Ti si farà, per tuo ben far, nimico* ‘to thee will make itself, for thy doing well, an enemy’ → “will make itself an enemy to thee for thy good deeds”: “to thee” moves after “an enemy”, and the phrase giving the cause moves to the end. The substantivized infinitive ‘doing well’ becomes the plural noun phrase “good deeds”.

> 65 Ed è ragion; chè tra gli lazzi sorbi  
> 66 Si disconvien fruttare al dolce fico.  

And there is cause: for amongst the tart sorb-trees, it befits not the sweet fig to fructify.

- *Ed è ragion* ‘And it is reason’ → “And there is cause”: the copular construction becomes an existential one. The Italian idiom can mean ‘and it is reasonable’; Carlyle instead expresses the existence of a cause or justification.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *Si disconvien fruttare al dolce fico* ‘it is unfitting to fructify for the sweet fig’ → “it befits not the sweet fig to fructify”: the verb expressing unsuitability becomes the negated “befits”. The impersonal construction with a dative becomes a transitive construction with “the sweet fig” as direct object, and the infinitive moves after that object.

> 67 Vecchia fama nel mondo li chiama orbi,  
> 68 Gente avara, invidiosa, e superba:  

Old report on earth proclaims them blind, a people avaricious, envious, and proud:

- *nel mondo* ‘in the world’ → “on earth”: both the preposition and the noun change, and the article is omitted.
- *li chiama orbi* ‘them calls blind’ → “proclaims them blind”: “proclaims” is more emphatic than ‘calls’, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Gente* ‘people’ → “a people”: an indefinite article is supplied.

## Lines 69–81 — A Warning and a Wish

> 69 Dai lor costumi fa che tu ti forbi.  

look that thou cleanse thyself of their customs.

- *Dai lor costumi* ‘from their customs’ → “of their customs”: “of” replaces ‘from’, and the phrase moves from the beginning of the clause to its end.
- *fa che* ‘make that’ → “look that”: the Italian injunction is rendered by an English expression meaning ‘see to it that’.
- *tu ti forbi* ‘thou thyself cleanse’ → “thou cleanse thyself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.

> 70 La tua fortuna tanto onor ti serba,  
> 71 Che l’ una parte e l’ altra avranno fame  
> 72 Di te; ma lungi fia dal becco l’ erba.  

Thy fortune reserves such honour for thee, that both parties will have a hunger of thee; but far from the goat shall be the grass.

- *tanto onor ti serba* ‘so much honour for thee reserves’ → “reserves such honour for thee”: the verb moves before its object, the recipient follows the object, and ‘so much’ becomes “such”.
- *l’ una parte e l’ altra* ‘the one party and the other’ → “both parties”: two coordinated singular expressions become one plural expression.
- *avranno fame* ‘will have hunger’ → “will have a hunger”: the indefinite article is supplied.
- *lungi fia dal becco l’ erba* ‘far shall be from the he-goat the grass’ → “far from the goat shall be the grass”: the phrase identifying what the grass is far from moves before the verb; “goat” does not retain the specifically male sense of *becco*.

> 73 Faccian le bestie Fiesolane strame  
> 74 Di lor medesme, e non tocchin la pianta,  
> 75 S’ alcuna surge ancor nel lor letame,  

Let the beasts of Fiesole make litter of themselves, and not touch the plant, if any yet springs up amid their rankness,

- *Faccian le bestie Fiesolane* ‘may the beasts Fiesolan make’ → “Let the beasts of Fiesole make”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”, with the subject preceding “make”; the adjective *Fiesolane* becomes the prepositional phrase “of Fiesole”.
- *e non tocchin* ‘and may they not touch’ → “and not touch”: the second subjunctive verb becomes another verb governed by the earlier “Let”.
- *surge ancor* ‘rises still’ → “yet springs up”: the adverb moves before the verb, and ‘rises’ is expressed by the phrasal verb “springs up”.
- *nel lor letame* ‘in their dung’ → “amid their rankness”: “amid” replaces ‘in’, and the concrete noun ‘dung’ becomes the abstract “rankness”.

> 76 In cui riviva la sementa santa  
> 77 Di quei Roman, che vi rimaser quando  
> 78 Fu fatto il nido di malizia tanta.  

in which the holy seed revives of those Romans who remained there when it became the nest of so much malice.”

- *riviva la sementa santa* ‘may live again the seed holy’ → “the holy seed revives”: the subjunctive in the relative clause becomes an indicative; the subject moves before the verb, and the adjective moves before its noun.
- *che vi rimaser* ‘who there remained’ → “who remained there”: the locative moves after the verb.
- *Fu fatto il nido* ‘was made the nest’ → “it became the nest”: the passive construction, whose postposed subject is ‘the nest’, becomes a construction with “became”; “it” is supplied as the subject, and “the nest” becomes its complement. This renders the making of the nest as something becoming the nest.
- *di malizia tanta* ‘of malice so much’ → “of so much malice”: the expression of quantity moves before the noun.

> 79 Se fosse pieno tutto il mio dimando,  
> 80 Risposi lui, voi non sareste ancora  
> 81 Dell’ umana natura posto in bando:  

“Were my desire all fulfilled,” I answered him, “you had not yet been banished from human nature;

- *Se fosse pieno tutto il mio dimando* ‘If were full all my request’ → “Were my desire all fulfilled,”: conditional inversion replaces *Se*, ‘if’; the subject moves before its predicate adjective; ‘request’ becomes “desire”, and ‘full’ becomes “fulfilled”. *Tutto*, which qualifies the request, becomes “all” qualifying its fulfilment.
- *non sareste ancora* ‘would not yet be’ → “had not yet been”: the present conditional becomes an English counterfactual pluperfect construction.
- *Dell’ umana natura* ‘from the human nature’ → “from human nature”: the definite article is omitted, and the phrase moves after the expression of banishment.
- *posto in bando* ‘placed in banishment’ → “banished”: the participle and prepositional phrase become a single participle.

## Lines 82–96 — Gratitude and Readiness for Fortune

> 82 Chè in la mente m’ è fitta, ed or m’ accuora  
> 83 La cara buona imagine paterna  
> 84 Di voi, quando nel mondo ad ora ad ora  
> 85 M’ insegnavate come l’ uom s’ eterna:  

for in my memory is fixed, and now goes to my heart, the dear, kind, paternal image of you, when in the world, hour by hour, you taught me how man makes himself eternal.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *in la mente m’ è fitta* ‘in the mind is fixed for me’ → “in my memory is fixed”: ‘mind’ becomes “memory,” and the personal pronoun marking whose mind is affected becomes the possessive “my.”
- *ed or m’ accuora* ‘and now pains me’ → “and now goes to my heart”: a verb expressing emotional pain becomes an English idiom of deeply felt emotion.
- *La cara buona imagine paterna* ‘the dear good image paternal’ → “the dear, kind, paternal image”: “kind” renders *buona*, and the postposed adjective *paterna* moves before the noun.
- *ad ora ad ora* ‘from hour to hour’ → “hour by hour”: the repeated Italian prepositional expression becomes an English distributive phrase.
- *M’ insegnavate* ‘me you were teaching’ → “you taught me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the imperfect becomes an English simple past.
- *come l’ uom s’ eterna* ‘how the man eternalizes himself’ → “how man makes himself eternal”: the generic definite article is omitted, and the reflexive verb becomes “makes himself” followed by an adjective.

> 86 E quant’ io l’ abbo in grado, mentr’ io vivo  
> 87 Convien, che nella mia lingua si scerna.  

And whilst I live, beseems my tongue should shew what gratitude I have for it.

- *quant’ io l’ abbo in grado, mentr’ io vivo* ‘how much I hold it in gratitude, whilst I live’ → “whilst I live” … “what gratitude I have for it”: the idiomatic construction becomes an explicit expression of possessing gratitude; the Italian object pronoun becomes “for it.” The gratitude clause moves from before *Convien* to after “shew.” The temporal clause moves ahead of the expression of gratitude.
- *Convien, che nella mia lingua si scerna* ‘it is fitting that in my tongue it be discerned’ → “beseems my tongue should shew”: the passive construction becomes active, with “my tongue” promoted from a prepositional phrase to the subject of “shew.” “Should” renders the subordinate subjunctive, and no explicit equivalent of *che* is retained.

> 88 Ciò che narrate di mio corso, scrivo,  
> 89 E serbolo a chiosar con altro testo  
> 90 A donna che il saprà, s’ a lei arrivo.  

That which you relate about my course, I write; and keep it, with another text, for a Lady to comment, who will be able if I get to her.

- *serbolo a chiosar con altro testo A donna* ‘I keep it for glossing with another text for a lady’ → “keep it, with another text, for a Lady to comment”: the purpose construction is recast with “a Lady” as the explicit subject of “to comment.” “With another text” moves ahead of that infinitive, while *chiosar*, ‘to gloss,’ becomes “to comment.”
- *che il saprà* ‘who will know it’ → “who will be able”: *sapere* is interpreted as knowing how, hence being able, rather than simply knowing something; the object pronoun *il* is omitted.
- *s’ a lei arrivo* ‘if to her I arrive’ → “if I get to her”: “get to” replaces ‘arrive,’ and the prepositional phrase moves after the verb.

> 91 Tanto vogl’ io che vi sia manifesto,  
> 92 Pur che mia coscïenza non mi garra,  
> 93 Che alla fortuna, come vuol, son presto.  

Thus much I would have you know: So conscience chide me not, I am prepared for Fortune as she wills.

- *Tanto vogl’ io che vi sia manifesto* ‘so much I wish that to you it be manifest’ → “Thus much I would have you know”: wishing that something be manifest to the addressee becomes the construction “would have you know.” The addressee becomes the subject of “know,” and the expressed subject *io* moves before the main verb.
- *Pur che* ‘provided that’ → “So”: the conditional phrase becomes the English conjunction “So,” used in its conditional sense.
- *mia coscïenza non mi garra* ‘my conscience not chide me’ → “conscience chide me not”: ‘my’ is omitted, and both the object pronoun and the negative move after the verb.
- *Che alla fortuna, come vuol, son presto* ‘that for fortune, as she wills, I am ready’ → “I am prepared for Fortune as she wills.”: the complement-introducing *Che* is omitted. The main subject and verb move ahead of the prepositional and comparative phrases, and ‘ready’ becomes “prepared.”

> 94 Non è nuova agli orecchi miei tale arra:  

Not new to my ears is such earnest.

- *Non è nuova agli orecchi miei tale arra* ‘Not is new to the ears of mine such earnest’ → “Not new to my ears is such earnest.”: the copula moves after the predicate adjective and its accompanying prepositional phrase; the postposed possessive *miei* becomes “my” before the noun.

> 95 Però giri Fortuna la sua rota,  
> 96 Come le piace, e il villan la sua marra.  

Therefore, let Fortune turn her wheel as pleases her, and the boor his mattock.”

- *giri Fortuna la sua rota* ‘may Fortune turn her wheel’ → “let Fortune turn her wheel”: the independent subjunctive becomes an English imperative construction with “let.”
- *il villan* ‘the peasant’ → “the boor”: the rural designation is rendered by an English word for a rustic, rather than the more neutral ‘peasant.’

## Lines 97–108 — The Master's Counsel and Brunetto's Companions

> 97 Lo mio Maestro allora in sulla gota  
> 98 Destra si volse indietro, e riguardommi;  

Thereupon my Master turned backward on his right, and looked at me,

- *allora* ‘then’ → “Thereupon”: the temporal adverb is moved before the subject and given a more connective rendering.
- *in sulla gota Destra si volse indietro* ‘on the right cheek he turned himself backward’ → “turned backward on his right”: the reference to the cheek becomes a general indication of direction, with “his” supplied; the reflexive verb becomes intransitive, and the directional phrase follows the verb.
- *riguardommi* ‘regarded me’ → “looked at me”: the transitive verb becomes a verb with a prepositional complement.

> 99 Poi disse: Bene ascolta chi la nota.  

then said: “He listens well who notes it.”

- *Bene ascolta chi la nota* ‘well listens whoever notes it’ → “He listens well who notes it”: the adverb moves after the verb, and the subject clause introduced by *chi* is recast as “He” followed by a relative clause.

> 100 Nè per tanto di men parlando vommi  
> 101 Con Ser Brunetto, e dimando chi sono  
> 102 Li suoi compagni più noti e più sommi.  

Not the less I go on speaking with Ser Brunetto, and ask who are the most noted and highest of his companions.

- *Nè per tanto di men* ‘nor for that any less’ → “Not the less”: the coordinating force of *Nè* and the explicit reference ‘for that’ are not separately expressed.
- *parlando vommi* ‘speaking I go myself’ → “I go on speaking”: the verb of movement with a gerund becomes an expression of continued speaking; the reflexive element is not separately rendered.
- *Li suoi compagni più noti e più sommi* ‘his companions most noted and most eminent’ → “the most noted and highest of his companions”: the adjectives become substantival superlatives, followed by the partitive “of his companions”; *sommi* is rendered as “highest”.

> 103 Ed egli a me: Saper d’ alcuno è buono:  

And he to me: “It is good to know of some.

- *Saper d’ alcuno è buono* ‘to know of someone is good’ → “It is good to know of some”: the infinitive subject is postponed after anticipatory “It”, and the singular indefinite *alcuno* becomes the plural “some”.

> 104 Degli altri fia laudabile il tacerci,  
> 105 Chè il tempo saria corto a tanto suono.  

Of the rest it will be laudable that we keep silence, as the time would be too short for so much talk.

- *Degli altri* ‘of the others’ → “Of the rest”: the plural expression becomes a collective one.
- *fia laudabile il tacerci* ‘will be laudable our keeping silent’ → “it will be laudable that we keep silence”: the substantival infinitive becomes a finite clause with anticipatory “it”; the pronominal *tacerci* is interpreted as referring to the speakers and rendered with “we”.
- *saria corto* ‘would be short’ → “would be too short”: “too” is supplied, making the insufficiency explicit.
- *a tanto suono* ‘for so much sound’ → “for so much talk”: the general noun ‘sound’ is interpreted specifically as speech.

> 106 In somma sappi, che tutti fur cherci,  
> 107 E letterati grandi e di gran fama;  
> 108 D’ un medesmo peccato al mondo lerci.  

In brief, know that all were clerks, and great scholars, and of great renown; by one same crime on earth defiled.

- *In somma* ‘in sum’ → “In brief”: the summarizing formula becomes an expression of brevity.
- *letterati grandi* ‘men of letters great’ → “great scholars”: *letterati* is rendered as “scholars”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *D’ un medesmo peccato* ‘with one same sin’ → “by one same crime”: “crime” replaces ‘sin’, and the preposition is adapted to “defiled”.
- *al mondo* ‘in the world’ → “on earth”: both the noun and the spatial expression change.
- *lerci* ‘filthy’ → “defiled”: the adjective describing a condition becomes a past participle expressing the result of defilement.

## Lines 109–120 — The Condemned and a Final Request

> 109 Priscian sen va con quella turba grama,  
> 110 E Francesco d’ Accorso; anco vedervi,  
> 111 S’ avessi avuto di tal tigna brama,  
> 112 Colui potei, che dal Servo de’ servi  

Priscian goes with that wretched crowd, and Francesco d’ Accorso; also, if thou hadst had any longing for such scurf, thou mightest have seen him there, who by the Servant of servants

- *sen va* ‘goes away’ → “goes”: the sense of departure expressed by the pronominal construction is not separately rendered.
- *quella turba grama* ‘that crowd wretched’ → “that wretched crowd”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *vedervi … Colui potei* ‘see there … that one thou couldst’ → “thou mightest have seen him there”: the translation takes *potei* as the shortened second-person imperfect *potevi*, and recasts the imperfect with an infinitive as a modal perfect. The interrupted construction is brought together after the conditional clause; the demonstrative *colui* becomes “him”, and the locative suffix *-vi* becomes “there” after the object.
- *S’ avessi avuto di tal tigna brama* ‘if thou hadst had of such scurf longing’ → “if thou hadst had any longing for such scurf”: “any” is supplied; the complement moves after “longing”, and *di* becomes “for” rather than ‘of’.

> 113 Fu trasmutato d’ Arno in Bacchiglione,  
> 114 Ove lasciò li mal protesi nervi.  

was translated from the Arno to the Bacchiglione, where he left his ill-strained nerves.

- *d’ Arno in Bacchiglione* ‘from Arno into Bacchiglione’ → “from the Arno to the Bacchiglione”: articles are supplied before the river names, and directional *in* becomes “to”.
- *li mal protesi nervi* ‘the ill-extended nerves’ → “his ill-strained nerves”: the definite article becomes a possessive, while “strained” renders *protesi* as stretched under tension rather than merely extended.

> 115 Di più direi; ma il venir, e il sermone  
> 116 Più lungo esser non può, però ch’ io veggio  
> 117 Là surger nuovo fummo dal sabbione.  

I would say more, but my going and my speech must not be longer; for there I see new smoke arising from the great sand.

- *Di più direi* ‘Of more I would speak’ → “I would say more”: the initial prepositional phrase becomes an object following “say”; speaking of more becomes saying more.
- *il venir, e il sermone / Più lungo esser non può* ‘the coming and the discourse longer be cannot’ → “my going and my speech must not be longer”: the articles become possessives, and ‘coming’ becomes “going”. The nominal infinitive *venir* is rendered by “going”, while *sermone* becomes “speech”. The negative modal moves before “be longer”, and inability or impossibility becomes a restriction expressed by “must not”.
- *però ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *io veggio / Là surger nuovo fummo* ‘I see there arise new smoke’ → “there I see new smoke arising”: “there” moves before the subject; the infinitive *surger* becomes the participle “arising” and moves after “new smoke”.
- *dal sabbione* ‘from the sandy expanse’ → “from the great sand”: the expansive sense of *sabbione*, an augmentative formation, is expressed through the adjective “great” modifying “sand”.

> 118 Gente vien con la quale esser non deggio.  

People are coming with whom I may not be.

- *Gente vien* ‘People come’ → “People are coming”: the simple present becomes a progressive construction, and the Italian singular collective noun and verb become an English plural noun and verb.
- *con la quale esser non deggio* ‘with whom to be I ought not’ → “with whom I may not be”: the negative modal moves before the infinitive, and the language of obligation becomes that of permission.

> 119 Sieti raccomandato il mio Tesoro  
> 120 Nel quale io vivo ancora; e più non cheggio.  

Let my Treasure, in which I still live, be commended to thee. And more I ask not.”

- *Sieti raccomandato il mio Tesoro* ‘Be to thee commended my Treasure’ → “Let my Treasure” … “be commended to thee”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”; the subject moves before the passive verb, and the attached dative pronoun *-ti* becomes “to thee” after it.
- *Nel quale io vivo ancora* ‘In which I live still’ → “in which I still live”: “still” moves before the verb, and the relative clause is placed within the main clause rather than after it.
- *e più non cheggio* ‘and more I do not ask’ → “And more I ask not”: the negative follows the verb in Carlyle’s archaic English rather than preceding it as in Italian.

## Lines 121–124 — Running Like the Winner

> 121 Poi si rivolse, e parve di coloro  
> 122 Che corrono a Verona il drappo verde  
> 123 Per la campagna; e parve di costoro  
> 124 Quegli che vince, e non colui che perde.  

Then he turned back, and seemed like one of those who run for the green cloth at Verona through the open field; and of them seemed he who gains, not he who loses.

- *si rivolse* ‘turned himself round’ → “he turned back”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, and turning round is expressed as turning back.
- *parve di coloro* ‘seemed of those’ → “seemed like one of those”: “like one” is supplied, making the comparison explicit.
- *Che corrono a Verona il drappo verde* ‘who run at Verona the cloth green’ → “who run for the green cloth at Verona”: the Italian direct object becomes a phrase with “for”, expressing the prize sought; the place phrase moves after it, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Per la campagna* ‘through the countryside’ → “through the open field”: “open field” replaces ‘countryside’.
- *parve di costoro* ‘seemed of these’ → “of them seemed”: the prepositional phrase moves before the verb, and the demonstrative ‘these’ becomes the personal pronoun “them”.
- *Quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *vince* ‘wins’ → “gains”: Carlyle uses “gains” in the sense of winning.
- *e non colui* ‘and not that one’ → “not he”: the conjunction is omitted, and the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.

## Summary — Close Following and English Recasting

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s sequence of thought, often retaining its images, repetitions, and distinctive vocabulary. Expressions such as “baked aspect”, “the holy seed”, and “his ill-strained nerves” preserve unusual features rather than smoothing them away. His departures from word-for-word rendering are nevertheless frequent, especially in word order. Subjects and objects commonly move into their ordinary English positions, adjectives precede their nouns, and pronouns follow verbs. Yet he also retains or introduces marked inversions, as in “then will I rejoin” and “Not new to my ears is such earnest”.

Changes of construction often make the English more explicit or idiomatic. *Perchè il mar si fuggia*, ‘so that the sea may flee’, becomes “to repel the sea”, changing both the clause structure and the sea’s grammatical role. *Dato t’ avrei … conforto*, ‘I would have given thee comfort’, becomes “I would have cheered thee”; *s’ eterna*, ‘eternalizes himself’, expands into “makes himself eternal”. Reflexive verbs frequently become intransitive, gerunds sometimes become finite clauses, and Italian subjunctives are variously rendered by indicatives, modals, or constructions with “let”. Collective nouns also prompt shifts from singular agreement to agreement with their individual members.

Word choice ranges from close correspondence to interpretation. *Aguzzavan le ciglia*, ‘sharpened their eyebrows’, becomes “sharpened their vision”, replacing the bodily image with its meaning; *letame*, ‘dung’, becomes the abstract “rankness”. Elsewhere the force or scope changes: *gridò*, ‘cried out’, is softened to “said”, *Temendo*, ‘fearing’, becomes “dreading”, and *peccato*, ‘sin’, becomes “crime”. Such choices coexist with passages that preserve the Italian’s vocabulary and construction with little alteration.

Small omissions and additions recur throughout. Carlyle supplies identifying nouns, as when *quelli*, ‘those’, becomes “those banks”, and makes possession explicit in phrases such as “their bulwark” and “my head”. Conversely, indirect objects, connective elements, and particles are sometimes left unexpressed. Beyond the changes individually noted in the comparison, English regularly requires personal subjects where Italian gives the person through the verb ending, while articles before possessives normally disappear. These routine adjustments, together with the more substantial recastings, produce readable English prose without wholly relinquishing the Italian’s compressed phrasing or unfamiliar turns of expression.
