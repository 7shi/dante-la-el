# Inferno 27

## Lines 1–12 — A New Flame’s Confused Voice

> 1 Già era dritta insù la fiamma, e queta  
> 2 Per non dir più; e già da noi sen gía  
> 3 Con la licenzia del dolce Poeta:  

The flame was now erect and quiet, having ceased to speak, and now went away from us with license of the sweet Poet;

- *Già era dritta insù la fiamma* ‘Already was upright upwards the flame’ → “The flame was now erect”: the subject moves before the verb; *già* becomes “now” rather than ‘already’; “erect” combines *dritta* and the upward direction expressed by *insù*.
- *Per non dir più* ‘For not speaking any more’ → “having ceased to speak”: the preposition and negative infinitive become an affirmative perfect participial construction, presenting the cessation of speech as a completed action.
- *già da noi sen gía* ‘already from us was going away’ → “now went away from us”: “now” replaces ‘already’; the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the phrase ‘from us’ moves after the verb. The pronominal expression *sen gía* is rendered idiomatically as “went away”.

> 4 Quando un’ altra, che dietro a lei venia,  
> 5 Ne fece volger gli occhi alla sua cima,  
> 6 Per un confuso suon che fuor n’ uscia.  

when another, that came behind it, made us turn our eyes to its top, for a confused sound that issued therefrom.

- *dietro a lei venia* ‘behind it was coming’ → “came behind it”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *Ne fece volger gli occhi* ‘Us made turn the eyes’ → “made us turn our eyes”: the object pronoun follows “made”, and the definite article before ‘eyes’ becomes the possessive “our”.
- *fuor n’ uscia* ‘out of it was coming’ → “issued therefrom”: the imperfect becomes a simple past; “issued” incorporates the outward movement, and “therefrom” places the expression of origin after the verb.

> 7 Come il bue Cicilian, che mugghiò prima  
> 8 Col pianto di colui, e ciò fu dritto,  
> 9 Che l’ avea temperato con sua lima,  

As the Sicilian bull (which bellowed first with the lament of him—and that was right—who had tuned it with his file)

- *il bue Cicilian* ‘the ox Sicilian’ → “the Sicilian bull”: “bull” replaces ‘ox’, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Col pianto* ‘with the weeping’ → “with the lament”: “lament” renders the weeping as a vocal expression of grief.
- *di colui* ‘of that one’ → “of him”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *l’ avea temperato* ‘it had tempered’ → “had tuned it”: the object pronoun moves after the verb; “tuned” interprets *temperato* as acoustic adjustment rather than simply tempering or fashioning.

> 10 Mugghiava con la voce dell’ afflitto,  
> 11 Sì che, con tutto ch’ e’ fosse di rame,  
> 12 Pure el pareva dal dolor trafitto:  

kept bellowing with the sufferer’s voice; so that, although it was of brass, it seemed transfixed with pain:

- *Mugghiava* ‘was bellowing’ → “kept bellowing”: “kept” explicitly brings out the continuing action expressed by the imperfect.
- *con la voce dell’ afflitto* ‘with the voice of the afflicted one’ → “with the sufferer’s voice”: the substantivized adjective becomes the noun “sufferer”, and the possessive construction moves the possessor before “voice”.
- *con tutto ch’ e’ fosse* ‘with all that it were’ → “although it was”: the concessive phrase becomes a single conjunction, and the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *rame* ‘copper’ → “brass”: the translation names a different metal.
- *Pure el pareva* ‘Nevertheless it seemed’ → “it seemed”: the concessive reinforcement *Pure* is omitted.
- *dal dolor trafitto* ‘by the pain transfixed’ → “transfixed with pain”: the participle moves before its complement, “with” replaces ‘by’, and the definite article is omitted.

## Lines 13–24 — Words From the Flame

> 13 Così, per non aver via nè forame  
> 14 Dal principio del fuoco, in suo linguaggio  
> 15 Si convertivan le parole grame.  

thus, having at their commencement no way or outlet from the fire, the dismal words were changed into its language.

- *per non aver via nè forame* ‘for having neither way nor opening’ → “having” and “no way or outlet”: the causal infinitive becomes a participial construction, leaving the causal connection implicit; ‘neither … nor’ becomes “no … or,” and ‘opening’ becomes “outlet”.
- *Dal principio* ‘at the beginning’ → “at their commencement”: “their” is supplied, explicitly associating the beginning with the words; the phrase is moved before “no way or outlet”.
- *in suo linguaggio Si convertivan* ‘into its language were turning’ → “were changed into its language”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes an explicit passive, and the phrase identifying the resulting language moves after the verb.
- *le parole grame* ‘the words wretched’ → “the dismal words”: “dismal” renders ‘wretched’; the adjective moves before its noun, and the subject moves from after the verb to before it.

> 16 Ma poscia ch’ ebber colto lor viaggio  
> 17 Su per la punta, dandole quel guizzo  
> 18 Che dato avea la lingua in lor passaggio,  

But after they had found their road up through the point, giving to it the vibration which the tongue had given in their passage,

- *ebber colto lor viaggio* ‘had taken their journey’ → “they had found their road”: taking a journey is rendered as finding a road, making the expression explicitly about discovering a route.
- *dandole quel guizzo* ‘giving it that flick’ → “giving to it the vibration”: the attached indirect-object pronoun becomes a separate prepositional phrase; the demonstrative ‘that’ becomes “the,” and the sudden movement expressed by ‘flick’ becomes “vibration”.
- *Che dato avea la lingua* ‘which given had the tongue’ → “which the tongue had given”: the subject moves before the verb, and the auxiliary precedes rather than follows the participle.

> 19 Udimmo dire: O tu, a cui io drizzo  
> 20 La voce, e che parlavi mo Lombardo,  
> 21 Dicendo: Issa ten va, più non t’ aizzo;  

we heard it say: “O thou, at whom I aim my voice! and who just now wast speaking Lombard, saying, ‘Now go, no more I urge thee;’

- *Udimmo dire* ‘we heard say’ → “we heard it say”: “it” is supplied as the object of “heard” and the understood subject of “say”.
- *a cui io drizzo La voce* ‘to whom I direct the voice’ → “at whom I aim my voice”: ‘direct to’ becomes “aim … at,” and the definite article becomes the possessive “my”.
- *mo* ‘now’ → “just now”: “just” makes the recent-past reference explicit, and the adverb moves from after the verb to before it.
- *Issa ten va* ‘now take yourself away’ → “Now go”: the pronominal expression becomes a simple verb; the reflexive element and the departure expressed by *ne* are not separately represented.
- *più non t’ aizzo* ‘further I do not spur you on’ → “no more I urge thee”: the negative and adverb are combined as “no more,” the object pronoun moves after the verb, and ‘spur on’ becomes “urge”.

> 22 Perch’ io sia giunto forse alquanto tardo,  
> 23 Non t’ incresca restare a parlar meco:  
> 24 Vedi che non incresce a me, e ardo.  

though I have come perhaps a little late, let it not irk thee to pause and speak with me: thou seest it irks not me, although I burn.

- *Perch’* ‘because’ → “though”: the conjunction is read concessively rather than causally.
- *sia giunto* ‘may have arrived’ → “have come”: the Italian perfect subjunctive becomes an English indicative present perfect, and ‘arrived’ becomes “come”.
- *Non t’ incresca* ‘may it not irk you’ → “let it not irk thee”: the independent subjunctive expressing a request becomes a construction with “let”; the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *restare a parlar* ‘to remain to speak’ → “to pause and speak”: ‘remain’ becomes “pause,” and the dependent infinitive expressing purpose becomes a coordinated verb.
- *Vedi che* ‘see that’ → “thou seest”: Carlyle reads *Vedi* as indicative rather than imperative, and omits the conjunction ‘that’.
- *non incresce a me* ‘is not irksome to me’ → “it irks not me”: the Italian impersonal construction with a dative becomes an English transitive construction, with “me” as its direct object.
- *e ardo* ‘and I burn’ → “although I burn”: coordination becomes explicit concessive subordination.

## Lines 25–40 — News of Peace and War in Romagna

> 25 Se tu pur mo in questo mondo cieco  
> 26 Caduto se’ di quella dolce terra  
> 27 Latina, onde mia colpa tutta reco;  

If thou art but now fallen into this blind world from that sweet Latian land, whence I bring all my guilt,

- *pur mo in questo mondo cieco Caduto se’* ‘only now into this world blind fallen art’ → “art but now fallen into this blind world”: the auxiliary precedes the participle, the destination follows it, and the adjective precedes “world”; restrictive *pur* becomes “but”.
- *di quella dolce terra Latina* ‘from that sweet land Latian’ → “from that sweet Latian land”: the adjective of nationality moves before the noun.
- *mia colpa tutta reco* ‘my guilt all I bring’ → “I bring all my guilt”: the verb moves before its object, and “all” precedes the possessive and noun.

> 28 Dimmi se i Romagnuoli han pace o guerra;  
> 29 Ch’ io fui de’ monti là intra Urbino  
> 30 E il giogo di che Tever si disserra.  

tell me if the Romagnuols have peace or war; for I was of the mountains there, between Urbino and the yoke from which the Tiber springs.”

- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating causal conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *di che Tever si disserra* ‘from which Tiber unlocks itself’ → “from which the Tiber springs”: the reflexive expression becomes the intransitive “springs”, and English adds the article before the river’s name.

> 31 Io era ingiuso ancora attento e chino,  
> 32 Quando il mio Duca mi tentò di costa,  
> 33 Dicendo: Parla tu, questi è Latino.  

I still was eager downwards and bent, when my Leader touched me on the side, saying: “Speak thou; this is a Latian.”

- *Io era ingiuso ancora attento* ‘I was downwards still attentive’ → “I still was eager downwards”: “still” moves before the verb and “downwards” after the adjective; *attento* becomes “eager” rather than ‘attentive’.
- *mi tentò* ‘me touched’ → “touched me”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *di costa* ‘at the side’ → “on the side”: the prepositional expression is recast in English.
- *è Latino* ‘is Latian’ → “is a Latian”: English adds an indefinite article, presenting the designation as a count noun.

> 34 Ed io ch’ avea già pronta la risposta,  
> 35 Senza indugio a parlare incominciai:  

And I, who had my answer ready then, began without delay to speak:

- *avea già pronta la risposta* ‘had already ready the answer’ → “had my answer ready then”: “ready” follows the object, the definite article becomes a possessive, and *già* is rendered temporally as “then” rather than ‘already’.
- *Senza indugio a parlare incominciai* ‘without delay to speak I began’ → “began without delay to speak”: the finite verb moves before both the adverbial phrase and the infinitive.

> 36 O anima, che sei laggiù nascosta,  

“O soul, that there below art hidden!

- *sei laggiù nascosta* ‘art down there hidden’ → “there below art hidden”: the locative expression moves before the verb.

> 37 Romagna tua non è, e non fu mai  
> 38 Senza guerra ne’ cuor de’ suoi tiranni;  
> 39 Ma palese nessuna or ven lasciai.  

Thy Romagna is not, and never was, without war in the hearts of her tyrants; but open war just now I there left none.

- *Romagna tua* ‘Romagna thy’ → “Thy Romagna”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *non è* ‘not is’ → “is not”: the negative follows the verb.
- *e non fu mai* ‘and not was ever’ → “and never was”: *non … mai* becomes the single negative adverb “never”, placed before the verb.
- *palese nessuna* ‘open none’ → “open war” and “none”: the understood noun ‘war’ is supplied, and “none” is moved to the end of the clause.
- *or* ‘now’ → “just now”: “just” is supplied.
- *ven* ‘there of it’ → “there”: the locative element is retained, but the partitive *ne* is not separately expressed.

> 40 Ravenna sta, come stata è molti anni:  

Ravenna stands, as it has stood for many years.

- *stata è* ‘stood is’ → “it has stood”: English forms the perfect with “has” rather than ‘is’ and places the auxiliary before the participle.
- *molti anni* ‘many years’ → “for many years”: English supplies “for” to mark duration.

## Lines 41–48 — Romagna’s Rulers

> 41 L’ aquila da Polenta la si cova,  
> 42 Sì che Cervia ricuopre co’ suoi vanni.  

The Eagle of Polenta broods over it, so that he covers Cervia with his pinions.

- *da Polenta* ‘from Polenta’ → “of Polenta”: origin is expressed as affiliation with “of”.
- *la si cova* ‘it for itself broods’ → “broods over it”: the preverbal direct object becomes a prepositional complement following the verb; the reflexive *si* is not separately rendered.
- *Cervia ricuopre* ‘Cervia covers’ → “he covers Cervia”: the object moves from before the verb to after it.

> 43 La terra che fe’ già la lunga prova,  
> 44 E di Franceschi sanguinoso mucchio,  
> 45 Sotto le branche verdi si ritrova.  

The city, which made erewhile the long probation, and sanguinary heap of Frenchmen, finds itself again under the Green Clutches.

- *La terra* ‘the land’ → “The city”: *terra* is rendered in its contextual sense of an urban settlement.
- *di Franceschi sanguinoso mucchio* ‘of Frenchmen sanguinary heap’ → “sanguinary heap of Frenchmen”: the prepositional modifier moves from before the noun phrase to after it.
- *Sotto le branche verdi* ‘under the clutches green’ → “under the Green Clutches”: the prepositional phrase moves from before the verb to after it, and the adjective moves before its noun.
- *si ritrova* ‘itself finds’ → “finds itself again”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb; “again” gives explicit repetitive force to *ri-*, rather than treating *ritrovarsi* simply as ‘to find oneself’ or ‘to be situated’.

> 46 E il Mastin vecchio, e il nuovo da Verrucchio,  
> 47 Che fecer di Montagna il mal governo,  
> 48 Là, dove soglion, fan de’ denti succhio.  

And the old Mastiff of Verrucchio and the young, who of Montagna made evil governance, there where they are wont to ply their teeth.

- *il Mastin vecchio* ‘the mastiff old’ → “the old Mastiff”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *e il nuovo* ‘and the new’ → “and the young”: “young” replaces ‘new’, making the contrast one of age.
- *da Verrucchio* ‘from Verrucchio’ → “of Verrucchio”: “of” replaces ‘from’; the modifier moves from after the second coordinated designation to immediately after the first.
- *fecer di Montagna* ‘made of Montagna’ → “of Montagna made”: the prepositional phrase moves before the verb.
- *il mal governo* ‘the evil governance’ → “evil governance”: the definite article is omitted.
- *soglion, fan de’ denti succhio* ‘they are wont, they make sucking of the teeth’ → “they are wont to ply their teeth”: the separate finite main verb *fan* becomes an infinitive governed by “are wont”, leaving no finite main verb for the opening subject. The expression ‘make sucking of the teeth’ becomes “ply their teeth”: the action is generalized to using the teeth, which become the direct object, and the Italian article becomes the possessive “their”.

## Lines 49–60 — Cities and a Speaking Flame

> 49 Le città di Lamone e di Santerno  
> 50 Conduce il leoncel dal nido bianco,  
> 51 Che muta parte dalla state al verno:  

The cities of Lamone and Santerno guides Lioncel of the white lair, who changes faction from the summer to the winter.

- *e di Santerno* ‘and of Santerno’ → “and Santerno”: the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *dal nido bianco* ‘from the nest white’ → “of the white lair”: origin expressed by ‘from’ becomes association expressed by “of”; ‘nest’ becomes “lair”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *parte* ‘side’ → “faction”: the translation makes the political sense explicit.

> 52 E quella, a cui il Savio bagna il fianco,  
> 53 Così com’ ella s’ è tra il piano e il monte,  
> 54 Tra tirannia si vive e stato franco.  

And that whose flank the Savio bathes, as it lies between the plain and mount, so lives it between tyranny and freedom.

- *a cui il Savio bagna il fianco* ‘to which the Savio bathes the flank’ → “whose flank the Savio bathes”: the possessive relationship expressed by the Italian dative becomes “whose”; the object moves before the subject and verb.
- *Così com’* ‘just as’ → “as” and “so”: the introductory comparison becomes a two-part English construction, with “so” introducing the corresponding main clause.
- *ella s’ è* ‘it is’ → “it lies”: the translation uses a specifically spatial verb; the Italian pronominal construction has no separate English equivalent.
- *il piano e il monte* ‘the plain and the mount’ → “the plain and mount”: the second definite article is omitted.
- *Tra tirannia si vive e* ‘between tyranny it lives and’ → “lives it between tyranny and”: the verb moves ahead of the coordinated prepositional phrase instead of interrupting it; English also inverts subject and verb after “so”. The Italian pronominal *si* has no separate equivalent.
- *stato franco* ‘free state’ → “freedom”: a noun qualified by an adjective becomes a single abstract noun.

> 55 Ora chi sei ti prego che ne conte:  

“Now I pray thee, tell us who thou art.

- *chi sei ti prego che ne conte* ‘who thou art thee I pray that thou tell us’ → “I pray thee, tell us who thou art”: the clause identifying what is to be told moves from before the request to after the verb of telling; the object pronoun of *prego* moves after its verb. The subordinate request, introduced by *che* and expressed in the subjunctive, becomes a direct imperative; the conjunction is omitted.

> 56 Non esser duro più ch’ altri sia stato,  
> 57 Se il nome tuo nel mondo tegna fronte.  

Be not more hard than one has been to thee: so may thy name on earth maintain its front.”

- *Non esser duro più* ‘not to be hard more’ → “Be not more hard”: the negative infinitive used as an Italian command becomes an English imperative; the negative follows the verb, and the comparative modifier precedes the adjective.
- *ch’ altri sia stato* ‘than another has been’ → “than one has been to thee”: ‘another’ becomes the indefinite “one”; the Italian perfect subjunctive becomes an English indicative, and “to thee” is supplied.
- *Se* ‘if’ → “so may”: the conditional form is rendered as an explicit wish rather than an ordinary condition.
- *il nome tuo* ‘the name thy’ → “thy name”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *nel mondo* ‘in the world’ → “on earth”: both the noun and the preposition change.
- *tegna fronte* ‘hold front’ → “maintain its front”: ‘hold’ becomes “maintain”, and the possessive “its” is supplied.

> 58 Poscia che il fuoco alquanto ebbe rugghiato  
> 59 Al modo suo, l’ aguta punta mosse  
> 60 Di qua, di là, e poi diè cotal fiato:  

After the flame had roared awhile as usual, it moved the sharp point to and fro, and then gave forth this breath:

- *il fuoco* ‘the fire’ → “the flame”: the translation substitutes a more specific noun.
- *alquanto* ‘somewhat’ → “awhile”: the quantity is interpreted as duration; the modifier also moves from before the compound verb to after it.
- *Al modo suo* ‘in its own manner’ → “as usual”: an expression of manner becomes an expression of customary behaviour.
- *l’ aguta punta mosse* ‘the sharp point it moved’ → “it moved the sharp point”: the object moves from before the verb to after it.
- *Di qua, di là* ‘this way, that way’ → “to and fro”: the two directional expressions become an idiom for alternating movement.
- *diè cotal fiato* ‘gave such breath’ → “gave forth this breath”: “forth” is supplied, and ‘such’ becomes the demonstrative “this”.

## Lines 61–75 — An Answer Without Fear of Infamy

> 61 S’ io credessi che mia risposta fosse  
> 62 A persona che mai tornasse al mondo,  
> 63 Questa fiamma staria senza più scosse:  

“If I thought my answer were to one who ever could return to the world, this flame should shake no more.

- *credessi che* ‘believed that’ → “thought”: the verb is rendered by “thought”, and the conjunction introducing its clause is omitted.
- *A persona* ‘to a person’ → “to one”: the noun becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *che mai tornasse* ‘who ever returned’ → “who ever could return”: the hypothetical subjunctive is rendered with the modal “could”.
- *staria senza più scosse* ‘would remain without further shakings’ → “should shake no more”: remaining without movement becomes a negated verb of movement; the plural noun becomes the verb “shake”.

> 64 Ma perciocchè giammai di questo fondo  
> 65 Non tornò vivo alcun, s’ io odo il vero,  
> 66 Senza tema d’ infamia ti rispondo.  

But since none ever did return alive from this depth, if what I hear be true, without fear of infamy I answer thee.

- *perciocchè* ‘because’ → “since”: the causal conjunction is rendered by another causal connective.
- *giammai di questo fondo Non tornò vivo alcun* ‘never from this depth did anyone return alive’ → “none ever did return alive from this depth”: Italian negative concord is recast with the negative subject “none”, moved before the verb, and affirmative “ever”; “from this depth” follows the completed verb clause rather than preceding its verb.
- *s’ io odo il vero* ‘if I hear the truth’ → “if what I hear be true”: the object ‘the truth’ becomes a predicate, and the act of hearing becomes the subject clause “what I hear”.
- *ti rispondo* ‘to thee I answer’ → “I answer thee”: the object pronoun follows the verb, without a preposition.

> 67 Io fui uom d’ arme, e poi fui Cordigliero,  
> 68 Credendomi, sì cinto, fare ammenda:  

“I was a man of arms; and then became a Cordelier, hoping, thus girt, to make amends.

- *uom d’ arme* ‘man of arms’ → “a man of arms”: the indefinite article is supplied.
- *fui Cordigliero* ‘was Cordelier’ → “became a Cordelier”: a state is expressed as entry into that state, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *Credendomi* ‘believing myself’ → “hoping”: belief becomes hope, and the reflexive pronoun has no separate English equivalent.

> 69 E certo il creder mio veniva intero,  
> 70 Se non fosse il Gran Prete, a cui mal prenda,  
> 71 Che mi rimise nelle prime colpe:  

And certainly my hope were come in full, but for the Great Priest, may ill befall him! who brought me back to my first sins:

- *il creder mio* ‘the believing of mine’ → “my hope”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun expressing hope rather than belief, and the possessive moves before it.
- *veniva intero* ‘was coming whole’ → “were come in full”: the Italian imperfect indicative, used counterfactually here, becomes an English perfect subjunctive; the adjective becomes the adverbial phrase “in full”.
- *Se non fosse* ‘if it were not’ → “but for”: the conditional clause becomes a prepositional construction.
- *a cui mal prenda* ‘to whom may ill come’ → “may ill befall him”: the relative construction becomes an independent wish, and the relative pronoun becomes the object “him”.
- *mi rimise* ‘put me back’ → “brought me back”: putting is rendered as bringing, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *nelle prime colpe* ‘into the first faults’ → “to my first sins”: “to” replaces ‘into’, “my” replaces the definite article, and the faults are explicitly termed “sins”.

> 72 E come, e quare voglio che m’ intenda.  

and how and why, I wish thee to hear from me.

- *voglio che m’ intenda* ‘I want that thou understand me’ → “I wish thee to hear from me”: the subordinate subjunctive becomes an object-and-infinitive construction after “wish”; understanding is interpreted as hearing, and the object pronoun becomes the source phrase “from me”.

> 73 Mentre ch’ io forma fui d’ ossa e di polpe,  
> 74 Che la madre mi diè, l’ opere mie  
> 75 Non furon leonine, ma di volpe.  

Whilst I was the form of bones and pulp, which my mother gave me, my deeds were not those of the lion, but of the fox.

- *forma fui* ‘form I was’ → “I was the form”: the predicate noun moves after the verb, and a definite article is supplied.
- *di polpe* ‘of fleshy parts’ → “pulp”: the repeated preposition is omitted, and the plural noun becomes a singular mass noun.
- *la madre* ‘the mother’ → “my mother”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *mi diè* ‘to me gave’ → “gave me”: the recipient pronoun follows the verb, without a preposition.
- *l’ opere mie* ‘the works of mine’ → “my deeds”: “deeds” renders ‘works’, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *Non furon leonine* ‘not were leonine’ → “were not those of the lion”: the negative follows the verb, and the adjective becomes a pronoun followed by a possessive phrase.
- *di volpe* ‘of fox’ → “of the fox”: a definite article is supplied before the generic singular noun.

## Lines 76–84 — Guile and Late Repentance

> 76 Gli accorgimenti e le coperte vie  
> 77 Io seppi tutte; e sì menai lor arte,  
> 78 Ch’ al fine della terra il suono uscie.  

All wiles and covert ways I knew; and used the art of them so well, that to the ends of the earth the sound went forth.

- *Gli accorgimenti e le coperte vie Io seppi tutte* ‘The wiles and the covert ways I knew all’ → “All wiles and covert ways I knew”: the postponed *tutte* moves to the beginning of the object phrase, and both definite articles are omitted.
- *e sì menai lor arte* ‘and so I practised their art’ → “and used the art of them so well”: *menai* is rendered as “used”; the possessive *lor* becomes the following phrase “of them”; and *sì* is expanded to “so well” and placed after the object.
- *al fine* ‘to the end’ → “to the ends”: the singular becomes plural.
- *uscie* ‘went out’ → “went forth”: outward movement is expressed with “forth” rather than ‘out’.

> 79 Quando mi vidi giunto in quella parte  
> 80 Di mia età, dove ciascun dovrebbe  
> 81 Calar le vele e raccoglier le sarte,  

When I saw myself come to that period of my age at which every one should lower sails and gather in his ropes,

- *mi vidi* ‘myself I saw’ → “I saw myself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *giunto in quella parte Di mia età* ‘arrived in that part of my age’ → “come to that period of my age”: *giunto* becomes “come,” the preposition becomes “to” rather than ‘in,’ and “period” makes the temporal sense of *parte* explicit.
- *dove* ‘where’ → “at which”: the relative adverb becomes a preposition and relative pronoun referring to the period of life.
- *Calar le vele* ‘lower the sails’ → “lower sails”: the definite article is omitted.
- *e raccoglier le sarte* ‘and gather the shrouds’ → “and gather in his ropes”: “in” is supplied, the definite article becomes the possessive “his,” and the specific nautical term *sarte* is rendered by the broader “ropes”.

> 82 Ciò che pria mi piaceva, allor m’ increbbe;  

that which before had pleased me, grieved me then;

- *Ciò che pria mi piaceva* ‘that which formerly was pleasing to me’ → “that which before had pleased me”: the imperfect becomes a pluperfect; the Italian construction with an indirect object becomes the transitive “pleased me”.
- *allor m’ increbbe* ‘then was displeasing to me’ → “grieved me then”: “then” moves to the end, and the Italian construction with an indirect object becomes a transitive verb with “me” as its direct object. “Grieved” gives the displeasure a stronger emotional expression.

> 83 E pentuto e confesso mi rendei,  

and with repentance and confession I yielded myself,

- *E pentuto e confesso* ‘and repentant and having confessed’ → “and with repentance and confession”: descriptions of the speaker’s state become nouns governed by the supplied preposition “with”.
- *mi rendei* ‘myself I yielded’ → “I yielded myself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.

> 84 Ahi miser lasso! e giovato sarebbe.  

ah woe alas! and it would have availed me.

- *Ahi miser lasso!* ‘Ah, wretched, hapless [me]!’ → “ah woe alas!”: the self-descriptive adjectives *miser* and *lasso* become the exclamations “woe” and “alas,” leaving the reference to the speaker implicit.
- *e giovato sarebbe* ‘and availed it would have’ → “and it would have availed me”: the participle, which precedes its auxiliary in Italian, follows the auxiliaries in English; “me” is supplied as the person who would have benefited.

## Lines 85–97 — A Call to Cure Pride

> 85 Lo Principe de’ nuovi Farisei  
> 86 Avendo guerra presso a Laterano,  
> 87 E non con Saracin, nè con Giudei;  

The Prince of the new Pharisees—waging war near to the Lateran, and not with Saracens or Jews;

- *Avendo guerra* ‘having war’ → “waging war”: the construction with ‘having’ becomes the idiomatic English expression “waging war”.
- *Laterano* ‘Lateran’ → “the Lateran”: the definite article is supplied.
- *E non con Saracin, nè con Giudei* ‘and not with Saracens, nor with Jews’ → “and not with Saracens or Jews”: the second negative conjunction becomes “or” under the preceding “not”, and the repeated preposition is omitted.

> 88 Chè ciascun suo nemico era Cristiano,  
> 89 E nessuno era stato a vincer Acri,  
> 90 Nè mercatante in terra di Soldano:  

for every enemy of his was Christian, and none had been to conquer Acre, nor been a merchant in the Soldan’s land—

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *ciascun suo nemico* ‘every his enemy’ → “every enemy of his”: the possessive before the noun becomes a possessive phrase after it.
- *Nè mercatante* ‘nor merchant’ → “nor been a merchant”: “been”, understood from the preceding clause in Italian, is repeated explicitly; the indefinite article is also supplied.
- *in terra di Soldano* ‘in land of Soldan’ → “in the Soldan’s land”: the postnominal possessive phrase becomes a possessive before the noun, and a definite article is supplied.

> 91 Nè sommo uficio, nè ordini sacri  
> 92 Guardò in sè, nè in me quel capestro  
> 93 Che solea far li suoi cinti più macri.  

regarded not the Highest Office nor Holy Orders in himself, nor in me that Cord which used to make those whom it girded leaner;

- *Nè sommo uficio, nè ordini sacri / Guardò* ‘neither highest office nor holy orders he regarded’ → “regarded not the Highest Office nor Holy Orders”: the verb moves before its objects; the first negative moves with it, changing ‘neither … nor’ into “not … nor”; and the article before “Highest Office” is supplied.
- *capestro* ‘halter’ → “Cord”: the translation uses a less specific term for the rope.
- *li suoi cinti* ‘its girded ones’ → “those whom it girded”: a substantivized passive participle becomes a pronoun followed by an active relative clause; the possessive becomes the clause’s subject, “it”.

> 94 Ma come Costantin chiese Silvestro  
> 95 Dentro Siratti a guarir della lebbre,  

but as Constantine called Silvestro out of Soracte to cure his leprosy,

- *chiese* ‘asked for’ → “called”: requesting someone is expressed as summoning him.
- *Dentro Siratti* ‘within Soracte’ → “out of Soracte”: the place where someone is located becomes the place from which he is summoned.
- *a guarir della lebbre* ‘to recover from the leprosy’ → “to cure his leprosy”: the construction of recovering from a disease becomes a transitive construction with the disease as object; “his” replaces the definite article.

> 96 Così mi chiese questi per maestro  
> 97 A guarir della sua superba febbre.  

so this man called me as an adept to cure the fever of his pride.

- *mi chiese questi* ‘me requested this one’ → “this man called me”: object–verb–subject order becomes subject–verb–object; the demonstrative pronoun becomes “this man”, and requesting becomes calling.
- *per maestro* ‘for a master’ → “as an adept”: the role expressed by *per* is rendered with “as”, and “adept” emphasizes proficiency rather than mastery.
- *A guarir della sua superba febbre* ‘to recover from his proud fever’ → “to cure the fever of his pride”: recovering from a disease again becomes curing it transitively; the adjective ‘proud’ becomes the noun “pride” in an added possessive phrase, and the possessive shifts from the fever to the pride.

## Lines 98–111 — Absolution and Deceitful Counsel

> 98 Domandommi consiglio; ed io tacetti,  
> 99 Perchè le sue parole parvero ebbre.  

He demanded counsel of me; and I kept silent, for his words seemed drunken.

- *Domandommi consiglio* ‘he asked me for counsel’ → “He demanded counsel of me”: ‘asked’ becomes “demanded,” and the person asked is expressed by “of me” after “counsel.”
- *io tacetti* ‘I was silent’ → “I kept silent”: the simple verb becomes a phrase suggesting continued silence.
- *Perchè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.

> 100 E poi mi disse: Tuo cor non sospetti:  
> 101 Fin or ti assolvo, e tu m’ insegna fare  
> 102 Sì come Penestrino in terra getti.  

And then he said to me: ‘Let not thy heart misdoubt: even now I do absolve thee, and do thou teach me so to act, that I may cast Penestrino to the ground.

- *E poi mi disse* ‘and then to me he said’ → “And then he said to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *Tuo cor non sospetti* ‘let thy heart not suspect’ → “Let not thy heart misdoubt”: the subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”; the negative precedes the subject, and ‘suspect’ is rendered by the archaic “misdoubt.”
- *Fin or ti assolvo* ‘from now I absolve thee’ → “even now I do absolve thee”: the temporal expression becomes emphatic “even now”; “do” is supplied, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *e tu m’ insegna* ‘and thou teach me’ → “and do thou teach me”: “do” is supplied to reinforce the imperative, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *fare Sì come* ‘to do in such a way that’ → “so to act, that”: ‘do’ becomes “act,” and the expression of manner is compressed into “so” and placed before the infinitive.
- *Penestrino in terra getti* ‘Penestrino to the ground I cast’ → “I may cast Penestrino to the ground”: the subjunctive is expressed with “may,” and the object and directional phrase move after the verb.

> 103 Lo Ciel poss’ io serrare e disserrare,  
> 104 Come tu sai; però son duo le chiavi,  
> 105 Che il mio antecessor non ebbe care.  

Heaven I can shut and open, as thou knowest; for two are the keys that my predecessor held not dear.’

- *Lo Ciel* ‘the heaven’ → “Heaven”: the definite article is omitted.
- *poss’ io* ‘can I’ → “I can”: subject and modal verb exchange places.
- *però* ‘therefore’ → “for”: the translation makes the following clause an explanation rather than a consequence.
- *son duo* ‘are two’ → “two are”: the numeral moves before the verb.
- *non ebbe care* ‘did not have dear’ → “held not dear”: the Italian idiom with ‘have’ becomes the English idiom with “held,” and the negative follows the finite verb.

> 106 Allor mi pinser gli argomenti gravi  
> 107 Là ’ve il tacer mi fu avviso il peggio,  

Then the weighty arguments impelled me to think silence worst;

- *mi pinser gli argomenti gravi* ‘me impelled the arguments weighty’ → “the weighty arguments impelled me”: the subject moves before the verb, the object pronoun moves after it, and the adjective moves before its noun.
- *Là ’ve il tacer mi fu avviso il peggio* ‘there where keeping silent seemed to me the worst’ → “to think silence worst”: the locative relative construction becomes an infinitive expressing the result of being impelled; ‘seemed to me’ becomes the active “think.” The substantivized infinitive ‘keeping silent’ becomes the noun “silence,” and the article before ‘worst’ is omitted.

> 108 E dissi: Padre, da che tu mi lavi  
> 109 Di quel peccato, ove mo cader deggio,  

and I said: ‘Father! since thou cleansest me from that guilt into which I now must fall,

- *tu mi lavi* ‘thou washest me’ → “thou cleansest me”: ‘wash’ becomes the broader ‘cleanse,’ and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Di quel peccato* ‘of that sin’ → “from that guilt”: the preposition changes to suit “cleansest,” and ‘sin’ becomes “guilt.”
- *ove mo cader deggio* ‘where now to fall I must’ → “into which I now must fall”: the relative adverb becomes a preposition and relative pronoun, making the direction explicit; the modal precedes rather than follows the infinitive.

> 110 Lunga promessa con l’ attender corto  
> 111 Ti farà trionfar nell’ alto seggio.  

large promise, with small observance of it, will make thee triumph in thy High Seat.’

- *Lunga promessa* ‘long promise’ → “large promise”: length is recast as magnitude.
- *con l’ attender corto* ‘with the fulfilling short’ → “with small observance of it”: the substantivized infinitive, understood as fulfilling a promise, becomes “observance”; ‘short’ becomes “small” and moves before the noun. The article is omitted, and “of it” is supplied.
- *Ti farà trionfar* ‘thee will make triumph’ → “will make thee triumph”: the object pronoun moves after “make.”
- *nell’ alto seggio* ‘in the high seat’ → “in thy High Seat”: the definite article is replaced by the possessive “thy.”

## Lines 112–127 — The Logician Devil Claims His Soul

> 112 Francesco venne poi, com’ io fu’ morto,  
> 113 Per me; ma un de’ neri Cherubini  
> 114 Gli disse: Nol portar; non mi far torto.  

“Saint Francis afterwards, when I was dead, came for me; but one of the Black Cherubim said to him: ‘Do not take him; wrong me not.

- *Francesco* ‘Francis’ → “Saint Francis”: the title is supplied.
- *venne poi, com’ io fu’ morto, Per me* ‘came afterwards, when I was dead, for me’ → “afterwards, when I was dead, came for me”: the main verb moves after the temporal clause.
- *Gli disse* ‘to him said’ → “said to him”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *Nol portar* ‘do not carry him’ → “Do not take him”: ‘carry’ becomes “take”; the Italian negative command uses an infinitive, whereas English uses an imperative construction, with the object following the verb.
- *non mi far torto* ‘do not do me wrong’ → “wrong me not”: the noun ‘wrong’ becomes a verb, replacing ‘do wrong’; the object and negative follow that verb.

> 115 Venir se ne dee giù tra’ miei meschini,  
> 116 Perchè diede il consiglio frodolente,  
> 117 Dal quale in qua stato gli sono a’ crini:  

He must come down amongst my menials; because he gave the fraudulent counsel, since which I have kept fast by his hair.

- *Venir se ne dee giù* ‘come away he must down’ → “He must come down”: the initial infinitive moves after the modal verb; the sense of departure expressed by *se ne* is not separately rendered.
- *meschini* ‘wretches’ → “menials”: the translation takes the word in its alternative sense of servants or dependants.
- *diede il consiglio frodolente* ‘gave the counsel fraudulent’ → “gave the fraudulent counsel”: the adjective moves before its noun.
- *Dal quale in qua* ‘from which until now’ → “since which”: the temporal expression is compressed, leaving ‘until now’ implicit.
- *stato gli sono a’ crini* ‘I have been at his hairs’ → “I have kept fast by his hair”: the participle preceding its auxiliary becomes a normal English perfect construction; ‘been at’ becomes “kept fast by,” making the hold explicit. The possessive dative *gli* becomes “his,” and plural ‘hairs’ becomes the collective singular “hair.”

> 118 Ch’ assolver non si può chi non si pente;  
> 119 Nè pentere e volere insieme puossi,  
> 120 Per la contraddizion che nol consente.  

For he who repents not, cannot be absolved; nor is it possible to repent and will a thing at the same time, the contradiction not permitting it.’

- *assolver non si può* ‘one cannot absolve’ → “cannot be absolved”: the impersonal active construction becomes passive, and the initial infinitive moves to the end of the verb phrase.
- *chi non si pente* ‘whoever does not repent’ → “he who repents not”: the indefinite relative becomes a pronoun followed by a relative clause and moves before the main predicate as its subject. The Italian pronominal verb becomes nonreflexive English “repents.”
- *pentere e volere* ‘to repent and to will’ → “to repent and will a thing”: “a thing” is supplied as the object of “will.”
- *insieme* ‘together’ → “at the same time”: the single adverb becomes an explicitly temporal phrase.
- *puossi* ‘one can’ → “is it possible”: the impersonal modal becomes a construction with dummy “it” and the adjective “possible”; it moves before the infinitives, with inversion after “nor.”
- *Per la contraddizion che nol consente* ‘because of the contradiction that does not permit it’ → “the contradiction not permitting it”: the causal prepositional phrase and its relative clause become an absolute participial construction, leaving the causal connection implicit.

> 121 O me dolente! come mi riscossi  
> 122 Quando mi prese, dicendomi: Forse  
> 123 Tu non pensavi ch’ io loico fossi!  

O wretched me! how I started when he seized me, saying to me: ‘May be thou didst not think that I was a logician!’

- *O me dolente!* ‘O suffering me!’ → “O wretched me!”: ‘suffering’ becomes “wretched,” and the adjective moves before “me.”
- *come mi riscossi* ‘how I shook myself’ → “how I started”: the pronominal expression becomes the nonreflexive English verb “started,” conveying a sudden involuntary movement.
- *Quando mi prese* ‘when he took me’ → “when he seized me”: “seized” gives ‘took’ a stronger sense, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Forse* ‘perhaps’ → “May be”: the adverb becomes a two-word expression of possibility.
- *Tu non pensavi* ‘thou wast not thinking’ → “thou didst not think”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by an English simple past rather than an explicitly continuing past construction.
- *ch’ io loico fossi* ‘that I a logician were’ → “that I was a logician”: the subjunctive becomes indicative, the predicate noun moves after the verb, and English supplies its indefinite article.

> 124 A Minos mi portò: e quegli attorse  
> 125 Otto volte la coda al dosso duro;  

“To Minos he bore me, who twined his tail eight times round his fearful back,

- *A Minos mi portò* ‘to Minos me he bore’ → “To Minos he bore me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *e quegli* ‘and that one’ → “who”: a coordinated clause with a demonstrative subject becomes a relative clause.
- *attorse Otto volte la coda* ‘twisted eight times the tail’ → “twined his tail eight times”: ‘twisted’ becomes “twined,” the object moves before the expression of frequency, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *al dosso duro* ‘at the hard back’ → “round his fearful back”: the preposition is interpreted as encirclement, the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘hard’ is interpreted as “fearful”; the adjective moves before the noun.

> 126 E poi che per gran rabbia la si morse,  
> 127 Disse: Questi è de’ rei del fuoco furo:  

and then biting it in great rage, said: ‘This is a sinner for the thievish fire.’

- *E poi che per gran rabbia la si morse* ‘and after he bit it for himself out of great rage’ → “and then biting it in great rage”: the temporal subordinate clause becomes “then” followed by a participial phrase, replacing the finite past verb. The reflexive dative *si* is not separately expressed; ‘out of great rage’ becomes “in great rage” and moves after the verb and object.
- *Questi è de’ rei* ‘this one is of the guilty ones’ → “This is a sinner”: the partitive expression identifying one among a class becomes a singular predicate noun, “a sinner.”
- *del fuoco furo* ‘of the fire thievish’ → “for the thievish fire”: “for” interprets the relation expressed by ‘of’ as assignment or destination, and the adjective moves before its noun.

## Lines 128–136 — Grief and the Next Ditch

> 128 Per ch’ io là, dove vedi, son perduto;  

Therefore I, where thou seest, am lost;

- *Per ch’* ‘for which reason’ → “Therefore”: the connective phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *là, dove vedi* ‘there, where thou seest’ → “where thou seest”: *là*, ‘there’, is omitted.

> 129 E sì vestito andando mi rancuro.  

and going thus clothed, in heart I grieve.”

- *sì vestito andando* ‘thus clothed going’ → “going thus clothed”: the participle of motion precedes the description of dress rather than following it.
- *mi rancuro* ‘I grieve’ → “in heart I grieve”: the pronominal verb is expanded with “in heart”, which has no separate counterpart in the Italian.

> 130 Quand’ egli ebbe il suo dir così compiuto,  
> 131 La fiamma dolorando si partio,  
> 132 Torcendo e dibattendo il corno aguto.  

When he his words had ended thus, the flame, sorrowing, departed, writhing and tossing its sharp horn.

- *ebbe il suo dir così compiuto* ‘had his speaking thus completed’ → “his words had ended thus”: the substantivized infinitive *dir*, ‘speaking’, becomes the plural noun “words”; the object moves before the auxiliary, and “thus” moves after the main participle. *Compiuto*, ‘completed’, becomes “ended”, and the Italian past anterior is rendered by the English past perfect.
- *Torcendo e dibattendo* ‘twisting and beating about’ → “writhing and tossing”: the verbs are rendered by alternative expressions for contortion and agitated movement.
- *il corno aguto* ‘the horn sharp’ → “its sharp horn”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 133 Noi passammo oltre, ed io e il Duca mio,  
> 134 Su per lo scoglio infino in su l’ altr’ arco  
> 135 Che copre il fosso, in che si paga il fio  
> 136 A quei che, scommettendo, acquistan carco.  

We passed on, I and my Guide, along the cliff up to the other arch that covers the foss, in which their fee is paid to those who, sowing discord, gather guilt.

- *oltre* ‘beyond’ → “on”: the spatial adverb becomes an expression of continued movement.
- *ed io e il Duca mio* ‘and I and the leader my’ → “I and my Guide”: the first conjunction is omitted; *Duca*, ‘leader’, is rendered as “Guide”; and the possessive moves before the noun, without its Italian article.
- *Su per lo scoglio* ‘up along the rock’ → “along the cliff”: the initial upward direction is not separately expressed here, and *scoglio*, ‘rock’, is rendered more specifically as “cliff”.
- *infino in su l’altr’ arco* ‘as far as up onto the other arch’ → “up to the other arch”: the sequence expressing extent and upward movement onto something is compressed into “up to”.
- *si paga il fio* ‘is paid the penalty’ → “their fee is paid”: the subject moves before the passive verb; *fio*, ‘penalty’, is rendered as “fee”; and the definite article becomes “their”, supplying a possessive reference to the recipients.
- *scommettendo* ‘sundering’ → “sowing discord”: the verb of separating or putting asunder is interpreted through an explicit expression for causing discord.
- *acquistan carco* ‘acquire a burden’ → “gather guilt”: “gather” replaces ‘acquire’, and the figurative “guilt” replaces the literal ‘burden’.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Recasting

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s sequence of clauses, repetitions, and distinctive expressions, while frequently rearranging words within them. Adjectives generally move before nouns, object pronouns follow verbs, and auxiliaries precede participles. Yet he also preserves marked Italian ordering, as in “The cities of Lamone and Santerno guides Lioncel” and “Heaven I can shut and open”. His prose thus combines ordinary English syntax with conspicuously literal turns of phrase.

Changes of construction often go beyond rearrangement. *Per non dir più*, ‘for not speaking any more’, becomes “having ceased to speak”; *staria senza più scosse*, ‘would remain without further shakings’, becomes “should shake no more”. Subjunctives are variously rendered by indicatives, modal verbs, or constructions with “let”, while impersonal expressions may become passive ones: *assolver non si può*, ‘one cannot absolve’, becomes “cannot be absolved”. Logical relations are sometimes made more explicit, notably when *e ardo*, ‘and I burn’, becomes “although I burn”; elsewhere a participial construction leaves a causal or temporal connection less explicitly stated.

Vocabulary ranges from close verbal echoes to interpretive substitutions. “thievish fire” closely preserves *fuoco furo*, whereas *stato franco*, ‘free state’, is compressed into “freedom”, and *scommettendo*, ‘sundering’, becomes “sowing discord”. “tuned” gives *temperato* a specifically acoustic sense, while “hoping” and “my hope” recast *Credendomi* and *il creder mio* in terms of hope rather than belief. More conspicuous departures include “brass” for *rame*, ‘copper’, and “fearful” for *duro*, ‘hard’.

Small additions and omissions also recur. Carlyle supplies possessives where Italian uses definite articles, as in “our eyes” and “his tail”, and makes understood material explicit through “war”, “Saint”, or “a thing”. Conversely, particles such as *Pure* and elements of pronominal expressions are sometimes left without separate equivalents. Beyond the individual changes recorded in the comparison, English regularly supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending, and omits the articles that Italian places before possessives. These routine adjustments support a translation that remains recognizably close to Dante’s wording without consistently reproducing it word for word.
