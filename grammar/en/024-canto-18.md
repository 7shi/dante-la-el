# Inferno 18

## Lines 1–9 — The Layout of Malebolge

> 1 Luogo è in inferno, detto Malebolge,  
> 2 Tutto di pietra e di color ferrigno,  
> 3 Come la cerchia che d’ intorno il volge.  

There is a place in Hell called Malebolge, all of stone, and of an iron colour, like the barrier which winds round it.

- *Luogo è* ‘place is’ → “There is a place”: the opening becomes an English existential construction, supplying “There” and an indefinite article.
- *di color ferrigno* ‘of colour iron-grey’ → “of an iron colour”: the descriptive adjective becomes attributive “iron”, placed before “colour”, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *cerchia* ‘encircling ring’ → “barrier”: the translation substitutes a word emphasizing enclosure rather than circular form.
- *d’ intorno il volge* ‘round about it encircles’ → “winds round it”: the transitive verb becomes intransitive “winds”; the preceding adverbial expression and direct object become the following prepositional phrase “round it”.

> 4 Nel dritto mezzo del campo maligno  
> 5 Vaneggia un pozzo assai largo e profondo,  
> 6 Di cui suo luogo conterà l’ ordigno.  

Right in the middle of the malignant field yawns a well exceeding wide and deep, whose structure its own place shall tell.

- *Nel dritto mezzo* ‘in the exact middle’ → “Right in the middle”: the adjective qualifying ‘middle’ becomes the adverb “Right”, placed before the prepositional phrase.
- *del campo maligno* ‘of the field malignant’ → “of the malignant field”: the adjective moves before its noun.
- *Di cui suo luogo conterà l’ ordigno* ‘of which its place will tell the structure’ → “whose structure its own place shall tell”: the possessive relative “whose” replaces ‘of which’, and “structure” moves forward to join it, placing the complete object before the subject and verb. “Own” makes the possessive more emphatic.

> 7 Quel cinghio, che rimane, adunque è tondo,  
> 8 Tra il pozzo e il piè dell’ alta ripa dura,  
> 9 E ha distinto in dieci valli il fondo.  

The border therefore that remains, between the well and the foot of the high rocky bank, is round; and it has its bottom divided into ten valleys.

- *Quel* ‘that’ → “The”: the demonstrative becomes a definite article.
- *cinghio* ‘belt’ → “border”: the word for an encircling band is rendered as a boundary or marginal area.
- *che rimane, adunque* ‘that remains, therefore’ → “therefore that remains”: the connective moves before the relative clause “that remains”, rather than following it.
- *è tondo, Tra il pozzo e il piè dell’ alta ripa dura* ‘is round, between the well and the foot of the high bank hard’ → “between the well and the foot of the high rocky bank, is round”: the prepositional phrase moves before “is round”; in the Italian it follows that predicate. “Rocky” interprets ‘hard’ as referring to the bank’s material, and this adjective moves before the noun.
- *E ha distinto in dieci valli il fondo* ‘and has divided into ten valleys the bottom’ → “and it has its bottom divided into ten valleys”: the object moves immediately after “has”, before the participle and its accompanying prepositional phrase. This gives the English construction ‘has something divided’, describing the bottom’s condition; the definite article before ‘bottom’ also becomes the possessive “its”.

## Lines 10–21 — Bridges Across the Ditches

> 10 Quale, dove per guardia delle mura  
> 11 Più e più fossi cingon li castelli,  
> 12 La parte dov’ ei son rende figura;  

As is the form that ground presents, where to defend the walls successive ditches begird a castle;

- *Quale ... La parte dov’ ei son rende figura* ‘Such a figure as the tract where they are presents’ → “As is the form that ground presents”: the Italian object ‘figure’ becomes the subject of the supplied “is,” and ‘the tract presents’ becomes the relative clause “that ground presents.” ‘The tract’ becomes the articleless “ground,” and ‘where they are’ is omitted.
- *per guardia delle mura* ‘for protection of the walls’ → “to defend the walls”: the noun expressing purpose becomes an infinitive, with ‘the walls’ as its direct object.
- *Più e più* ‘more and more’ → “successive”: the repeated expression of multiplicity becomes an adjective emphasizing succession.
- *li castelli* ‘the castles’ → “a castle”: the definite plural becomes an indefinite singular.

> 13 Tale immagine quivi facean quelli:  

such image these made here.

- *quivi* ‘there’ → “here”: the locative changes its point of reference and moves to the end of the clause.
- *facean quelli* ‘made those’ → “these made”: the demonstrative subject moves before the verb, and ‘those’ becomes “these.”

> 14 E come a tai fortezze da’ lor sogli  
> 15 Alla ripa di fuor son ponticelli;  

And as, from the thresholds of the fortress, there are bridges to the outward bank;

- *a tai fortezze da’ lor sogli* ‘at such fortresses from their thresholds’ → “from the thresholds of the fortress”: the separate phrase ‘at such fortresses’ is absorbed into a possessive construction replacing ‘their.’ The plural becomes singular, and ‘such’ is omitted.
- *Alla ripa di fuor* ‘to the bank on the outside’ → “to the outward bank”: the phrase ‘on the outside’ becomes an adjective before the noun, and the whole phrase moves after the verb and its subject.
- *son ponticelli* ‘are little bridges’ → “there are bridges”: English supplies existential “there,” while the diminutive force of *ponticelli* is not expressed.

> 16 Così da imo della roccia scogli  
> 17 Movien, che ricidean gli argini e i fossi,  
> 18 Infino al pozzo che i tronca e raccogli.  

so from the basis of the rock proceeded cliffs that crossed the embankments and the ditches, down to the well which truncates and collects them.

- *da imo della roccia* ‘from the bottom of the rock’ → “from the basis of the rock”: “basis” renders the substantival *imo*, ‘lowest part’ or ‘bottom.’
- *scogli Movien* ‘crags proceeded’ → “proceeded cliffs”: the subject moves after the verb, and “cliffs” renders the rocky masses denoted by *scogli*.
- *ricidean* ‘cut across’ → “crossed”: the translation retains the crossing but does not explicitly retain the sense of cutting.
- *Infino al pozzo* ‘as far as the well’ → “down to the well”: the expression of extent acquires an explicit downward direction.
- *i tronca e raccogli* ‘them truncates and collects’ → “truncates and collects them”: the shared object pronoun moves from before the first verb to after both verbs.

> 19 In questo luogo, dalla schiena scossi  
> 20 Di Gerion, trovammoci: e il Poeta  
> 21 Tenne a sinistra, ed io dietro mi mossi.  

In this place, shaken from the back of Geryon, we found ourselves; and the Poet kept to the left, and I moved behind.

- *dalla schiena scossi Di Gerion* ‘from the back shaken of Geryon’ → “shaken from the back of Geryon”: the participle moves before the prepositional phrase, bringing ‘of Geryon’ directly beside ‘the back.’
- *io dietro mi mossi* ‘I behind myself moved’ → “I moved behind”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an English intransitive verb without a reflexive pronoun, and the adverb moves after the verb.

## Lines 22–33 — Opposing Streams of Sinners

> 22 Alla man destra vidi nuova pieta;  
> 23 Nuovi tormenti e nuovi frustatori,  
> 24 Di che la prima bolgia era repleta.  

On the right hand I saw new misery, new torments, and new tormentors, wherewith the first chasm was filled.

- *Alla man destra* ‘at the hand right’ → “On the right hand”: the preposition changes, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *pieta* ‘pity’ → “misery”: Carlyle renders the suffering that occasions pity rather than the feeling itself.
- *frustatori* ‘scourgers’ → “tormentors”: the English uses a broader term, without specifying whipping.
- *Di che* ‘with which’ → “wherewith”: the preposition and relative pronoun become a single relative adverb.
- *bolgia* ‘pouch’ → “chasm”: the figurative Italian designation becomes a term for a deep opening.

> 25 Nel fondo erano ignudi i peccatori:  

In its bottom the sinners were naked:

- *Nel fondo* ‘in the bottom’ → “In its bottom”: the definite article becomes a possessive referring to the chasm.
- *erano ignudi i peccatori* ‘were naked the sinners’ → “the sinners were naked”: the subject moves before the verb and its complement.

> 26 Dal mezzo in qua ci venian verso il volto;  
> 27 Di là con noi, ma con passi maggiori:  

from the middle, on our side, they came facing us; and, on the other side, along with us, but with larger steps.

- *in qua* ‘hither’ → “on our side”: a directional expression becomes an expression of position, with an explicit possessive.
- *ci … verso il volto* ‘to us … towards the face’ → “facing us”: the Italian pronoun and prepositional phrase become a participial expression; the reference to the face is absorbed into the verb.
- *Di là* ‘on that side’ → “and, on the other side”: the demonstrative becomes a contrastive expression, and a coordinating conjunction is supplied.
- *con noi* ‘with us’ → “along with us”: “along” makes the accompanying movement explicit.
- *passi maggiori* ‘steps larger’ → “larger steps”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 28 Come i Roman, per l’ esercito molto,  
> 29 L’ anno del Giubbileo, su per lo ponte  
> 30 Hanno a passar la gente modo tolto;  

Thus the Romans, because of the great throng, in the year of Jubilee, upon the bridge have taken means to pass the people over;

- *Come* ‘as’ → “Thus”: the comparative conjunction becomes an adverb introducing the corresponding example.
- *per l’ esercito molto* ‘because of the host numerous’ → “because of the great throng”: the collective noun becomes “throng,” the adjective becomes “great,” and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *L’ anno del Giubbileo* ‘the year of the Jubilee’ → “in the year of Jubilee”: English supplies a preposition for the Italian expression of time and omits the article before “Jubilee”.
- *su per lo ponte* ‘up along the bridge’ → “upon the bridge”: the compound prepositional expression becomes a single preposition of position.
- *Hanno … modo tolto* ‘have … a method taken’ → “have taken means”: the participle moves beside the auxiliary and before the object; “means” renders the singular noun *modo*.
- *a passar la gente* ‘to pass the people’ → “to pass the people over”: the purpose phrase moves after the verb and object, and “over” is supplied to express crossing.

> 31 Che dall’ un lato tutti hanno la fronte  
> 32 Verso il castello, e vanno a santo Pietro,  

so that, on the one side, all have their faces toward the Castle, and go to St. Peter’s;

- *Che* ‘that’ → “so that”: the English makes the consequential force of the conjunction explicit.
- *dall’ un lato* ‘from the one side’ → “on the one side”: the Italian preposition is rendered as one of position rather than origin.
- *la fronte* ‘the forehead’ → “their faces”: the definite article becomes a possessive, the singular becomes plural, and the part named becomes the whole face.
- *a santo Pietro* ‘to Saint Peter’ → “to St. Peter’s”: the name becomes an English possessive used to designate the destination.

> 33 Dall’ altra sponda vanno verso il monte.  

at the other ledge, they go towards the Mount.

- *Dall’ altra sponda* ‘from the other bank’ → “at the other ledge”: the preposition becomes positional, and “ledge” replaces the Italian term for a bank or edge.

## Lines 34–50 — Demons Scourge and Dante Recognizes

> 34 Di qua, di là, su per lo sasso tetro  
> 35 Vidi Dimon cornuti con gran ferze,  
> 36 Che li battean crudelmente di retro.  

On this side, on that, along the hideous stone, I saw horned Demons with large scourges, who smote them fiercely from behind.

- *su per lo sasso tetro* ‘up along the dark stone’ → “along the hideous stone”: the upward direction of *su* is not expressed; “hideous” substitutes ugliness or horror for darkness, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Dimon cornuti* ‘Demons horned’ → “horned Demons”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *li battean* ‘them were beating’ → “smote them”: the object follows the verb, and the Italian imperfect becomes a simple past, without explicitly marking continued or repeated action.
- *crudelmente* ‘cruelly’ → “fiercely”: the emphasis shifts from cruelty to violence or intensity.

> 37 Ahi come facean lor levar le berze  
> 38 Alle prime percosse! E già nessuno  
> 39 Le seconde aspettava nè le terze.  

Ah! how they made them lift their legs at the first strokes! And truly none waited for the second or the third.

- *facean lor levar le berze* ‘made them raise the shanks’ → “they made them lift their legs”: the colloquial *berze* becomes the neutral “legs,” and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *già* ‘already’ → “truly”: Carlyle takes the adverb as emphatic rather than temporal.
- *Le seconde aspettava nè le terze* ‘the second [strokes] awaited nor the third’ → “waited for the second or the third”: the first object moves after the verb; transitive *aspettava* becomes “waited for”; and “or,” governed by the preceding “none,” replaces *nè*.

> 40 Mentr’ io andava, gli occhi miei in uno  
> 41 Furo scontrati; ed io sì tosto dissi:  
> 42 Già di veder costui non son digiuno.  

As I went on, my eyes were met by one, and instantly I said: “This one I have seen before.”

- *Mentr’ io andava* ‘While I was going’ → “As I went on”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and “on” is supplied.
- *gli occhi miei in uno / Furo scontrati* ‘the eyes my upon one were encountered’ → “my eyes were met by one”: the possessive precedes the noun, and the verb precedes the prepositional phrase. “By one” presents the person as the agent of the passive construction, rather than as someone upon whom Dante’s eyes encountered or lighted.
- *sì tosto* ‘so soon’ → “instantly”: the phrase becomes a single adverb expressing immediacy, placed before “I” rather than after it.
- *Già di veder costui non son digiuno* ‘Already of seeing this one I am not fasting’ → “This one I have seen before”: the negative metaphor becomes a positive statement of previous experience. The present *son* with its infinitival complement becomes a present perfect; *costui* becomes the opening object; and the temporal force of *Già* is expressed by the final “before.”

> 43 Perciò a figurarlo i piedi affissi;  
> 44 E il dolce Duca meco si ristette,  
> 45 E assentì ch’ alquanto indietro gissi.  

I therefore stayed my feet to recognize him; and the kind Guide stood still with me, and allowed me to go back a little.

- *a figurarlo* ‘to make out his features’ → “to recognize him”: Carlyle expresses identification rather than the scrutiny of features; the purpose phrase moves from before the main clause to after it.
- *Perciò ... i piedi affissi* ‘Therefore ... the feet I fixed’ → “I therefore stayed my feet”: the connective follows the English subject instead of opening the clause; “stayed” renders fixing or planting the feet as stopping them; the object follows the verb, and the definite article becomes “my.”
- *dolce Duca* ‘sweet Leader’ → “kind Guide”: “kind” replaces the affectionate ‘sweet,’ and “Guide” specifies the role more narrowly than ‘leader.’
- *meco si ristette* ‘with me stopped himself’ → “stood still with me”: the reflexive verb becomes an English phrase without a reflexive pronoun, and “with me” moves after it.
- *assentì ch’ alquanto indietro gissi* ‘assented that somewhat backward I should go’ → “allowed me to go back a little”: assent becomes permission; the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object-plus-infinitive construction; and the adverbial expressions move after “go.”

> 46 E quel frustato celar si credette  
> 47 Bassando il viso; ma poco gli valse,  
> 48 Ch’ io dissi: Tu che l’ occhio a terra gette,  

And that scourged spirit thought to hide himself, lowering his face; but little it availed him, for I said: “Thou, that dost cast thy eye upon the ground!

- *quel frustato* ‘that scourged one’ → “that scourged spirit”: “spirit” supplies a noun where Italian uses the participle substantivally.
- *celar si credette* ‘to hide himself believed’ → “thought to hide himself”: “thought” renders *credette*, and the governing verb moves before the infinitive and its reflexive pronoun.
- *Bassando il viso* ‘lowering the face’ → “lowering his face”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *gli valse* ‘to him it availed’ → “it availed him”: the preverbal indirect-object pronoun becomes an object following the verb.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *l’ occhio a terra gette* ‘the eye to earth thou castest’ → “dost cast thy eye upon the ground”: the verb moves before its object and directional phrase; the simple present becomes an auxiliary construction with “dost”; the definite article becomes “thy”; and *a terra* becomes “upon the ground,” with an English article supplied.

> 49 Se le fazion che porti non son false,  
> 50 Venedico sei tu Caccianimico.  

If the features which thou wearest be not false, thou art Venedico Caccianimico.

- *porti* ‘thou carriest’ → “thou wearest”: the verb is interpreted as wearing or bearing features rather than carrying them.
- *non son false* ‘not are false’ → “be not false”: the Italian indicative becomes an English subjunctive after “If,” and the negative follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *Venedico sei tu Caccianimico* ‘Venedico art thou Caccianimico’ → “thou art Venedico Caccianimico”: subject–verb order replaces verb–subject order, and the two parts of the name are brought together after the verb.

## Lines 51–61 — Confession and Fellow Bolognese

> 51 Ma che ti mena a sì pungenti salse?  

But what brings thee to such a biting pickle?”

- *che ti mena* ‘what thee leads’ → “what brings thee”: “brings” replaces ‘leads’, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *a sì pungenti salse* ‘to so stinging sauces’ → “to such a biting pickle”: plural ‘sauces’ becomes singular “pickle”; ‘stinging’ becomes “biting”; and the degree adverb ‘so’ becomes “such a” before the singular noun phrase.

> 52 Ed egli a me: Mal volentier lo dico;  

And he to me, “Unwillingly I tell it;

- *Mal volentier* ‘ill willingly’ → “Unwillingly”: the two-word expression becomes a single negative adverb.
- *lo dico* ‘it I say’ → “I tell it”: ‘say’ becomes “tell”, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 53 Ma sforzami la tua chiara favella,  
> 54 Che mi fa sovvenir del mondo antico.  

but thy clear speech, that makes me recollect the former world, compels me.

- *sforzami la tua chiara favella* ‘compels me thy clear speech’ → “thy clear speech” … “compels me”: the subject moves before the verb, with its relative clause now intervening between subject and verb.
- *mi fa sovvenir del mondo antico* ‘me makes remember of the world ancient’ → “makes me recollect the former world”: the pronoun follows “makes”; the Italian construction with ‘of’ becomes the directly transitive “recollect”; and *antico* becomes “former” rather than ‘ancient’, with the adjective placed before the noun.

> 55 Io fui colui, che la Ghisola bella  
> 56 Condussi a far la voglia del Marchese,  
> 57 Come che suoni la sconcia novella.  

It was I who led the fair Ghisola to do the Marquis’ will, however the unseemly tale may sound.

- *Io fui colui, che* ‘I was the one who’ → “It was I who”: the identifying construction becomes an English cleft, introducing “It” and leaving ‘the one’ without a separate equivalent.
- *la Ghisola bella Condussi* ‘the Ghisola fair I led’ → “led the fair Ghisola”: the object moves after the verb, and the adjective moves before the name.
- *la voglia del Marchese* ‘the will of the Marquis’ → “the Marquis’ will”: the following *di*-phrase becomes a possessive placed before the noun.
- *suoni la sconcia novella* ‘sounds the unseemly tale’ → “the unseemly tale may sound”: the subject moves before the verb, and the Italian present subjunctive is expressed with “may”.

> 58 E non pur io qui piango Bolognese;  

And I am not the only Bolognese that weeps here:

- *non pur io qui piango Bolognese* ‘not only I here weep, Bolognese’ → “I am not the only Bolognese that weeps here”: the main assertion ‘I weep’ becomes “I am”, with “the only Bolognese” as its complement; ‘weep’ becomes the third-person verb of an added relative clause, “that weeps”; and “here” moves after that verb.

> 59 Anzi n’ è questo luogo tanto pieno,  
> 60 Che tante lingue non son ora apprese  
> 61 A dicer sipa tra Savena e il Reno:  

nay, this place is so filled with us, that as many tongues are not now taught to say Sipa between Savena and the Reno.

- *n’ è questo luogo tanto pieno* ‘of them is this place so full’ → “this place is so filled with us”: the subject moves before the verb; ‘full’ becomes “filled”; and *ne*, referring to Bolognese people, becomes “with us”, explicitly including the speaker and moving after “filled”.
- *tante* ‘so many’ → “as many”: the Italian expression of quantity becomes an explicit comparative expression.
- *non son* ‘not are’ → “are not”: the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.

## Lines 62–72 — The Lash and the Ascent

> 62 E se di ciò vuoi fede o testimonio,  
> 63 Recati a mente il nostro avaro seno.  

And if thou desirest assurance and testimony thereof, recall to thy memory our avaricious heart.”

- *di ciò* ‘of this’ → “thereof”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb and moves from before the verb to after “testimony”.
- *fede* ‘faith’ → “assurance”: the translation selects the sense of confirmation or certainty.
- *o* ‘or’ → “and”: alternatives become coordinated requirements.
- *Recati a mente* ‘bring to thyself to mind’ → “recall to thy memory”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes a verb of remembering, with the personal reference expressed through “thy”.
- *seno* ‘bosom’ → “heart”: one figurative bodily term replaces another.

> 64 Così parlando il percosse un Demonio  
> 65 Della sua scuriada, e disse: Via,  
> 66 Ruffian, qui non son femmine da conio.  

And as he thus spake, a Demon smote him with his lash, and said: “Away! Ruffian, there are no women here to coin.”

- *Così parlando* ‘thus speaking’ → “And as he thus spake”: the gerund becomes a finite temporal clause, and “And” is supplied.
- *il percosse un Demonio* ‘him smote a Demon’ → “a Demon smote him”: the postposed subject moves before the verb, and the object pronoun moves after it.
- *Della sua scuriada* ‘of his lash’ → “with his lash”: instrumental *di* is expressed by English “with”.
- *qui non son femmine da conio* ‘here are not women for coinage’ → “there are no women here to coin”: English supplies existential “there”, moves “here” after the noun, and renders verbal negation by “no” before “women”. The noun phrase *da conio* becomes the infinitive “to coin”, with “women” understood as its object.

> 67 Io mi raggiunsi con la Scorta mia:  

I rejoined my Escort.

- *mi raggiunsi con* ‘rejoined myself with’ → “rejoined”: the reflexive verb and its prepositional complement become a simple transitive construction.
- *la Scorta mia* ‘the Escort my’ → “my Escort”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 68 Poscia con pochi passi divenimmo,  
> 69 Dove uno scoglio della ripa uscia.  

Then, with a few steps, we came to where a cliff proceeded from the bank.

- *con pochi passi* ‘with few steps’ → “with a few steps”: “a” is supplied before “few”.
- *Dove* ‘where’ → “to where”: English supplies a directional preposition.
- *uno scoglio della ripa uscia* ‘a crag from the bank came out’ → “a cliff proceeded from the bank”: *scoglio* is rendered as “cliff”, and *uscia* as “proceeded” rather than ‘came out’. The phrase expressing origin moves after the verb.

> 70 Assai leggieramente quel salimmo,  
> 71 E volti a destra su per la sua scheggia,  
> 72 Da quelle cerchie eterne ci partimmo.  

This we very easily ascended; and, turning to the right upon its jagged ridge, we quitted those eternal circles.

- *Assai leggieramente quel salimmo* ‘very lightly that we ascended’ → “This we very easily ascended”: “easily” expresses ease rather than lightness; the demonstrative changes from ‘that’ to “This”; and the adverbial phrase moves from before the object to immediately before the verb.
- *E volti* ‘and turned’ → “and, turning”: the past participle becomes a present participle.
- *su per la sua scheggia* ‘up along its crag’ → “upon its jagged ridge”: directional *su per* becomes “upon”, while *scheggia* is expanded into “jagged ridge”.
- *Da quelle cerchie eterne ci partimmo* ‘from those circles eternal we departed’ → “we quitted those eternal circles”: the pronominal verb with *da* becomes the transitive “quitted”. The fronted prepositional phrase becomes an object following the verb, and the adjective moves before its noun.

## Lines 73–85 — Watching the Oncoming Spirits

> 73 Quando noi fummo là, dov’ ei vaneggia  
> 74 Di sotto, per dar passo agli sferzati,  

When we reached the part where it yawns beneath to leave a passage for the scourged,

- *Quando noi fummo là* ‘When we were there’ → “When we reached the part”: being at a place becomes reaching it, and the adverb *là* becomes the noun phrase “the part”.
- *dov’ ei vaneggia Di sotto* ‘where it is hollow below’ → “where it yawns beneath”: the condition of being hollow is expressed through the image of yawning.
- *per dar passo agli sferzati* ‘to give passage to the scourged’ → “to leave a passage for the scourged”: giving passage becomes leaving a passage; an indefinite article is supplied, and “for” replaces ‘to’.

> 75 Lo Duca disse: Attienti, e fa che feggia  
> 76 Lo viso in te di questi altri mal nati,  
> 77 A’ quali ancor non vedesti la faccia,  
> 78 Perocchè son con noi insieme andati.  

my Guide said: “Stay, and let the look strike on thee of these other ill-born spirits, whose faces thou hast not yet seen, for they have gone along with us.”

- *Lo Duca disse* ‘The Leader said’ → “my Guide said”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and *Duca* is rendered as “Guide” rather than ‘Leader’.
- *Attienti* ‘Hold thyself’ → “Stay”: the reflexive imperative becomes an intransitive command to remain in place.
- *fa che feggia Lo viso in te* ‘cause that the look strike in thee’ → “let the look strike on thee”: the causative construction with a subjunctive becomes “let” with an infinitive; the subject “the look” moves before “strike”, and “on” replaces ‘in’.
- *di questi altri mal nati* ‘of these other ill-born’ → “of these other ill-born spirits”: “spirits” is supplied as the noun for the substantivized *mal nati*.
- *A’ quali ancor non vedesti la faccia* ‘to whom thou didst not yet see the face’ → “whose faces thou hast not yet seen”: the relative construction becomes possessive; singular *faccia* becomes plural “faces”; the simple past becomes a present perfect; and the object moves before the subject and verb.
- *Perocchè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *son con noi insieme andati* ‘they have with us together gone’ → “they have gone along with us”: “gone” moves next to its auxiliary, and *insieme* is expressed by “along” in the phrase “along with us”.

> 79 Dal vecchio ponte guardavam la traccia,  
> 80 Che venia verso noi dall’ altra banda,  
> 81 E che la ferza similmente scaccia.  

From the ancient bridge we viewed the train, who were coming towards us, on the other side, chased likewise by the scourge.

- *vecchio* ‘old’ → “ancient”: the translation gives the adjective a stronger implication of age.
- *guardavam* ‘we were watching’ → “we viewed”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by a simple past rather than an explicitly continuing action.
- *la traccia, Che venia* ‘the line, which was coming’ → “the train, who were coming”: “train” renders *traccia* as a procession; the relative becomes personal, and the singular verb becomes plural, agreeing with the people collectively denoted.
- *dall’ altra banda* ‘from the other side’ → “on the other side”: a phrase indicating origin becomes one indicating location.
- *E che la ferza similmente scaccia* ‘and which the scourge likewise drives off’ → “chased likewise by the scourge”: the coordinated active relative clause becomes a passive participial phrase, with ‘and’ omitted; the scourge changes from grammatical subject to agent, and ‘drives off’ becomes “chased”.

> 82 Il buon Maestro, senza mia dimanda,  
> 83 Mi disse: Guarda quel grande che viene,  
> 84 E per dolor non par lagrima spanda.  

The kind Master, without my asking, said to me: “Look at that great soul who comes, and seems to shed no tear for pain.

- *buon* ‘good’ → “kind”: the adjective is given the more specific sense of kindness.
- *senza mia dimanda* ‘without my question’ → “without my asking”: the noun naming a question or request becomes a gerund naming the act of asking.
- *Mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the preverbal indirect-object pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase after the verb.
- *quel grande* ‘that great one’ → “that great soul”: “soul” supplies a specific noun for the substantivized adjective.
- *E per dolor non par lagrima spanda* ‘and for pain it does not seem that he sheds a tear’ → “and seems to shed no tear for pain”: the impersonal construction with a subjunctive clause becomes a personal verb with an infinitive; negation moves from ‘does not seem’ to “no tear”, and the phrase expressing cause moves to the end.

> 85 Quanto aspetto reale ancor ritiene!  

What a regal aspect he yet retains!

- *Quanto aspetto reale* ‘How much aspect royal’ → “What a regal aspect”: the quantitative exclamation becomes an English “What a” construction, and the adjective moves before its noun.

## Lines 86–99 — Jason’s Deceit and Punishment

> 86 Quelli è Jason, che per cuore e per senno  
> 87 Li Colchi del monton privati fene.  

That is Jason, who, by courage and by counsel, bereft the Colchians of the ram.

- *per cuore e per senno* ‘by heart and by judgment’ → “by courage and by counsel”: “courage” renders *cuore* figuratively, while “counsel” gives *senno* the sense of deliberation or practical wisdom.
- *Li Colchi del monton privati fene* ‘the Colchians of the ram deprived he made’ → “bereft the Colchians of the ram”: the construction ‘made deprived’ becomes the single verb “bereft”; the verb precedes its object, and “of the ram” follows it.

> 88 Ello passò per l’ isola di Lenno,  
> 89 Poi che le ardite femmine spietate  
> 90 Tutti li maschi loro a morte dienno.  

He passed, by the isle of Lemnos, after the bold merciless women had given all their males to death.

- *per l’ isola di Lenno* ‘through the island of Lemnos’ → “by the isle of Lemnos”: “by” presents the island as a place passed on the journey rather than one traversed.
- *ardite femmine spietate* ‘bold women merciless’ → “bold merciless women”: the postposed adjective *spietate* moves before the noun.
- *Tutti li maschi loro a morte dienno* ‘all the males their to death gave’ → “had given all their males to death”: the simple past becomes a past perfect; the verb moves before its object, and the possessive moves before “males”.

> 91 Ivi, con segni e con parole ornate,  
> 92 Isifile ingannò la giovinetta,  
> 93 Che prima l’ altre avea tutte ingannate.  

There, with tokens and fair words, did he deceive the young Hypsipyle, who had before deceived all the rest.

- *con segni e con parole ornate* ‘with signs and with words adorned’ → “with tokens and fair words”: “tokens” interprets the signs as expressive gestures or indications; the second *con* is omitted, and “fair” replaces ‘adorned’ and precedes its noun.
- *Isifile ingannò la giovinetta* ‘Hypsipyle he deceived, the young girl’ → “did he deceive the young Hypsipyle”: the object moves after the verb; the appositional noun *la giovinetta* becomes the adjective “young”, leaving ‘girl’ unexpressed. The simple verb is expanded into the inverted auxiliary construction “did he deceive”.
- *prima l’ altre avea tutte ingannate* ‘before the others she had all deceived’ → “had before deceived all the rest”: “before” moves after the auxiliary, and the object and its quantifier are brought together after the verb. The feminine ‘others’ becomes the gender-neutral “rest”.

> 94 Lasciolla quivi gravida e soletta:  

He left her there pregnant and forlorn.

- *soletta* ‘quite alone’ → “forlorn”: the diminutive’s emphasis on being alone becomes an expression of abandonment and distress.

> 95 Tal colpa a tal martiro lui condanna;  

Such guilt condemns him to such torment;

- *Tal colpa a tal martiro lui condanna* ‘such guilt to such torment him condemns’ → “Such guilt condemns him to such torment”: the verb moves immediately after the subject, followed by its object and then the phrase specifying the punishment.

> 96 E anche di Medea si fa vendetta.  

and also for Medea vengeance is taken.

- *di Medea* ‘of Medea’ → “for Medea”: the relation expressed by *di* is interpreted as vengeance on Medea’s behalf.
- *si fa vendetta* ‘vengeance is made’ → “vengeance is taken”: the Italian passive construction becomes an explicit English passive, with “taken” replacing ‘made’; “vengeance” moves before the verb.

> 97 Con lui sen va chi da tal parte inganna:  

With him go all who practise the like deceit.

- *sen va chi* ‘goes away whoever’ → “go all who”: the singular indefinite relative becomes the plural “all who”, with a corresponding plural verb; the departure expressed by *ne* is not separately rendered.
- *da tal parte inganna* ‘in such a respect deceives’ → “practise the like deceit”: the verb ‘deceives’ becomes the verb-and-noun expression “practise … deceit”, while the phrase specifying the respect in which someone deceives becomes the adjectival “like”.

> 98 E questo basti della prima valle  
> 99 Sapere, e di color che in sè assanna.  

And let this suffice to know respecting the first valley, and those whom it devours.”

- *questo basti* ‘this suffice’ → “let this suffice”: the independent subjunctive is expressed through “let”.
- *della prima valle Sapere* ‘of the first valley to know’ → “to know respecting the first valley”: the infinitive moves before the phrase dependent on it, and “respecting” replaces ‘of’.
- *e di color* ‘and of those’ → “and those”: the repeated preposition *di* is omitted; “respecting” governs both coordinated phrases.
- *che in sè assanna* ‘whom within itself it grips with its fangs’ → “whom it devours”: “devours” replaces the specific action of gripping with fangs, and *in sè* is left unexpressed.

## Lines 100–114 — The Filth of the Second Bolgia

> 100 Già eravam là ’ve lo stretto calle  
> 101 Con l’ argine secondo s’ incrocicchia,  
> 102 E fa di quello ad un altr’ arco spalle.  

We had already come to where the narrow pathway crosses the second bank, and makes of it a buttress for another arch.

- *Già eravam là ’ve* ‘already we were there where’ → “We had already come to where”: the imperfect of being becomes the past perfect of coming; location becomes the destination of an arrival, and “already” moves between the auxiliary and participle.
- *Con l’argine secondo s’incrocicchia* ‘with the bank second intersects’ → “crosses the second bank”: the pronominal verb with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object. The verb precedes its complement, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *E fa di quello ad un altr’arco spalle* ‘and makes of that for another arch shoulders’ → “and makes of it a buttress for another arch”: the demonstrative becomes “it”; plural ‘shoulders’ becomes singular “a buttress”, rendering the architectural function rather than the bodily image. The phrase specifying the other arch moves after “a buttress”.

> 103 Quindi sentimmo gente che si nicchia  
> 104 Nell’ altra bolgia, e che col muso sbuffa,  
> 105 E sè medesma con le palme picchia.  

Here we heard people whining in the other chasm, and puffing with mouth and nostrils, and knocking on themselves with their palms.

- *Quindi* ‘from there’ → “Here”: a phrase indicating the point from which something is heard becomes a simple indication of location.
- *che si nicchia* ‘that whines’ → “whining”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial modifier.
- *bolgia* ‘pouch’ → “chasm”: the translation substitutes a description of the cavity for the Italian noun’s literal image.
- *e che col muso sbuffa* ‘and that with the muzzle puffs’ → “and puffing with mouth and nostrils”: the finite relative clause becomes another participial modifier, and the verb moves before the accompanying phrase. “Mouth and nostrils” expands and anatomically specifies ‘muzzle’, while omitting its definite article.
- *E sè medesma con le palme picchia* ‘and itself with the palms beats’ → “and knocking on themselves with their palms”: the finite verb becomes a participle and moves before its complements. Direct beating becomes “knocking on”; the singular reflexive agreeing with *gente* becomes plural “themselves”, and the definite article before ‘palms’ becomes the possessive “their”.

> 106 Le ripe eran grommate d’ una muffa,  
> 107 Per l’ alito di giù che vi si appasta,  
> 108 Che con gli occhi e col naso facea zuffa.  

The banks were crusted over with a mould from the vapour below, which concretes upon them, which did battle with the eyes and with the nose.

- *Per l’alito di giù* ‘because of the breath from below’ → “from the vapour below”: the causal preposition becomes “from”, and ‘breath’ becomes “vapour”. The Italian phrase indicating origin below becomes a modifier locating the vapour below.
- *che vi si appasta* ‘which there pastes itself’ → “which concretes upon them”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb expressing solidification. The locative *vi* is expanded into “upon them”, explicitly referring to the banks.
- *Che con gli occhi e col naso facea zuffa* ‘which with the eyes and with the nose made a brawl’ → “which did battle with the eyes and with the nose”: the idiom ‘made a brawl’ becomes “did battle”, and the verb phrase moves before the phrases naming its opponents.

> 109 Lo fondo è cupo sì, che non ci basta  
> 110 Luogo a veder senza montare al dosso  
> 111 Dell’ arco, ove lo scoglio più sovrasta.  

The bottom is so deep, that we could see it nowhere without mounting to the ridge of the arch, where the cliff stands highest.

- *è cupo sì* ‘is deep so’ → “is so deep”: the degree word moves before the adjective.
- *che non ci basta Luogo a veder* ‘that no place suffices for us to see’ → “that we could see it nowhere”: a construction with ‘place’ as subject and ‘us’ as beneficiary becomes one with “we” as subject and “nowhere” as an adverb. Present-tense sufficiency becomes past-tense modal ability, and “it” is supplied as the object of seeing.
- *senza montare* ‘without to mount’ → “without mounting”: the Italian infinitive after a preposition becomes the English *-ing* form.
- *al dosso* ‘to the back’ → “to the ridge”: the bodily term is replaced by a topographical one.
- *lo scoglio* ‘the rock’ → “the cliff”: the translation gives the rock a more specific topographical designation.
- *più sovrasta* ‘most towers above’ → “stands highest”: the verb expressing elevation above something becomes “stands”, while its implication of height is transferred to the superlative “highest”, placed after the verb.

> 112 Quivi venimmo, e quindi giù nel fosso  
> 113 Vidi gente attuffata in uno sterco,  
> 114 Che dagli uman privati parea mosso:  

We got upon it; and then, in the ditch beneath, I saw a people dipped in excrement, that seemed as it had flowed from human privies.

- *Quivi venimmo* ‘there we came’ → “We got upon it”: ‘came’ becomes “got”, and the initial place adverb becomes a following prepositional phrase specifying movement onto the arch.
- *e quindi* ‘and from there’ → “and then”: the translation takes *quindi* temporally rather than spatially.
- *giù nel fosso* ‘down in the ditch’ → “in the ditch beneath”: the initial directional adverb becomes “beneath”, following and locating the ditch.
- *gente* ‘people’ → “a people”: an indefinite article is supplied, presenting the people as a collective body.
- *in uno sterco* ‘in an excrement’ → “in excrement”: the indefinite article is omitted, treating “excrement” as an English mass noun.
- *Che dagli uman privati parea mosso* ‘that from the human privies seemed moved’ → “that seemed as it had flowed from human privies”: the origin phrase moves after the verb construction, and its definite article is omitted. Seeming followed by a passive participle becomes “seemed as” followed by an explicit subject and an active past-perfect verb; ‘moved’ is interpreted more specifically as “flowed”.

## Lines 115–124 — Recognizing Alessio in the Filth

> 115 E mentre ch’ io là giù con l’ occhio cerco,  
> 116 Vidi un col capo sì di merda lordo,  
> 117 Che non parea s’ era laico o cherco.  

And whilst I was searching with my eyes, down amongst it, I beheld one with a head so smeared in filth, that it did not appear whether he was layman or clerk.

- *là giù* ‘down there’ → “down amongst it”: the simple indication of place becomes a phrase specifying position within something; it also moves after the phrase about searching with the eyes.
- *con l’occhio* ‘with the eye’ → “with my eyes”: the singular becomes plural, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *cerco* ‘I search’ → “was searching”: the present tense becomes a past progressive.
- *col capo* ‘with the head’ → “with a head”: the definite article becomes indefinite.
- *sì di merda lordo* ‘so with shit befouled’ → “so smeared in filth”: “filth” softens the explicit noun; “smeared” gives a more specific description than ‘befouled’, and the phrase naming the substance follows rather than precedes it.

> 118 Quei mi sgridò: Perchè sei tu sì ingordo  
> 119 Di riguardar più me che gli altri brutti?  

He bawled to me: “Why art thou so eager in gazing at me, more than the others in their nastiness?”

- *Quei* ‘that one’ → “He”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *mi sgridò* ‘me he scolded’ → “bawled to me”: the verb of reprimand becomes one describing loud shouting; the preceding direct-object pronoun becomes a following prepositional phrase.
- *ingordo* ‘greedy’ → “eager”: the translation uses a less strongly disparaging adjective.
- *Di riguardar* ‘to gaze at’ → “in gazing at”: the infinitive construction becomes a preposition followed by a gerund.
- *più me* ‘more me’ → “me, more”: the object precedes rather than follows the comparative adverb.
- *gli altri brutti* ‘the other filthy ones’ → “the others in their nastiness”: the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase containing an abstract noun and a supplied possessive.

> 120 Ed io a lui: Perchè, se ben ricordo,  
> 121 Già t’ ho veduto coi capelli asciutti,  
> 122 E sei Alessio Interminei da Lucca:  
> 123 Però t’ adocchio più che gli altri tutti.  

And I to him: “Because, if I rightly recollect, I have seen thee before with thy hair dry: and thou art Alessio Interminei of Lucca. Therefore do I eye thee more than all the rest.”

- *Già* ‘already’ → “before”: the adverb is rendered as a reference to an earlier occasion and moves from before the verb to after its object.
- *t’ho veduto* ‘thee I have seen’ → “I have seen thee”: the object pronoun follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *coi capelli asciutti* ‘with the hairs dry’ → “with thy hair dry”: the plural count noun becomes an English singular mass noun, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Però t’adocchio* ‘therefore thee I eye’ → “Therefore do I eye thee”: the translation supplies the auxiliary “do”, places it before the subject, and moves the object pronoun after the main verb.
- *gli altri tutti* ‘the others all’ → “all the rest”: the quantifier moves before the noun phrase, and “the rest” replaces ‘the others’.

> 124 Ed egli allor, battendosi la zucca:  

And he then, beating his pate:

- *battendosi la zucca* ‘beating himself the gourd’ → “beating his pate”: the reflexive pronoun and definite article are expressed through the possessive “his”; the figurative ‘gourd’ becomes “pate”, retaining a colloquial word for the head without retaining the vegetable metaphor.

## Lines 125–136 — Flattery and Thais

> 125 Quaggiù m’ hanno sommerso le lusinghe,  
> 126 Ond’ io non ebbi mai la lingua stucca.  

“Down to this, the flatteries wherewith my tongue was never weary have sunk me!”

- *Quaggiù* ‘down here’ → “Down to this”: a location becomes the condition or depth to which the speaker has sunk.
- *m’ hanno sommerso le lusinghe* ‘me have submerged the flatteries’ → “the flatteries” … “have sunk me”: the subject moves before the verb, and the object pronoun moves after it.
- *Ond’ io non ebbi mai la lingua stucca* ‘with which I never had my tongue cloyed’ → “wherewith my tongue was never weary”: the construction with ‘I’ possessing a tongue in a particular state becomes one with “my tongue” as subject; ‘cloyed’ or ‘sated’ becomes “weary”.

> 127 Appresso ciò lo Duca: Fa che pinghe,  
> 128 Mi disse, un poco il viso più avante,  
> 129 Sì che la faccia ben con gli occhi attinghe  

Thereupon my Guide said to me: “Stretch thy face a little forwards, that thy eyes may fully reach the visage

- *Appresso ciò* ‘after this’ → “Thereupon”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *lo Duca … Mi disse* ‘the Leader … said to me’ → “my Guide said to me”: the article becomes a possessive, ‘Leader’ becomes “Guide”, and the reporting verb moves from within the speech to before it.
- *Fa che pinghe* ‘see to it that you push’ → “Stretch”: an imperative followed by a subordinate subjunctive becomes a single imperative, with ‘push’ rendered as “Stretch”.
- *un poco il viso più avante* ‘a little the face further forward’ → “thy face a little forwards”: the object precedes the adverbial phrase, the article becomes a possessive, and the explicit comparative *più* is omitted.
- *Sì che* ‘so that’ → “that”: the explicit ‘so’ is omitted.
- *la faccia ben con gli occhi attinghe* ‘the face well with the eyes you may reach’ → “thy eyes may fully reach the visage”: the instrumental phrase ‘with the eyes’ becomes the subject, with a possessive supplied; the original object moves after the verb, and the subjunctive is expressed with “may”.

> 130 Di quella sozza scapigliata fante,  
> 131 Che là si graffia con l’ unghie merdose,  
> 132 Ed or s’ accoscia, ed ora è in piede stante.  

of that unclean, dishevelled strumpet, who yonder with her filthy nails scratches herself, now cowering low, now standing on her feet.

- *fante* ‘serving-girl’ → “strumpet”: the translation gives the word a specifically sexual designation rather than its ordinary occupational sense.
- *Che là si graffia con l’ unghie merdose* ‘who there scratches herself with the shit-covered nails’ → “who yonder with her filthy nails scratches herself”: the instrumental phrase moves before the verb, the article becomes “her”, and “filthy” softens the explicitly excremental adjective.
- *Ed or s’ accoscia* ‘and now she squats’ → “now cowering low”: the conjunction is omitted, the finite reflexive verb becomes a participle, and squatting becomes “cowering low”.
- *ed ora è in piede stante* ‘and now she is standing on foot’ → “now standing on her feet”: the conjunction and finite copula are omitted; singular *piede* becomes plural “feet”, with a possessive supplied.

> 133 Taida è, la puttana che rispose  
> 134 Al drudo suo, quando disse: Ho io grazie  
> 135 Grandi appo te? Anzi meravigliose.  

It is Thais, the harlot, who answered her paramour, when he said: ‘Dost thou thank me much?’ ‘Nay, wondrously.’

- *Taida è* ‘Thais she is’ → “It is Thais”: the identifying name moves after the copula, and the implied personal subject becomes identifying “It”.
- *rispose Al drudo suo* ‘replied to the paramour her’ → “answered her paramour”: a verb followed by a prepositional complement becomes a directly transitive verb, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *Ho io grazie Grandi appo te* ‘Have I great thanks with you’ → “Dost thou thank me much”: possessing thanks becomes receiving the action of thanking; ‘I’ becomes the object “me”, ‘you’ becomes the subject “thou”, and the adjective ‘great’ becomes the adverb “much”.
- *Anzi meravigliose* ‘rather, marvellous [thanks]’ → “Nay, wondrously”: the corrective ‘rather’ becomes “Nay”, and the feminine plural adjective, referring to *grazie*, becomes an adverb modifying the implied act of thanking.

> 136 E quinci sien le nostre viste sazie.  

And herewith let our view rest sated.”

- *quinci* ‘from here’ → “herewith”: the translation takes the expression as ‘with this’ rather than as a point of departure.
- *sien le nostre viste sazie* ‘be our sights sated’ → “let our view rest sated”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”, the subject moves before the lexical verb, plural ‘sights’ becomes singular “view”, and ‘be’ becomes “rest”.

## Summary — Close Correspondence, Reshaped English

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s sequence of details, often preserving its repetitions, images, and unusual expressions, while rearranging its syntax for English. Adjectives generally move before nouns, object pronouns follow verbs, and subjects precede them. Separated elements are brought together: *Venedico sei tu Caccianimico* becomes “thou art Venedico Caccianimico”. Yet the translation does not consistently impose ordinary English order: “whose structure its own place shall tell” retains a fronted object, and “proceeded cliffs” retains an inverted arrangement.

Changes of construction frequently go beyond rearrangement. Italian reflexive or pronominal verbs become simple English verbs, as *mi raggiunsi con* becomes “rejoined”; finite relative clauses become participial phrases, as *che si nicchia* becomes “whining”. Elsewhere Carlyle supplies a new grammatical framework: *Io fui colui, che* becomes “It was I who”, while *Ho io grazie Grandi appo te?* becomes “Dost thou thank me much?”, changing possession into an action and exchanging the participants’ grammatical roles. Tense and aspect also shift: *cerco* becomes “was searching”, and *Già eravam* becomes “We had already come”. More routinely, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits the Italian articles before possessives, as in *la Scorta mia*, “my Escort”.

Word choice sometimes clarifies an implied meaning, sometimes changes the image or its force. *frustatori*, ‘scourgers’, becomes the broader “tormentors”; *seno*, ‘bosom’, becomes “heart”; and architectural *spalle*, ‘shoulders’, becomes “a buttress”. The negative metaphor *non son digiuno*, ‘I am not fasting’, disappears into “I have seen before”. Coarse expressions are occasionally softened: *merda* becomes “filth”, and *unghie merdose* becomes “filthy nails”. But other physical and figurative language remains conspicuous, including “yawns”, “biting pickle”, “women here to coin”, and “did battle with the eyes and with the nose”.

Small additions and omissions likewise recur. Carlyle supplies nouns for substantivized adjectives or participles—“spirit”, “spirits”, and “soul”—and often replaces definite articles with possessives, particularly for body parts. Direction or movement may be made explicit through “down”, “along”, or “over”; conversely, the diminutive in *ponticelli* and the phrase *in sè* accompanying *assanna* receive no separate equivalent. Such adjustments make the English more explicit or more idiomatic without generally compressing the passage. The result remains closely tied to the Italian’s verbal texture, though its local constructions and shades of meaning are repeatedly recast.
