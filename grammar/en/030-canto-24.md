# Inferno 24

## Lines 1–11 — Frost and the Peasant’s Lament

> 1 In quella parte del giovinetto anno,  
> 2 Che il Sole i crin sotto l’ Aquario tempra,  
> 3 E già le notti al mezzo dì sen vanno;  

In that part of the youthful year, when the Sun tempers his locks beneath Aquarius, and the nights already wane towards half the day;

- *Che* ‘in which’ → “when”: the temporal relative construction becomes a temporal conjunction.
- *il Sole i crin sotto l’ Aquario tempra* ‘the Sun the locks beneath Aquarius tempers’ → “the Sun tempers his locks beneath Aquarius”: the verb moves before its object and the prepositional phrase; the definite article before *crin* becomes the possessive “his”.
- *già le notti* ‘already the nights’ → “the nights already”: the adverb moves after the subject.
- *al mezzo dì sen vanno* ‘to the half-day go away’ → “wane towards half the day”: the pronominal expression *sen vanno* is rendered by “wane”, specifying diminution rather than departure; the verb moves before the prepositional phrase. *Mezzo dì* is interpreted as half the day rather than midday.

> 4 Quando la brina in su la terra assempra  
> 5 L’ immagine di sua sorella bianca,  
> 6 Ma poco dura alla sua penna tempra;  

when the hoar-frost copies his white sister’s image on the ground, but short while lasts the temper of his plumes:

- *in su la terra* ‘upon the earth’ → “on the ground”: the prepositional phrase moves from before the verb to after its object, and *terra* is rendered as “ground”.
- *L’ immagine di sua sorella bianca* ‘the image of her sister white’ → “his white sister’s image”: the *di* construction becomes a possessive preceding “image”, and the adjective moves before “sister”. The feminine personification associated with *brina* becomes masculine in “his”.
- *poco* ‘little’ → “short while”: an adverb expressing duration becomes a temporal noun phrase.
- *alla sua penna tempra* ‘to her pen temper’ → “the temper of his plumes”: the possessive relationship expressed by *a* becomes an “of” construction, with “temper” placed first and given an article. Singular *penna*, ‘pen’ or ‘quill’, becomes plural “plumes”, choosing feathers rather than a writing implement; “his” again makes the personification masculine.

> 7 Lo villanello, a cui la roba manca,  
> 8 Si leva e guarda, e vede la campagna  
> 9 Biancheggiar tutta, ond’ ei si batte l’ anca;  

the peasant, whose fodder fails, rises, and looks, and sees the fields all white; whereat he smites his thigh,

- *Lo villanello* ‘the little peasant’ → “the peasant”: the diminutive force of *villanello* is not separately expressed.
- *a cui la roba manca* ‘to whom possessions are lacking’ → “whose fodder fails”: the dative relative construction becomes a possessive relative. The broad *roba*, ‘possessions’ or ‘supplies’, is specifically interpreted as “fodder”, and ‘is lacking’ becomes “fails”.
- *Si leva* ‘raises himself’ → “rises”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *la campagna Biancheggiar tutta* ‘the countryside be white all’ → “the fields all white”: the singular collective becomes plural “fields”; the infinitive *biancheggiar* becomes the adjective “white”, and *tutta* moves before it as “all”.
- *ond’* ‘whence’ → “whereat”: the connective is rendered as ‘at which’, expressing the occasion for the reaction rather than retaining its literal ‘from which’ form.
- *ei si batte l’ anca* ‘he strikes himself the hip’ → “he smites his thigh”: the reflexive construction and definite article become a possessive identifying the body part; *anca*, ‘hip’, is rendered as “thigh”.

> 10 Ritorna a casa, e qua e là si lagna,  
> 11 Come il tapin che non sa che si faccia;  

goes back into the house, and to and fro laments like a poor wight who knows not what to do;

- *Ritorna a casa* ‘returns home’ → “goes back into the house”: the single verb becomes “goes back”, while “into the house” makes entry explicit rather than simply giving home as the destination.
- *qua e là si lagna* ‘here and there he complains’ → “to and fro laments”: the locative expression becomes a directional one, and the Italian pronominal verb is rendered by the simple intransitive “laments”.
- *Come il tapin* ‘like the wretched one’ → “like a poor wight”: the definite article becomes indefinite, and the substantival adjective becomes an adjective modifying an explicit noun.
- *non sa* ‘not knows’ → “knows not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *che si faccia* ‘what he should do for himself’ → “what to do”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an infinitival construction, with no separate expression of *si*.

## Lines 12–26 — Hope Restored and Help Given

> 12 Poi riede, e la speranza ringavagna,  
> 13 Veggendo il mondo aver cangiata faccia  
> 14 In poco d’ ora: e prende suo vincastro,  
> 15 E fuor le pecorelle a pascer caccia:  

then comes out again, and recovers hope, observing how the world has changed its face in little time; and takes his staff, and chases forth his lambs to feed.

- *riede* ‘returns’ → “comes out again”: returning is rendered as coming out again, making the outward movement explicit.
- *la speranza ringavagna* ‘hope gathers up again’ → “recovers hope”: the object moves after the verb, and the verb’s image of gathering back into a basket becomes the unfigurative “recovers”.
- *Veggendo* ‘seeing’ → “observing”: simple seeing becomes attentive observation.
- *il mondo aver cangiata faccia* ‘the world to have changed face’ → “how the world has changed its face”: the infinitive construction becomes a finite clause introduced by “how”; “its” is supplied before “face”.
- *In poco d’ ora* ‘in little of an hour’ → “in little time”: the expression is recast, replacing the specific unit *ora* with general “time”.
- *vincastro* ‘shepherd’s crook’ → “staff”: the specific implement becomes a more general one.
- *E fuor le pecorelle a pascer caccia* ‘and out the little sheep to graze drives’ → “and chases forth his lambs to feed”: the verb moves before its object, and the purpose phrase moves after it; “chases” replaces ‘drives’, “lambs” interprets the diminutive ‘little sheep’ as young animals, “his” replaces the definite article, and “feed” is less specific than ‘graze’.

> 16 Così mi fece sbigottir lo Mastro,  
> 17 Quand’ io gli vidi sì turbar la fronte,  
> 18 E così tosto al mal giunse lo impiastro.  

Thus the Master made me despond, when I saw his brow so troubled; and thus quickly to the sore the plaster came.

- *mi fece sbigottir lo Mastro* ‘me made grow dismayed the Master’ → “the Master made me despond”: the subject moves before the verb and the object after it; dismay or alarm becomes despondency.
- *gli vidi sì turbar la fronte* ‘I saw his brow become so troubled’ → “I saw his brow so troubled”: the possessive relationship expressed by *gli* becomes “his”; the infinitive describing the brow’s becoming troubled becomes an adjective describing its state.
- *così tosto* ‘so soon’ → “thus quickly”: “thus” treats *così* as indicating manner rather than degree, while “quickly” replaces the temporal ‘soon’.
- *al mal giunse lo impiastro* ‘to the ill came the plaster’ → “to the sore the plaster came”: “sore” gives *mal* a specifically bodily sense, and the subject moves before the verb.

> 19 Chè come noi venimmo al guasto ponte,  
> 20 Lo Duca a me si volse con quel piglio  
> 21 Dolce, ch’ io vidi in prima a piè del monte.  

For when we reached the shattered bridge, my Guide turned to me with that sweet aspect which I saw before at the foot of the mountain.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *come* ‘as’ → “when”: the temporal relationship is expressed more explicitly.
- *noi venimmo al guasto ponte* ‘we came to the ruined bridge’ → “we reached the shattered bridge”: ‘came to’ becomes the transitive “reached”, and “shattered” specifies a more particular kind of damage than ‘ruined’.
- *Lo Duca a me si volse* ‘the Leader to me turned himself’ → “my Guide turned to me”: “my” replaces the definite article, “Guide” interprets the leader’s function, the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, and “to me” moves after the verb.
- *quel piglio Dolce* ‘that aspect sweet’ → “that sweet aspect”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *in prima* ‘at first’ → “before”: the expression is rendered as an earlier occasion rather than explicitly as the first occasion.

> 22 Le braccia aperse, dopo alcun consiglio  
> 23 Eletto seco, riguardando prima  
> 24 Ben la ruina, e diedemi di piglio.  

He opened his arms after having chosen some plan within himself, first looking well at the ruin, and took hold of me.

- *Le braccia aperse* ‘the arms he opened’ → “He opened his arms”: the object moves after the verb, and “his” replaces the definite article.
- *dopo alcun consiglio Eletto seco* ‘after some counsel chosen with himself’ → “after having chosen some plan within himself”: the noun phrase with its passive participle becomes an active perfect gerund construction; “plan” interprets *consiglio* as a course of action, and “within himself” renders *seco* as inward deliberation rather than literally ‘with himself’.
- *riguardando prima Ben la ruina* ‘looking first well at the ruin’ → “first looking well at the ruin”: “first” moves before the participle.
- *e diedemi di piglio* ‘and gave me a grasp’ → “and took hold of me”: the Italian idiom is replaced by an English idiom, changing ‘gave’ to “took” and making “me” the complement of “of”.

> 25 E come quei che adopera ed istima,  
> 26 Che sempre par che innanzi si provveggia;  

And as one who works, and calculates, always seeming to provide beforehand;

- *come quei che* ‘as he who’ → “as one who”: the demonstrative referring to a person becomes the indefinite “one”.
- *istima* ‘estimates’ → “calculates”: estimation is expressed as calculation.
- *Che sempre par che innanzi si provveggia* ‘who always seems that he provides for himself beforehand’ → “always seeming to provide beforehand”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase; the subordinate clause with its subjunctive verb becomes an infinitive; the reflexive sense ‘for himself’ is not separately expressed; and “beforehand” moves after the verb.

## Lines 27–42 — Climbing the Broken Rock

> 27 Così, levando me su vêr la cima  
> 28 D’ un ronchione, avvisava un’ altra scheggia,  

so, lifting me up towards the top of one big block, he looked out another splinter,

- *D’ un ronchione* ‘of a large crag’ → “of one big block”: the augmentative noun becomes an adjective and noun; “block” replaces ‘crag’, and the indefinite article becomes “one”.
- *avvisava* ‘was observing’ → “looked out”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and observation is rendered as looking for and selecting something.

> 29 Dicendo: Sovra quella poi t’ aggrappa;  
> 30 Ma tenta pria s’ è tal ch’ ella ti reggia.  

saying: “Now clamber over that, but try first if it will carry thee.”

- *Sovra quella poi t’ aggrappa* ‘onto that then cling thyself’ → “Now clamber over that”: “Now” replaces *poi* (‘then’) and moves to the beginning; the reflexive verb becomes the intransitive “clamber”, and the phrase identifying its object moves after it. “Over” replaces ‘onto’.
- *s’ è tal ch’ ella ti reggia* ‘whether it is such that it may support thee’ → “if it will carry thee”: the construction ‘is such that’ is omitted, and the subordinate subjunctive becomes “will carry”. The object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 31 Non era via da vestito di cappa,  
> 32 Chè noi a pena, ei lieve ed io sospinto,  
> 33 Potevam su montar di chiappa in chiappa.  

It was no way for one clad with cloak of lead; for scarcely we, he light and I pushed on, could mount up from jagg to jagg.

- *Non era via* ‘It was not a way’ → “It was no way”: negation of the verb becomes the negative determiner “no”.
- *da vestito di cappa* ‘for someone clothed in a cloak’ → “for one clad with cloak of lead”: the substantivized participle is expanded with “one”; ‘in a cloak’ becomes “with cloak”, without an article, and “of lead” is supplied.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *noi a pena* ‘we scarcely’ → “scarcely we”: the adverb moves before the subject.
- *ed io sospinto* ‘and I pushed’ → “and I pushed on”: “on” makes the onward direction explicit.
- *Potevam su montar* ‘could up mount’ → “could mount up”: the directional adverb moves after the infinitive.
- *di chiappa in chiappa* ‘from rock to rock’ → “from jagg to jagg”: “jagg” specifies a projecting or jagged piece rather than simply a rock.

> 34 E se non fosse, che da quel precinto,  
> 35 Più che dall’ altro, era la costa corta,  
> 36 Non so di lui, ma io sarei ben vinto.  

And were it not that on that precinct the ascent was shorter than on the other, I know not about him, but I certainly had been defeated.

- *E se non fosse* ‘And if it were not’ → “And were it not”: conditional inversion replaces the explicit conjunction ‘if’.
- *da quel precinto* ‘from that precinct’ → “on that precinct”: “on” replaces the preposition ‘from’.
- *Più che dall’ altro, era la costa corta* ‘more than from the other, the slope was short’ → “the ascent was shorter than on the other”: the separated comparative elements become “shorter”, and the comparison moves after the predicate. “Ascent” replaces ‘slope’, and “on” again replaces ‘from’.
- *Non so di lui* ‘I do not know of him’ → “I know not about him”: the negative follows the verb rather than preceding it, and “about” renders *di*.
- *ma io sarei ben vinto* ‘but I would indeed be defeated’ → “but I certainly had been defeated”: the present conditional becomes a counterfactual past-perfect construction; “certainly” gives *ben* an explicitly assertive force.

> 37 Ma perchè Malebolge in vêr la porta  
> 38 Del bassissimo pozzo tutta pende,  
> 39 Lo sito di ciascuna valle porta,  
> 40 Che l’ una costa surge e l’ altra scende:  

But as Malebolge all hangs towards the entrance of the lowest well, the site of every valley imports that one side rises and the other descends.

- *perchè* ‘because’ → “as”: the causal connection is expressed with “as” rather than ‘because’.
- *Malebolge in vêr la porta Del bassissimo pozzo tutta pende* ‘Malebolge towards the gate of the lowest well all hangs’ → “Malebolge all hangs towards the entrance of the lowest well”: the predicate moves before the directional phrase, and “entrance” replaces ‘gate’.
- *porta* ‘carries’ → “imports”: the verb is rendered in its consequential sense, ‘entails’, rather than by its physical meaning.
- *l’ una costa* ‘the one slope’ → “one side”: the article is omitted, and “side” replaces ‘slope’.

> 41 Noi pur venimmo al fine in su la punta  
> 42 Onde l’ ultima pietra si scoscende.  

We, however, came at length to the point from which the last stone breaks off.

- *pur* ‘still’ → “however”: the particle is rendered as an explicitly adversative connective.
- *al fine* ‘at the end’ → “at length”: the phrase becomes an idiom marking eventual achievement.
- *in su la punta* ‘up onto the point’ → “to the point”: the combined directional expression is reduced to “to”, leaving the upward direction unexpressed.
- *Onde* ‘whence’ → “from which”: a relative adverb becomes a preposition and relative pronoun.
- *si scoscende* ‘breaks itself away’ → “breaks off”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes an English intransitive phrasal verb.

## Lines 43–56 — The Ascent Requires Resolve

> 43 La lena m’ era del polmon sì munta  
> 44 Quando fui su, ch’ io non potea più oltre,  
> 45 Anzi m’ assisi nella prima giunta.  

The breath was so exhausted from my lungs, when I was up, that I could no farther; nay, seated me at my first arrival.

- *m’ era del polmon sì munta* ‘to me was from the lung so milked’ → “was so exhausted from my lungs”: the metaphor of milking becomes exhaustion; the possessive dative becomes “my,” attached to “lungs”; singular *polmon* becomes plural, and the phrase identifying the source follows the participle.
- *più oltre* ‘more beyond’ → “farther”: the two-word expression is compressed into one comparative adverb.
- *Anzi* ‘rather’ → “nay”: the corrective conjunction becomes an emphatic English corrective.
- *m’ assisi* ‘I seated myself’ → “seated me”: the reflexive is expressed by the archaic “me” rather than ‘myself,’ and the subject is carried over from the preceding clause.
- *nella prima giunta* ‘at the first arrival’ → “at my first arrival”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 46 Omai convien che tu così ti spoltre,  
> 47 Disse il Maestro; chè, seggendo in piuma,  
> 48 In fama non si vien, nè sotto coltre:  

“Now it behooves thee thus to free thyself from sloth,” said the Master; “for sitting on down, or under coverlet, men come not into fame;

- *convien che tu così ti spoltre* ‘it is necessary that you thus shake off your sloth’ → “it behooves thee thus to free thyself from sloth”: the finite subordinate clause becomes an infinitive construction with “thee” as the object of “behooves”; the single reflexive verb *spoltre* is expanded into “free thyself from sloth.”
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *in piuma* ‘in feathers’ → “on down”: the preposition changes, and “down” specifies the feather filling.
- *In fama non si vien* ‘into fame one does not come’ → “men come not into fame”: the impersonal construction becomes an explicit plural subject; the phrase “into fame” moves after the verb, and the negative follows rather than precedes it.
- *nè sotto coltre* ‘nor under coverlet’ → “or under coverlet”: “or” replaces ‘nor’ within the negative sentence, and the phrase moves before the main clause to join “sitting on down.”

> 49 Sanza la qual chi sua vita consuma,  
> 50 Cotal vestigio in terra di sè lascia,  
> 51 Qual fummo in aere, o in acqua la schiuma:  

without which whoso consumes his life, leaves such vestige of himself on earth, as smoke in air or foam in water.

- *Sanza la qual* ‘without the which’ → “without which”: English omits the article belonging to the Italian relative-pronoun construction.
- *chi sua vita consuma* ‘whoever his life consumes’ → “whoso consumes his life”: the object moves after the verb.
- *Cotal vestigio in terra di sè lascia* ‘such vestige on earth of himself leaves’ → “leaves such vestige of himself on earth”: the verb moves before its object, and “of himself” moves before “on earth.”
- *o in acqua la schiuma* ‘or in water the foam’ → “or foam in water”: the noun moves before the prepositional phrase, and its definite article is omitted.

> 52 E però leva su, vinci l’ ambascia  
> 53 Con l’ animo che vince ogni battaglia,  
> 54 Se col suo grave corpo non s’ accascia.  

And therefore rise! Conquer thy panting with the soul, that conquers every battle, if with its heavy body it sinks not down.

- *leva su* ‘get up’ → “rise”: the verb and directional adverb become a single verb.
- *vinci l’ ambascia* ‘conquer the distress’ → “Conquer thy panting”: the broader *ambascia* is interpreted specifically as breathlessness, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Con l’ animo* ‘with the spirit’ → “with the soul”: “soul” renders *animo*, rather than ‘spirit’ or ‘mind.’
- *non s’ accascia* ‘does not collapse’ → “it sinks not down”: the pronominal verb becomes an English verb with a directional adverb, and the negative follows the finite verb rather than preceding it.

> 55 Più lunga scala convien che si saglia:  

A longer ladder must be climbed.

- *Più lunga scala convien che si saglia* ‘a longer ladder it is necessary that one climb’ → “A longer ladder must be climbed”: the construction expressing necessity through *convien* and a finite subordinate clause becomes a modal passive; the fronted object becomes the grammatical subject.

> 56 Non basta da costoro esser partito:  

To have quitted these is not enough.

- *Non basta da costoro esser partito* ‘it does not suffice from these people to have departed’ → “To have quitted these is not enough.”: the infinitive phrase moves to the beginning as subject; *basta* becomes ‘is enough’; departure expressed with *da* becomes the transitive “quitted”; and the explicitly personal demonstrative *costoro* becomes simply “these.”

## Lines 57–71 — Climbing and Hearing a Voice

> 57 Se tu m’ intendi, or fa sì che ti vaglia.  

If thou understandest me, now act so that it may profit thee.”

- *Se tu m’ intendi* ‘If thou me understandest’ → “If thou understandest me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *or fa sì che ti vaglia* ‘now do so that to thee it may avail’ → “now act so that it may profit thee”: ‘do’ becomes “act”; *vaglia* is rendered as “may profit,” with “thee” following the verb rather than preceding it.

> 58 Levàmi allor, mostrandomi fornito  
> 59 Meglio di lena ch’ io non mi sentia;  

I then rose, showing myself better furnished with breath than I felt,

- *Levàmi allor* ‘I raised myself then’ → “I then rose”: the reflexive construction becomes the intransitive “rose,” and “then” moves before the verb.
- *mostrandomi fornito Meglio* ‘showing myself furnished better’ → “showing myself better furnished”: the adverb moves before the participle.
- *di lena* ‘with strength’ → “with breath”: “breath” gives a more specific bodily sense to *lena*, which can mean strength or stamina.
- *ch’ io non mi sentia* ‘than I felt myself’ → “than I felt”: the reflexive pronoun is omitted, as is the Italian’s expletive *non*, which does not negate the comparative clause.

> 60 E dissi: Va, ch’ io son forte e ardito.  

and said: “Go on; for I am strong and confident.”

- *Va* ‘Go’ → “Go on”: “on” is supplied, making continuation explicit.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *ardito* ‘bold’ → “confident”: the rendering shifts from boldness to assurance.

> 61 Su per lo scoglio prendemmo la via,  
> 62 Ch’ era ronchioso, stretto e malagevole,  
> 63 Ed erto più assai che quel di pria.  

We took our way up the cliff, which was rugged, narrow, and difficult, and greatly steeper than the former.

- *Su per lo scoglio* ‘Up along the rock’ → “up the cliff”: the phrase moves from the beginning of the clause to after “We took our way”; *su per* is compressed into “up,” and *scoglio* is rendered more specifically as “cliff.”
- *prendemmo la via* ‘we took the way’ → “We took our way”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Ed erto più assai* ‘and steep more much’ → “and greatly steeper”: the Italian’s separate comparative adverb becomes the comparative ending of “steeper,” and the degree modifier moves before it.
- *che quel di pria* ‘than that of before’ → “than the former”: the demonstrative and its modifying phrase become the nominal expression “the former.”

> 64 Parlando andava per non parer fievole,  
> 65 Onde una voce uscìo dall’ altro fosso,  
> 66 A parole formar disconvenevole.  

Speaking I went, that I might not seem faint; whereat a voice came from the other fosse, unsuitable for forming words.

- *Parlando andava* ‘Speaking I was going’ → “Speaking I went”: the imperfect’s ongoing action is expressed by a simple past rather than a progressive construction.
- *per non parer fievole* ‘in order not to seem faint’ → “that I might not seem faint”: the infinitival purpose clause becomes a finite clause with “might.”
- *Onde* ‘whence’ → “whereat”: the translation takes the connective as referring to the preceding action as an occasion or cause, rather than as indicating a place of origin.
- *uscìo* ‘came out’ → “came”: the explicit sense of emergence conveyed by *uscire* is reduced to “came.”
- *A parole formar disconvenevole* ‘for words to form unsuitable’ → “unsuitable for forming words”: the adjective moves before its complement, the infinitive becomes an English *-ing* form, and “words” moves after the verb.

> 67 Non so che disse, ancor che sovra il dosso  
> 68 Fossi dell’ arco già, che varca quivi;  
> 69 Ma chi parlava ad ira parea mosso.  

I know not what it said, though I already was on the ridge of the arch which crosses there; but he who spake seemed moved to anger.

- *Non so* ‘not I know’ → “I know not”: the negative moves from before the verb to after it.
- *ancor che* ‘even though’ → “though”: the concessive expression is reduced to “though,” without an equivalent for the emphasis of ‘even.’
- *sovra il dosso … dell’ arco* ‘upon the back … of the arch’ → “on the ridge of the arch”: *dosso*, literally ‘back,’ becomes “ridge”; the interrupted noun phrase is brought together and placed after “I already was.”
- *Fossi … già* ‘I were … already’ → “I already was”: the Italian imperfect subjunctive, governed by the concessive construction, becomes an English indicative; “already” moves before the verb.
- *chi parlava* ‘who was speaking’ → “he who spake”: the independent relative is expanded into “he who,” and the imperfect’s ongoing action becomes a simple past.
- *ad ira parea mosso* ‘to anger seemed moved’ → “seemed moved to anger”: the complement moves from before the verb to after the participle.

> 70 Io era volto in giù; ma gli occhi vivi  
> 71 Non potean ire al fondo per l’ oscuro:  

I had turned myself downwards; but my living eyes could not reach the bottom for the darkness.

- *Io era volto in giù* ‘I was turned downwards’ → “I had turned myself downwards”: the Italian’s description of a resulting posture becomes an explicit reflexive action in the past perfect.
- *ma gli occhi vivi* ‘but the eyes living’ → “but my living eyes”: “my” replaces the definite article, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Non potean* ‘not could’ → “could not”: the negative moves after the modal verb.
- *ire al fondo* ‘go to the bottom’ → “reach the bottom”: motion towards a destination becomes attainment of it, and the prepositional complement becomes a direct object.
- *per l’ oscuro* ‘because of the dark’ → “for the darkness”: the substantivized adjective *oscuro* becomes the abstract noun “darkness.”

## Lines 72–85 — Descent to the Serpents

> 72 Perch’ io: Maestro, fa che tu arrivi  
> 73 Dall’ altro cinghio, e dismontiam lo muro;  

Wherefore I: “Master, see thou get to the other boundary, and let us dismount the wall;

- *fa che tu arrivi* ‘make that thou arrive’ → “see thou get”: the causative expression becomes an English injunction to ensure the action; *arrivi* is rendered by “get”.
- *Dall’ altro cinghio* ‘at the other girdle’ → “to the other boundary”: the destination is expressed with “to”, and the image of an encircling girdle becomes “boundary”.

> 74 Chè com’ io odo quinci, e non intendo,  
> 75 Così giù veggio, e niente affiguro.  

for as I hear from hence and do not understand, so I see down and distinguish nothing.”

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *Così giù veggio* ‘so down I see’ → “so I see down”: the directional adverb moves after the verb.
- *niente affiguro* ‘nothing I distinguish’ → “distinguish nothing”: the object moves after the verb.

> 76 Altra risposta, disse, non ti rendo,  
> 77 Se non lo far: chè la dimanda onesta  
> 78 Si dee seguir con l’ opera tacendo.  

“Other answer I give thee not,” he said “than the deed: for a fit request should be followed with the work in silence.”

- *Altra risposta, disse, non ti rendo* ‘other answer, he said, I do not render to thee’ → “Other answer I give thee not,” he said”: the reporting clause moves from immediately after the opening object to after the completed negative clause; “give” replaces ‘render’, and the recipient pronoun and the negative follow the verb rather than preceding it.
- *Se non lo far* ‘except the doing’ → “than the deed”: the exception is expressed through “Other … than”, and the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun naming the deed rather than the doing.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *la dimanda onesta* ‘the honourable request’ → “a fit request”: the definite article becomes indefinite, *onesta* is interpreted as fitting or appropriate, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *tacendo* ‘keeping silent’ → “in silence”: the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase.

> 79 Noi discendemmo il ponte dalla testa,  
> 80 Ove s’ aggiunge con l’ ottava ripa,  
> 81 E poi mi fu la bolgia manifesta:  

We went down the bridge, at the head where it joins with the eighth bank; and then the chasm was manifest to me.

- *Noi discendemmo* ‘we descended’ → “We went down”: the single verb is rendered by a verb with a directional adverb.
- *dalla testa* ‘from the head’ → “at the head”: a phrase marking the starting point becomes one marking location.
- *s’ aggiunge* ‘it joins itself’ → “it joins”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive verb without a reflexive pronoun.
- *mi fu la bolgia manifesta* ‘to me was the pouch manifest’ → “the chasm was manifest to me”: *bolgia*, literally a pouch, is rendered by the topographical “chasm”; the subject moves before the verb, and “to me” moves after the predicate adjective.

> 82 E vidivi entro terribile stipa  
> 83 Di serpenti, e di sì diversa mena,  
> 84 Che la memoria il sangue ancor mi scipa.  

And I saw within it a fearful throng of serpents, and of so strange a look, that even now the recollection scares my blood.

- *E vidivi entro* ‘and I saw there within’ → “And I saw within it”: the locative enclitic *vi*, ‘there’, is recast as “it”, governed by “within”.
- *terribile stipa* ‘terrible packed mass’ → “a fearful throng”: an indefinite article is supplied, and the densely packed mass denoted by *stipa* becomes a “throng”.
- *di sì diversa mena* ‘of so diverse a kind’ → “of so strange a look”: *diversa* is interpreted as strange rather than varied, while *mena*, ‘kind’ or ‘sort’, is rendered as outward appearance.
- *il sangue … mi* ‘the blood … to me’ → “my blood”: the possessive relationship expressed by the dative pronoun becomes an English possessive determiner; the object moves after the verb.
- *ancor* ‘still’ → “even now”: a single adverb becomes an emphatic phrase, placed before the subject rather than later in the clause.
- *scipa* ‘spoils’ → “scares”: a verb of damaging or impairing is rendered as one of frightening.

> 85 Più non si vanti Libia con sua rena;  

Let Libya boast no longer with its sand;

- *Più non si vanti Libia* ‘no longer may Libya boast’ → “Let Libya boast no longer”: the independent subjunctive becomes a “Let” construction; the negative expression moves after the verb, the subject moves before it, and the Italian reflexive verb is rendered by intransitive “boast”.

## Lines 86–99 — Serpents Bind and Strike

> 86 Chè se Chelidri, Iaculi e Faree  
> 87 Produce, e Cencri con Anfesibena,  

for, though it engenders Chelydri, Jaculi and Pareæ, and Cenchres with Amphisbæna,

- *se* ‘if’ → “though”: the conditional conjunction is read concessively.
- *Produce* ‘produces’ → “engenders”: the verb expresses generation rather than production and is moved before the entire list of its objects.

> 88 Non tante pestilenze, nè sì ree  
> 89 Mostrò giammai con tutta l’ Etiopia,  
> 90 Nè con ciò che di sopra il mar Rosso èe.  

plagues so numerous or so dire it never shewed, with all Ethiopia, nor with the land that lies by the Red Sea.

- *tante pestilenze* ‘so many plagues’ → “plagues so numerous”: the preceding expression of quantity becomes an adjective phrase following the noun.
- *Non … nè … giammai* ‘not … nor … ever’ → “or” and “never”: the Italian negative expressions are consolidated into “never”; the conjunction consequently becomes “or”, and the negative adverb precedes rather than follows the verb.
- *sì ree* ‘so evil’ → “so dire”: moral or harmful badness is expressed as dreadfulness.
- *tutta l’Etiopia* ‘all the Ethiopia’ → “all Ethiopia”: the definite article before the geographical name is omitted.
- *ciò che* ‘that which’ → “the land that”: the demonstrative is replaced by an explicit noun identifying what it denotes.
- *di sopra il mar Rosso èe* ‘above the Red Sea is’ → “lies by the Red Sea”: “lies” replaces ‘is’, the verb moves before the geographical phrase, and “by” renders ‘above’ as proximity rather than relative position.

> 91 Tra questa cruda e tristissima copia  
> 92 Correvan genti nude e spaventate,  
> 93 Senza sperar pertugio o elitropia.  

Amid this cruel and most dismal swarm were people running, naked and terrified, without hope of lurking hole or heliotrope.

- *tristissima* ‘most sad’ → “most dismal”: sadness is rendered as dismalness.
- *copia* ‘abundance’ → “swarm”: the translation substitutes a collective noun for the expression of multitude.
- *Correvan genti* ‘ran people’ → “were people running”: the imperfect becomes an English past progressive, with the subject placed between the auxiliary and participle.
- *Senza sperar* ‘without hoping for’ → “without hope of”: the infinitive becomes a noun, with a corresponding change of preposition.
- *pertugio* ‘hole’ → “lurking hole”: “lurking” is supplied to specify the hole’s possible use.

> 94 Con serpi le man dietro avean legate:  

They had their hands tied behind with serpents:

- *Con serpi le man dietro avean legate* ‘With serpents the hands behind they had tied’ → “They had their hands tied behind with serpents”: the opening prepositional phrase moves to the end of the clause; the definite article becomes a possessive; the finite verb moves before its object, and “tied” precedes rather than follows “behind”.

> 95 Quelle ficcavan per le ren la coda  
> 96 E il capo, ed eran dinanzi aggroppate.  

these through their loins fixed the tail and the head, and were coiled in knots before.

- *Quelle* ‘those’ → “these”: the distal demonstrative becomes a proximal one.
- *ficcavan* ‘were thrusting’ → “fixed”: insertion is expressed as fixing in place, and the imperfect is rendered by a simple past.
- *per le ren* ‘through the loins’ → “through their loins”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves before the verb.
- *ed eran dinanzi aggroppate* ‘and were in front knotted’ → “and were coiled in knots before”: ‘knotted’ is expanded into “coiled in knots”, and the expression of position moves after it.

> 97 Ed ecco ad un, ch’ era da nostra proda,  
> 98 S’ avventò un serpente, che il trafisse  
> 99 Là dove il collo alle spalle s’ annoda.  

And lo! at one, who was near our shore, sprang up a serpent, which transfixed him there where the neck is bound upon the shoulders.

- *da nostra proda* ‘on our side’ → “near our shore”: the translation reads *da* as proximity and gives *proda* the concrete sense of a shore rather than a side.
- *S’avventò* ‘hurled itself’ → “sprang up”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and “up” is supplied.
- *il collo alle spalle s’annoda* ‘the neck to the shoulders knots itself’ → “the neck is bound upon the shoulders”: the reflexive construction becomes passive, knotting is rendered as binding, ‘to’ becomes “upon”, and the prepositional phrase moves after the verb.

## Lines 100–111 — Ashes and Rebirth

> 100 Nè O sì tosto mai, nè I si scrisse,  
> 101 Com’ ei s’ accese, e arse, e cener tutto  
> 102 Convenne che cascando divenisse.  

Neither ‘O’ nor ‘I’ was ever written so quickly as he took fire, and burnt, and dropt down all changed to ashes.

- *Nè O sì tosto mai, nè I si scrisse* ‘Neither O so quickly ever, nor I, was written’ → “Neither ‘O’ nor ‘I’ was ever written so quickly”: the coordinated subjects are brought together before the verb; *mai* moves before “written”, and *sì tosto* follows it.
- *Com’ ei s’ accese* ‘as he kindled’ → “as he took fire”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes the English idiom “took fire”.
- *e cener tutto Convenne che cascando divenisse* ‘and wholly ash it was necessary that, falling, he should become’ → “and dropt down all changed to ashes”: *Convenne che*, expressing necessity, is omitted. The subordinate subjunctive construction is replaced by a main clause: *cascando*, a gerund, becomes “dropt down”, while *divenisse*, ‘should become’, becomes the participial “changed to”. The result, placed first in Italian, comes last in English; singular collective *cener* becomes plural “ashes”.

> 103 E poi che fu a terra sì distrutto,  
> 104 La cener si raccolse, e per sè stessa  
> 105 In quel medesmo ritornò di butto.  

And after he was thus dissolved upon the ground, the ashes reunited, and of themselves at once resumed the former shape.

- *E poi che fu a terra sì distrutto* ‘And after he was on the ground thus destroyed’ → “And after he was thus dissolved upon the ground”: “dissolved” renders *distrutto*, ‘destroyed’, and the phrase locating him on the ground moves after the participle.
- *La cener si raccolse* ‘the ash gathered itself’ → “the ashes reunited”: the singular collective noun becomes plural, and the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb. “Reunited” explicitly presents the gathering as a reunion.
- *per sè stessa* ‘by itself’ → “of themselves”: “of” replaces ‘by’ in the English idiom, and the pronoun becomes plural to agree with “ashes”.
- *In quel medesmo ritornò di butto* ‘into that same one it returned at once’ → “at once resumed the former shape”: ‘returned into’ becomes the transitive “resumed”; the substantival ‘that same one’ is interpreted as “the former shape”, supplying “shape”. The expression of immediacy moves from the end to the beginning.

> 106 Così per li gran savi si confessa,  
> 107 Che la Fenice muore, e poi rinasce,  
> 108 Quando al cinquecentesimo anno appressa.  

Thus by great sages ’tis confest the Phœnix dies, and then is born again, when it approaches the five-hundredth year.

- *li gran savi* ‘the great wise men’ → “great sages”: the definite article is omitted, and the substantivized adjective *savi* is rendered by the noun “sages”.
- *si confessa* ‘one acknowledges’ → “’tis confest”: the Italian *si*-construction becomes an explicit passive with anticipatory “it”.
- *Che la Fenice* ‘that the Phœnix’ → “the Phœnix”: the conjunction introducing the reported proposition is omitted.
- *rinasce* ‘comes to birth again’ → “is born again”: the active intransitive verb is rendered by the English passive expression “is born”, with the prefix *ri-* expressed separately as “again”.
- *Quando al cinquecentesimo anno appressa* ‘when to the five-hundredth year it draws near’ → “when it approaches the five-hundredth year”: the prepositional complement becomes the direct object of “approaches” and moves after the verb.

> 109 Erba nè biada in sua vita non pasce,  
> 110 Ma sol d’ incenso lagrime e d’ amomo;  
> 111 E nardo e mirra son l’ ultime fasce.  

In its life it eats no herb or grain, but only tears of incense and amomum: and nard and myrrh are its last swathings.

- *Erba nè biada in sua vita non pasce* ‘Herb nor grain in its life it does not feed on’ → “In its life it eats no herb or grain”: the time phrase moves to the beginning, and the objects move after the verb. Italian negative concord, *nè … non*, becomes “no … or”; *pasce*, ‘feeds on’, becomes “eats”.
- *d’ incenso lagrime e d’ amomo* ‘of incense tears and of amomum’ → “tears of incense and amomum”: “tears” moves before its first dependent phrase, and the second ‘of’ is not repeated.
- *l’ ultime fasce* ‘the last swaddling bands’ → “its last swathings”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “swathings” renders ‘swaddling bands’.

## Lines 112–126 — The Sinner Names Himself

> 112 E quale è quei che cade, e non sa como,  
> 113 Per forza di Demon ch’ a terra il tira,  
> 114 O d’ altra oppilazion che lega l’ uomo,  

And as one who falls, and knows not how, through force of Demon which drags him to the ground, or of other oppilation that fetters men;

- *quale è quei* ‘as is he’ → “as one”: the copula is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *non sa como* ‘not knows how’ → “knows not how”: the negative follows the verb instead of preceding it.
- *ch’ a terra il tira* ‘which to earth him pulls’ → “which drags him to the ground”: “drags” strengthens ‘pulls’; the object and the phrase expressing direction move after the verb, and ‘earth’ becomes “the ground”.
- *lega l’ uomo* ‘binds the man’ → “fetters men”: ‘binds’ becomes “fetters”, and the generic definite singular becomes a plural without an article.

> 115 Quando si leva, che intorno si mira,  
> 116 Tutto smarrito dalla grande angoscia  
> 117 Ch’ egli ha sofferta, e guardando sospira;  

who, when he rises, looks fixtly round him, all bewildered by the great anguish he has undergone, and looking sighs:

- *Quando si leva, che intorno si mira* ‘when he raises himself, who around himself looks’ → “who, when he rises, looks fixtly round him”: the resumptive relative pronoun moves before the temporal clause; the reflexive ‘raises himself’ becomes intransitive “rises”; “round him” follows the verb; and “fixtly” is supplied.
- *Ch’ egli ha sofferta* ‘that he has suffered’ → “he has undergone”: the object relative pronoun is omitted, and ‘suffered’ becomes “undergone”.

> 118 Tal era il peccator levato poscia.  

such was the sinner when he rose.

- *levato poscia* ‘risen afterward’ → “when he rose”: the participle becomes a finite temporal clause, and ‘afterward’ is omitted.

> 119 O Giustizia di Dio, quant’ è severa!  
> 120 Che cotai colpi per vendetta croscia.  

Justice of God! O how severe, that showers such blows in vengeance!

- *O Giustizia di Dio, quant’ è severa* ‘O Justice of God, how severe it is’ → “Justice of God! O how severe”: “O” moves from the address to the exclamation about severity, and the copula is omitted.
- *Che cotai colpi per vendetta croscia* ‘that such blows for vengeance showers’ → “that showers such blows in vengeance”: the verb moves before its object, and ‘for vengeance’, expressing purpose, becomes “in vengeance”.

> 121 Lo Duca il dimandò poi chi egli era:  

The Guide then asked him who he was.

- *Lo Duca* ‘the leader’ → “The Guide”: the title is rendered by a word emphasizing guidance.
- *il dimandò poi* ‘him asked then’ → “then asked him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, while the temporal adverb moves before it.

> 122 Perch’ ei rispose: Io piovvi di Toscana,  
> 123 Poco tempo è, in questa gola fera.  

Whereupon he answered: “I rained from Tuscany, short while ago, into this fierce gullet.

- *Perch’* ‘for which reason’ → “Whereupon”: the connective presents the answer as following upon the question rather than explicitly giving its cause.
- *Poco tempo è* ‘little time it is’ → “short while ago”: the present-tense expression of elapsed time becomes an adverbial phrase.
- *in questa gola fera* ‘in this gullet fierce’ → “into this fierce gullet”: the preposition explicitly marks motion inward, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 124 Vita bestial mi piacque, e non umana,  
> 125 Sì come a mul ch’ io fui: son Vanni Fucci  
> 126 Bestia, e Pistoia mi fu degna tana.  

Bestial life, not human, pleased me, mule that I was. I am Vanni Fucci, savage beast; and Pistoia was a fitting den for me.”

- *Vita bestial mi piacque, e non umana* ‘life bestial to me pleased, and not human’ → “Bestial life, not human, pleased me”: “Bestial” moves before its noun; the contrasting phrase moves before the verb, with ‘and’ omitted; and the Italian indirect-object pronoun becomes the following direct object of “pleased”.
- *Sì come a mul ch’ io fui* ‘just as to a mule that I was’ → “mule that I was”: the comparative construction and its preposition are omitted, as is the indefinite article; the phrase becomes an appositional description of the speaker.
- *Bestia* ‘beast’ → “savage beast”: “savage” is supplied.
- *mi fu degna tana* ‘to me was worthy den’ → “was a fitting den for me”: the pronoun becomes a final prepositional phrase, ‘worthy’ becomes “fitting”, and the indefinite article is supplied.

## Lines 127–139 — The Sinner Confesses His Theft

> 127 Ed io al Duca: Dilli che non mucci,  

And I to the Guide: “Tell him not to budge;

- *Duca* ‘leader’ → “Guide”: the title is rendered by a word emphasizing guidance rather than leadership.
- *Dilli che non mucci* ‘Tell him that he not slip away’ → “Tell him not to budge”: the subordinate clause with a subjunctive becomes an infinitive construction; slipping away becomes not moving at all.

> 128 E dimanda qual colpa quaggiù il pinse;  
> 129 Ch’ io il vidi uom già di sangue e di corrucci.  

and ask what crime thrust him down here, for I saw him once a man of rage and blood.”

- *qual colpa quaggiù il pinse* ‘what fault down here him thrust’ → “what crime thrust him down here”: “crime” gives *colpa* a specifically criminal sense; the verb precedes its object, and the expression of place moves to the end.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *io il vidi* ‘I him saw’ → “I saw him”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *uom già di sangue e di corrucci* ‘man once of blood and of fits of anger’ → “once a man of rage and blood”: “once” moves before the noun, an indefinite article is supplied, and the two qualities exchange places. The plural *corrucci* becomes the singular mass noun “rage,” and the repeated *di* is not retained.

> 130 E il peccator, che intese, non s’ infinse,  

And the sinner who heard, feigned not;

- *non s’ infinse* ‘did not disguise himself’ → “feigned not”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive use of “feigned,” without an expressed reflexive object; the negative follows the verb.

> 131 Ma drizzò verso me l’ animo e il volto,  
> 132 E di trista vergogna si dipinse.  

but directed towards me his mind and face, with a look of dismal shame.

- *l’ animo e il volto* ‘the mind and the face’ → “his mind and face”: the two definite articles are replaced by one possessive governing both nouns.
- *E di trista vergogna si dipinse* ‘and with sad shame he painted himself’ → “with a look of dismal shame”: the coordinated reflexive clause becomes a prepositional phrase. The conjunction and the verb’s image of painting are dropped, while “a look of” is supplied to express the visible manifestation of shame.

> 133 Poi disse: Più mi duol che tu m’ hai colto  
> 134 Nella miseria, dove tu mi vedi,  
> 135 Che quand’ io fui dell’ altra vita tolto.  

Then he said: “It pains me more that thou hast caught me in the misery wherein thou seest me, than when I was taken from the other life.

- *Più mi duol* ‘more me it pains’ → “It pains me more”: the degree word moves after the verb and its object, and the object pronoun also moves after the verb.
- *che tu m’ hai colto* ‘that thou me hast caught’ → “that thou hast caught me”: the object pronoun moves after the compound verb.
- *dove tu mi vedi* ‘where thou me seest’ → “wherein thou seest me”: “wherein” makes the relation ‘in which’ explicit; the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *dell’ altra vita tolto* ‘from the other life taken’ → “taken from the other life”: the participle moves before the phrase expressing separation.

> 136 Io non posso negar quel che tu chiedi:  

I cannot deny thee what thou askest:

- *negar* ‘deny’ → “deny thee”: an indirect object, “thee,” is supplied.
- *quel che* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun are combined into a single relative word.

> 137 In giù son messo tanto, perch’ io fui  
> 138 Ladro alla sagrestia de’ belli arredi;  

I am put down so far, because I robbed the sacristy of its goodly furniture;

- *In giù son messo tanto* ‘downward I am put so far’ → “I am put down so far”: the initial expression of direction moves after the passive verb.
- *io fui Ladro alla sagrestia de’ belli arredi* ‘I was a thief at the sacristy of the beautiful furnishings’ → “I robbed the sacristy of its goodly furniture”: the construction ‘was a thief’ becomes the active verb “robbed.” The sacristy changes from a prepositional complement into the direct object; the furnishings become what it was robbed of. The definite article before *belli arredi* becomes the possessive “its,” and plural *arredi* becomes the mass noun “furniture.”

> 139 E falsamente già fuo apposto altrui.  

and falsely once it was imputed to others.

- Literal.

## Lines 140–151 — A Prophecy of Defeat

> 140 Ma perchè di tal vista tu non godi,  
> 141 Se mai sarai di fuor de’ luoghi bui,  
> 142 Apri gli orecchi al mio annunzio, e odi:  

But that thou mayest not joy in this sight, if ever thou escape the dark abodes, open thy ears and hear what I announce.

- *Ma perchè di tal vista tu non godi* ‘But so that in such a sight thou rejoice not’ → “But that thou mayest not joy in this sight”: the purpose clause is expressed with “mayest”; ‘such’ becomes “this”; and the phrase describing the sight moves after the verb.
- *sarai di fuor de’* ‘thou wilt be outside the’ → “thou escape the”: a future state becomes an act of escape, expressed in the present tense after “if”.
- *luoghi bui* ‘places dark’ → “dark abodes”: ‘places’ becomes “abodes”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *gli orecchi* ‘the ears’ → “thy ears”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *al mio annunzio, e odi* ‘to my announcement, and hear’ → “and hear what I announce”: the noun and its possessive become a clause with a verb; what was attached to the opening of the ears becomes the object of “hear”, and the command to hear moves before it.

> 143 Pistoia in pria di Neri si dimagra,  

Pistoia first is thinned of Neri;

- *di Neri si dimagra* ‘of Blacks grows lean’ → “is thinned of Neri”: the reflexive construction describing becoming lean becomes a passive; the phrase beginning “of” moves after the verb; and *Neri* is retained untranslated.

> 144 Poi Firenze rinnova genti e modi.  

then Florence renovates her people and her laws.

- *genti e modi* ‘peoples and ways’ → “her people and her laws”: the plural ‘peoples’ becomes the collective singular “people”; the broader ‘ways’ is interpreted as “laws”; and “her” is supplied before both nouns.

> 145 Tragge Marte vapor di val di Magra,  
> 146 Ch’ è di torbidi nuvoli involuto,  
> 147 E con tempesta impetuosa ed agra  
> 148 Sopra campo Picen fia combattuto:  

Mars brings from Valdimagra a fiery vapour, which is wrapt in turbid clouds, and on Piceno’s field shall be assailed with angry and impetuous storm;

- *Tragge Marte* ‘draws Mars’ → “Mars brings”: the subject moves before the verb, and ‘draws’ becomes “brings”.
- *vapor di val di Magra* ‘vapour from Valdimagra’ → “from Valdimagra a fiery vapour”: the phrase of origin moves before the noun; the indefinite article and “fiery” are supplied.
- *di torbidi nuvoli involuto* ‘in turbid clouds wrapt’ → “wrapt in turbid clouds”: the participle moves before the phrase describing what surrounds the vapour.
- *con tempesta impetuosa ed agra* ‘with tempest impetuous and bitter’ → “with angry and impetuous storm”: ‘bitter’ becomes “angry”; the adjectives exchange order and move before the noun; and the whole phrase moves after the verb.
- *Sopra campo Picen* ‘on Picene field’ → “on Piceno’s field”: the geographical adjective becomes a possessive placed before the noun.
- *fia combattuto* ‘shall be fought’ → “shall be assailed”: the passive is interpreted as an assault upon the vapour, which remains the subject of the English clause.

> 149 Ond’ ei repente spezzerà la nebbia,  
> 150 Sì ch’ ogni Bianco ne sarà feruto:  

whence it suddenly shall rend the mist, so that every Bianco shall be wounded by it.

- *ei repente spezzerà* ‘he suddenly shall break apart’ → “it suddenly shall rend”: the grammatically masculine pronoun becomes English neuter “it”, and breaking apart becomes rending.
- *ogni Bianco* ‘every White’ → “every Bianco”: *Bianco* is retained untranslated.
- *ne sarà feruto* ‘from it shall be wounded’ → “shall be wounded by it”: the pronominal *ne* becomes a following prepositional phrase, with “by” presenting the source of injury as its agent.

> 151 E detto l’ ho, perchè doler ten debbia.  

And I have said this, for it must grieve thee.”

- *detto l’ ho* ‘said it I have’ → “I have said this”: the auxiliary moves before the participle and the object after it; ‘it’ becomes the demonstrative “this”.
- *perchè* ‘so that’ → “for”: the purpose conjunction becomes an explanatory coordinating conjunction.
- *doler ten debbia* ‘thou shouldst grieve over it’ → “it must grieve thee”: the person experiencing sorrow becomes the object of a causative verb, while its cause becomes the subject; the subjunctive construction is recast with “must”.

## Summary — Close Phrasing, Recast Syntax

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s sequence of statements, comparisons, and images, while frequently rearranging their internal word order. Verbs generally move before their objects, adjectives before their nouns, and complements after the words they qualify. Thus *la speranza ringavagna* becomes “recovers hope”, and *quel piglio Dolce* becomes “that sweet aspect”. Yet the English retains some conspicuously Italian arrangements and older English constructions, including “to the sore the plaster came” and “I know not”. Its fidelity is therefore not simply a matter of replacing Italian syntax with ordinary English prose.

Changes of construction recur throughout. Reflexive verbs often become English intransitives, as *si leva* becomes “rises”; impersonal expressions acquire explicit subjects, as *non si vien* becomes “men come not”; and finite clauses become infinitives, or vice versa. *Che si faccia* becomes “what to do”, whereas *per non parer fievole* becomes “that I might not seem faint”. More substantial recasting appears in *io fui Ladro*, ‘I was a thief’, rendered as “I robbed”. Possession is also regularly reorganized: Italian definite articles and possessive datives become English possessives, as in “his thigh” and “my blood”. Beyond the changes individually recorded in the comparison, English routinely supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits the articles that Italian places before possessives.

Word choice sometimes narrows or redirects the Italian. The broad *roba* becomes “fodder”, *ambascia* becomes “panting”, and *modi* becomes “laws”; conversely, *vincastro*, ‘shepherd’s crook’, becomes the more general “staff”. Some figurative wording is softened: the gathering image in *ringavagna* gives way to “recovers”, and *munta*, ‘milked’, becomes “exhausted”. Elsewhere the imagery remains strikingly close, notably in “I rained from Tuscany”, “tears of incense”, and the paired images of smoke and foam. Literal phrasing thus coexists with interpretive choices, including “plumes” for *penna* and “lambs” for *pecorelle*.

Additions and omissions range from grammatical adjustments to explanatory expansion. “Of lead”, “fiery”, and “savage” supply details not separately expressed in their Italian phrases; “the former shape” makes explicit what *quel medesmo* leaves implicit. By contrast, the necessity expressed by *Convenne che* disappears, and *levato poscia* becomes “when he rose” without separately retaining ‘afterward’. Connectives can also shift the relationship between clauses: *se* becomes concessive “though”, while the final purpose expressed by *perchè* becomes explanatory “for”. Overall, Carlyle preserves much of the Italian’s vocabulary, imagery, and rhetorical progression, but repeatedly adjusts grammar, emphasis, and specificity to produce an English prose rendering rather than a word-for-word transcription.
