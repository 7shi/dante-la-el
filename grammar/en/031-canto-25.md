# Inferno 25

## Lines 1–12 — The Thief’s Defiance and Pistoia’s Condemnation

> 1 Al fine delle sue parole il ladro  
> 2 Le mani alzò con ambeduo le fiche,  
> 3 Gridando: Togli, Dio, chè a te le squadro.  

At the conclusion of his words, the thief raised up his hands with both the figs, shouting: “Take them, God, for at thee I aim them!”

- *Al fine* ‘At the end’ → “At the conclusion”: “conclusion” replaces ‘end’.
- *il ladro Le mani alzò* ‘the thief the hands raised’ → “the thief raised up his hands”: the object moves after the verb; the definite article becomes a possessive, and “up” is supplied.
- *Togli* ‘Take’ → “Take them”: the unexpressed object is supplied as “them”.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *a te le squadro* ‘at thee them I aim’ → “at thee I aim them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 4 Da indi in qua mi fur le serpi amiche,  
> 5 Perch’ una gli s’ avvolse allora al collo,  
> 6 Come dicesse: Io non vo’ che più diche;  

From this time forth the serpents were my friends; for one of them then coiled itself about his neck, as if saying, ‘Thou shalt speak no farther!’

- *Da indi in qua* ‘From then until now’ → “From this time forth”: the Italian looks back over the interval from that moment to the speaker’s present; the English looks forward from that moment.
- *mi fur le serpi amiche* ‘to me were the serpents friendly’ → “the serpents were my friends”: the subject moves before the verb, and the construction with a dative pronoun and predicative adjective becomes one with a possessive and the noun “friends”.
- *Perch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *una* ‘one’ → “one of them”: the partitive phrase “of them” is supplied.
- *gli s’ avvolse allora al collo* ‘to him itself coiled then at the neck’ → “then coiled itself about his neck”: “then” moves before the verb and the reflexive pronoun follows it; the dative *gli* becomes the possessive “his”, and “about” replaces ‘at’.
- *Come dicesse* ‘as though it were saying’ → “as if saying”: the finite subjunctive construction is reduced to a participial phrase.
- *Io non vo’ che più diche* ‘I do not wish that thou speak any more’ → “Thou shalt speak no farther!”: the speaker’s refusal or wish, expressed through a main verb and subordinate subjunctive, becomes a direct prohibition with “shalt”; ‘any more’ becomes “no farther”.

> 7 E un’ altra alle braccia, e rilegollo  
> 8 Ribadendo sè stessa sì dinanzi,  
> 9 Che non potea con esse dare un crollo.  

and another about his arms; and it tied him again, rivetting itself in front so firmly, that he could not give a jog with them.

- *alle braccia* ‘at the arms’ → “about his arms”: “about” replaces ‘at’, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *rilegollo* ‘bound him fast’ → “it tied him again”: the translation takes the prefix *ri-* as indicating repetition rather than intensified or secure binding.
- *Ribadendo sè stessa sì dinanzi* ‘Rivetting itself so in front’ → “rivetting itself in front so firmly”: “firmly” is supplied to specify the force of *sì*, and the resulting phrase moves after “in front”.
- *con esse dare un crollo* ‘with them give a shake’ → “give a jog with them”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb and its object, and *crollo* is rendered as “jog”.

> 10 Ah Pistoia, Pistoia! chè non stanzi  
> 11 D’ incenerarti, sì che più non duri,  
> 12 Poi che in mal far lo seme tuo avanzi?  

Ah, Pistoia! Pistoia! why dost thou not decree to turn thyself to ashes, that thou mayest endure no longer since thou outgoest thy seed in evil-doing?

- *incenerarti* ‘reduce thyself to ashes’ → “turn thyself to ashes”: the Italian verb is expanded into a verb-and-complement phrase.
- *sì che* ‘so that’ → “that”: *sì* has no separate equivalent.
- *più non duri* ‘no longer thou endure’ → “thou mayest endure no longer”: the negative adverbial moves after the verb, and the Italian subjunctive is represented by “mayest” with an infinitive.
- *in mal far lo seme tuo avanzi* ‘in evil-doing thy seed thou surpassest’ → “thou outgoest thy seed in evil-doing”: the verb moves before its object, the initial phrase moves to the end, and the possessive precedes its noun rather than following it; “outgoest” renders ‘surpassest’.

## Lines 13–24 — Pride and a Raging Centaur

> 13 Per tutti i cerchi dello Inferno oscuri  
> 14 Spirto non vidi in Dio tanto superbo,  
> 15 Non quel che cadde a Tebe giù de’ muri.  

Through all the dark circles of Hell, I saw no spirit against God so proud, not even him who fell at Thebes down from the walls!

- *tutti i cerchi dello Inferno oscuri* ‘all the circles of Hell dark’ → “all the dark circles of Hell”: the adjective moves from after the noun and its dependent phrase to before the noun.
- *Spirto non vidi* ‘a spirit I did not see’ → “I saw no spirit”: the object moves after the verb, and the negation becomes “no” modifying “spirit”.
- *in Dio* ‘toward God’ → “against God”: the relation expressed by *in* is rendered explicitly as opposition.
- *Non quel* ‘not that one’ → “not even him”: “even” is supplied, and the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.

> 16 Ei si fuggì, che non parlò più verbo:  

He fled, speaking not another word.

- *si fuggì* ‘fled away’ → “fled”: the pronominal construction becomes a plain intransitive verb, without a separate equivalent for *si*.
- *che non parlò più verbo* ‘so that he spoke no further word’ → “speaking not another word”: the finite clause becomes a participial phrase, leaving the connection expressed by *che* implicit; *più*, ‘more’ or ‘further’, is rendered by “another”.

> 17 Ed io vidi un Centauro pien di rabbia  
> 18 Venir gridando: Ov’ è, ov’ è l’ acerbo?  

And I saw a Centaur, full of rage, come crying: “Where is, where is the surly one?”

- *l’ acerbo* ‘the harsh one’ → “the surly one”: the adjective is interpreted specifically as describing surliness rather than harshness more generally.

> 19 Maremma non cred’ io che tante n’ abbia,  
> 20 Quante bisce egli avea su per la groppa,  
> 21 Infino ove comincia nostra labbia.  

Maremma, I do believe, has not so many snakes as he had on his haunch, to where our human form begins.

- *Maremma non cred’ io che tante n’ abbia, Quante bisce egli avea* ‘Maremma, I do not believe that it has as many of them as snakes he had’ → “Maremma, I do believe, has not so many snakes as he had”: the governing clause becomes an affirmative parenthesis, with emphatic “do”; its subject moves before the verb, and its negation transfers to “has”. The dependent clause becomes the main assertion, with no equivalent for *che* and with indicative “has” replacing subjunctive *abbia*. “snakes” moves from the comparative clause into the main clause, replacing the partitive *n’*, ‘of them’; the quantity comparison is consequently recast as “so many snakes as he had”.
- *su per la groppa* ‘up over the rump’ → “on his haunch”: the extended spatial expression becomes simply “on”; the definite article becomes a possessive, and “haunch” replaces ‘rump’.
- *Infino ove* ‘as far as where’ → “to where”: the fuller expression marking a limit is reduced to “to”.
- *comincia nostra labbia* ‘begins our appearance’ → “our human form begins”: the subject moves before the verb; *labbia* is interpreted as bodily “form”, and “human” is supplied.

> 22 Sopra le spalle, dietro dalla coppa,  
> 23 Con l’ ale aperte gli giaceva un draco,  

Over his shoulders, behind the head, a dragon lay with outstretched wings;

- *le spalle … gli* ‘the shoulders … on him’ → “his shoulders”: the reference expressed by the dative pronoun *gli* is absorbed into the possessive “his”, replacing the definite article.
- *dietro dalla coppa* ‘behind the nape’ → “behind the head”: the Italian construction *dietro da* becomes the simple preposition “behind”, while the more specific *coppa*, ‘nape’ or ‘back of the head’, becomes “head”.
- *Con l’ ale aperte* ‘with the wings open’ → “with outstretched wings”: the phrase moves from before the verb to after it; the article is omitted, the adjective moves before the noun, and ‘open’ becomes “outstretched”.
- *giaceva un draco* ‘lay a dragon’ → “a dragon lay”: the subject moves before the verb.

> 24 E quello affuoca qualunque s’ intoppa.  

and it sets on fire every one he meets.

- *quello* ‘that one’ → “it”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *affuoca* ‘ignites’ → “sets on fire”: a single verb becomes a verbal phrase.
- *qualunque s’ intoppa* ‘whoever comes in the way’ → “every one he meets”: the indefinite relative becomes “every one” followed by a relative clause; the pronominal encounter construction is recast with transitive “meets” and an explicit “he” as the encountering subject.

## Lines 25–37 — Cacus and Three Spirits

> 25 Lo mio Maestro disse: Quegli è Caco,  
> 26 Che sotto il sasso di monte Aventino  
> 27 Di sangue fece spesse volte laco.  

My Master said: “That is Cacus, who, beneath the rock of Mount Aventine, full often made a lake of blood.

- *Di sangue fece spesse volte laco* ‘of blood he made many times lake’ → “full often made a lake of blood”: the phrase ‘of blood’ moves after the noun it qualifies; ‘many times’ becomes the adverbial “full often” and moves before the verb; an indefinite article is supplied before “lake”.

> 28 Non va co’ suoi fratei per un cammino,  
> 29 Per lo furar frodolente ch’ ei fece  
> 30 Del grande armento ch’ egli ebbe a vicino;  

He goes not with his brethren on one same road, because of the cunning theft he made from the great herd that lay near him:

- *Non va* ‘not goes’ → “He goes not”: the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *per un cammino* ‘along one road’ → “on one same road”: “on” replaces ‘along’, and “same” explicitly marks the road as shared.
- *Per lo furar frodolente* ‘for the fraudulent stealing’ → “because of the cunning theft”: the causal force of *Per* is made explicit; the substantivized infinitive becomes the noun “theft”; ‘fraudulent’ becomes “cunning”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *ch’ ei fece* ‘that he made’ → “he made”: the object relative pronoun is omitted.
- *Del* ‘of the’ → “from the”: the herd is expressed as the source from which the theft was made.
- *ch’ egli ebbe a vicino* ‘that he had as a neighbour’ → “that lay near him”: the construction changes from his having the herd nearby to the herd’s lying nearby; the subject ‘he’ becomes the object “him”, and the neighbour expression becomes one of location.

> 31 Onde cessar le sue opere biece  
> 32 Sotto la mazza d’ Ercole, che forse  
> 33 Gliene diè cento, e non sentì le diece.  

whence his crooked actions ceased beneath the club of Hercules, who gave him perhaps a hundred blows with it; and he felt not the first ten.”

- *cessar le sue opere biece* ‘ceased his actions crooked’ → “his crooked actions ceased”: the subject moves before the verb, and the adjective moves before its noun.
- *che forse Gliene diè cento* ‘who perhaps to him of them gave a hundred’ → “who gave him perhaps a hundred blows with it”: the indirect object follows the verb instead of preceding it as a clitic, and “perhaps” moves after that object. The implied noun “blows” is supplied; the partitive *ne*, ‘of them’, is not separately retained, while “with it” explicitly connects the blows to the club.
- *e non sentì le diece* ‘and he did not feel the ten’ → “and he felt not the first ten”: the negative follows the finite verb; “first” is supplied, interpreting the definite cardinal expression as the initial ten blows.

> 34 Mentre che sì parlava, ed ei trascorse,  

Whilst he thus spake, the Centaur ran past,

- *Mentre che* ‘while that’ → “Whilst”: the two-word conjunction becomes a single conjunction.
- *ed ei trascorse* ‘and he ran past’ → “the Centaur ran past”: the conjunction is omitted, and the pronoun is replaced by an explicit identifying noun.

> 35 E tre spiriti venner sotto noi,  
> 36 De’ quai nè io, nè il Duca mio s’ accorse,  
> 37 Se non quando gridar: Chi siete voi?  

and also under us there came three spirits, whom neither I nor my Guide perceived, until they cried: “Who are ye?”

- *E* ‘and’ → “and also”: “also” is supplied.
- *tre spiriti venner sotto noi* ‘three spirits came under us’ → “under us there came three spirits”: the phrase of location moves to the front, the subject follows the verb, and presentational “there” is supplied.
- *De’ quai … s’accorse* ‘of whom … became aware’ → “whom” … “perceived”: the Italian pronominal verb with an *of*-complement becomes a simple transitive verb with a direct-object relative pronoun.
- *il Duca mio* ‘the leader my’ → “my Guide”: the possessive moves before the noun, and “Guide” renders the designation ‘leader’.
- *Se non quando* ‘except when’ → “until”: an exception introduced by ‘when’ becomes a temporal limit on the preceding failure to perceive.

## Lines 38–51 — A Pause and a Serpent’s Attack

> 38 Per che nostra novella si ristette,  
> 39 E intendemmo pure ad essi poi.  

Our story therefore paused, and we then gave heed to them alone.

- *Per che* ‘for which reason’ → “therefore”: the connective phrase becomes an adverb, moved from the beginning of the clause to after its subject.
- *nostra novella* ‘our conversation’ → “Our story”: the exchange of speech is rendered as a narrative.
- *si ristette* ‘came to a stop’ → “paused”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes a simple intransitive verb suggesting a temporary interruption.
- *intendemmo* ‘we attended’ → “we then gave heed”: the verb becomes the phrase “gave heed”, with the rendering of *poi* moved forward into it from the end of the Italian clause.
- *pure ad essi* ‘only to them’ → “to them alone”: the restrictive word follows the pronoun instead of preceding the prepositional phrase.

> 40 Io non gli conoscea; ma ei seguette,  
> 41 Come suol seguitar per alcun caso,  
> 42 Che l’ un nomare all’ altro convenette,  
> 43 Dicendo: Cianfa dove fia rimaso?  

I knew them not; but it happened, as usually it happens by some chance, that one had to name another, saying: “Where has Cianfa stopt?”

- *Io non gli conoscea* ‘I not them knew’ → “I knew them not”: both the object pronoun and the negative follow the verb rather than preceding it.
- *Come suol seguitar* ‘as it is wont to happen’ → “as usually it happens”: the verb expressing customary action becomes “usually”, while the infinitive becomes a finite verb.
- *l’ un nomare all’ altro convenette* ‘to name the one was necessary for the other’ → “one had to name another”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal one with “had to”. The person designated by *all’ altro* becomes the subject “one”; the person named, *l’ un*, becomes the object “another”.
- *Cianfa dove fia rimaso* ‘Cianfa, where will he have remained’ → “Where has Cianfa stopt?”: the question word moves before the name, and the conjectural future perfect becomes a present perfect. ‘Remained’ is rendered as “stopt”.

> 44 Perch’ io, acciocchè il Duca stesse attento,  
> 45 Mi posi il dito su dal mento al naso.  

Whereat I, in order that my Guide might stand attentive, placed my finger upwards from the chin to the nose.

- *Perch’* ‘wherefore’ → “Whereat”: the causal connection is expressed as a reaction to what has just been said.
- *il Duca* ‘the Leader’ → “my Guide”: “Guide” replaces ‘Leader’, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *stesse attento* ‘should be attentive’ → “might stand attentive”: the subjunctive is expressed through “might”, and the verb of being or remaining is rendered as “stand”.
- *Mi posi il dito* ‘I placed to myself the finger’ → “placed my finger”: the reflexive dative becomes the possessive “my” before the noun.

> 46 Se tu sei or, Lettore, a creder lento  
> 47 Ciò ch’ io dirò, non sarà maraviglia,  
> 48 Chè io, che il vidi, appena il mi consento.  

If thou art now, O Reader, slow to credit what I have to tell, it will be no wonder; for I who saw it, scarce allow it to myself.

- *Lettore* ‘Reader’ → “O Reader”: the vocative marker “O” is supplied.
- *a creder lento* ‘to believe slow’ → “slow to credit”: the adjective moves before its dependent infinitive, and ‘believe’ is rendered as “credit”.
- *Ciò ch’ io dirò* ‘that which I shall say’ → “what I have to tell”: demonstrative and relative become “what”; the future verb becomes a present construction expressing something awaiting narration.
- *non sarà maraviglia* ‘it will not be a wonder’ → “it will be no wonder”: negation moves from the verb to the noun.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *io, che il vidi* ‘I, who it saw’ → “I who saw it”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *appena il mi consento* ‘scarcely it to myself I allow’ → “scarce allow it to myself”: both pronouns move after the verb, and the reflexive dative becomes the prepositional phrase “to myself”.

> 49 Com’ io tenea levate in lor le ciglia,  

Whilst I kept gazing on them,

- *io tenea levate in lor le ciglia* ‘I kept raised towards them the eyebrows’ → “I kept gazing on them”: the construction describing the eyebrows held raised becomes one describing continued looking. The bodily detail is omitted, and the direction of the gaze is expressed with “on”.

> 50 Ed un serpente con sei piè si lancia  
> 51 Dinanzi all’ uno, e tutto a lui s’ appiglia.  

lo! a serpent with six feet darts up in front of one, and fastens itself all upon him.

- *Ed* ‘and’ → “lo!”: the conjunction becomes an exclamation drawing attention to the appearance.
- *si lancia* ‘launches itself’ → “darts up”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and “up” is supplied.
- *Dinanzi all’ uno* ‘in front of the one’ → “in front of one”: the definite article is omitted.
- *tutto a lui s’ appiglia* ‘all to him it attaches itself’ → “fastens itself all upon him”: the verb moves before both “all” and the prepositional phrase; attachment ‘to’ him is expressed as fastening “upon him”.

## Lines 52–66 — Entwining and Melting Together

> 52 Co’ piè di mezzo gli avvinse la pancia,  

With its middle feet it clasped his belly,

- *Co’ piè di mezzo* ‘with the feet of the middle’ → “With its middle feet”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and *di mezzo* becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *gli avvinse la pancia* ‘bound his belly’ → “it clasped his belly”: *avvinse* becomes “clasped” rather than ‘bound’; the possessive dative *gli* is expressed by “his” before “belly”.

> 53 E con gli anterior le braccia prese;  

with the anterior it seized his arms;

- *E con* ‘and with’ → “with”: the conjunction is omitted.
- *le braccia prese* ‘the arms it seized’ → “it seized his arms”: the object moves after the verb, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 54 Poi gli addentò e l’ una e l’ altra guancia.  

then fixed its teeth in both his cheeks.

- *gli addentò e l’ una e l’ altra guancia* ‘bit him both the one and the other cheek’ → “fixed its teeth in both his cheeks”: the verb meaning ‘bit’ is expanded into “fixed its teeth in”; the possessive dative becomes “his”, and the coordinated singular expressions become “both” with a plural noun.

> 55 Gli diretani alle cosce distese,  
> 56 E miseli la coda tr’ amendue,  
> 57 E dietro per le ren su lo ritese.  

The hinder feet it stretched along his thighs; and put its tail between the two, and bent it upwards on his loins behind.

- *Gli diretani* ‘the hinder ones’ → “The hinder feet”: the noun understood with the substantival adjective is supplied.
- *alle cosce distese* ‘to the thighs it stretched’ → “it stretched along his thighs”: the verb precedes the prepositional phrase; ‘to’ becomes “along”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *miseli la coda* ‘put to him the tail’ → “put its tail”: the dative *li* is not separately expressed, and the definite article becomes “its”.
- *tr’ amendue* ‘between both’ → “between the two”: “the two” replaces ‘both’.
- *E dietro per le ren su lo ritese* ‘and behind along the loins upward it stretched it again’ → “and bent it upwards on his loins behind”: *ritese* becomes “bent”, without separately expressing renewed stretching; “behind” moves to the end, and “upwards” follows the verb and object. *Per* becomes “on” rather than ‘along’, and the definite article becomes “his”. The translation takes the printed masculine *lo* as referring to the tail, although *coda* is feminine.

> 58 Ellera abbarbicata mai non fue  
> 59 Ad alber sì, come l’ orribil fiera  
> 60 Per l’ altrui membra avviticchiò le sue.  

Ivy was never so rooted to a tree, as round the other’s limbs the hideous monster entwined its own.

- *Ellera abbarbicata mai non fue Ad alber sì* ‘ivy rooted never was to a tree so’ → “Ivy was never so rooted to a tree”: the participle moves after the finite verb, and *sì* moves from the end to precede “rooted”. The Italian negative combination *mai non* is expressed by “never”, and an indefinite article is supplied before “tree”.
- *l’ orribil fiera* ‘the horrible beast’ → “the hideous monster”: both adjective and noun receive different lexical renderings.
- *Per l’ altrui membra* ‘over the other’s limbs’ → “round the other’s limbs”: *per* is interpreted as encirclement, and the phrase moves before the subject.

> 61 Poi s’ appiccar, come di calda cera  
> 62 Fossero stati, e mischiar lor colore;  

Then they stuck together, as if they had been of heated wax, and mingled their colours:

- *s’ appiccar* ‘attached themselves’ → “they stuck together”: the reflexive construction is rendered reciprocally through “together”, with “stuck” replacing ‘attached’.
- *come di calda cera Fossero stati* ‘as of hot wax they had been’ → “as if they had been of heated wax”: “if” makes the subjunctive comparison explicit, and the material phrase moves after the verb. *Calda*, ‘hot’, becomes the participial adjective “heated”.
- *mischiar lor colore* ‘mixed their colour’ → “mingled their colours”: “mingled” replaces ‘mixed’, and the singular *colore* becomes plural.

> 63 Nè l’ un, nè l’ altro già parea quel ch’ era;  

neither the one, nor the other, now seemed what it was at first;

- *già* ‘already’ → “now”: the adverb is interpreted as marking the present stage of the change rather than something already accomplished.
- *quel ch’ era* ‘that which it was’ → “what it was at first”: “what” combines the demonstrative and relative pronoun, and “at first” is supplied.

> 64 Come procede innanzi dall’ ardore  
> 65 Per lo papiro suso un color bruno,  
> 66 Che non è nero ancora, e il bianco muore.  

as up before the flame on paper, goes a brown colour which is not yet black, and the white dies away.

- *procede* ‘advances’ → “goes”: the more specific verb of forward movement becomes a general verb of motion, and moves after the directional and locative phrases.
- *innanzi dall’ ardore* ‘ahead of the heat’ → “before the flame”: “before” expresses spatial precedence, while “flame” replaces ‘heat’.
- *Per lo papiro* ‘along the paper’ → “on paper”: the preposition of movement along a surface becomes “on”, and the definite article is omitted.
- *suso* ‘upwards’ → “up”: the directional adverb moves from after the paper phrase to the beginning of the comparison.
- *un color bruno* ‘a colour brown’ → “a brown colour”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Che non è nero ancora* ‘which is not black yet’ → “which is not yet black”: “yet” moves before the adjective.
- *muore* ‘dies’ → “dies away”: “away” is supplied, making the verb a phrasal expression of gradual disappearance.

## Lines 67–78 — Two Shapes Become One

> 67 Gli altri duo riguardavano, e ciascuno  
> 68 Gridava: O me, Agnèl, come ti muti!  

The other two looked on, and each cried: “O me! Agnello, how thou changest!

- *riguardavano* ‘were watching’ → “looked on”: the verb is rendered by a phrasal expression, with the simple past replacing the Italian imperfect.
- *Gridava* ‘was crying’ → “cried”: the imperfect’s ongoing action is expressed by the simple past.
- *ti muti* ‘you change yourself’ → “thou changest”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, without a reflexive pronoun.

> 69 Vedi che già non se’ nè duo nè uno.  

Lo, thou art already neither two nor one!”

- *Vedi* ‘See’ → “Lo”: the imperative becomes a presentational exclamation.
- *che* ‘that’ → “”: the conjunction introducing what is to be seen is omitted.
- *già non se’ nè duo nè uno* ‘already you are not either two or one’ → “thou art already neither two nor one”: “already” moves after the verb, and the Italian negative construction is expressed by “neither … nor,” without a separate equivalent of *non*.

> 70 Già eran li duo capi un divenuti,  
> 71 Quando n’ apparver duo figure miste  
> 72 In una faccia, ov’ eran duo perduti.  

The two heads had now become one, when two shapes appeared to us mixed in one face, where both were lost.

- *Già eran li duo capi un divenuti* ‘Already had the two heads one become’ → “The two heads had now become one”: “now” replaces ‘already’ and follows the auxiliary; the subject moves before the auxiliary, and “one” follows the participle.
- *n’ apparver duo figure miste* ‘to us appeared two figures mixed’ → “two shapes appeared to us mixed”: the subject moves before the verb, and “to us” follows it; *figure* is rendered as “shapes.”
- *ov’ eran duo perduti* ‘where were two lost’ → “where both were lost”: the subject moves before the verb, and the numeral ‘two’ becomes the inclusive “both.”

> 73 Fersi le braccia duo di quattro liste;  

Two arms were made of the four lists.

- *Fersi le braccia duo* ‘were made the arms two’ → “Two arms were made”: the subject moves before the verb, the numeral precedes the noun, and the definite article is omitted.
- *di quattro liste* ‘of four strips’ → “of the four lists”: the definite article is supplied.

> 74 Le cosce con le gambe, il ventre, e il casso  
> 75 Divenner membra che non fur mai viste.  

The thighs with the legs, the belly, and the chest, became such members as were never seen.

- *membra che* ‘members that’ → “such members as”: “such” is supplied, and the direct relative construction becomes the correlative construction “such … as.”

> 76 Ogni primaio aspetto ivi era casso:  

The former shape was all extinct in them:

- *Ogni primaio aspetto ivi era casso* ‘Every original appearance there was effaced’ → “The former shape was all extinct in them”: the distributive ‘every’ becomes “The” with “all” transferred to the predicate; ‘original appearance’ becomes “former shape,” and ‘effaced’ becomes “extinct.” The locative *ivi*, ‘there,’ is interpreted as “in them” and moved to the end.

> 77 Due e nessun l’ immagine perversa  
> 78 Parea, e tal sen gìa con lento passo.  

both, and neither, the perverse image seemed; and such it went away with languid step.

- *Due e nessun* ‘Two and no one’ → “both, and neither”: the numeral and negative pronoun become paired terms referring to the two.
- *l’ immagine perversa* ‘the image perverse’ → “the perverse image”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *sen gìa* ‘was going away’ → “it went away”: the imperfect becomes the simple past; the reflexive element of the Italian pronominal verb has no separate English equivalent.
- *lento* ‘slow’ → “languid”: the adjective expresses languor rather than simply slowness.

## Lines 79–93 — The Serpent’s Bite and the Meeting Smoke

> 79 Come il ramarro, sotto la gran fersa  
> 80 Dei dì canicular, cangiando siepe,  
> 81 Folgore par, se la via attraversa:  

As the lizard, beneath the mighty scourge of the canicular days, going from hedge to hedge, appears a flash of lightning, if it cross the way;

- *ramarro* ‘green lizard’ → “lizard”: the more specific Italian noun becomes a general term.
- *gran fersa* ‘great scourge’ → “mighty scourge”: magnitude is expressed as force.
- *dì canicular* ‘days canicular’ → “canicular days”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *cangiando siepe* ‘changing hedge’ → “going from hedge to hedge”: changing one hedge for another is expanded into an explicit movement between hedges.
- *Folgore par* ‘lightning appears’ → “appears a flash of lightning”: the verb precedes its complement, and “a flash of” is supplied.
- *se la via attraversa* ‘if the way it crosses’ → “if it cross the way”: the object moves after the verb; the Italian indicative is rendered by the English subjunctive “cross”.

> 82 Così parea venendo verso l’ epe  
> 83 Degli altri due un serpentello acceso,  
> 84 Livido e nero come gran di pepe.  

so, coming towards the bowels of the other two, appeared a little reptile burning with rage, livid and black as pepper corn.

- *parea venendo verso l’ epe Degli altri due* ‘appeared coming towards the bowels of the other two’ → “coming towards the bowels of the other two, appeared”: the participial phrase moves before the main verb.
- *un serpentello* ‘a little snake’ → “a little reptile”: the diminutive ending becomes a separate adjective, and the noun becomes less specific.
- *acceso* ‘kindled’ → “burning with rage”: the participle becomes a phrase expressing an ongoing state; “with rage” supplies an interpretation not explicit in the Italian.
- *gran di pepe* ‘grain of pepper’ → “pepper corn”: the construction with *di* becomes a compound noun, with “pepper” preceding “corn”.

> 85 E quella parte, donde prima è preso  
> 86 Nostro alimento, all’ un di lor trafisse;  

And it pierced that part, in one of them, at which we first receive our nourishment;

- *quella parte … trafisse* ‘that part … pierced’ → “pierced that part”: the verb moves before its object.
- *donde prima è preso Nostro alimento* ‘whence first is taken our nourishment’ → “at which we first receive our nourishment”: the passive becomes active, with “we” supplied as the recipient; ‘whence’ becomes “at which”, and taking becomes receiving.
- *all’ un di lor* ‘to the one of them’ → “in one of them”: the dative identifying the affected person becomes a phrase locating the pierced part; the definite article before *un* is not retained.

> 87 Poi cadde giuso innanzi lui disteso.  

then fell down stretched out before him.

- *innanzi lui disteso* ‘before him stretched out’ → “stretched out before him”: the participle moves before the phrase of position.

> 88 Lo trafitto il mirò, ma nulla disse;  
> 89 Anzi co’ piè fermati sbadigliava,  
> 90 Pur come sonno o febbre l’ assalisse.  

The pierced thief gazed on it, but said nothing; nay, with his feet motionless, yawned only as if sleep or fever had come upon him.

- *Lo trafitto* ‘the pierced one’ → “The pierced thief”: the substantival participle becomes an adjective modifying the supplied noun “thief”.
- *il mirò* ‘it gazed at’ → “gazed on it”: the object pronoun moves after the verb and its English preposition.
- *nulla disse* ‘nothing said’ → “said nothing”: the object moves after the verb.
- *co’ piè fermati* ‘with the feet stopped’ → “with his feet motionless”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the participle becomes an adjective describing a state.
- *Pur come* ‘just as’ → “only as if”: the intensive *pur* is rendered as “only”, and the hypothetical force of the comparison is made explicit by “if”.
- *l’ assalisse* ‘should assail him’ → “had come upon him”: the imperfect subjunctive becomes an English past perfect; ‘assail’ becomes the less forceful phrase “come upon”, and the object pronoun follows it.

> 91 Egli il serpente, e quei lui riguardava:  

He eyed the reptile, the reptile him:

- *Egli il serpente, e quei lui riguardava* ‘he the snake, and that one him regarded’ → “He eyed the reptile, the reptile him:”: the expressed verb moves from the second clause to the first, reversing which clause leaves it understood; “eyed” renders ‘regarded’, “reptile” generalizes ‘snake’, the second “reptile” replaces the demonstrative ‘that one’, and the conjunction is omitted.

> 92 L’ un per la piaga, e l’ altro per la bocca  
> 93 Fumavan forte, e il fumo s’ incontrava.  

the one from his wound, the other from its mouth, smoked violently, and their smoke met.

- *per la piaga* ‘through the wound’ → “from his wound”: passage through an opening becomes origin from it, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *e l’ altro* ‘and the other’ → “the other”: the conjunction is omitted.
- *per la bocca* ‘through the mouth’ → “from its mouth”: passage through an opening becomes origin from it, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *forte* ‘strongly’ → “violently”: strength or intensity is expressed as violence.
- *il fumo s’ incontrava* ‘the smoke met’ → “their smoke met”: the definite article becomes a possessive identifying the smoke with its two sources.

## Lines 94–108 — Rival Poets and Exchanged Forms

> 94 Taccia Lucano omai, là dove tocca  
> 95 Del misero Sabello e di Nassidio,  

Let Lucan now be silent, where he tells of poor Sabellus and Nasidius;

- *Taccia Lucano omai* ‘Be silent Lucan now’ → “Let Lucan now be silent”: the third-person subjunctive expressing a command becomes a construction with “Let”; the subject precedes “be silent,” and “now” moves before it.
- *là dove tocca* ‘there where he touches’ → “where he tells”: *là* is omitted, and the figurative ‘touches’ becomes “tells.”
- *Del misero Sabello e di Nassidio* ‘of the poor Sabellus and of Nasidius’ → “of poor Sabellus and Nasidius”: the article before *misero Sabello* and the repeated preposition before *Nassidio* are omitted.

> 96 E attenda a udir quel ch’ or si scocca.  

and wait to hear that which is now sent forth.

- *attenda a udir* ‘attend to hearing’ → “wait to hear”: *attenda* is interpreted as waiting rather than attending or giving attention.
- *quel ch’ or si scocca* ‘that which now shoots forth’ → “that which is now sent forth”: *si scocca* is rendered passively, and the specific sense of shooting or discharging becomes the broader “sent forth.”

> 97 Taccia di Cadmo e d’ Aretusa Ovidio:  

Of Cadmus and of Arethusa be Ovid silent;

- *Taccia di Cadmo e d’ Aretusa Ovidio* ‘Be silent of Cadmus and of Arethusa Ovid’ → “Of Cadmus and of Arethusa be Ovid silent”: the prepositional phrases move before the verb, and the subject stands between “be” and “silent.”

> 98 Chè se quello in serpente, e quella in fonte  
> 99 Converte poetando, io non l’ invidio:  

for if he, poetizing, converts the one into a serpent and the other into a fount, I envy him not.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *quello in serpente, e quella in fonte Converte poetando* ‘that one into serpent, and that one into fount he converts poetizing’ → “he, poetizing, converts the one into a serpent and the other into a fount”: the objects and their complements move after the verb, while *poetando* moves before it; the gendered demonstratives become “the one” and “the other,” and indefinite articles are supplied before “serpent” and “fount.”
- *io non l’ invidio* ‘I do not envy him’ → “I envy him not”: the object pronoun and the negative, both preceding the Italian verb, follow the English verb.

> 100 Chè duo nature mai a fronte a fronte  
> 101 Non trasmutò, sì ch’ amendue le forme  
> 102 A cambiar lor materie fosser pronte.  

For never did he so transmute two natures front to front, that both forms were ready to exchange their substance.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *duo nature mai a fronte a fronte Non trasmutò* ‘two natures never front to front did he transmute’ → “never did he” and “transmute two natures front to front”: the object and the phrase *a fronte a fronte* move after the verb; *mai … Non* becomes the single negative “never,” whose initial position requires inversion with “did.”
- *sì ch’* ‘so that’ → “so” and “that”: “so” is moved into the preceding clause, before “transmute,” rather than remaining beside the conjunction.
- *amendue le forme* ‘both the forms’ → “both forms”: the definite article is omitted.
- *A cambiar lor materie fosser pronte* ‘to change their substances were ready’ → “were ready to exchange their substance”: the infinitive phrase moves after “were ready”; *cambiar* is interpreted reciprocally as “exchange,” and plural *materie* becomes singular “substance.” The Italian subjunctive *fosser* is rendered by an ordinary English past-tense construction.

> 103 Insieme si risposero a tai norme,  
> 104 Che il serpente la coda in forca fesse,  
> 105 E il feruto ristrinse insieme l’ orme.  

They mutually responded in such a way, that the reptile cleft its tail into a fork, and the wounded spirit drew his steps together.

- *Insieme si risposero* ‘together they answered one another’ → “They mutually responded”: “mutually” combines the force of *Insieme* and the reciprocal *si*, rather than rendering them separately.
- *a tai norme* ‘according to such rules’ → “in such a way”: the expression is rendered as one of manner, and the plural noun becomes singular.
- *il serpente* ‘the serpent’ → “the reptile”: a broader term replaces the specific noun.
- *la coda in forca fesse* ‘the tail into a fork cleft’ → “cleft its tail into a fork”: the verb moves before its object and complement, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *il feruto* ‘the wounded one’ → “the wounded spirit”: the substantivized adjective is expanded with the supplied noun “spirit.”
- *ristrinse insieme l’ orme* ‘drew together the footprints’ → “drew his steps together”: *orme* is interpreted as “steps,” the definite article becomes a possessive, and “together” moves after the object.

> 106 Le gambe con le cosce seco stesse  
> 107 S’ appiccar sì, che in poco la giuntura  
> 108 Non facea segno alcun che si paresse.  

The legs and the thighs along with them so stuck to one another, that soon their juncture left no mark that was discernible.

- *con le cosce seco stesse* ‘with the thighs with themselves’ → “and the thighs along with them”: *con* becomes the coordinating “and,” while the reflexive expression *seco stesse* becomes “along with them.”
- *S’ appiccar sì* ‘stuck themselves so’ → “so stuck to one another”: *si* is interpreted reciprocally as “to one another,” and “so” moves before the verb.
- *in poco* ‘in little time’ → “soon”: the prepositional expression becomes an adverb.
- *la giuntura* ‘the juncture’ → “their juncture”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Non facea segno alcun* ‘did not make any mark’ → “left no mark”: “left” replaces ‘made,’ and the verbal negative with *alcun* becomes “no” modifying “mark.”
- *che si paresse* ‘that showed itself’ → “that was discernible”: the pronominal verb becomes a copular construction with an adjective expressing perceptibility; the Italian subjunctive is not separately marked in English.

## Lines 109–123 — The Exchange of Bodily Forms

> 109 Togliea la coda fessa la figura,  
> 110 Che si perdeva là; e la sua pelle  
> 111 Si facea molle, e quella di là dura.  

The cloven tail assumed the figure that was lost in the other; and its skin grew soft, the other’s hard.

- *Togliea la coda fessa* ‘was taking the tail cloven’ → “The cloven tail assumed”: the subject moves before the verb, and the adjective before its noun; ‘taking’ becomes “assumed,” and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *Che si perdeva là* ‘which was being lost there’ → “that was lost in the other”: the imperfect’s ongoing action is not explicit; the deictic ‘there’ is interpreted as “in the other.”
- *Si facea molle* ‘made itself soft’ → “grew soft”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive expression of change.
- *quella di là dura* ‘that of there hard’ → “the other’s hard”: the demonstrative referring to the skin and the deictic expression identifying its possessor are compressed into a possessive.

> 112 Io vidi entrar le braccia per l’ ascelle,  
> 113 E i duo piè della fiera, ch’ eran corti,  
> 114 Tanto allungar, quanto accorciavan quelle.  

I saw the arms enter at the armpits, and the two feet of the brute, which were short, lengthen themselves as much as those arms were shortened.

- *entrar le braccia* ‘enter the arms’ → “the arms enter”: the subject of the infinitive moves before it.
- *per l’ ascelle* ‘through the armpits’ → “at the armpits”: a preposition expressing passage becomes one expressing location.
- *della fiera* ‘of the wild beast’ → “of the brute”: “brute” replaces the more explicitly animal designation.
- *Tanto allungar* ‘so much to lengthen’ → “lengthen themselves as much”: the expression of degree moves after the verb, and the intransitive infinitive is rendered with a reflexive object.
- *quanto accorciavan quelle* ‘as were shortening those’ → “as those arms were shortened”: the demonstrative is expanded by supplying “arms,” the subject moves before the verb, and an intransitive imperfect becomes a simple-past passive.

> 115 Poscia li piè dirietro insieme attorti  
> 116 Diventaron lo membro che l’ uom cela,  
> 117 E il misero del suo n’ avea duo porti.  

Then the two hinder feet, twisted together, became the member which man conceals; and the wretch from his had two thrust forth.

- *li piè dirietro* ‘the feet behind’ → “the two hinder feet”: “two” is supplied, and the postposed expression ‘behind’ becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *insieme attorti* ‘together twisted’ → “twisted together”: the participle and adverb exchange places.
- *che l’ uom cela* ‘which the man conceals’ → “which man conceals”: the Italian definite article marking a generic noun is omitted in English.
- *il misero* ‘the wretched one’ → “the wretch”: a substantivized adjective becomes a noun.
- *del suo n’ avea duo porti* ‘from his had two of them put forth’ → “from his had two thrust forth”: the pronominal *n’* is not separately represented, the imperfect becomes a simple past, and ‘put forth’ is rendered more forcefully as “thrust forth.”

> 118 Mentre che il fummo l’ uno e l’ altro vela  
> 119 Di color nuovo, e genera il pel suso  
> 120 Per l’ una parte, e dall’ altra il dipela,  

Whilst the smoke with a new colour veils them both, and generates on one part hair, and strips it from another;

- *l’ uno e l’ altro vela* ‘the one and the other veils’ → “veils them both”: the object moves after the verb, and the paired singular expressions become a plural pronoun with “both.”
- *Di color nuovo* ‘with colour new’ → “with a new colour”: an indefinite article is supplied, the adjective moves before its noun, and the whole phrase moves before the verb it modifies.
- *genera il pel suso Per l’ una parte* ‘generates the hair above on the one part’ → “generates on one part hair”: “on one part” moves before the object; the definite articles are omitted, and *suso*, ‘above,’ has no explicit rendering.
- *dall’ altra il dipela* ‘from the other plucks it off’ → “strips it from another”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb and object, ‘the other’ becomes the indefinite “another,” and the hair-removing verb is rendered by the more general “strips.”

> 121 L’ un si levò, e l’ altro cadde giuso,  
> 122 Non torcendo però le lucerne empie,  
> 123 Sotto le quai ciascun cambiava muso.  

the one rose upright, and prostrate the other fell, not therefore turning the impious lights, under which they mutually exchanged visages:

- *si levò* ‘raised itself’ → “rose upright”: the reflexive verb becomes intransitive, and “upright” makes the resulting posture explicit.
- *e l’ altro cadde giuso* ‘and the other fell down’ → “and prostrate the other fell”: the directional adverb becomes an adjective expressing the resulting posture and moves before the subject.
- *Non torcendo però* ‘not turning however’ → “not therefore turning”: *però* is rendered as “therefore” rather than the concessive ‘however’; within the negative phrase, this conveys ‘not on that account.’ The adverb also moves before the participle.
- *le lucerne empie* ‘the lamps impious’ → “the impious lights”: “lights” replaces the more concrete ‘lamps,’ and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *ciascun cambiava muso* ‘each was changing muzzle’ → “they mutually exchanged visages”: the distributive singular becomes a plural subject, ‘changing’ becomes reciprocal exchange, and “mutually” makes that reciprocity explicit. The imperfect becomes a simple past, while singular *muso* becomes plural “visages,” replacing the animal-associated noun with a more elevated term.

## Lines 124–138 — The Exchange of Faces

> 124 Quel ch’ era dritto, il trasse in vêr le tempie;  

he that was erect, drew his towards the temples;

- *il trasse* ‘it he drew’ → “drew his”: the object pronoun becomes a possessive standing for the previously mentioned feature, and follows the verb.

> 125 E di troppa materia, che in là venne,  
> 126 Uscir gli orecchi delle gote scempie:  

and from the too much matter that went thither, ears came out of the smooth cheeks.

- *di troppa materia* ‘from too much matter’ → “from the too much matter”: the definite article is supplied.
- *che in là venne* ‘that thither came’ → “that went thither”: “went” replaces ‘came’, and the directional adverb follows the verb.
- *Uscir gli orecchi* ‘came out the ears’ → “ears came out”: the subject moves before the verb, and its definite article is omitted.
- *delle gote scempie* ‘from the bare cheeks’ → “of the smooth cheeks”: *scempie* is interpreted as “smooth”; the adjective precedes its noun, and the preposition becomes “of” within the English construction “came out of”.

> 127 Ciò che non corse in dietro, e si ritenne,  
> 128 Di quel soverchio fe’ naso alla faccia,  
> 129 E le labbra ingrossò quanto convenne.  

That which went not back, but was retained, of its superfluity formed a nose, and enlarged the lips to a fit size.

- *non corse in dietro* ‘did not run backwards’ → “went not back”: ‘ran’ becomes the less specific “went”, and the negative follows the main verb.
- *e si ritenne* ‘and held itself back’ → “but was retained”: the conjunction becomes adversative, and the reflexive construction becomes passive.
- *Di quel soverchio* ‘of that excess’ → “of its superfluity”: the demonstrative becomes a possessive. The English attaches the phrase to “formed”, expressing the material used, rather than retaining the Italian partitive connection with *Ciò*.
- *fe’ naso alla faccia* ‘made nose for the face’ → “formed a nose”: “formed” replaces ‘made’, an indefinite article is supplied, and *alla faccia* is omitted.
- *le labbra ingrossò* ‘the lips it thickened’ → “enlarged the lips”: the object moves after the verb, and thickening becomes the more general enlargement.
- *quanto convenne* ‘as much as was fitting’ → “to a fit size”: a clause expressing degree becomes a prepositional phrase, with “size” supplied.

> 130 Quel, che giaceva, il muso innanzi caccia,  
> 131 E gli orecchi ritira per la testa,  
> 132 Come face le corna la lumaccia:  

He that lay prone, thrusts forward his sharpened visage, and draws back his ears into the head, as the snail does its horns;

- *giaceva* ‘lay’ → “lay prone”: “prone” explicitly supplies the position.
- *il muso innanzi caccia* ‘the muzzle forward thrusts’ → “thrusts forward his sharpened visage”: the verb moves before the adverb and object; the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘muzzle’ becomes the descriptive phrase “sharpened visage”.
- *gli orecchi ritira* ‘the ears draws back’ → “draws back his ears”: the object follows the verb, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *per la testa* ‘through the head’ → “into the head”: the preposition is interpreted as expressing inward movement rather than passage through.
- *Come face le corna la lumaccia* ‘as does the horns the snail’ → “as the snail does its horns”: the subject moves before the verb and object, and the article before ‘horns’ becomes a possessive.

> 133 E la lingua, che aveva unita e presta  
> 134 Prima a parlar, si fende; e la forcuta  
> 135 Nell’ altro si richiude, e il fummo resta.  

and his tongue, which was before united and apt for speech, cleaves itself; and in the other the forked tongue recloses: and the smoke now rests.

- *la lingua* ‘the tongue’ → “his tongue”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *che aveva unita e presta Prima a parlar* ‘which he had united and ready before to speak’ → “which was before united and apt for speech”: the relative pronoun changes from the object of ‘had’ to the subject of “was”, removing the possessor from the construction. “before” moves ahead of the adjectives, and ‘ready to speak’ becomes “apt for speech”, replacing an infinitive with a prepositional phrase containing a noun.
- *e la forcuta Nell’ altro si richiude* ‘and the forked one in the other closes itself again’ → “and in the other the forked tongue recloses”: the locative phrase moves before the subject; the substantival adjective is expanded with “tongue”; and the reflexive construction becomes the intransitive “recloses”.
- *resta* ‘ceases’ → “now rests”: cessation is expressed as resting, and “now” is supplied.

> 136 L’ anima, ch’ era fiera divenuta,  
> 137 Si fugge sufolando per la valle,  

The soul that had become a brute, flies hissing along the valley,

- *ch’ era fiera divenuta* ‘that was beast become’ → “that had become a brute”: the Italian pluperfect with *essere* takes English “had”; the predicative noun moves after the participle, gains an indefinite article, and becomes “brute” rather than ‘beast’.
- *Si fugge* ‘flees away’ → “flies”: the pronominal construction becomes a simple English verb, with “flies” used in the sense of fleeing.
- *per la valle* ‘through the valley’ → “along the valley”: passage through becomes movement along.

> 138 E l’ altro dietro a lui parlando sputa.  

and after it the other talking and sputtering.

- *E l’ altro dietro a lui* ‘and the other behind him’ → “and after it the other”: the prepositional phrase moves before the subject; ‘behind’ becomes “after”, and the masculine pronoun becomes neuter “it”.
- *parlando sputa* ‘speaking spits’ → “talking and sputtering”: the subordinate gerund and finite verb become two coordinated participles, with “and” supplied and no finite verb expressed. ‘Spits’ also becomes “sputtering”.

## Lines 139–151 — Transformations and Recognitions

> 139 Poscia gli volse le novelle spalle,  

Then he turned his novel shoulders towards it,

- *gli* ‘to him’ → “towards it”: the masculine dative pronoun becomes a directional phrase with “it”, placed after the object rather than before the verb.
- *le novelle spalle* ‘the novel shoulders’ → “his novel shoulders”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 140 E disse all’ altro: I’ vo’ che Buoso corra,  
> 141 Com’ ho fatt’ io, carpon per questo calle.  

and said to the other: “Buoso shall run crawling, as I have done, along this road!”

- *I’ vo’ che Buoso corra* ‘I want Buoso to run’ → “Buoso shall run”: the statement of the speaker’s wish, governing a subjunctive clause, becomes an independent declaration with “shall”; the explicit first-person subject and verb of wishing disappear.
- *Com’ ho fatt’ io* ‘as have done I’ → “as I have done”: the explicit subject moves before the auxiliary.
- *carpon* ‘on all fours’ → “crawling”: an adverb becomes a participle, and moves before the comparison rather than following it.
- *per questo calle* ‘through this path’ → “along this road”: the route is expressed with “along”, and *calle* is rendered as “road” rather than ‘path’.

> 142 Così vid’ io la settima zavorra  
> 143 Mutare e trasmutare; e qui mi scusi  
> 144 La novità, se fior la lingua abborra.  

Thus I beheld the seventh ballast change and re-change; and here let the novelty excuse me, if my tongue goes aught astray.

- *Così vid’ io* ‘thus saw I’ → “Thus I beheld”: “beheld” replaces ‘saw’, and the explicit subject moves before the verb.
- *trasmutare* ‘transform’ → “re-change”: the translation presents transformation as another or repeated change.
- *e qui mi scusi La novità* ‘and here may the novelty excuse me’ → “and here let the novelty excuse me”: the optative subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”; the subject moves before the verb, and the object pronoun follows it.
- *fior la lingua abborra* ‘at all the tongue strays’ → “my tongue goes aught astray”: the definite article becomes a possessive; *fior*, an adverbial expression meaning ‘at all’, becomes “aught” and moves into the verbal phrase; the single verb becomes “goes” with “astray”.

> 145 E avvegnachè gli occhi miei confusi  
> 146 Fossero alquanto, e l’ animo smagato,  
> 147 Non poter quei fuggirsi tanto chiusi,  
> 148 Ch’ io non scorgessi ben Puccio Sciancato:  

And though my eyes were somewhat perplexed, and my mind dismayed, they could not flee so covertly, but that I well distinguished Puccio Sciancato:

- *gli occhi miei* ‘the eyes mine’ → “my eyes”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *confusi Fossero alquanto* ‘perplexed were somewhat’ → “were somewhat perplexed”: the predicative adjective moves after the verb and its modifying adverb; the Italian concessive subjunctive is rendered by an English indicative.
- *Non poter quei* ‘not could those’ → “they could not”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun and moves before the modal; the negative moves after it.
- *fuggirsi* ‘get themselves away’ → “flee”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes a simple intransitive verb, without a separate equivalent for *si*.
- *tanto chiusi* ‘so concealed’ → “so covertly”: an adjective describing the subjects becomes an adverb describing their flight.
- *Ch’ io non scorgessi ben* ‘that I should not discern well’ → “but that I well distinguished”: the negative result clause becomes an exceptive clause with “but that”, without a separate negative; the subjunctive becomes an indicative, “distinguished” renders *scorgessi*, and “well” moves before the verb.

> 149 Ed era quei che sol, de’ tre compagni  
> 150 Che venner prima, non era mutato:  

and it was he alone, of the three companions that first came, who was not changed.

- *Ed era quei che sol* ‘and he was the one who alone’ → “and it was he alone” and “who”: the identification becomes an English cleft construction with introductory “it”; “alone” moves out of the relative clause to follow “he”.
- *Che venner prima* ‘that came first’ → “that first came”: the adverb moves before the verb.

> 151 L’ altro era quel che tu, Gaville, piagni.  

The other was he whom thou, Gaville, lamentest.

- *quel* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.

## Summary — Close Detail, Recast Syntax

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s sequence of details, comparisons, and bodily descriptions, but regularly rearranges its syntax into English prose. Subjects placed after verbs move before them, objects generally follow their verbs, and adjectives precede their nouns: *un color bruno* becomes “a brown colour”. Longer phrases also change position to clarify their attachment. Yet some unusual arrangements remain, as in “The hinder feet it stretched along his thighs”, preserving the Italian’s initial emphasis on the object.

Changes of construction often go beyond rearrangement. *mi fur le serpi amiche*, ‘the serpents were friendly to me’, becomes “the serpents were my friends”; possessive datives elsewhere become possessive adjectives, as when *gli avvinse la pancia* becomes “it clasped his belly”. Reflexive and pronominal verbs frequently become plain English verbs, while finite clauses sometimes become participial phrases: *che non parlò più verbo* becomes “speaking not another word”. More decisively, *Io non vo’ che più diche* becomes “Thou shalt speak no farther!”, replacing an expressed wish with a direct prohibition. Italian subjunctives receive varied treatment through English auxiliaries, constructions with “let”, or ordinary past-tense forms.

The vocabulary combines close, sometimes conspicuously literal equivalents with interpretation and expansion. “canicular days”, “impious lights”, and “seventh ballast” retain distinctive features of the Italian diction. Elsewhere, *serpente* becomes the broader “reptile”, *muso* becomes “visages”, and *lento* becomes “languid”. Supplied words can sharpen or redirect the sense: *acceso* becomes “burning with rage”, *nostra labbia* becomes “our human form”, and *le diece* becomes “the first ten”. The rendering “it tied him again” also gives the prefix of *rilegollo* an explicitly repetitive force.

Additions frequently make implicit references explicit: *una* becomes “one of them”, and substantival adjectives acquire nouns such as “thief”, “spirit”, or “tongue”. Conversely, conjunctions, repeated prepositions, and some spatial expressions disappear; *alla faccia*, for example, has no equivalent in “formed a nose”. Alongside these individually noted departures, two routine adjustments run throughout: English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits Italian articles before possessives, as in *Lo mio Maestro*, “My Master”. Overall, the translation preserves much of the Italian’s concrete wording and rhetorical force while repeatedly recasting its grammatical relationships and making its compressed expressions more explicit.
