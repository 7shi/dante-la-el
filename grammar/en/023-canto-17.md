# Inferno 17

## Lines 1–13 — The Beast Comes Ashore

> 1 Ecco la fiera con la coda aguzza,  
> 2 Che passa i monti, e rompe mura ed armi;  

“Behold the savage beast with the pointed tail, that passes mountains, and breaks through walls and weapons!

- *fiera* ‘beast’ → “savage beast”: the adjective makes explicit the wildness implicit in the Italian noun.
- *coda aguzza* ‘tail pointed’ → “pointed tail”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *i monti* ‘the mountains’ → “mountains”: the definite article is omitted.
- *rompe* ‘breaks’ → “breaks through”: the added particle presents the breaking as penetration.

> 3 Ecco colei che tutto il mondo appuzza:  

Behold him that pollutes the whole world.”

- *colei che* ‘she who’ → “him that”: the feminine pronoun becomes masculine and takes the English object form after “Behold”; the relative pronoun becomes “that”.
- *tutto il mondo appuzza* ‘all the world makes stink’ → “pollutes the whole world”: the object moves after the verb; ‘all’ becomes the adjective “whole”, placed after the article; the specific action of making something stink becomes the broader “pollutes”.

> 4 Sì cominciò lo mio Duca a parlarmi;  

Thus began my Guide to speak to me;

- *Duca* ‘leader’ → “Guide”: leadership is rendered more specifically as guidance.

> 5 Ed accennolle che venisse a proda,  
> 6 Vicino al fin de’ passeggiati marmi:  

and beckoned him to come ashore, near the end of our rocky path.

- *accennolle* ‘made a sign to her’ → “beckoned him”: the gesture is specified as beckoning, and the feminine indirect-object pronoun becomes a masculine direct object.
- *che venisse* ‘that she should come’ → “to come”: a finite subordinate clause with an imperfect subjunctive becomes an infinitive dependent on “beckoned”.
- *a proda* ‘to the bank’ → “ashore”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *de’ passeggiati marmi* ‘of the traversed marbles’ → “of our rocky path”: the plural noun for marble or stone becomes a singular “path” qualified by “rocky”; the participle describing the stones as traversed is not explicitly rendered, while “our” is supplied.

> 7 E quella sozza imagine di froda,  
> 8 Sen venne, ed arrivò la testa e il busto;  
> 9 Ma in su la riva non trasse la coda.  

And that uncleanly image of Fraud came onward, and landed his head and bust, but drew not his tail upon the bank.

- *Sen venne* ‘came along’ → “came onward”: the Italian pronominal expression becomes a plain verb with a directional adverb; its reflexive element has no separate English equivalent.
- *la testa e il busto* ‘the head and the bust’ → “his head and bust”: the two definite articles are replaced by one possessive specifying the owner.
- *in su la riva non trasse la coda* ‘upon the bank did not draw the tail’ → “drew not his tail upon the bank”: the phrase of place moves after the verb and object; the negative follows rather than precedes the verb; and the definite article before ‘tail’ becomes a possessive.

> 10 La faccia sua era faccia d’ uom giusto,  
> 11 Tanto benigna avea di fuor la pelle;  
> 12 E d’ un serpente tutto l’ altro fusto.  
> 13 Duo branche avea pilose infin l’ ascelle:  

His face was the face of a just man, so mild an aspect had it outwardly; and the rest was all a reptile’s body.

- *La faccia sua* ‘the face his’ → “His face”: the possessive moves before the noun, replacing the article.
- *faccia d’ uom giusto* ‘face of man just’ → “the face of a just man”: English supplies definite and indefinite articles, and the adjective moves before ‘man’.
- *Tanto benigna avea di fuor la pelle* ‘so benign had he on the outside the skin’ → “so mild an aspect had it outwardly”: ‘skin’ becomes “aspect”, shifting from the bodily surface to its appearance, and ‘benign’ becomes “mild”. The noun is brought forward to join its adjective in “so mild an aspect”, with an indefinite article supplied; the phrase ‘on the outside’ becomes the adverb “outwardly” and moves to the end.
- *E d’ un serpente tutto l’ altro fusto* ‘and of a serpent all the other trunk’ → “and the rest was all a reptile’s body”: the elliptical Italian gains “was”; ‘other’ is recast as the noun phrase “the rest”; and the phrase expressing possession moves before ‘body’ as an English possessive. ‘Serpent’ becomes the broader “reptile”, and ‘trunk’ becomes “body”.
- *Duo branche avea pilose infin l’ ascelle* ‘two paws had he, hairy up to the armpits’ → no English counterpart: line 13 is not rendered in the supplied English extract.

## Lines 14–27 — The Beast’s Markings and Posture

> 14 Lo dosso, e il petto, ed ambedue le coste  
> 15 Dipinte avea di nodi e di rotelle.  

He had two paws, hairy to the armpits; the neck, and the breast, and both the flanks, were painted with knots and circlets.

- *Lo dosso* ‘the back’ → “the neck”: the translation names a different part of the body.
- *Dipinte avea* ‘painted he had’ → “were painted”: the active possessive construction becomes passive, with the body parts as subject. The translation also prefixes “He had two paws, hairy to the armpits;”, which has no counterpart in the supplied lines: it renders the preceding Italian line.
- *e di rotelle* ‘and with little wheels’ → “and circlets”: the repeated preposition is omitted, and the wheel-shaped markings become small circles.

> 16 Con più color, sommesse e soprapposte  
> 17 Non fer mai in drappo Tartari nè Turchi,  
> 18 Nè fur tai tele per Aragne imposte.  

Never did Tartars or Turks with more colours make ground or broidery in cloth; nor by Arachne were such webs laid on her loom.

- *Con più color, sommesse e soprapposte / Non fer mai in drappo Tartari nè Turchi* ‘with more colours, things laid beneath and laid above never made in cloth Tartars nor Turks’ → “Never did Tartars or Turks with more colours make ground or broidery in cloth”: the colour phrase moves from the beginning of the clause to a position after the subject. Substantivized participles become the technical textile nouns “ground” and “broidery”, and their conjunction ‘and’ becomes “or”. The simple past becomes an auxiliary construction, with “did” before the subject. The cloth phrase moves from before the subject to after the object. The subject moves before the main verb, and the negative coordination becomes “or” under “Never”.
- *Nè fur tai tele per Aragne imposte* ‘nor were such webs by Arachne laid on’ → “nor by Arachne were such webs laid on her loom”: the agent moves from after the subject to before the verb, and the translation supplies the loom and its possessive.

> 19 Come tal volta stanno a riva i burchi,  
> 20 Che parte sono in acqua e parte in terra,  

As at times the wherries lie on shore, that are part in water and part on land;

- *stanno a riva i burchi* ‘stand at the shore the barges’ → “the wherries lie on shore”: the subject moves before the verb; the general verb of remaining or standing becomes “lie”; and the boats are designated as “wherries” rather than barges.
- *Che parte sono* ‘that in part are’ → “that are part”: the verb moves before the first indication of how the boats are divided between water and land.

> 21 E come là tra li Tedeschi lurchi  
> 22 Lo bevero s’ assetta a far sua guerra;  

and as there amongst the guzzling Germans, the beaver adjusts himself to wage his war;

- *li Tedeschi lurchi* ‘the Germans gluttonous’ → “the guzzling Germans”: the adjective moves before the noun, and a term for voracious consumption is rendered with a word especially suggestive of drinking.
- *s’ assetta* ‘itself positions’ → “adjusts himself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb, and the animal receives the personal pronoun “himself”.
- *a far sua guerra* ‘to make his war’ → “to wage his war”: “wage” replaces ‘make’ in the English idiom.

> 23 Così la fiera pessima si stava  
> 24 Su l’ orlo, che di pietra il sabbion serra.  

so lay that worst of savage beasts upon the brim which closes the great sand with stone.

- *la fiera pessima* ‘the worst wild beast’ → “that worst of savage beasts”: the definite article becomes demonstrative, and the singular noun with its superlative adjective becomes a superlative followed by a plural partitive phrase.
- *si stava* ‘remained’ → “lay”: the verb specifies a posture rather than simply remaining in place, and moves before the subject.
- *che di pietra il sabbion serra* ‘which with stone the sandy expanse encloses’ → “which closes the great sand with stone”: the verb moves before its object, while the material phrase moves to the end. The expansive or augmentative force of *sabbion* is expressed through “great sand”, and ‘encloses’ becomes “closes”.

> 25 Nel vano tutta sua coda guizzava,  
> 26 Torcendo in su la venenosa forca,  
> 27 Che, a guisa di scorpion, la punta armava.  

In the void glanced all his tail, twisting upwards the venomed fork, which, as in scorpions, armed the point.

- *tutta sua coda guizzava* ‘all his tail darted about’ → “glanced all his tail”: the verb moves before the subject, and the tail’s quick movement is rendered by “glanced”.
- *venenosa* ‘venomous’ → “venomed”: the adjective becomes a participial form meaning furnished with venom.
- *a guisa di scorpion* ‘in the manner of a scorpion’ → “as in scorpions”: the comparison of manner becomes a comparison with what occurs in scorpions, and the singular becomes plural.
- *la punta armava* ‘the point armed’ → “armed the point”: the verb moves before its object.

## Lines 28–40 — A Detour and Brief Talk

> 28 Lo Duca disse: Or convien che si torca  
> 29 La nostra via un poco, infino a quella  
> 30 Bestia malvagia, che colà si corca.  

My Guide said: “Now must we bend our way a little, to that wicked brute which couches there.”

- *Lo Duca* ‘the leader’ → “My Guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”.
- *convien che si torca La nostra via* ‘it is necessary that our way be turned’ → “must we bend our way”: the impersonal construction with a subordinate subjunctive becomes a modal construction; the travellers become the subject acting upon their way.
- *infino a* ‘as far as’ → “to”: the explicit indication of extent is reduced to a simple preposition.
- *Bestia malvagia* ‘beast wicked’ → “wicked brute”: “brute” replaces ‘beast’, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *che colà si corca* ‘which there lays itself down’ → “which couches there”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and the adverb moves after it.

> 31 Però scendemmo alla destra mammella,  
> 32 E dieci passi femmo in sullo stremo,  
> 33 Per ben cessar la rena e la fiammella:  

Then we descended on the right, and made ten paces towards the edge, that we might quite avoid the sand and flames.

- *Però* ‘therefore’ → “Then”: a connective expressing consequence becomes one expressing sequence.
- *alla destra mammella* ‘to the right breast’ → “on the right”: the bodily expression is omitted, leaving its directional meaning.
- *dieci passi femmo* ‘ten paces we made’ → “made ten paces”: the object moves after the verb.
- *in sullo stremo* ‘upon the edge’ → “towards the edge”: position on the edge is rendered as movement towards it.
- *Per ben cessar* ‘in order well to avoid’ → “that we might quite avoid”: the purpose infinitive becomes a finite clause with “might”; *ben* is rendered as the intensifier “quite”.
- *la fiammella* ‘the little flame’ → “flames”: the singular becomes plural, and the diminutive and definite article are omitted.

> 34 E quando noi a lei venuti semo,  
> 35 Poco più oltre veggio in su la rena  
> 36 Gente seder propinqua al luogo scemo.  

And when we came to him, I saw upon the sand, a little farther onwards, people sitting near the empty space.

- *noi a lei venuti semo* ‘we to her have come’ → “we came to him”: the compound perfect becomes a simple past, the directional phrase moves after the verb, and the grammatically feminine pronoun referring to *Bestia* becomes masculine.
- *Poco più oltre* ‘a little farther on’ → “a little farther onwards”: the phrase moves from before the verb of seeing to after “upon the sand”.
- *veggio* ‘I see’ → “I saw”: the present tense becomes past.
- *Gente seder* ‘people to sit’ → “people sitting”: the infinitive dependent on a verb of perception becomes a present participle.
- *propinqua al luogo scemo* ‘close to the diminished place’ → “near the empty space”: the adjective *propinqua*, agreeing with *Gente*, is represented by the preposition “near”; *luogo* becomes “space”, and *scemo* is interpreted as “empty”, with the adjective placed before the noun.

> 37 Quivi il Maestro: Acciocchè tutta piena  
> 38 Esperïenza d’ esto giron porti,  
> 39 Mi disse, or va, e vedi la lor mena.  

Here my Master said to me: “That thou mayest carry full experience of this round, go now and see the state of these.

- *Quivi* ‘there’ → “Here”: the spatial reference changes from ‘there’ to “Here”.
- *il Maestro … Mi disse* ‘the Master … to me said’ → “my Master said to me”: the article becomes a possessive, the separated parts of the reporting clause are brought together before the speech, and “to me” follows the verb.
- *Acciocchè … porti* ‘so that … thou mayest carry’ → “That thou mayest carry”: the purpose conjunction is rendered by “That”, and the clause-final verb moves before its object; the Italian subjunctive is expressed with “mayest”.
- *tutta piena Esperïenza* ‘wholly full experience’ → “full experience”: the intensifier *tutta* is omitted.
- *d’ esto giron* ‘of this circuit’ → “of this round”: “round” is used for *giron*.
- *or va* ‘now go’ → “go now”: the adverb moves after the imperative.
- *la lor mena* ‘their behaviour’ → “the state of these”: behaviour or manner becomes “state”, and the possessive becomes a following phrase with the demonstrative “these”.

> 40 Li tuoi ragionamenti sien là corti:  

Let thy talk with them be brief.

- *Li tuoi ragionamenti* ‘thy discourses’ → “thy talk”: the plural noun becomes a singular collective expression.
- *sien … corti* ‘may they be … short’ → “Let” and “be brief”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”, and ‘short’ is rendered as “brief”.
- *là* ‘there’ → “with them”: the indication of place becomes an explicit reference to the interlocutors, attached to “talk” rather than following the verb.

## Lines 41–51 — Strong Shoulders and Suffering Souls

> 41 Mentre che torni, parlerò con questa,  
> 42 Che ne conceda i suoi omeri forti.  

Till thou returnest, I will speak with this beast, that he may lend us his strong shoulders.”

- *Mentre che* ‘while’ → “Till”: the temporal expression is interpreted as marking an endpoint rather than simultaneous action.
- *questa* ‘this one’ → “this beast”: the demonstrative pronoun is expanded by supplying “beast”.
- *Che ne conceda* ‘that to us he may grant’ → “that he may lend us”: granting becomes lending, and the object pronoun follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *i suoi omeri forti* ‘his shoulders strong’ → “his strong shoulders”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 43 Così ancor, su per la strema testa  
> 44 Di quel settimo cerchio, tutto solo  
> 45 Andai ove sedea la gente mesta.  

Thus also, on the utmost limit of that seventh circle, all alone I went to where the woful folk were seated.

- *su per la strema testa* ‘up along the extreme head’ → “on the utmost limit”: the spatial expression becomes simply “on”, and the figurative ‘head’ is rendered as “limit”.
- *Andai ove* ‘I went where’ → “I went to where”: “to” is supplied to mark the destination explicitly.
- *sedea la gente mesta* ‘was sitting the folk sad’ → “the woful folk were seated”: the subject moves before the verb, and its adjective moves before the noun; ‘sad’ becomes “woful”, and ‘was sitting’ becomes “were seated”, with plural agreement replacing the Italian collective singular.

> 46 Per gli occhi fuori scoppiava lor duolo:  

Through the eyes their grief was bursting forth;

- *fuori scoppiava lor duolo* ‘out was bursting their grief’ → “their grief was bursting forth”: the subject moves before the verb, while *fuori* moves after it and becomes “forth”.

> 47 Di quà, di là soccorrien con le mani,  
> 48 Quando a’ vapori, e quando al caldo suolo.  

on this side, on that, they with their hands kept warding off, sometimes the flames, sometimes the burning soil.

- *Di quà, di là* ‘here, there’ → “on this side, on that”: the paired locative expressions are expanded into phrases with “on” and an explicit “side” in the first.
- *soccorrien con le mani* ‘they were coming to aid with the hands’ → “they with their hands kept warding off”: the verb is recast as a defensive action, “kept” makes its repeated character explicit, the phrase describing the means moves before the verb, and “their” replaces the definite article.
- *Quando … e quando* ‘when … and when’ → “sometimes” … “sometimes”: the paired expressions become adverbs of intermittent action, and the conjunction is omitted.
- *a’ vapori* ‘to the vapours’ → “the flames”: “flames” interprets *vapori*, and the preposition disappears because the phrase becomes a direct object of “warding off”.
- *al caldo suolo* ‘to the hot ground’ → “the burning soil”: the preposition likewise disappears; “burning” replaces ‘hot’ and moves before the noun.

> 49 Non altrimenti fan di state i cani,  
> 50 Or col ceffo or col piè, quando son morsi  
> 51 O da pulci o da mosche o da tafani.  

Not otherwise the dogs in summer do, now with snout, now with paw, when they are bitten by fleas, or flies, or breeses.

- *fan di state i cani* ‘do in summer the dogs’ → “the dogs in summer do”: the subject moves before both the temporal phrase and the verb.
- *col ceffo* ‘with the snout’ → “with snout”: the definite article is omitted.
- *col piè* ‘with the foot’ → “with paw”: “paw” specifies the animal’s foot, and the definite article is omitted.
- *O da pulci o da mosche o da tafani* ‘either by fleas or by flies or by breeses’ → “by fleas, or flies, or breeses”: the initial disjunctive marker is omitted, and “by” is expressed only once rather than repeated with each noun.

## Lines 52–69 — Pouches and Their Emblems

> 52 Poi che nel viso a certi gli occhi porsi,  
> 53 Ne’ quali il doloroso fuoco casca,  

After I had set my eyes upon the visages of several on whom the dolorous fire falls,

- *gli occhi porsi* ‘the eyes I set’ → “I had set my eyes”: the simple past becomes a past perfect; the object follows the verb, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *nel viso a certi* ‘on the face of certain ones’ → “upon the visages of several”: the singular face becomes plural, and the indefinite *certi* is rendered quantitatively as “several”.
- *Ne’ quali* ‘in whom’ → “on whom”: the preposition is rendered as indicating where the fire falls.

> 54 Non ne conobbi alcun; ma io m’ accorsi  

I knew not any of them; but I observed

- *Non ne conobbi alcun* ‘not of them I recognized any’ → “I knew not any of them”: recognition becomes knowing; the negative follows the verb, and the equivalent of *ne* follows “any”.
- *io m’ accorsi* ‘I became aware’ → “I observed”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes a simple English verb of observation.

> 55 Che dal collo a ciascun pendea una tasca,  
> 56 Ch’ avea certo colore e certo segno,  
> 57 E quindi par che il loro occhio si pasca.  

that from the neck of each there hung a pouch, which had a certain colour and a certain impress, and thereon it seems their eye is feasting.

- *dal collo a ciascun* ‘from the neck to each’ → “from the neck of each”: the Italian construction assigning a neck to each person becomes an English possessive construction.
- *pendea una tasca* ‘hung a pouch’ → “there hung a pouch”: introductory “there” is supplied.
- *certo colore e certo segno* ‘certain colour and certain sign’ → “a certain colour and a certain impress”: indefinite articles are supplied, and the general word for a sign or mark becomes “impress”.
- *quindi* ‘from there’ → “thereon”: the Italian expression of source becomes an expression of what the eye feeds upon.
- *par che il loro occhio si pasca* ‘it seems that their eye feeds itself’ → “it seems their eye is feasting”: “that” is omitted, and the pronominal subjunctive becomes an English progressive indicative; feeding becomes feasting.

> 58 E com’ io riguardando tra lor vegno,  
> 59 In una borsa gialla vidi azzurro,  
> 60 Che di lione avea faccia e contegno.  

And as I came amongst them looking, on a yellow purse I saw azure, that had the semblance and gesture of a lion.

- *com’ io riguardando tra lor vegno* ‘as I, looking, come among them’ → “as I came amongst them looking”: the historical present becomes past, and the participial phrase moves after the verb and its accompanying phrase.
- *In una borsa gialla* ‘in a purse yellow’ → “on a yellow purse”: the preposition changes, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *Che di lione avea faccia e contegno* ‘that of a lion had face and bearing’ → “that had the semblance and gesture of a lion”: the phrase specifying the lion moves after the nouns; face becomes semblance, bearing becomes gesture, and a definite article is supplied before the paired nouns.

> 61 Poi procedendo di mio sguardo il curro,  
> 62 Vidine un’ altra più che sangue rossa  
> 63 Mostrare un’ oca bianca più che burro.  

Then, my look continuing its course, I saw another of them, redder than blood, display a goose more white than butter.

- *procedendo di mio sguardo il curro* ‘the chariot of my gaze proceeding’ → “my look continuing its course”: the chariot becomes a course, while the gaze becomes the grammatical subject rather than a possessive modifier. The intransitive proceeding becomes transitive “continuing”, with “its” supplied.
- *Vidine un’ altra* ‘I saw of them another’ → “I saw another of them”: the equivalent of the attached pronoun *ne* moves after “another”.
- *più che sangue rossa* ‘more than blood red’ → “redder than blood”: the adjective moves before the comparison, and the separate comparative adverb becomes the ending of “redder”.
- *bianca più che burro* ‘white more than butter’ → “more white than butter”: the comparative adverb moves before the adjective.

> 64 Ed un, che d’ una scrofa azzurra e grossa  
> 65 Segnato avea lo suo sacchetto bianco,  
> 66 Mi disse: Che fai tu in questa fossa?  

And one who, with a sow azure and pregnant, had his argent sacklet stamped, said to me: “What art thou doing in this pit?

- *grossa* ‘large’ → “pregnant”: the translation takes the adjective in its specific sense of being with young, rather than simply indicating size.
- *Segnato avea lo suo sacchetto bianco* ‘marked he had his sacklet white’ → “had his argent sacklet stamped”: the Italian pluperfect, with its participle placed first, becomes an English construction with the object between “had” and “stamped”. Marking becomes stamping; white becomes the heraldic “argent”, placed before its noun.
- *Mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “said to me”: the recipient follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *Che fai tu* ‘what dost thou do’ → “What art thou doing”: the simple present becomes progressive.

> 67 Or te ne va: e perchè se’ vivo anco,  
> 68 Sappi che il mio vicin Vitaliano  
> 69 Sederà qui dal mio sinistro fianco.  

Get thee gone: and, as thou art still alive, know that my neighbour Vitaliano shall sit here at my left side.

- *Or te ne va* ‘now go away’ → “Get thee gone”: ‘now’ is omitted, and the imperative of going away becomes an idiomatic English expression with “Get”.
- *perchè se’ vivo anco* ‘because thou art alive still’ → “as thou art still alive”: the causal conjunction becomes “as”, and “still” moves before “alive”.
- *dal mio sinistro fianco* ‘from my left side’ → “at my left side”: *da* is rendered according to its locative use here, rather than as indicating movement from a place.

## Lines 70–78 — The Paduan’s Taunt and Dante’s Return

> 70 Con questi Fiorentin son Padovano;  

With these Florentines am I, a Paduan.

- *Padovano* ‘Paduan’ → “a Paduan”: the predicate adjective becomes a noun with an indefinite article.

> 71 Spesse fïate m’ intruonan gli orecchi,  
> 72 Gridando: Vegna il cavalier sovrano,  
> 73 Che recherà la tasca coi tre becchi.  

Many a time they din my ears, shouting: ‘Let the sovereign cavalier come, who will bring the pouch with the three goats!’”

- *Spesse fïate* ‘frequent times’ → “Many a time”: the plural expression becomes an idiomatic singular construction.
- *m’ intruonan gli orecchi* ‘they deafen the ears for me’ → “they din my ears”: the Italian pronoun marking the person affected becomes the possessive “my”; “din” expresses the noise rather than its deafening effect.
- *Vegna il cavalier sovrano* ‘may come the cavalier sovereign’ → “Let the sovereign cavalier come”: the third-person subjunctive expressing a wish becomes a construction with “Let”; the subject precedes “come,” and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *becchi* ‘he-goats’ → “goats”: the translation leaves the animals’ sex unspecified.

> 74 Quindi storse la bocca, e di fuor trasse  
> 75 La lingua, come bue che il naso lecchi.  

Then he writhed his mouth, and thrust his tongue out, like an ox that licks his nose.

- *storse la bocca* ‘twisted the mouth’ → “he writhed his mouth”: “writhed” renders the twisting movement, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *di fuor trasse La lingua* ‘out drew the tongue’ → “thrust his tongue out”: drawing out becomes thrusting out; “out” moves after the object, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *come bue che il naso lecchi* ‘like an ox that the nose licks’ → “like an ox that licks his nose”: English supplies an indefinite article before “ox,” places the object after the verb, and substitutes a possessive for the definite article. The Italian subjunctive *lecchi*, used in the comparison, becomes an English indicative.

> 76 Ed io, temendo nol più star crucciasse  
> 77 Lui che di poco star m’ avea ammonito,  
> 78 Tornai indietro dall’ anime lasse.  

And I, dreading lest longer stay might anger him who had admonished me to stay short time, turned back from those fore-wearied souls.

- *temendo nol più star crucciasse* ‘fearing that the staying longer should not anger’ → “dreading lest longer stay might anger”: the negative construction after a verb of fearing becomes “lest,” without a separate English negative. The substantivized infinitive *star* becomes the noun “stay,” with *più* expressed as the preceding adjective “longer”; the imperfect subjunctive becomes “might anger.”
- *di poco star* ‘of staying little’ → “to stay short time”: the prepositional construction becomes a to-infinitive, and the adverb *poco* becomes the noun phrase “short time.”
- *m’ avea ammonito* ‘me had admonished’ → “had admonished me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *dall’ anime lasse* ‘from the weary souls’ → “from those fore-wearied souls”: the definite article becomes the demonstrative “those,” and the adjective moves before the noun. “Fore-wearied” adds an explicit suggestion of prior weariness not separately expressed by *lasse*.

## Lines 79–90 — Courage Before the Descent

> 79 Trovai lo Duca mio ch’ era salito  
> 80 Già sulla groppa del fiero animale,  

I found my Guide, who had already mounted on the haunch of the dreadful animal;

- *lo Duca mio* ‘the leader my’ → “my Guide”: ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *ch’ era salito Già* ‘who had climbed already’ → “who had already mounted”: ‘climbed’ becomes “mounted”, and “already” moves before the participle.
- *groppa* ‘rump’ → “haunch”: a different anatomical term is used.
- *fiero* ‘fierce’ → “dreadful”: the adjective describes the fear inspired rather than fierceness itself.

> 81 E disse a me: Or sie forte e ardito.  

and he said to me: “Now be stout and bold!

- *forte* ‘strong’ → “stout”: physical strength is expressed as firmness or courage.

> 82 Omai si scende per sì fatte scale:  

Now by such stairs must we descend.

- *si scende per sì fatte scale* ‘one descends by stairs made thus’ → “by such stairs must we descend”: the impersonal construction becomes first-person plural, “must” adds necessity, and the phrase describing the route moves before the verb. ‘Made thus’ is compressed into “such”, and the subject follows the modal.

> 83 Monta dinanzi, ch’ io voglio esser mezzo,  
> 84 Sì che la coda non possa far male.  

Mount thou in front; for I wish to be in the middle, that the tail may not do hurt to thee.”

- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *mezzo* ‘midmost’ → “in the middle”: a predicative adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *Sì che* ‘so that’ → “that”: ‘so’ is not separately rendered.
- *non possa* ‘cannot’ → “may not”: the Italian subjunctive of ‘can’ is rendered by “may” in a purpose clause, and the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *far male* ‘do harm’ → “do hurt to thee”: “hurt” replaces ‘harm’, and “to thee” supplies a recipient not expressed in the Italian.

> 85 Qual è colui, ch’ ha sì presso il ribrezzo  
> 86 Della quartana, ch’ ha già l’ unghie smorte,  
> 87 E trema tutto, pur guardando il rezzo;  

As one who has the shivering of the quartan so near, that he has his nails already pale, and trembles all, still keeping the shade;

- *Qual è colui* ‘As is he’ → “As one”: the verb ‘is’ disappears, and the demonstrative becomes the indefinite “one”.
- *ch’ ha sì presso il ribrezzo Della quartana* ‘who has so near the shivering of the quartan’ → “who has the shivering of the quartan so near”: the phrase “so near” moves after the object.
- *ch’ ha già l’ unghie smorte* ‘that he has already the nails pale’ → “that he has his nails already pale”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “already” moves after “nails”.
- *pur guardando il rezzo* ‘merely looking at the shade’ → “still keeping the shade”: Carlyle takes *pur* as continuative rather than restrictive, and *guardando* as keeping to the shade rather than looking at it.

> 88 Tal divenn’ io alle parole porte:  

such I became when these words were uttered.

- *Tal divenn’ io* ‘Such became I’ → “such I became”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *alle parole porte* ‘at the words proffered’ → “when these words were uttered”: a prepositional phrase becomes a temporal clause, the definite article becomes “these”, and the participle expands into a finite passive. ‘Proffered’ is rendered as “uttered”.

> 89 Ma vergogna mi fer le sue minacce,  
> 90 Che innanzi a buon signor fa servo forte.  

But his threats excited in me shame, which makes a servant brave in presence of a worthy master.

- *Ma vergogna mi fer le sue minacce* ‘But shame to me made his threats’ → “But his threats excited in me shame”: Carlyle takes *le sue minacce* as the subject and *vergogna* as the resulting feeling. The subject moves before the verb and the object after it; ‘made’ becomes “excited”, and the indirect pronoun becomes “in me”.
- *Che innanzi a buon signor fa servo forte* ‘which before a good lord makes a servant strong’ → “which makes a servant brave in presence of a worthy master”: the phrase beginning ‘before’ moves to the end and becomes “in presence of”; ‘good lord’ becomes “worthy master”, and ‘strong’ becomes “brave”.

## Lines 91–101 — Mounting Geryon and Beginning the Descent

> 91 Io m’ assettai in su quelle spallacce:  
> 92 Sì volli dir, ma la voce non venne  
> 93 Com’ io credetti: Fa che tu m’ abbracce.  

I placed myself on those huge shoulders, and wished to say, only the voice came not as I thought: “See that thou embrace me.”

- *Io m’ assettai* ‘I seated myself’ → “I placed myself”: the specific action of sitting becomes the more general placing; the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *in su* ‘up on’ → “on”: the compound preposition is reduced to a single preposition.
- *quelle spallacce* ‘those great ugly shoulders’ → “those huge shoulders”: the augmentative and disparaging suffix becomes the adjective “huge”, retaining size without explicitly retaining disparagement.
- *Sì* ‘indeed’ → “and”: the affirmative adverb becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *ma* ‘but’ → “only”: the contrast is expressed as a qualification.
- *non venne* ‘not came’ → “came not”: the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *credetti* ‘I believed’ → “I thought”: believing is expressed as thinking.
- *Fa che tu m’ abbracce* ‘Make it that you embrace me’ → “See that thou embrace me”: the causative expression becomes the English idiom “See that”; the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 94 Ma esso che altra volta mi sovvenne  
> 95 Ad altro forte, tosto ch’ io montai,  
> 96 Con le braccia m’ avvinse e mi sostenne:  

But he, who at other times assisted me in other difficulties, soon as I mounted, clasped me with his arms, and held me up.

- *altra volta* ‘another time’ → “at other times”: the singular becomes plural, and English supplies a preposition.
- *mi sovvenne* ‘came to my aid’ → “assisted me”: the Italian indirect-object pronoun becomes the direct object of “assisted” and follows the verb.
- *Ad altro forte* ‘at another difficult pass’ → “in other difficulties”: the substantival *forte*, denoting a difficult situation, becomes the abstract noun “difficulties”; singular becomes plural, and the preposition changes.
- *Con le braccia m’ avvinse* ‘With the arms me he clasped’ → “clasped me with his arms”: the phrase naming the means moves after the verb and object, the object pronoun follows the verb, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *e mi sostenne* ‘and me he supported’ → “and held me up”: “held me up” replaces the single verb meaning ‘supported’, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 97 E disse: Gerion, moviti omai:  
> 98 Le ruote larghe, e lo scender sia poco:  
> 99 Pensa la nuova soma che tu hai.  

Then he said: “Geryon, now move thee. Be thy circles large, and gradual thy descent: think of the unusual burden that thou hast.”

- *E disse* ‘And he said’ → “Then he said”: the coordinating conjunction becomes a temporal adverb.
- *moviti omai* ‘move yourself now’ → “now move thee”: the temporal adverb moves before the imperative.
- *Le ruote larghe, e lo scender sia poco* ‘The circles wide, and the descending be little’ → “Be thy circles large, and gradual thy descent”: “Be” is supplied for the first clause, where Italian leaves it understood, and is understood rather than repeated in the second. Both definite articles become possessives; the substantival infinitive becomes the noun “descent”; and ‘little’ is interpreted as “gradual”, which moves before its subject.
- *Pensa* ‘Consider’ → “think of”: a verb taking a direct object becomes a construction with “of”.
- *la nuova soma* ‘the new burden’ → “the unusual burden”: “unusual” interprets *nuova* as unfamiliar or unaccustomed rather than simply new.

> 100 Come la navicella esce di loco  
> 101 In dietro in dietro, sì quindi si tolse:  

As the bark goes from its station backwards, backwards, so the monster took himself from thence;

- *la navicella* ‘the little ship’ → “the bark”: a vessel-name replaces the diminutive noun, without an explicit equivalent of ‘little’.
- *esce di loco* ‘comes out of place’ → “goes from its station”: the verb of emergence becomes the more general “goes”; “station” specifies the sense of *loco*, and “its” is supplied.
- *sì quindi si tolse* ‘so from there it removed itself’ → “so the monster took himself from thence”: the phrase of departure moves after the verb; ‘removed itself’ becomes “took himself”, with masculine personification; and the translation explicitly names the subject as “the monster”.

## Lines 102–114 — Flight and Fear in the Air

> 102 E poi ch’ al tutto si sentì a giuoco,  

and when he felt himself quite loose,

- *poi ch’* ‘after’ → “when”: temporal succession is rendered as coincidence in time.
- *al tutto* ‘entirely’ → “quite”: the adverb moves from before the verb to before “loose”.
- *si sentì* ‘himself he felt’ → “he felt himself”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.
- *a giuoco* ‘at liberty’ → “loose”: the prepositional expression becomes an adjective.

> 103 Là ov’ era il petto, la coda rivolse;  

there where his breast had been he turned his tail,

- *era il petto* ‘was the breast’ → “his breast had been”: the imperfect becomes a past perfect, the subject precedes the verb, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *la coda rivolse* ‘the tail he turned’ → “he turned his tail”: the object follows the verb, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 104 E quella tesa, come anguilla, mosse;  

and stretching moved it, like an eel,

- *quella tesa … mosse* ‘that, stretched out, he moved’ → “stretching moved it”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun following the verb; the past participle describing its extended state becomes an active present participle.
- *come anguilla* ‘like eel’ → “like an eel”: an indefinite article is supplied, and the comparison follows rather than precedes the verb.

> 105 E con le branche l’ aere a sè raccolse.  

and with his paws gathered the air to him.

- *le branche* ‘the paws’ → “his paws”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *l’ aere a sè raccolse* ‘the air to himself he gathered’ → “gathered the air to him”: the verb precedes its object and the directional phrase; the reflexive pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.

> 106 Maggior paura non credo che fosse,  
> 107 Quando Fetonte abbandonò li freni,  
> 108 Per che il ciel, come appare ancor, si cosse;  

Greater fear there was not, I believe, when Phaeton let loose the reins, whereby the sky, as yet appears, was burnt;—

- *Maggior paura non credo che fosse* ‘Greater fear I do not believe there was’ → “Greater fear there was not, I believe”: the negative moves from the believing to the existence of greater fear. The subordinate clause becomes the main assertion, with the subjunctive *fosse* rendered by the indicative “was”; the verb of belief becomes parenthetical.
- *abbandonò* ‘abandoned’ → “let loose”: abandonment is expressed as loosening.
- *Per che* ‘because of which’ → “whereby”: the causal relative phrase becomes a single relative adverb.
- *come appare ancor* ‘as appears still’ → “as yet appears”: “yet” renders *ancor* and precedes rather than follows the verb.
- *si cosse* ‘became baked’ → “was burnt”: the pronominal verb becomes an English passive, and baking is rendered as burning.

> 109 Nè quando Icaro misero le reni  
> 110 Sentì spennar per la scaldata cera,  
> 111 Gridando il padre a lui: Mala via tieni;  

nor when poor Icarus felt his loins unfeathering by the heating of the wax, his father crying to him, “An ill way thou goest!”—

- *Icaro misero* ‘Icarus poor’ → “poor Icarus”: the adjective precedes the name.
- *le reni Sentì spennar* ‘the loins he felt lose their feathers’ → “felt his loins unfeathering”: the object follows the verb of perception, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the infinitive becomes a present participle.
- *per la scaldata cera* ‘because of the heated wax’ → “by the heating of the wax”: the participial adjective becomes a noun denoting the process, with *cera* made dependent on it through “of”; the causal phrase is introduced by “by”.
- *Gridando il padre a lui* ‘crying the father to him’ → “his father crying to him”: the subject of the absolute construction precedes the participle, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Mala via tieni* ‘An ill way thou keepest’ → “An ill way thou goest!”: keeping or following a course is expressed as going, so that the way becomes an expression of direction rather than the object of a transitive verb.

> 112 Che fu la mia, quando vidi ch’ io era  
> 113 Nell’ aer d’ ogni parte, e vidi spenta  
> 114 Ogni veduta, fuor che della fiera.  

than was mine, when I saw myself in the air on all sides, and saw extinguished every sight, save of the beast.

- *vidi ch’ io era* ‘I saw that I was’ → “I saw myself”: the finite subordinate clause becomes a reflexive object followed by a phrase describing its situation.
- *d’ ogni parte* ‘on every side’ → “on all sides”: distributive singular becomes plural.

## Lines 115–126 — The Wheeling Descent

> 115 Ella sen va notando lenta lenta;  
> 116 Ruota, e discende; ma non me n’ accorgo,  
> 117 Se non ch’ al viso e disotto mi venta.  

He goes on swimming slowly, slowly; wheels and descends; but I perceive it not, otherwise than by a wind upon my face and from below.

- *Ella* ‘She’ → “He”: the grammatical gender of the pronoun changes.
- *sen va notando* ‘goes away swimming’ → “goes on swimming”: the pronominal expression *se ne va* becomes “goes on”, emphasizing continued action rather than movement away.
- *lenta lenta* ‘slow slow’ → “slowly, slowly”: the repeated adjective, agreeing with *Ella* but describing the manner of movement, becomes repeated adverbs.
- *non me n’ accorgo* ‘I do not become aware of it’ → “I perceive it not”: the pronominal construction with *accorgersi* and *ne* becomes a transitive verb with “it” as its direct object; the negative follows the verb and object.
- *Se non ch’* ‘except that’ → “otherwise than by”: a conjunction introducing a finite clause becomes a phrase introducing a means of perception.
- *al viso e disotto mi venta* ‘at the face and from below it blows upon me’ → “a wind upon my face and from below”: the impersonal verb *venta* becomes the noun phrase “a wind”; *mi*, marking the person affected, is represented through the possessive “my”. The noun precedes the phrases specifying where the wind is felt.

> 118 Io sentia già dalla man destra il gorgo  
> 119 Far sotto noi un orribile stroscio;  
> 120 Per che con gli occhi in giù la testa sporgo.  

Already, on the right hand, I heard the whirlpool make a hideous roaring under us; whereat, with eyes downwards, my head I stretched.

- *Io sentia già* ‘I heard already’ → “Already” and “I heard”: “Already” moves before the subject and verb; the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past.
- *dalla man destra* ‘from the right hand’ → “on the right hand”: the preposition indicating direction or source becomes one indicating position, and the phrase moves before “I heard”.
- *Far sotto noi un orribile stroscio* ‘make under us a horrible crashing’ → “make a hideous roaring under us”: the phrase “under us” moves after the object; *stroscio*, a crashing or rushing noise, is rendered as “roaring”.
- *Per che* ‘because of which’ → “whereat”: the explicitly causal connection becomes a relative adverb marking the occasion for the response.
- *con gli occhi in giù* ‘with the eyes downwards’ → “with eyes downwards”: the definite article is omitted.
- *la testa sporgo* ‘the head I thrust out’ → “my head I stretched”: the definite article becomes a possessive, the present becomes the past, and the verb’s explicit sense of projecting outwards becomes “stretched”.

> 121 Allor fu’ io più timido allo scoscio:  
> 122 Però ch’ io vidi fuochi, e sentii pianti;  
> 123 Ond’ io tremando tutto mi raccoscio.  

Then was I more timorous at the precipice; for I saw fires and heard lamentings, so that I cowered all trembling.

- *allo scoscio* ‘at the plunge’ → “at the precipice”: the translation takes *scoscio* as the steep drop itself rather than the action of plunging or falling.
- *Però ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating causal conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *Ond’* ‘whence’ → “so that”: the relative adverb becomes an explicit conjunction of consequence.
- *tremando tutto* ‘trembling all over’ → “all trembling”: “all” precedes “trembling”, and the participial phrase moves after the main verb.
- *mi raccoscio* ‘I draw up my thighs’ → “I cowered”: the anatomically specific reflexive verb becomes a general verb of shrinking in fear; the present becomes the past.

> 124 E vidi poi, che nol vedea davanti,  
> 125 Lo scendere e il girar, per li gran mali  
> 126 Che s’ appressavan da diversi canti.  

And then I saw—what I had not seen before—the sinking and the wheeling, through the great evils which drew near on diverse sides.

- *E vidi poi* ‘And I saw then’ → “And then I saw”: the adverb moves before the subject and verb.
- *che nol vedea davanti* ‘that I did not see it before’ → “what I had not seen before”: the clause is construed as a parenthetical relative introduced by “what”, with *lo*, contracted into *nol*, no longer separately expressed. The imperfect becomes the past perfect.
- *Lo scendere e il girar* ‘the descending and the turning’ → “the sinking and the wheeling”: the substantivized infinitives become English gerunds; descent is expressed as “sinking”, and turning as the more specifically circular “wheeling”.
- *s’ appressavan* ‘were approaching’ → “drew near”: the pronominal verb becomes an intransitive verbal phrase, and the imperfect becomes the simple past.
- *da diversi canti* ‘from diverse sides’ → “on diverse sides”: the preposition indicating the directions from which the evils approach becomes one indicating their positions.

## Lines 127–136 — Geryon’s Landing and Swift Departure

> 127 Come il falcon ch’ è stato assai su l’ ali,  
> 128 Che, senza veder logoro o uccello,  
> 129 Fa dire al falconiere: Oimè tu cali!  

As the falcon, that has been long upon his wings—that, without seeing bird or lure, makes the falconer cry, “Ah, ah! thou stoopest”—

- *assai* ‘much’ → “long”: the quantity is expressed specifically as duration.
- *su l’ ali* ‘upon the wings’ → “upon his wings”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *senza veder logoro o uccello* ‘without seeing lure or bird’ → “without seeing bird or lure”: the two objects exchange places.
- *Fa dire al falconiere* ‘makes say to the falconer’ → “makes the falconer cry”: the Italian causative construction becomes an English construction with the person caused to speak as its direct object; ‘say’ becomes the more expressive “cry”.
- *Oimè* ‘alas’ → “Ah, ah!”: a single exclamation of distress becomes a repeated interjection.
- *tu cali* ‘thou descendest’ → “thou stoopest”: the general verb of descent becomes a term associated with a falcon’s downward flight.

> 130 Discende lasso, onde si muove snello  
> 131 Per cento ruote, e da lungi si pone  
> 132 Dal suo maestro, disdegnoso e fello;  

descends weary; then swiftly moves himself with many a circle, and far from his master sets himself disdainful and sullen;

- *onde si muove snello* ‘whence he moves himself agile’ → “then swiftly moves himself”: the local relative ‘whence’ is interpreted temporally as “then”; the adjective describing the falcon becomes an adverb describing his movement.
- *Per cento ruote* ‘through a hundred circles’ → “with many a circle”: “with” replaces the preposition of passage; the definite number becomes an indefinite quantity, and the plural noun becomes singular after “many a”.
- *e da lungi si pone Dal suo maestro* ‘and far away sets himself from his master’ → “and far from his master sets himself”: the phrase identifying whom he is far from moves before the verb, joining “far” directly.
- *fello* ‘angry’ → “sullen”: anger is rendered specifically as withdrawn ill temper.

> 133 Così ne pose al fondo Gerione  
> 134 A piede a piè della stagliata rocca:  

so at the bottom Geryon set us, close to the foot of the ragged rock;

- *Così ne pose al fondo Gerione* ‘so us set at the bottom Geryon’ → “so at the bottom Geryon set us”: the place phrase moves before the verb, the subject moves from after the verb to before it, and the object pronoun moves after it.
- *A piede a piè* ‘at the foot, at the foot’ → “close to the foot”: the repeated expression is rendered by the intensifying “close” rather than by repetition.
- *stagliata rocca* ‘sheer rock’ → “ragged rock”: the description shifts from a sharply cut, precipitous surface to an irregular or jagged one.

> 135 E, discarcate le nostre persone,  
> 136 Si dileguò, come da corda cocca.  

and, from our weight relieved, he bounded off like an arrow from the string.

- *discarcate le nostre persone* ‘our persons unloaded’ → “from our weight relieved”: the participial absolute describing the unloading of the travellers becomes a participial phrase describing Geryon’s relief; their ‘persons’ become their “weight”, introduced by “from” and placed before the participle.
- *Si dileguò* ‘he vanished’ → “he bounded off”: disappearance becomes a particular manner of departure, with bounding movement supplied.
- *come da corda cocca* ‘like from a string a nock’ → “like an arrow from the string”: the part named by *cocca*, the arrow’s nock, is rendered as the whole arrow; that noun moves before the phrase identifying its point of departure, and the string is made definite.

## Summary — Close Detail, Reshaped Syntax

Carlyle remains close to the Italian’s sequence of details, comparisons, and emphases, but frequently rearranges their expression. Subjects and objects placed after or before the verb for emphasis move into more usual English positions, and descriptive adjectives generally precede their nouns. Yet the translation also preserves marked inversions, as in “In the void glanced all his tail”, and reproduces emphatic repetition in “backwards, backwards” and “slowly, slowly”. Its closeness is therefore not simply lexical: it sometimes retains the Italian’s unusual arrangement and rhythm of expression.

Changes of construction recur throughout. Finite subordinate clauses become infinitives, as *che venisse* becomes “to come”; infinitives after verbs of perception become participles, as *Gente seder* becomes “people sitting”. Pronominal verbs often yield simpler English expressions, while impersonal constructions acquire personal subjects: *si scende* becomes “must we descend”, also making necessity explicit. Elsewhere, phrases expand into clauses, as *alle parole porte* becomes “when these words were uttered”, or verbs become nouns, as *mi venta* is recast through “a wind”. Italian narrative presents sometimes become English past tenses, bringing them into line with the surrounding narration.

Vocabulary ranges from close equivalents to interpretive substitutions. *Appuzza*, ‘makes stink’, becomes the broader “pollutes”; *pelle*, ‘skin’, becomes “aspect”; and *serpente* becomes “reptile”. Other choices specify a particular sense: *grossa* becomes “pregnant”, and *bianco* receives the heraldic equivalent “argent”. Some departures alter the physical description more substantially, notably *dosso*, ‘back’, rendered as “neck”, and *stagliata*, ‘sheer’, as “ragged”. Such choices show that even where the sentence follows the Italian closely, an individual word may redirect its precision or emphasis.

Additions commonly make an implication explicit: Carlyle supplies “her loom” in the reference to weaving, “to thee” as the recipient of possible harm, and nouns such as “beast” or “monster” where Italian uses a demonstrative or leaves the subject understood. Conversely, intensifiers, repeated prepositions, and diminutive or disparaging nuances are sometimes reduced or omitted. Grammatical gender also changes, with feminine references to the beast becoming masculine. The rendering of line 13 is displaced into the following English block rather than omitted from the translation as a whole.

Two pervasive grammatical adjustments underlie these more individual departures. English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and normally omits the Italian definite article before a possessive. Definite articles with body parts also frequently become possessives, producing such phrases as “his head and bust”. Together, these routine adjustments and the more interpretive changes make Carlyle’s prose recognizably English while preserving much of the Italian’s concrete vocabulary, repeated structures, and detailed progression.
