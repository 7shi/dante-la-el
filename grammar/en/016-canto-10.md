# Inferno 10

## Lines 1–12 — The Open Sepulchres

> 1 Ora sen va per uno stretto calle,  
> 2 Tra il muro della terra e li martìri,  
> 3 Lo mio Maestro, ed io dopo le spalle.  

Now by a narrow path, between the city-wall and the torments, my Master goes on, and I behind him.

- *sen va* ‘goes away’ → “goes on”: the pronominal expression is rendered as continued movement; the verb follows its subject and the two phrases describing the route, rather than preceding them.
- *per uno stretto calle* ‘through a narrow path’ → “by a narrow path”: “by” replaces ‘through’ as the preposition describing the route.
- *il muro della terra* ‘the wall of the city’ → “the city-wall”: the phrase modifying ‘wall’ becomes the first element of a compound noun.
- *dopo le spalle* ‘behind the shoulders’ → “behind him”: the reference to shoulders is replaced by a personal pronoun identifying their possessor.

> 4 O virtù somma, che per gli empi giri  
> 5 Mi volvi, cominciai, come a te piace;  
> 6 Parlami, e soddisfammi a’ miei desiri.  

“O Virtue supreme! who through the impious circles thus wheelest me, as it pleases thee,” I began; “speak to me, and satisfy my wishes.

- *Mi volvi* ‘me thou turnest’ → “thus wheelest me”: ‘turnest’ becomes “wheelest,” “thus” is supplied, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *cominciai, come a te piace* ‘I began, as to thee it pleases’ → “as it pleases thee,” I began”: the reporting clause is moved after “as it pleases thee,” rather than preceding it; the experiencer, expressed by a prepositional phrase before the Italian verb, becomes an object pronoun after the English verb.
- *soddisfammi a’ miei desiri* ‘satisfy me as to my desires’ → “satisfy my wishes”: the personal object ‘me’ is omitted, while the prepositional phrase becomes the direct object; ‘desires’ is rendered as “wishes.”

> 7 La gente, che per li sepolcri giace,  
> 8 Potrebbesi veder? già son levati  
> 9 Tutti i coperchi, e nessun guardia face.  

Might those people, who lie within the sepulchres, be seen? The covers all are raised, and none keeps guard.”

- *La gente* ‘the people’ → “those people”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative, and the Italian singular collective noun becomes an English plural.
- *che per li sepolcri giace* ‘who through the sepulchres lies’ → “who lie within the sepulchres”: “within” renders the spatial use of *per*; the verb moves before the prepositional phrase and becomes plural to agree with “people.”
- *Potrebbesi veder* ‘could be seen’ → “Might” … “be seen”: the conditional modal is rendered by “Might”; the auxiliary moves before the subject to form the English question, while the passive infinitive remains after the relative clause.
- *già son levati Tutti i coperchi* ‘already are raised all the covers’ → “The covers all are raised”: ‘already’ is omitted; the subject moves before the verb, and “all” follows “covers” instead of preceding the article.
- *nessun guardia face* ‘no one makes guard’ → “none keeps guard”: the Italian expression becomes the English idiom “keeps guard,” with the verb before its object rather than after it.

> 10 Ed egli a me: Tutti saran serrati,  
> 11 Quando di Iosaphat qui torneranno  
> 12 Coi corpi, che lassù hanno lasciati.  

And he to me: “All shall be closed up, when, from Jehosaphat, they return here with the bodies which they have left above.

- *Tutti saran serrati* ‘All shall be shut’ → “All shall be closed up”: the single verb ‘shut’ is rendered by the phrasal expression “closed up.”
- *Quando di Iosaphat qui torneranno* ‘when from Jehosaphat here they shall return’ → “when, from Jehosaphat, they return here”: the Italian future becomes an English present in a future-time clause; “here” moves after the verb.
- *che lassù hanno lasciati* ‘which up there they have left’ → “which they have left above”: the deictic ‘up there’ becomes “above,” and moves from before the verb to the end of the clause.

## Lines 13–24 — Burial, Hidden Wishes, and a Tuscan Voice

> 13 Suo cimitero da questa parte hanno  
> 14 Con Epicuro tutti i suoi seguaci,  
> 15 Che l’ anima col corpo morta fanno.  

In this part are entombed with Epicurus all his followers, who make the soul die with the body.

- *Suo cimitero … hanno* ‘their burial ground … they have’ → “are entombed”: possession of a burial ground becomes a passive verb describing burial; the possessive is not separately represented.
- *da questa parte* ‘on this side’ → “In this part”: the spatial expression changes, and it is moved to the beginning of the sentence.
- *Che l’ anima col corpo morta fanno* ‘who the soul with the body dead make’ → “who make the soul die with the body”: the verb precedes its object, the adjective ‘dead’ becomes the infinitive “die”, and “with the body” moves to the end.

> 16 Però alla dimanda che mi faci  
> 17 Quinci entro soddisfatto sarai tosto,  
> 18 E al disio ancor, che tu mi taci.  

Therefore to the question, which thou askest me, thou shalt soon have satisfaction here within; and also to the wish which thou holdest secret from me.”

- *che mi faci* ‘which thou makest to me’ → “which thou askest me”: making a question becomes asking one, and the pronoun follows the verb.
- *Quinci entro soddisfatto sarai tosto* ‘here within satisfied thou wilt be soon’ → “thou shalt soon have satisfaction here within”: being satisfied becomes having satisfaction, replacing the participial adjective with a noun; the spatial phrase moves to the end, and “soon” precedes “have”.
- *E al disio ancor* ‘and to the wish also’ → “and also to the wish”: “also” moves before the prepositional phrase.
- *che tu mi taci* ‘which thou keepest silent to me’ → “which thou holdest secret from me”: the transitive use of *tacere*, meaning to leave something unspoken, becomes holding it secret; the indirect-object pronoun becomes “from me”.

> 19 Ed io: Buon Duca, non tegno nascosto  
> 20 A te mio cor, se non per dicer poco;  
> 21 E tu m’ hai non pur ora a ciò disposto.  

And I: “Kind Guide, I do not keep my heart concealed from thee, except for brevity of speech, to which thou hast ere now disposed me.”

- *Buon Duca* ‘Good Leader’ → “Kind Guide”: goodness is expressed as kindness, and leadership as guidance.
- *non tegno nascosto A te mio cor* ‘I do not keep concealed to thee my heart’ → “I do not keep my heart concealed from thee”: the object moves before its complement, and *A te* becomes “from thee”, placed after “concealed”.
- *se non per dicer poco* ‘except to say little’ → “except for brevity of speech”: the infinitive phrase becomes an abstract noun phrase, expressing the reason for restraint rather than its purpose.
- *E tu m’ hai non pur ora a ciò disposto* ‘and thou hast me not only now to this disposed’ → “to which thou hast ere now disposed me”: the coordinated clause becomes a relative clause, with “to which” replacing ‘and … to this’; ‘not only now’ is recast as “ere now”, and the object pronoun moves after “disposed”.

> 22 O Tosco, che per la città del foco  
> 23 Vivo ten vai così parlando onesto,  
> 24 Piacciati di ristare in questo loco.  

“O Tuscan! who through the city of fire goest alive, speaking thus decorously; may it please thee to stop in this place.

- *Vivo ten vai* ‘alive thou goest thy way’ → “goest alive”: the adjective moves after the verb, and the pronominal expression *te ne vai* is reduced to “goest”, without separately representing its reflexive and adverbial elements.
- *così parlando onesto* ‘thus speaking decorously’ → “speaking thus decorously”: “thus” moves after “speaking”; the Italian adjective *onesto*, used adverbially, is rendered by an English adverb.

## Lines 25–34 — Farinata Speaks and Rises

> 25 La tua loquela ti fa manifesto  
> 26 Di quella nobil patria natio,  
> 27 Alla qual forse fui troppo molesto.  

Thy speech clearly shews thee a native of that noble country, which perhaps I vexed too much.”

- *ti fa manifesto* ‘makes thee manifest’ → “clearly shews thee”: the construction with ‘makes’ and an adjective becomes a verb modified by an adverb; “clearly” precedes the verb.
- *Di quella nobil patria natio* ‘of that noble country native’ → “a native of that noble country”: the adjective *natio* becomes the noun phrase “a native”, placed before its dependent phrase.
- *Alla qual forse fui troppo molesto* ‘to which perhaps I was too troublesome’ → “which perhaps I vexed too much”: ‘was troublesome to’ becomes the transitive verb “vexed”, with “which” as its direct object; ‘too’, modifying an adjective, becomes “too much”, modifying the verb.

> 28 Subitamente questo suono uscìo  
> 29 D’ una dell’ arche: però m’ accostai,  
> 30 Temendo, un poco più al Duca mio.  

Suddenly this sound issued from one of the chests, whereat in fear I drew a little closer to my Guide.

- *arche* ‘tombs’ → “chests”: the translation uses the noun’s chest or box sense rather than its contextual sense of tombs.
- *però* ‘therefore’ → “whereat”: an expression of consequence becomes a relative adverb linking the reaction to the preceding event.
- *m’ accostai, Temendo, un poco più* ‘I drew myself near, fearing, a little more’ → “in fear I drew a little closer”: the reflexive construction becomes the non-reflexive “drew … closer”; ‘more’ is incorporated into the comparative “closer”. The participle ‘fearing’ becomes the prepositional phrase “in fear” and moves before the main clause.
- *Duca mio* ‘leader my’ → “my Guide”: ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”, and the possessive moves before the noun.

> 31 Ed ei mi disse: Volgiti: che fai?  

And he said to me: “Turn thee round. What art thou doing?

- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *Volgiti* ‘Turn thyself’ → “Turn thee round”: “round” is supplied to make the direction of the turning explicit.
- *che fai* ‘what dost thou do’ → “What art thou doing”: the simple present becomes the English present progressive.

> 32 Vedi là Farinata, che s’ è dritto:  

Lo there Farinata! who has raised himself erect.

- *Vedi* ‘See’ → “Lo”: the imperative verb becomes a presentational interjection.
- *che s’ è dritto* ‘who has straightened himself’ → “who has raised himself erect”: the reflexive verb is expanded into “raised himself” with “erect” expressing the resulting posture.

> 33 Dalla cintola in su tutto il vedrai.  

From the girdle upwards thou shalt see him all.”

- *tutto il vedrai* ‘all him thou shalt see’ → “thou shalt see him all”: the object pronoun and its qualifier move after the verb, and “all” follows rather than precedes “him”.

> 34 Io avea già il mio viso nel suo fitto;  

Already I had fixed my look on his:

- *Io avea già il mio viso nel suo fitto* ‘I had already my face in his fixed’ → “Already I had fixed my look on his”: the participle moves next to the auxiliary instead of following the object and prepositional phrase; the adverb moves from after the auxiliary to the beginning of the sentence. The translation takes *viso* in the sense of sight or gaze rather than face. The preposition changes to suit the English construction ‘fix one’s look on’.

## Lines 35–48 — Farinata Questions Dante’s Ancestry

> 35 Ed ei s’ ergea col petto e colla fronte,  
> 36 Come avesse lo Inferno in gran dispitto:  

and he rose upright with breast and countenance, as if he entertained great scorn of Hell.

- *s’ ergea* ‘was raising himself’ → “rose upright”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, with “upright” expressing the erect posture; the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *col petto e colla fronte* ‘with the breast and with the forehead’ → “with breast and countenance”: the repeated preposition and both definite articles are omitted; “countenance” broadens ‘forehead’ to the face or its expression.
- *Come* ‘as’ → “as if”: “if” makes the hypothetical comparison explicit.
- *avesse lo Inferno in gran dispitto* ‘had Hell in great disdain’ → “he entertained great scorn of Hell”: having something in disdain becomes entertaining scorn of it; Hell changes from the verb’s direct object to the complement of “scorn”.

> 37 E le animose man del Duca e pronte  
> 38 Mi pinser tra le sepolture a lui,  
> 39 Dicendo: Le parole tue sien conte.  

And the bold and ready hands of my Guide pushed me amongst the sepultures to him, saying: “Let thy words be numbered.”

- *le animose man del Duca e pronte* ‘the spirited hands of the Leader and ready’ → “the bold and ready hands of my Guide”: “bold” renders *animose*; the separated adjectives are brought together before “hands”; ‘the Leader’ becomes “my Guide”, changing the noun and supplying a possessive.
- *Mi pinser* ‘me pushed’ → “pushed me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Le parole tue sien conte* ‘the words thy be counted’ → “Let thy words be numbered”: the possessive moves before the noun, and the exhortative subjunctive becomes an English construction with “Let”. “Numbered” retains the counting sense of *conte*, rather than explicitly rendering its contextual sense of measured or carefully considered.

> 40 Tosto che al piè della sua tomba fui,  
> 41 Guardommi un poco, e poi quasi sdegnoso  
> 42 Mi dimandò: Chi fur li maggior tui?  

As soon as I was at the foot of his tomb, he looked at me a little; and then, almost contemptuously, he asked me: “Who were thy ancestors?”

- *al piè della sua tomba fui* ‘at the foot of his tomb I was’ → “I was at the foot of his tomb”: the verb moves before the expression of place.
- *Guardommi* ‘regarded me’ → “he looked at me”: the directly transitive Italian verb becomes “looked” with the preposition “at”.
- *quasi sdegnoso* ‘almost disdainful’ → “almost contemptuously”: an adjective describing the speaker becomes an adverb describing his manner.
- *Mi dimandò* ‘me he asked’ → “he asked me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *li maggior tui* ‘the elders thy’ → “thy ancestors”: the substantivized comparative *maggior* is rendered by the explicit kinship noun “ancestors”, and the possessive moves before it.

> 43 Io, ch’ era d’ ubbedir disideroso,  
> 44 Non gliel celai, ma tutto gliel’ apersi:  
> 45 Ond’ ei levò le ciglia un poco in soso;  

I, being desirous to obey, concealed it not; but opened the whole to him: whereupon he raised his brows a little.

- *Io, ch’ era d’ ubbedir disideroso* ‘I, who was of obeying desirous’ → “I, being desirous to obey”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase; the infinitive complement moves after “desirous”, and Italian *di* gives way to the English infinitive marker “to”.
- *Non gliel celai* ‘not to him it I concealed’ → “concealed it not”: the indirect object ‘to him’ is omitted, while the direct object and negative move after the verb.
- *tutto gliel’ apersi* ‘all of it to him I opened’ → “opened the whole to him”: *tutto* and the direct-object component of *gliel’* are represented together by “the whole”; the verb moves before its objects.
- *Ond’* ‘for which reason’ → “whereupon”: the causal connection is expressed as a consequential sequel.
- *le ciglia* ‘the eyebrows’ → “his brows”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *in soso* ‘upward’ → “raised”: the direction is not separately expressed, but is left implicit in the verb.

> 46 Poi disse: Fieramente furo avversi  
> 47 A me ed a’ miei primi, ed a mia parte,  
> 48 Sì che per due fiate gli dispersi.  

Then he said: “Fiercely adverse were they to me, and to my progenitors, and to my party; so that twice I scattered them.”

- *Fieramente furo avversi* ‘fiercely were adverse’ → “Fiercely adverse were they”: the predicate adjective moves before the verb, and the supplied subject follows it.
- *primi* ‘first ones’ → “progenitors”: the substantivized adjective is interpreted as forebears and rendered by an explicit noun.
- *per due fiate* ‘for two times’ → “twice”: the prepositional phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *gli dispersi* ‘them I scattered’ → “I scattered them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

## Lines 49–63 — Exile and a Father’s Question

> 49 S’ ei fur cacciati, ei tornar d’ ogni parte,  
> 50 Risposi io lui, l’ una e l’ altra fiata;  

“If they were driven forth, they returned from every quarter, both times,” I answered him.

- *cacciati* ‘expelled’ → “driven forth”: a single Italian verb is rendered by a verb and directional adverb.
- *d’ ogni parte* ‘from every part’ → “from every quarter”: “quarter” gives *parte* an explicitly geographical sense.
- *Risposi io lui* ‘answered I him’ → “I answered him”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *l’ una e l’ altra fiata* ‘the one and the other time’ → “both times”: the paired singular expressions become a plural phrase, placed before rather than after the reporting clause.

> 51 Ma i vostri non appreser ben quell’ arte.  

“But yours have not rightly learnt that art.”

- *non appreser ben* ‘did not learn well’ → “have not rightly learnt”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, and the adverb moves before the main verb; “rightly” renders *ben* as correctness rather than simply proficiency.

> 52 Allor surse alla vista scoperchiata  
> 53 Un’ ombra lungo questa infino al mento:  
> 54 Credo che s’ era inginocchion levata.  

Then, beside him, there rose a shadow, visible to the chin. It had raised itself, I think, upon its knees.

- *surse … Un’ ombra* ‘rose a shadow’ → “there rose a shadow”: introductory “there” is supplied.
- *alla vista scoperchiata … infino al mento* ‘at the uncovered opening … as far as the chin’ → “visible to the chin”: the location at an uncovered opening becomes a description of the shadow’s visibility; *scoperchiata* is not separately rendered.
- *lungo questa* ‘beside this one’ → “beside him”: the feminine demonstrative becomes a masculine personal pronoun, identifying the person beside whom the shadow rises; the phrase moves before the verb.
- *Credo che s’ era inginocchion levata* ‘I believe that it had raised itself kneeling’ → “It had raised itself, I think, upon its knees.”: the main clause and its dependent clause become an assertion interrupted by a parenthetical qualification. “Think” replaces ‘believe’, and the adverb *inginocchion* becomes a prepositional phrase with a supplied possessive.

> 55 D’ intorno mi guardò, come talento  
> 56 Avesse di veder s’ altri era meco;  

It looked around me, as if it had a wish to see whether some one were with me.

- *D’ intorno mi guardò* ‘around me it looked’ → “It looked around me”: the phrase describing the direction of the gaze moves after the verb.
- *come talento Avesse di veder* ‘as if a wish it had to see’ → “as if it had a wish to see”: *talento*, placed before the verb in Italian, follows it in English.
- *s’ altri era meco* ‘whether another was with me’ → “whether some one were with me”: “some one” renders the indefinite *altri*, and the indicative *era* becomes the subjunctive “were”.

> 57 Ma poi che il sospicar fu tutto spento,  
> 58 Piangendo disse: Se per questo cieco  
> 59 Carcere vai per altezza d’ ingegno,  
> 60 Mio figlio ov’ è? e perchè non è teco?  

But when all its expectation was quenched, it said, weeping: “If through this blind prison thou goest by height of genius, where is my son? And why is he not with thee?”

- *poi che* ‘after’ → “when”: the explicit temporal succession becomes a more general temporal connection.
- *il sospicar fu tutto spento* ‘the surmising was wholly extinguished’ → “all its expectation was quenched”: the substantivized infinitive becomes an ordinary noun, interpreted as hopeful expectation rather than suspicion or conjecture. The article becomes the possessive “its”, while *tutto* moves from the predicate to quantify “expectation”.
- *Piangendo disse* ‘weeping it said’ → “it said, weeping”: the participial expression moves after the verb.
- *per altezza d’ ingegno* ‘by height of intellect’ → “by height of genius”: “genius” gives *ingegno* the more marked sense of exceptional intellectual ability.
- *Mio figlio ov’ è* ‘my son where is he?’ → “where is my son?”: the initially foregrounded subject moves after the interrogative and verb.

> 61 Ed io a lui: Da me stesso non vegno:  
> 62 Colui, che attende là, per qui mi mena,  
> 63 Forse cui Guido vostro ebbe a disdegno.  

And I to him: “Of myself I come not. He, that waits yonder, leads me through this place; whom perhaps thy Guido held in disdain.”

- *Da me stesso* ‘from myself’ → “Of myself”: “Of” expresses independent agency rather than literal origin.
- *Colui, che attende là* ‘that one, who waits there’ → “He, that waits yonder”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, “that” replaces the personal relative ‘who’, and “yonder” makes the distance expressed by *là* more explicit.
- *per qui mi mena* ‘through here me leads’ → “leads me through this place”: the verb precedes its object and the phrase describing the route; the adverb *qui* is expanded into “this place”.
- *Forse cui* ‘perhaps whom’ → “whom perhaps”: the relative pronoun moves before the adverb.
- *Guido vostro* ‘Guido your’ → “thy Guido”: the possessive moves before the name, and the Italian plural or respectful form becomes an explicitly singular English form.
- *ebbe a disdegno* ‘had in disdain’ → “held in disdain”: “held” replaces the literal ‘had’ in the English idiom.

## Lines 64–72 — A Question and a Collapse

> 64 Le sue parole, e il modo della pena  
> 65 M’ avevan di costui già letto il nome:  

Already his words and the manner of his punishment had read his name to me.

- *Le sue parole … già* ‘his words … already’ → “Already his words”: the adverb moves from within the verb phrase to the beginning of the sentence.
- *della pena* ‘of the punishment’ → “of his punishment”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *M’ avevan … letto* ‘to me had … read’ → “had read” … “to me”: the indirect-object pronoun preceding the verb becomes a prepositional phrase following the object.
- *di costui … il nome* ‘of this man … the name’ → “his name”: the demonstrative expression becomes a possessive, and the inverted noun phrase is reordered.

> 66 Però fu la risposta così piena.  

Hence my answer was so full.

- *fu la risposta* ‘was the answer’ → “my answer was”: the subject moves before the verb, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 67 Di subito drizzato gridò: Come  
> 68 Dicesti: egli ebbe? non viv’ egli ancora?  
> 69 Non fiere gli occhi suoi lo dolce lome?  

Rising instantly erect, he cried: “How saidst thou? He had? Lives he not still? Does not the sweet light strike his eyes?”

- *Di subito drizzato* ‘suddenly straightened up’ → “Rising instantly erect”: the past participle becomes a present participle with an adjective, presenting the movement as occurring rather than completed; the adverb moves after the participle.
- *Non fiere … lo dolce lome* ‘does not strike … the sweet light’ → “Does not the sweet light strike”: the subject, which follows the verb and object in Italian, moves before the main verb.
- *gli occhi suoi* ‘the eyes his’ → “his eyes”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 70 Quando s’ accorse d’ alcuna dimora  
> 71 Ch’ io faceva dinanzi alla risposta,  
> 72 Supin ricadde, e più non parve fuora.  

When he perceived that I made some delay in answering, supine he fell again, and shewed himself no more.

- *s’ accorse d’ alcuna dimora Ch’ io faceva* ‘became aware of some delay that I was making’ → “he perceived that I made some delay”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes a transitive verb; its prepositional complement and relative clause become a clause expressing what he perceived. The imperfect is rendered by the simple past “made”.
- *dinanzi alla risposta* ‘before the answer’ → “in answering”: the temporal preposition becomes “in”, and the noun becomes a gerund.
- *ricadde* ‘fell back’ → “he fell again”: the prefix indicating return is rendered as repetition by “again”.
- *più non parve fuora* ‘no longer appeared outside’ → “shewed himself no more”: the intransitive verb becomes a transitive verb with a reflexive object; the explicit indication of place in *fuora* is omitted, and the negative expression moves after the verb.

## Lines 73–84 — Farinata’s Warning and Question

> 73 Ma quell’ altro magnanimo, a cui posta  
> 74 Restato m’ era, non mutò aspetto,  
> 75 Nè mosse collo, nè piegò sua costa.  

But that other, magnanimous, at whose desire I had stopped, changed not his aspect, nor moved his neck, nor bent his side.

- *a cui posta* ‘at whose bidding’ → “at whose desire”: the expression of bidding or request is rendered as desire.
- *Restato m’ era* ‘stayed I had’ → “I had stopped”: staying is rendered as stopping; the auxiliary precedes the participle, and the Italian pronominal construction becomes a simple English verb.
- *non mutò aspetto* ‘did not change appearance’ → “changed not his aspect”: the negative follows the verb, and “his” is supplied before the noun.
- *nè mosse collo* ‘nor moved neck’ → “nor moved his neck”: “his” is supplied.

> 76 E se, continuando al primo detto,  
> 77 Egli han quell’ arte, disse, male appresa,  
> 78 Ciò mi tormenta più che questo letto.  

“And if,” continuing his former words, he said, “they have learnt that art badly, it more torments me than this bed.

- *continuando al primo detto* ‘continuing upon the first thing said’ → “continuing his former words”: the prepositional construction becomes a direct object; the substantivized singular *detto* becomes plural “words,” “his” is supplied, and *primo* is rendered as “former.”
- *disse* ‘said’ → “he said”: the reporting clause is moved before the conditional clause’s subject and verb, rather than interrupting its object and participle.
- *Egli han quell’ arte … male appresa* ‘they have that art badly learnt’ → “they have learnt that art badly”: the participle moves before the object, and the adverb moves after it.
- *Ciò mi tormenta più* ‘that me torments more’ → “it more torments me”: the demonstrative becomes “it,” the object pronoun follows the verb, and “more” moves before it.

> 79 Ma non cinquanta volte fia raccesa  
> 80 La faccia della donna, che qui regge,  
> 81 Che tu saprai quanto quell’ arte pesa.  

But the face of the Queen, who reigns here, shall not be fifty times rekindled ere thou shalt know the hardness of that art.

- *non cinquanta volte fia raccesa La faccia* ‘not fifty times shall be rekindled the face’ → “the face” … “shall not be fifty times rekindled”: the subject moves before the verb; the negative and the expression of frequency move after the future auxiliary.
- *della donna* ‘of the lady’ → “of the Queen”: the general designation becomes a royal title.
- *che qui regge* ‘who here rules’ → “who reigns here”: ruling is rendered as reigning, and the adverb follows the verb.
- *Che* ‘that’ → “ere”: the conjunction completing the Italian construction with the preceding negative becomes an explicitly temporal conjunction meaning ‘before.’
- *quanto quell’ arte pesa* ‘how much that art weighs’ → “the hardness of that art”: an indirect question becomes a noun phrase, and the metaphor of weight is rendered as difficulty.

> 82 E se tu mai nel dolce mondo regge,  
> 83 Dimmi, perchè quel popolo è sì empio  
> 84 Incontro a’ miei in ciascuna sua legge?  

And so mayest thou once return to the sweet world, tell me why that people is so fierce against my kindred in all its laws?”

- *E se tu mai … regge* ‘And if thou ever return’ → “And so mayest thou once return”: the conditional wording is rendered as an explicit wish; ‘ever’ becomes “once,” and the verb moves before the destination.
- *nel dolce mondo* ‘in the sweet world’ → “to the sweet world”: the preposition is made explicitly directional.
- *empio* ‘impious, pitiless’ → “fierce”: the adjective is rendered in terms of ferocity rather than impiety or lack of pity.
- *Incontro a’ miei* ‘against my own’ → “against my kindred”: the substantivized possessive is interpreted explicitly as referring to relatives.
- *in ciascuna sua legge* ‘in each law of its’ → “in all its laws”: the distributive singular becomes a collective plural.

## Lines 85–93 — Slaughter and Florence’s Defence

> 85 Ond’ io a lui: Lo strazio e il grande scempio,  
> 86 Che fece l’ Arbia colorata in rosso,  
> 87 Tale orazion fa far nel nostro tempio.  

Whereat I to him: “The havoc, and the great slaughter, which dyed the Arbia red, causes such orations in our temple.”

- *Ond’* ‘whence’ → “Whereat”: the connective is rendered as a response to what precedes rather than literally as ‘from which’.
- *Lo strazio* ‘the mangling’ → “The havoc”: the specific sense of violent bodily injury becomes the broader sense of destruction.
- *Che fece l’Arbia colorata in rosso* ‘which made the Arbia coloured in red’ → “which dyed the Arbia red”: the construction with ‘made’ and a participle becomes the single verb “dyed”; ‘in red’ becomes the complement “red”.
- *Tale orazion fa far* ‘such a prayer makes [people] make’ → “causes such orations”: the two-verb causative becomes “causes”, and its object moves after it; singular *orazion* becomes plural “orations”, with a broader sense than ‘prayer’.

> 88 Poi ch’ ebbe sospirando il capo scosso;  
> 89 A ciò non fui io sol, disse, nè certo  
> 90 Senza cagion sarei con gli altri mosso:  

And sighing, he shook his head; then said: “In that I was not single; nor without cause, assuredly, should I have stirred with the others.

- *Poi ch’ ebbe sospirando il capo scosso* ‘After he had, sighing, shaken the head’ → “And sighing, he shook his head; then”: the subordinate temporal clause becomes an independent clause followed by “then”; the past perfect becomes the simple past. The gerund moves before the subject, the participle’s position after its object becomes ordinary English verb–object order, and the definite article becomes possessive “his”.
- *A ciò non fui io sol, disse* ‘in that not was I alone, he said’ → “said: “In that I was not single”: the reporting verb moves from after the first clause of the speech to before the speech begins; within the speech, the subject moves before the verb and the negative after it, and ‘alone’ is expressed as “single”.
- *certo* ‘certainly’ → “assuredly”: the adverb moves from before ‘without cause’ to after it.
- *sarei con gli altri mosso* ‘I would have moved with the others’ → “should I have stirred with the others”: the conditional perfect is expressed with “should”, with subject–auxiliary inversion following “nor”. “Stirred” replaces ‘moved’, and the phrase ‘with the others’ moves from between the auxiliary and participle to after the completed verb phrase.

> 91 Ma fu’ io sol colà, dove sofferto  
> 92 Fu per ciascuno di torre via Fiorenza,  
> 93 Colui che la difese a viso aperto.  

But I was single there, where all consented to extirpate Florence, I alone with open face defended her.”

- *fu’ io sol* ‘was I alone’ → “I was single”: the subject moves before the verb, and ‘alone’ becomes “single”.
- *dove sofferto / Fu per ciascuno* ‘where it was permitted by each’ → “where all consented”: the impersonal passive becomes an active construction; the agent ‘each’ becomes the collective subject “all”, and permitting something becomes consenting to it.
- *di torre via Fiorenza* ‘to take away Florence’ → “to extirpate Florence”: the phrasal expression ‘take away’ becomes the stronger single verb “extirpate”, interpreting the removal as complete destruction.
- *Colui che* ‘the one who’ → “I alone”: the identifying phrase and its relative pronoun become a repeated first-person subject. “Alone” is supplied here, repeating the exclusiveness already expressed earlier, and the relative clause becomes an independent assertion.
- *a viso aperto* ‘with face open’ → “with open face”: the adjective moves before the noun, and the entire phrase moves from after the verb and its object to before them.

## Lines 94–105 — Distant Sight and Present Ignorance

> 94 Deh se riposi mai vostra semenza,  
> 95 Pregai io lui, solvetemi quel nodo,  
> 96 Che qui ha inviluppata mia sentenza.  

“Ah! so may thy seed sometime have rest,” I prayed him, “solve the knot which has here involved my judgment.

- *se riposi mai* ‘if [it] may ever rest’ → “so may” … “sometime have rest”: the Italian wish introduced by *se* becomes an English optative construction; “sometime” renders *mai*, and the verb ‘rest’ becomes “have rest”. The subject now comes between the auxiliary and the main verb.
- *vostra semenza* ‘your seed’ → “thy seed”: the formally plural possessive becomes singular.
- *Pregai io lui* ‘prayed I him’ → “I prayed him”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *solvetemi* ‘loosen for me’ → “solve”: the verb is rendered as “solve”, and the indirect object *mi* is omitted.
- *quel nodo* ‘that knot’ → “the knot”: the demonstrative becomes a definite article.
- *qui ha inviluppata* ‘here has entangled’ → “has here involved”: the adverb moves after the auxiliary, and “involved” renders ‘entangled’.

> 97 E’ par che voi veggiate, se ben odo,  
> 98 Dinanzi quel, che il tempo seco adduce,  
> 99 E nel presente tenete altro modo.  

It seems that you see beforehand what time brings with it, if I rightly hear; and have a different manner with the present.”

- *veggiate* ‘see (subjunctive)’ → “see”: the Italian subjunctive dependent on *par che* becomes English indicative.
- *se ben odo* ‘if well I hear’ → “if I rightly hear”: “rightly” renders *ben*, and the qualification moves from immediately after ‘you see’ to the end of the clause about what time brings.
- *quel, che* ‘that which’ → “what”: demonstrative and relative pronoun become a single relative pronoun.
- *il tempo seco adduce* ‘the time with itself brings’ → “time brings with it”: the article is omitted, the prepositional phrase moves after the verb, and the reflexive pronoun becomes “it”.
- *nel presente tenete altro modo* ‘in the present you keep another manner’ → “have a different manner with the present”: ‘keep’ becomes “have”, ‘another’ becomes “a different”, and the opening phrase moves to the end, with “with” replacing ‘in’.

> 100 Noi veggiam come quei, che ha mala luce,  
> 101 Le cose, disse, che ne son lontano;  
> 102 Cotanto ancor ne splende il sommo Duce:  

“Like one who has imperfect vision, we see the things,” he said, “which are remote from us: so much light the Supreme Ruler still gives to us.

- *Noi veggiam come quei, che ha mala luce, Le cose* ‘we see, like that one who has bad light, the things’ → “Like one who has imperfect vision, we see the things”: the comparison moves before the main clause, bringing the verb and its object together. The generic demonstrative *quei* becomes “one”, and *mala luce* is interpreted as “imperfect vision” rather than rendered literally as ‘bad light’.
- *che ne son lontano* ‘which from us are far’ → “which are remote from us”: “from us” moves after the predicate, and the adverb *lontano* becomes the adjective “remote”.
- *Cotanto ancor ne splende il sommo Duce* ‘so much still shines to us the supreme Leader’ → “so much light the Supreme Ruler still gives to us”: ‘shines’ becomes “gives”, with “light” supplied as its object; *cotanto* consequently specifies the quantity of that light. The subject moves before the verb, “still” and “to us” are redistributed around it, and ‘Leader’ becomes “Ruler”.

> 103 Quando s’ appressano, o son, tutto è vano  
> 104 Nostro intelletto; e, s’ altri nol ci apporta,  
> 105 Nulla sapem di vostro stato umano.  

When they draw nigh, or are, our intellect is altogether void; and except what others bring us, we know nothing of your human state.

- *s’ appressano* ‘bring themselves near’ → “draw nigh”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive expression without a reflexive pronoun.
- *tutto è vano Nostro intelletto* ‘all is vain our intellect’ → “our intellect is altogether void”: the subject moves before the copula; *tutto*, qualifying the intellect as a whole, becomes the adverb “altogether”, and *vano* is rendered as “void”.
- *e, s’ altri nol ci apporta* ‘and, if someone else does not bring it to us’ → “and except what others bring us”: the negative conditional becomes an exception construction. Singular indefinite *altri* becomes plural “others”; the object pronoun within *nol* becomes relative “what”, and the negative is absorbed into “except”. The recipient is expressed by “us” without a preposition.
- *Nulla sapem* ‘nothing we know’ → “we know nothing”: the object moves after the verb.
- *stato umano* ‘state human’ → “human state”: the adjective moves before the noun.

## Lines 106–115 — Knowledge’s End and a Correction

> 106 Però comprender puoi che tutta morta  
> 107 Fia nostra conoscenza da quel punto,  
> 108 Che del futuro fia chiusa la porta.  

Therefore thou mayest understand that all our knowledge shall be dead, from that moment when the portal of the Future shall be closed.”

- *comprender puoi* ‘understand thou canst’ → “thou mayest understand”: the modal precedes the infinitive instead of following it, and ‘canst’ becomes “mayest”.
- *tutta morta / Fia nostra conoscenza* ‘wholly dead shall be our knowledge’ → “all our knowledge shall be dead”: the subject moves before the verb and its complement; *tutta*, qualifying the completeness of being dead, becomes “all”, directly quantifying “our knowledge”.
- *da quel punto, / Che* ‘from that point that’ → “from that moment when”: “moment” makes the temporal meaning of *punto* explicit, and “when” replaces the relative ‘that’.
- *del futuro fia chiusa la porta* ‘of the future shall be closed the door’ → “the portal of the Future shall be closed”: the subject moves before the verb, and its dependent phrase follows it; *porta* becomes the more elevated “portal”.

> 109 Allor, come di mia colpa compunto,  
> 110 Dissi: Or direte dunque a quel caduto,  
> 111 Che il suo nato è co’ vivi ancor congiunto.  

Then, as compunctious for my fault, I said: “Now will you therefore tell that fallen one, that his child is still joined to the living.

- *come di mia colpa compunto* ‘as, by my fault, pricked’ → “as compunctious for my fault”: the participle becomes an adjective expressing remorse, and the phrase identifying its cause follows rather than precedes it; ‘by’ becomes “for”.
- *Or direte dunque a quel caduto* ‘now you will therefore say to that fallen one’ → “Now will you therefore tell that fallen one”: the future statement used as a request becomes an inverted “will you” construction; ‘say to’ becomes “tell” with a direct object.
- *il suo nato* ‘his born one’ → “his child”: the substantivized participle becomes an ordinary noun.
- *è co’ vivi ancor congiunto* ‘is with the living still joined’ → “is still joined to the living”: the adverb and participle move before the prepositional phrase, and ‘with’ becomes “to”.

> 112 E s’ io fui dianzi alla risposta muto,  
> 113 Fat’ ei saper che il fei, perchè pensava  
> 114 Già nell’ error che m’ avete soluto.  

And if I was mute before, at the response, let him know, it was because my thoughts already were in that error which you have resolved for me.”

- *dianzi alla risposta muto* ‘before, at the response, mute’ → “mute before, at the response”: the predicative adjective moves before the adverb and prepositional phrase.
- *Fat’ ei saper* ‘make him know’ → “let him know”: the causative imperative becomes the English idiom for communicating information.
- *che il fei, perchè* ‘that I did it, because’ → “it was because”: the explicit statement of the speaker’s action becomes an explanatory “it was” construction, and the introductory ‘that’ is omitted.
- *pensava / Già nell’ error* ‘I was thinking already in the error’ → “my thoughts already were in that error”: the verb of thinking becomes the noun “thoughts”, with the speaker represented by “my”; “were” supplies the new predicate, and the definite article becomes the demonstrative “that”.
- *che m’ avete soluto* ‘which for me you have resolved’ → “which you have resolved for me”: the pronoun preceding the verb becomes a prepositional phrase following it.

> 115 E già il Maestro mio mi richiamava:  

And now my Master was recalling me.

- *già* ‘already’ → “now”: the adverb marks the current moment rather than explicitly indicating that something has already happened.
- *il Maestro mio* ‘the Master my’ → “my Master”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *mi richiamava* ‘me he was calling back’ → “was recalling me”: the object pronoun follows rather than precedes the verb, and ‘calling back’ becomes “recalling”.

## Lines 116–123 — The Spirit’s Companions and a Hostile Saying

> 116 Per ch’ io pregai lo spirito più avaccio,  
> 117 Che mi dicesse, chi con lui si stava.  

Wherefore I, in more haste, besought the spirit to tell me who was with him.

- *più avaccio* ‘more quickly’ → “in more haste”: an adverbial phrase becomes a prepositional phrase and moves from after “the spirit” to before “besought”.
- *Che mi dicesse* ‘that he should tell me’ → “to tell me”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an infinitive clause dependent on “besought”.
- *chi con lui si stava* ‘who with him was staying’ → “who was with him”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase; the pronominal *si stava* becomes the simple “was”, without separately rendering *si*.

> 118 Dissemi: Qui con più di mille giaccio:  

He said to me: “With more than a thousand lie I here.

- *Qui con più di mille giaccio* ‘here with more than thousand I lie’ → “With more than a thousand lie I here”: the adverb moves from the beginning of the statement to its end, the verb precedes its subject, and English supplies the indefinite article before the numeral.

> 119 Qua entro è lo secondo Federico,  
> 120 E il Cardinale, e degli altri mi taccio  

The second Frederick is here within, and the Cardinal; and of the rest I speak not.”

- *Qua entro è lo secondo Federico* ‘here within is the second Frederick’ → “The second Frederick is here within”: the subject moves before the verb, and the adverbs of place move after it.
- *degli altri* ‘of the others’ → “of the rest”: a plural substantivized adjective becomes a collective noun phrase.
- *mi taccio* ‘I keep silent’ → “I speak not”: the affirmative pronominal verb becomes a negated verb of speaking.

> 121 Indi s’ ascose: ed io in vêr l’ antico  
> 122 Poeta volsi i passi, ripensando  
> 123 A quel parlar che mi parea nemico.  

Therewith he hid himself. And I towards the ancient Poet turned my steps, revolving that saying which seemed hostile to me.

- *Indi* ‘thence’ → “Therewith”: the adverb is understood as marking the transition to the next action rather than movement from a place.
- *i passi* ‘the steps’ → “my steps”: the definite article becomes a possessive determiner.
- *ripensando A* ‘thinking again about’ → “revolving”: the verb with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb, using the figurative sense of turning something over in the mind.
- *quel parlar* ‘that speaking’ → “that saying”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun denoting an utterance.
- *che mi parea nemico* ‘which to me seemed hostile’ → “which seemed hostile to me”: the personal complement moves from before the verb to after the adjective.

## Lines 124–133 — Counsel and a Promised Revelation

> 124 Egli si mosse; e poi così andando  
> 125 Mi disse: Perchè sei tu sì smarrito?  

He moved on; and then, as we were going, he said to me: “Why art thou so bewildered?”

- *Egli si mosse* ‘He moved’ → “He moved on”: “on” is supplied, suggesting continued movement.
- *così andando* ‘thus going’ → “as we were going”: the gerund becomes a finite temporal clause, with a plural subject; *così* is not separately rendered.
- *Mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “he said to me”: the recipient follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *smarrito* ‘lost’ → “bewildered”: the translation makes explicit the word’s figurative sense of mental confusion.

> 126 Ed io li soddisfeci al suo dimando.  

And I satisfied him in his question.

- *li soddisfeci al suo dimando* ‘to him I gave satisfaction to his question’ → “I satisfied him in his question”: the Italian dative becomes the direct object “him” and follows the verb; the relation expressed by *al* is rendered with “in” rather than ‘to’.

> 127 La mente tua conservi quel che udito  
> 128 Hai contra te, mi comandò quel Saggio,  

“Let thy memory retain what thou hast heard against thee,” that Sage exhorted me.

- *La mente tua conservi* ‘may the mind thy retain’ → “Let thy memory retain”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”; *mente* is rendered more specifically as “memory”, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *quel che* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun become a single fused relative.
- *udito Hai* ‘heard thou hast’ → “thou hast heard”: the auxiliary precedes the participle rather than following it.
- *mi comandò quel Saggio* ‘to me commanded that Sage’ → “that Sage exhorted me”: the subject moves before the verb and the recipient after it; “exhorted” renders the command as an urging.

> 129 Ed ora attendi qui: e drizzò il dito.  

“And now mark here,” and he raised his finger.

- *attendi* ‘pay attention’ → “mark”: the instruction to attend is expressed by a verb meaning ‘notice’ or ‘heed’.
- *drizzò il dito* ‘straightened the finger’ → “he raised his finger”: straightening is rendered as raising, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 130 Quando sarai dinanzi al dolce raggio  
> 131 Di quella, il cui bell’ occhio tutto vede,  
> 132 Da lei saprai di tua vita il viaggio.  

“When thou shalt stand before the sweet ray of that Lady, whose bright eye seeth all, from her shalt thou know the journey of thy life.”

- *sarai* ‘thou shalt be’ → “thou shalt stand”: the general verb of being becomes a verb specifying posture.
- *Di quella* ‘of that one’ → “of that Lady”: the feminine demonstrative pronoun is expanded with the supplied noun “Lady”.
- *bell’* ‘beautiful’ → “bright”: beauty is rendered as brightness.
- *tutto vede* ‘all sees’ → “seeth all”: the object moves after the verb.
- *saprai* ‘thou shalt know’ → “shalt thou know”: the English subject and auxiliary are inverted after the preceding phrase “from her”.
- *di tua vita il viaggio* ‘of thy life the journey’ → “the journey of thy life”: the dependent possessive phrase moves after its governing noun.

> 133 Appresso volse a man sinistra il piede:  

Then to the sinister hand he turned his feet.

- *volse a man sinistra il piede* ‘turned to hand left the foot’ → “to the sinister hand he turned his feet”: the directional phrase moves before the verb; an article is supplied before “hand”, and its adjective moves before it. The singular ‘foot’ becomes plural “feet”, and its definite article becomes a possessive.

## Lines 134–136 — Toward the Foul-Smelling Valley

> 134 Lasciammo il muro, e gimmo in vêr lo mezzo  

We left the wall, and went towards the middle,

- Literal.

> 135 Per un sentier, che ad una valle fiede,  

by a path that strikes into a valley,

- *ad una valle fiede* ‘to a valley strikes’ → “strikes into a valley”: the verb precedes the prepositional phrase, and *ad* is rendered as “into” rather than ‘to’.

> 136 Che in fin lassù facea spiacer suo lezzo.  

which even up there annoyed us with its fetor.

- *in fin lassù* ‘as far as up there’ → “even up there”: the expression of spatial extent becomes an emphatic phrase with “even”.
- *facea spiacer suo lezzo* ‘made its stench displease’ → “annoyed us with its fetor”: the causative construction becomes a direct verb of annoyance; the stench, which causes displeasure in the Italian, becomes a phrase introduced by “with”. The translation also supplies “us” as the people affected.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Syntax

Carlyle generally keeps close to the Italian vocabulary and sequence of expression, but adjusts word order to English usage. Object pronouns commonly move after their verbs, adjectives and possessives before their nouns, and subjects before predicates that precede them in Italian. Thus *tutto vede* becomes “seeth all,” and *di tua vita il viaggio* becomes “the journey of thy life.” Yet he also preserves marked inversions, as in “Fiercely adverse were they” and “With more than a thousand lie I here.” More generally, the translation supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending, and omits the articles that Italian regularly places before possessives.

Changes of construction sometimes go beyond rearrangement. *Suo cimitero … hanno*, ‘have their burial ground’, becomes the passive “are entombed”; *soddisfatto sarai*, ‘thou shalt be satisfied’, becomes “thou shalt … have satisfaction.” Verbal expressions can become nouns: *per dicer poco*, ‘to say little’, is rendered as “for brevity of speech,” while *quanto quell’ arte pesa*, ‘how much that art weighs’, becomes “the hardness of that art.” Elsewhere, relative clauses become participial phrases, finite clauses become infinitives, and Italian exhortative subjunctives are expressed with “Let.” Tense also shifts where English usage favours it, notably from future to present in “when, from Jehosaphat, they return here.”

Word choice ranges from close lexical correspondence to contextual interpretation. *Duca* regularly becomes “Guide,” *donna* becomes “Queen,” and *mala luce*, literally ‘bad light’, is interpreted as “imperfect vision.” Idiomatic substitutions include “keeps guard” for *guardia face* and “held in disdain” for *ebbe a disdegno*. At other points Carlyle retains wording that sounds unusual in English: “Let thy words be numbered,” “had read his name to me,” and “all our knowledge shall be dead” preserve the Italian’s particular expressions rather than replacing them with explanations.

Omissions and additions are usually local. *Già* is left unrepresented in “The covers all are raised,” and the personal complement in *solvetemi* disappears in “solve”; conversely, “thus” is supplied with “wheelest me,” “Lady” expands *quella*, and “us” identifies those affected in “annoyed us with its fetor.” Possessives also make relationships explicit where Italian uses an article or no determiner, as in “his brows” and “his neck.” Overall, Carlyle combines substantial verbal closeness with freer handling of grammar and idiom: he often preserves the Italian’s images and rhetorical shape even when the English requires reordered phrases, supplied words, or a different construction.
