# Inferno 34

## Lines 1–12 — A Distant Shape and Frozen Souls

> 1 Vexilla Regis prodeunt Inferni  
> 2 Verso di noi: però dinanzi mira,  
> 3 Disse il Maestro mio, se tu il discerni.  

“Vexilla Regis prodeunt Inferni towards us: therefore look in front of thee,” my Master said, “if thou discernest him.”

- *Vexilla Regis prodeunt Inferni* ‘The banners of the King of Hell advance’ → “Vexilla Regis prodeunt Inferni”: the Latin words are retained untranslated.
- *dinanzi mira* ‘in front look’ → “look in front of thee”: the verb precedes the adverbial phrase, and “of thee” makes its reference explicit.
- *Disse il Maestro mio* ‘said my Master’ → “my Master said”: subject and verb exchange places, and the possessive precedes its noun.

> 4 Come quando una grossa nebbia spira,  
> 5 O quando l’ emisperio nostro annotta  
> 6 Par da lungi un mulin che al vento gira;  
> 7 Veder mi parve un tal dificio allotta:  

As, when a thick mist breathes, or when the night comes on our hemisphere, a mill, turning with the wind, appears at distance: such an edifice did I now seem to see;

- *l’ emisperio nostro annotta* ‘our hemisphere grows dark’ → “the night comes on our hemisphere”: the hemisphere ceases to be the subject; “the night” is supplied as the subject of a new verbal construction. The possessive also moves before its noun.
- *Par da lungi un mulin che al vento gira* ‘appears from afar a mill that turns in the wind’ → “a mill, turning with the wind, appears at distance”: the subject moves before the main verb, and the relative clause becomes a participial phrase. ‘From afar’ becomes “at distance,” and ‘in the wind’ becomes “with the wind.”
- *Veder mi parve un tal dificio allotta* ‘To see such an edifice seemed to me then’ → “such an edifice did I now seem to see”: the construction with ‘to me’ becomes a personal construction with “I” as subject. The object is placed first, the infinitive moves to the end, and the past tense is expressed with “did.” *Allotta*, ‘then,’ becomes “now.”

> 8 Poi per lo vento mi ristrinsi retro  
> 9 Al Duca mio; chè non v’ era altra grotta.  

and, for the wind, shrunk back behind my Guide, because no other shed was there.

- *Poi* ‘Then’ → “and”: a temporal adverb becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *mi ristrinsi retro Al Duca mio* ‘I drew myself close behind my Guide’ → “shrunk back behind my Guide”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and “back” explicitly marks the backward movement alongside “behind.” The possessive moves before its noun.
- *chè non v’ era altra grotta* ‘because there was no other cave’ → “because no other shed was there”: the subject moves before the verb and “there” moves to the end; *grotta*, ‘cave,’ is rendered as “shed.”

> 10 Già era (e con paura il metto in metro)  
> 11 Là, dove l’ ombre tutte eran coverte,  
> 12 E trasparean come festuca in vetro.  

Already I had come (and with fear I put it into verse) where all the souls were covered, and shone through like straw in glass.

- *Già era* ‘Already I was’ → “Already I had come”: a state or location becomes an arrival, and the imperfect becomes a past perfect.
- *in metro* ‘into metre’ → “into verse”: the reference to metrical form is rendered more generally as verse.
- *Là, dove* ‘there, where’ → “where”: the preceding adverb ‘there’ is omitted.
- *l’ ombre tutte eran coverte* ‘the shades all were covered’ → “all the souls were covered”: “all” moves before the article and noun, and ‘shades’ becomes “souls.”
- *trasparean* ‘showed through’ → “shone through”: visibility through a covering is expressed as shining through it.
- *festuca* ‘a straw’ → “straw”: the singular count noun becomes an uncountable noun.

## Lines 13–25 — The Sight of Dis

> 13 Altre stanno a giacere, altre stanno erte,  
> 14 Quella col capo, e quella con le piante;  
> 15 Altra, com’ arco, il volto a’ piedi inverte.  

Some keep lying; some stand upright, this on its head, and that upon its soles; another, like a bow, bends face to feet.

- *Altre … altre* ‘Others … others’ → “Some” … “some”: the pronouns distinguish groups as indefinite portions rather than as others.
- *stanno a giacere* ‘stay lying’ → “keep lying”: the construction with *stare* and an infinitive becomes “keep” with a participle, emphasizing continuation.
- *Quella col capo, e quella con le piante* ‘That one with the head, and that one with the soles’ → “this on its head, and that upon its soles”: the repeated demonstrative becomes a contrasting “this” and “that”; “on” and “upon” interpret *con* as indicating the supporting part, and possessives replace the definite articles.
- *il volto a’ piedi inverte* ‘the face to the feet inverts’ → “bends face to feet”: the verb moves before its object; “bends” replaces ‘inverts’, and the articles before the body parts are omitted.

> 16 Quando noi fummo fatti tanto avante,  
> 17 Ch’ al mio Maestro piacque di mostrarmi  
> 18 La creatura ch’ ebbe il bel sembiante,  

When we had proceeded on so far, that it pleased my Guide to show to me the Creature which was once so fair,

- *noi fummo fatti tanto avante* ‘we were made so far forward’ → “we had proceeded on so far”: the Italian passive-form expression becomes an active verb of movement, and the simple past becomes a past perfect.
- *al mio Maestro piacque* ‘to my Master it pleased’ → “it pleased my Guide”: the experiencer introduced by *a* becomes the object of “pleased” and follows the verb; “Guide” replaces ‘Master’.
- *di mostrarmi* ‘to show me’ → “to show to me”: the attached indirect-object pronoun becomes a separate prepositional phrase.
- *ch’ ebbe il bel sembiante* ‘which had the fair appearance’ → “which was once so fair”: having a beautiful appearance becomes being beautiful; “once” makes the past reference explicit, and “so” adds a degree of emphasis absent from the Italian.

> 19 Dinanzi mi si tolse, e fe’ restarmi,  
> 20 Ecco Dite, dicendo, ed ecco il loco  
> 21 Ove convien che di fortezza t’ armi.  

he took himself from before me, and made me stop, saying: “Lo Dis! and lo the place where it behoves thee arm thyself with fortitude.”

- *Dinanzi mi si tolse* ‘From before me he removed himself’ → “he took himself from before me”: the initial phrase of position moves after the verb and reflexive pronoun; ‘removed himself’ is expressed as “took himself”.
- *e fe’ restarmi* ‘and made me remain’ → “and made me stop”: *restare* is interpreted as ceasing to move rather than remaining.
- *Ecco Dite, dicendo, ed ecco il loco* ‘Lo Dis, saying, and lo the place’ → “saying: “Lo Dis! and lo the place”: the participle moves from after the first announcement to before the whole quotation.
- *convien che di fortezza t’ armi* ‘it is fitting that with fortitude you arm yourself’ → “it behoves thee arm thyself with fortitude”: the clause introduced by *che*, with a subjunctive verb, becomes an object pronoun followed by a bare infinitive; the phrase expressing what to arm oneself with moves after the verb.

> 22 Com’ io divenni allor gelato e fioco,  
> 23 Nol dimandar, Lettor, ch’ io non lo scrivo,  
> 24 Però ch’ ogni parlar sarebbe poco.  

How icy chill and hoarse I then became, ask not, O Reader! for I write it not, because all speech would fail to tell.

- *Com’ io divenni allor gelato e fioco* ‘How I became then frozen and faint’ → “How icy chill and hoarse I then became”: the predicate adjectives move immediately after “How”, before the subject and verb, while “then” moves before “became”; “icy chill” replaces ‘frozen’, and “hoarse” interprets *fioco* specifically as weakness of voice rather than general faintness.
- *Nol dimandar* ‘Do not ask it’ → “ask not”: the object pronoun contained in *nol* is omitted, and the negative follows the imperative verb.
- *Lettor* ‘Reader’ → “O Reader”: the vocative marker “O” is supplied.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating causal conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *io non lo scrivo* ‘I do not write it’ → “I write it not”: the object pronoun follows the verb rather than preceding it, and the negative follows both.
- *ogni parlar* ‘every speaking’ → “all speech”: the distributive expression becomes a collective one, and the substantivized infinitive becomes the noun “speech”.
- *sarebbe poco* ‘would be little’ → “would fail to tell”: a statement of insufficiency becomes a verbal expression of failure; “to tell” supplies the activity for which speech would be insufficient.

> 25 Io non morii, e non rimasi vivo:  

I did not die, and did not remain alive:

- Literal.

## Lines 26–38 — The Emperor’s Size and Three Faces

> 26 Pensa oramai per te, s’ hai fior d’ ingegno,  
> 27 Qual io divenni, d’ uno e d’ altro privo.  

now think for thyself, if thou hast any grain of ingenuity, what I became, deprived of both death and life.

- *Pensa oramai* ‘think now’ → “now think”: the adverb moves before the imperative.
- *per te* ‘for thee’ → “for thyself”: the personal pronoun becomes an emphatic reflexive.
- *s’ hai fior d’ ingegno* ‘if thou hast a flower of ingenuity’ → “if thou hast any grain of ingenuity”: the figurative flower becomes a grain, expressing the smallest amount; “any” is supplied.
- *d’ uno e d’ altro privo* ‘of the one and of the other deprived’ → “deprived of both death and life”: the adjective moves before its complement; the unnamed alternatives are explicitly identified, and the repeated preposition becomes a single “of” with “both”.

> 28 Lo Imperador del doloroso regno  
> 29 Da mezzo il petto uscia fuor della ghiaccia;  

The Emperor of the dolorous realm, from mid breast stood forth out of the ice;

- *Da mezzo il petto* ‘from the middle of the breast’ → “from mid breast”: the expression is compressed into an articleless phrase with “mid”.
- *uscia fuor della ghiaccia* ‘was issuing out of the ice’ → “stood forth out of the ice”: the imperfect verb of emergence becomes a simple past describing position, with “forth” expressing projection.

> 30 E più con un gigante io mi convegno,  
> 31 Che i giganti non fan con le sue braccia:  

and I in size am liker to a giant, than the giants are to his arms.

- *più con un gigante io mi convegno* ‘more with a giant I correspond’ → “I in size am liker to a giant”: the comparison, placed before the subject in Italian, follows it in English; the pronominal verb becomes a copula and comparative adjective, ‘with’ becomes “to”, and “in size” is supplied.
- *Che i giganti non fan con le sue braccia* ‘than the giants do not do with his arms’ → “than the giants are to his arms”: the non-negative *non* used in the comparison is omitted; *fan*, standing for the preceding verb, becomes “are”, with the comparative adjective understood; ‘with’ becomes “to”.

> 32 Vedi oggimai quant’ esser dee quel tutto,  
> 33 Ch’ a così fatta parte si confaccia.  

Mark now how great that whole must be, which corresponds to such a part.

- *Vedi* ‘see’ → “Mark”: visual perception is expressed as attentive consideration.
- *quant’ esser dee quel tutto* ‘how great must be that whole’ → “how great that whole must be”: the subject moves before the verbal group, and the Italian infinitive–modal order becomes English modal–infinitive order.
- *Ch’ a così fatta parte si confaccia* ‘which to a part so fashioned conforms itself’ → “which corresponds to such a part”: the complement moves after the verb; the pronominal construction becomes non-reflexive, and the present subjunctive becomes indicative. The description ‘so fashioned’ is compressed into “such”.

> 34 S’ ei fu sì bel, com’ egli è ora brutto,  
> 35 E contra il suo Fattore alzò le ciglia,  
> 36 Ben dee da lui procedere ogni lutto.  

If he was once as beautiful as he is ugly now, and lifted up his brows against his Maker, well may all affliction come from him.

- *fu* ‘was’ → “was once”: “once” is supplied to make the past-time contrast explicit.
- *sì bel, com’* ‘so beautiful as’ → “as beautiful as”: the Italian correlative construction becomes the English “as … as” construction.
- *ora brutto* ‘now ugly’ → “ugly now”: the adverb moves after the adjective.
- *contra il suo Fattore alzò le ciglia* ‘against his Maker raised the brows’ → “lifted up his brows against his Maker”: the opening prepositional phrase moves after the verb and object; ‘raised’ becomes “lifted up”, and the definite article before the body part becomes the possessive “his”.
- *dee* ‘must’ → “may”: the modal expression of necessity or inference becomes one of possibility.
- *da lui procedere* ‘from him proceed’ → “come from him”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb, and ‘proceed’ becomes “come”.
- *ogni lutto* ‘every mourning’ → “all affliction”: distributive ‘every’ becomes collective “all”, and ‘mourning’ is broadened to “affliction”; the subject moves from after the verb to before it.

> 37 O quanto parve a me gran meraviglia,  
> 38 Quando vidi tre facce alla sua testa!  

Oh how great a marvel seemed it to me, when I saw three faces on his head!

- *O quanto parve a me gran meraviglia* ‘Oh how much it seemed to me a great marvel’ → “Oh how great a marvel seemed it to me”: the degree expressed by *quanto* is combined with *gran* in “how great”; the predicate noun phrase moves before the verb, producing the inverted English construction “seemed it”.
- *alla sua testa* ‘at his head’ → “on his head”: the Italian preposition is rendered by “on” rather than ‘at’.

## Lines 39–52 — The Three Faces And Wings

> 39 L’ una dinanzi, e quella era vermiglia:  

The one in front, and it was fiery red:

- *quella* ‘that one’ → “it”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *vermiglia* ‘vermilion’ → “fiery red”: a particular shade of red becomes a descriptive phrase, with “fiery” added.

> 40 L’ altre eran due, che s’ aggiungeano a questa  
> 41 Sovr’ esso il mezzo di ciascuna spalla,  
> 42 E si giungeano al luogo della cresta.  

the others were two, that were adjoined to this, above the very middle of each shoulder; and they were joined up to his crest;

- *s’ aggiungeano* ‘joined themselves’ → “were adjoined”: the pronominal construction becomes passive.
- *Sovr’ esso il mezzo* ‘right above the middle’ → “above the very middle”: the intensifying *esso* is represented by “very” modifying “middle”.
- *si giungeano* ‘joined themselves’ → “they were joined”: the pronominal construction again becomes passive.
- *al luogo della cresta* ‘at the place of the crest’ → “up to his crest”: ‘the place of’ is omitted; “up to” replaces the indication of a meeting point with an expression of extent, and the definite article becomes the possessive “his”.

> 43 E la destra parea tra bianca e gialla:  

and the right seemed between white and yellow;

- Literal.

> 44 La sinistra a vedere era tal, quali  
> 45 Vengon di là, ove il Nilo s’ avvalla.  

the left was such to look on, as they who come from where the Nile begins his valley.

- *a vedere era tal* ‘to see was such’ → “was such to look on”: the infinitive moves after the predicate, and ‘to see’ becomes “to look on”.
- *quali* ‘as’ → “as they who”: the plural comparative-relative pronoun is expanded, making its implied human referent explicit.
- *di là, ove* ‘from there, where’ → “from where”: the separate indication ‘there’ is omitted.
- *s’ avvalla* ‘descends into the valley’ → “begins his valley”: descending or entering a valley is interpreted as beginning a valley course. The pronominal verb becomes a transitive construction, with “valley” expressed as a noun and “his” supplied.

> 46 Sotto ciascuna uscivan duo grand’ ali,  
> 47 Quanto si conveniva a tanto uccello:  

Under each there issued forth two mighty wings, of size befitting such a bird:

- *uscivan* ‘came out’ → “there issued forth”: “there” is supplied to introduce the following subject, and the verb becomes the phrase “issued forth”.
- *grand’* ‘great’ → “mighty”: the adjective of size is rendered by one that also suggests power.
- *Quanto si conveniva a tanto uccello* ‘as much as was fitting for so great a bird’ → “of size befitting such a bird”: “of size” makes the implied dimension explicit; the finite verb becomes the participle “befitting”, taking a direct object rather than a phrase with *a*. ‘So great’ becomes “such”, leaving the reference to size less explicit in that phrase.

> 48 Vele di mar non vid’ io mai cotali.  

sea-sails I never saw so broad.

- *Vele di mar* ‘sails of the sea’ → “sea-sails”: the noun and its modifying prepositional phrase become a compound.
- *non vid’ io mai* ‘not saw I ever’ → “I never saw”: the expressed subject moves before the verb, and the two-part negative becomes “never”.
- *cotali* ‘such’ → “so broad”: the general comparison is narrowed to breadth.

> 49 Non avean penne, ma di vispistrello  
> 50 Era lor modo: e quelle svolazzava,  
> 51 Sì, che tre venti si movean da ello.  

No plumes had they; but were in form and texture like a bat’s: and he was flapping them, so that three winds went forth from him,

- *Non avean penne* ‘they did not have feathers’ → “No plumes had they”: verbal negation becomes the determiner “No”; the object moves before the verb, and the subject follows it. “Plumes” replaces the more general ‘feathers’.
- *ma di vispistrello Era lor modo* ‘but of a bat was their form’ → “but were in form and texture like a bat’s”: ‘their form’ ceases to be the grammatical subject; the wings remain the understood subject instead. *modo* is expanded into “form and texture”, and the relation expressed by *di* becomes an explicit comparison with “like”. The reference to the bat moves to the end.
- *e quelle svolazzava* ‘and those he fluttered’ → “and he was flapping them”: the demonstrative object becomes a personal pronoun and moves after the verb; ‘fluttered’ becomes “flapping”, and the imperfect is expressed by the past progressive.
- *si movean* ‘moved’ → “went forth”: the pronominal verb of movement becomes a phrase explicitly indicating outward motion.

> 52 Quindi Cocito tutto s’ aggelava.  

whereby Cocytus all was frozen.

- *Quindi* ‘from this’ → “whereby”: the causal adverb becomes a relative connective linking the freezing to the preceding clause.
- *s’ aggelava* ‘froze’ → “was frozen”: the pronominal verb expressing the process of freezing becomes a passive construction.

## Lines 53–66 — Three Sinners in Lucifer’s Mouths

> 53 Con sei occhi piangeva, e per tre menti  
> 54 Gocciava il pianto e sanguinosa bava.  

With six eyes he wept; and down three chins gushed tears and bloody foam.

- *per tre menti* ‘along three chins’ → “down three chins”: the preposition explicitly gives the downward direction.
- *Gocciava* ‘dripped’ → “gushed”: dripping becomes a stronger, more copious flow.
- *il pianto* ‘the weeping’ → “tears”: the singular noun naming the action becomes a plural noun naming what is shed, and the definite article is omitted.
- *bava* ‘slaver’ → “foam”: the translation emphasizes froth rather than saliva.

> 55 Da ogni bocca dirompea co’ denti  
> 56 Un peccatore, a guisa di maciulla;  
> 57 Sì che tre ne facea così dolenti.  

In every mouth he champed a sinner with his teeth, like a brake; so that he thus kept three of them in torment.

- *Da ogni bocca* ‘from every mouth’ → “In every mouth”: the preposition of origin becomes one of location.
- *dirompea* ‘tore apart’ → “champed”: the verb describes chewing rather than explicitly breaking or rending apart.
- *co’ denti* ‘with the teeth’ → “with his teeth”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves after the object.
- *a guisa di maciulla* ‘in the manner of a brake’ → “like a brake”: the comparative expression is compressed into “like”, and an indefinite article is supplied before the noun.
- *tre ne facea così dolenti* ‘three of them he made thus suffering’ → “he thus kept three of them in torment”: making becomes keeping; the adjective describing suffering becomes the prepositional phrase “in torment”; and “thus” moves before the verb, while “three of them” follows it.

> 58 A quel dinanzi il mordere era nulla  
> 59 Verso il graffiar, che tal volta la schiena  
> 60 Rimanea della pelle tutta brulla.  

To the one in front, the biting was nought, compared with the tearing; for at times the back of him remained quite stript of skin.

- *A quel dinanzi* ‘to that one in front’ → “To the one in front”: the demonstrative becomes a definite article.
- *Verso* ‘against’ → “compared with”: the comparative force of the preposition is expressed explicitly.
- *il graffiar* ‘the scratching’ → “the tearing”: the translation substitutes a more forceful action for scratching.
- *che* ‘that’ → “for”: a clause expressing the result becomes an explanatory clause.
- *tal volta* ‘at some time’ → “at times”: the singular temporal expression becomes a plural one.
- *la schiena* ‘the back’ → “the back of him”: an explicit indication of possession is added.
- *Rimanea della pelle tutta brulla* ‘remained of the skin wholly bare’ → “remained quite stript of skin”: the complement concerning the skin moves after the description of its absence; the adjective ‘bare’ becomes the participle “stript”, making removal explicit; and the article before ‘skin’ is omitted.

> 61 Quell’ anima lassù, ch’ ha maggior pena,  
> 62 Disse il Maestro, è Giuda Scariotto,  
> 63 Che il capo ha dentro, e fuor le gambe mena.  

“That soul up there, which suffers greatest punishment,” said the Master, “is Judas Iscariot, he who has his head within, and outside plies his legs.

- *ch’ ha maggior pena* ‘which has greater punishment’ → “which suffers greatest punishment”: ‘has’ becomes “suffers”, and the comparative ‘greater’ is interpreted as a superlative.
- *Che il capo ha dentro* ‘who the head has within’ → “he who has his head within”: “he” is added before the relative pronoun; the object moves after the verb; and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *e fuor le gambe mena* ‘and outside the legs moves’ → “and outside plies his legs”: the object moves after the verb, the definite article becomes a possessive, and “plies” renders the movement as repeated activity.

> 64 De gli altri duo, ch’ hanno il capo di sotto,  
> 65 Quei, che pende dal nero ceffo, è Bruto:  
> 66 Vedi come si storce, e non fa motto;  

Of the other two, who have their heads beneath, that one, who hangs from the black visage, is Brutus: see how he writhes himself, and utters not a word.

- *ch’ hanno il capo di sotto* ‘who have the head beneath’ → “who have their heads beneath”: the distributive singular ‘the head’ becomes the plural “their heads”, with a possessive replacing the definite article.
- *ceffo* ‘mug’ → “visage”: the derogatory Italian noun becomes a more elevated, neutral word for the face.
- *si storce* ‘himself he twists’ → “he writhes himself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb, and twisting is expressed as writhing.
- *non fa motto* ‘makes no utterance’ → “utters not a word”: the verb-and-noun expression becomes the verb “utters” with “a word” as its object, and the negative follows the verb.

## Lines 67–75 — Cassius and the Descent

> 67 E l’ altro è Cassio, che par sì membruto.  

And the other is Cassius, who seems so stark of limb.

- *membruto* ‘large-limbed’ → “stark of limb”: the single adjective becomes an adjectival phrase, emphasizing strength rather than size.

> 68 Ma la notte risurge; ed oramai  
> 69 E’ da partir, chè tutto avèm veduto.  

But night is reascending: and now must we depart; for we have seen the whole.”

- *risurge* ‘rises again’ → “is reascending”: the simple present becomes a progressive construction, with repetition expressed by the prefix ‘re-’.
- *ed oramai E’ da partir* ‘and by now it is necessary to depart’ → “and now must we depart”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal construction with “must”; “now” does not separately express the sense of ‘by this time’ in *oramai*, and the subject follows the modal verb.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *tutto avèm veduto* ‘everything we have seen’ → “we have seen the whole”: the object moves after the verb, and ‘everything’ becomes the substantival phrase “the whole”.

> 70 Come a lui piacque, il collo gli avvinghiai;  

As he desired, I clasped his neck:

- *Come a lui piacque* ‘as it pleased him’ → “As he desired”: the person pleased becomes the subject of a verb expressing desire.
- *il collo gli avvinghiai* ‘the neck to him I clasped’ → “I clasped his neck”: the object moves after the verb, and the indirect-object pronoun marking the person whose neck is clasped becomes the possessive “his”.

> 71 Ed ei prese di tempo e luogo poste:  

and he took opportunity of time and place;

- *prese di tempo e luogo poste* ‘took of time and place positions’ → “took opportunity of time and place”: *poste* is interpreted as favorable circumstances or opportunities, rather than physical positions; its plural becomes the singular abstract “opportunity”, and the modifying phrase follows its noun.

> 72 E, quando l’ ale furo aperte assai,  
> 73 Appigliò sè alle vellute coste.  

and when the wings were opened far, applied him to the shaggy sides,

- *assai* ‘much’ → “far”: degree is expressed as spatial extent.
- *Appigliò sè* ‘fastened himself’ → “applied him”: the verb of taking hold becomes “applied”, and the explicitly reflexive pronoun becomes the archaically reflexive “him”.
- *vellute coste* ‘hairy ribs’ → “shaggy sides”: the adjective emphasizes the hair’s shaggy texture, while the anatomical ‘ribs’ becomes the broader “sides”.

> 74 Di vello in vello giù discese poscia,  
> 75 Tra il folto pelo e le gelate croste.  

and then from shag to shag descended down, between the tangled hair and frozen crusts.

- *Di vello in vello giù discese poscia* ‘from fleece to fleece down he descended afterwards’ → “and then from shag to shag descended down”: “and” is supplied; the final temporal adverb moves to the beginning as “then”; “down” moves after the verb; and *vello* is rendered as “shag”, treating the hair as successive tufts rather than whole fleeces.
- *folto pelo* ‘thick hair’ → “tangled hair”: density becomes entanglement.
- *le gelate croste* ‘the frozen crusts’ → “frozen crusts”: the second definite article is not repeated in English.

## Lines 76–93 — Climbing Out of Hell

> 76 Quando noi fummo là dove la coscia  
> 77 Si volge appunto in sul grosso dell’ anche,  

When we had come to where the thigh revolves just on the swelling of the haunch,

- *fummo là dove* ‘we were there where’ → “we had come to where”: a simple past of being becomes a pluperfect of arrival; “to” expresses motion, and *là* has no separate equivalent.
- *si volge* ‘turns itself’ → “revolves”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an intransitive English verb.
- *grosso* ‘thick part’ → “swelling”: the adjective used substantivally becomes a noun describing the bulge.
- *dell’ anche* ‘of the haunches’ → “of the haunch”: the plural becomes singular.

> 78 Lo Duca con fatica e con angoscia  
> 79 Volse la testa ov’ egli avea le zanche,  

my Guide with labour and with difficulty turned his head where he had had his feet before,

- *Lo Duca* ‘the Leader’ → “my Guide”: the title changes, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *angoscia* ‘anguish’ → “difficulty”: distress is rendered as difficulty.
- *la testa* ‘the head’ → “his head”: English makes the bodily possessive explicit.
- *ov’ egli avea le zanche* ‘where he had his legs’ → “where he had had his feet before”: the imperfect becomes a pluperfect, “before” is supplied, *zanche* is rendered as “feet” rather than ‘legs’, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 80 Ed aggrappossi al pel, come uom che sale,  
> 81 Sì che in Inferno io credea tornar anche.  

and grappled on the hair, as one who mounts; so that I thought we were returning into Hell again.

- *aggrappossi al pel* ‘clung himself to the hair’ → “grappled on the hair”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive one, with “on” replacing ‘to’.
- *uom* ‘a man’ → “one”: the noun becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *in Inferno io credea tornar anche* ‘into Hell I believed to return again’ → “I thought we were returning into Hell again”: the infinitive becomes a finite progressive clause with the plural subject “we”; the destination moves from the beginning to after the verb.

> 82 Attienti ben, chè per cotali scale,  
> 83 Disse il Maestro, ansando com’ uom lasso,  
> 84 Conviensi dipartir da tanto male.  

“Hold thee fast! for by such stairs,” said my Guide, panting like a man forespent, “must we depart from so much ill.”

- *Attienti ben* ‘hold yourself well’ → “Hold thee fast!”: “fast” renders *ben* as firmness of grip.
- *il Maestro* ‘the Master’ → “my Guide”: the title changes, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Conviensi dipartir* ‘it is necessary to depart’ → “must we depart”: an impersonal expression of necessity becomes a personal construction with “must” and an explicit plural subject; the modal precedes that subject.

> 85 Poi uscì fuor per lo foro d’ un sasso,  
> 86 E pose me in su l’ orlo a sedere:  

Thereafter through the opening of a rock he issued forth, and put me on its brim to sit;

- *uscì fuor* ‘went out outside’ → “issued forth”: the Italian combination of an exit verb and an outward adverb becomes an English verb-and-adverb expression.
- *per lo foro d’ un sasso* ‘through the hole of a rock’ → “through the opening of a rock”: “opening” renders *foro*, and the phrase moves before the verb.
- *l’ orlo* ‘the edge’ → “its brim”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “brim” renders the edge.

> 87 Appresso porse a me l’ accorto passo.  

then towards me he stretched his wary step.

- *a me* ‘to me’ → “towards me”: the preposition becomes explicitly directional, and the phrase moves before the subject and verb.
- *l’ accorto passo* ‘the wary step’ → “his wary step”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 88 Io levai gli occhi, e credetti vedere  
> 89 Lucifero, com’ i’ l’ avea lasciato,  
> 90 E vidili le gambe in su tenere.  

I raised my eyes, and thought to see Lucifer as I had left him; and saw him with the legs turned upwards.

- *gli occhi* ‘the eyes’ → “my eyes”: English makes the bodily possessive explicit.
- *vidili le gambe in su tenere* ‘I saw him hold the legs upwards’ → “saw him with the legs turned upwards”: the infinitive *tenere* becomes a descriptive “with” phrase; “turned” is supplied in place of the verb of holding.

> 91 E s’ io divenni allora travagliato,  
> 92 La gente grossa il pensi, che non vede  
> 93 Qual era il punto ch’ io avea passato.  

And the gross people who see not what a point it was that I had passed, even they may judge if I grew toil-worn then.

- *s’ io divenni allora travagliato* ‘if I became then troubled’ → “if I grew toil-worn then”: the opening *se* clause is postponed and made explicitly dependent on “judge”; “then” moves to its end, and “toil-worn” interprets *travagliato* as exhaustion rather than bewilderment or distress.
- *La gente grossa* ‘the people gross’ → “the gross people”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *il pensi* ‘let it think of it’ → “even they may judge”: the singular exhortative subjunctive becomes a plural construction with “may”; “judge” replaces ‘think’, the resumptive object *il* disappears, and “even they” emphatically repeats the subject.
- *che non vede* ‘who does not see’ → “who see not”: singular agreement with the collective *gente* becomes plural agreement with “people”; the negative follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *Qual era il punto* ‘what was the point’ → “what a point it was”: the definite noun phrase becomes an indefinite, exclamatory one, with “it” supplied as the subject and the verb placed after it.

## Lines 94–105 — Rising and Asking Questions

> 94 Levati su, disse il Maestro, in piede:  

“Rise up!” said the Master, “upon thy feet:

- *Levati su* ‘raise thyself up’ → “Rise up”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *in piede* ‘on foot’ → “upon thy feet”: the singular becomes plural, and the possessive “thy” is supplied.

> 95 La via è lunga, e il cammino è malvagio,  

the way is long, and difficult the road;

- *il cammino è malvagio* ‘the road is bad’ → “difficult the road”: “difficult” interprets *malvagio* as describing difficulty of passage; the adjective precedes the subject, and the verb ‘is’ is omitted.

> 96 E già il Sole a mezza terza riede.  

and now to middle tierce the Sun returns.”

- *già* ‘already’ → “now”: prior attainment becomes a statement of the present time.
- *il Sole a mezza terza riede* ‘the Sun to middle tierce returns’ → “to middle tierce the Sun returns”: the phrase indicating time moves before the subject.

> 97 Non era camminata di palagio  
> 98 Là ov’ eravàm, ma natural burella  
> 99 Ch’ avea mal suolo, e di lume disagio.  

It was no palace-hall, there where we stood, but natural dungeon with an evil floor and want of light.

- *Non era camminata di palagio* ‘It was not a hall of a palace’ → “It was no palace-hall”: verbal negation becomes the determiner “no,” and the noun with its dependent phrase becomes a compound noun.
- *eravàm* ‘we were’ → “we stood”: the translation specifies a posture rather than simply presence.
- *burella* ‘underground passage’ → “dungeon”: a word for a subterranean passage is rendered by one suggesting an enclosed underground chamber.
- *Ch’ avea* ‘which had’ → “with”: the relative clause becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *mal suolo* ‘bad ground’ → “an evil floor”: the ground is described as a “floor,” “evil” renders its bad condition, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *di lume disagio* ‘of light want’ → “want of light”: the dependent phrase follows its noun instead of preceding it.

> 100 Prima ch’ io dell’ Abisso mi divella,  
> 101 Maestro mio, diss’ io quando fui dritto,  
> 102 A trarmi d’ erro un poco mi favella.  

“Before I pluck myself from the Abyss,” said I when risen up, “O Master! speak to me a little, to draw me out of error.

- *io dell’ Abisso mi divella* ‘I from the Abyss myself tear away’ → “I pluck myself from the Abyss”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb, and the phrase indicating separation moves to the end; “pluck” renders the force of *divella*. The Italian subjunctive after *prima che* becomes an English present indicative.
- *Maestro mio* ‘Master my’ → “O Master!”: the possessive is omitted, the vocative marker “O” is supplied, and the address moves after the reporting clause.
- *quando fui dritto* ‘when I was upright’ → “when risen up”: the finite clause becomes a reduced clause, and the upright state is expressed as the completed action of rising.
- *A trarmi d’ erro un poco mi favella* ‘To draw me out of error a little to me speak’ → “speak to me a little, to draw me out of error”: the purpose phrase moves from the beginning to the end, while the imperative precedes its pronoun and adverbial phrase.

> 103 Ov’ è la ghiaccia? e questi com’ è fitto  
> 104 Sì sottosopra? e come in sì poc’ ora  
> 105 Da sera a mane ha fatto il Sol tragitto?  

Where is the ice? And this, how is he fixed thus upside down? And how, in so short a time, has the Sun from eve to morn made transit?”

- *questi com’ è fitto* ‘this one how is fixed’ → “this, how is he fixed”: the demonstrative subject is placed separately at the front and resumed by “he” within the question.
- *in sì poc’ ora* ‘in so little an hour’ → “in so short a time”: *ora* is interpreted as a duration rather than a measured hour, and the small quantity expressed by *poco* becomes shortness of duration.
- *Da sera a mane ha fatto il Sol tragitto* ‘From evening to morning has made the Sun transit’ → “has the Sun from eve to morn made transit”: the auxiliary moves before the phrase indicating the interval, while the subject moves before both that phrase and the participle “made.”

## Lines 106–115 — Passing Beyond the Centre

> 106 Ed egli a me: Tu immagini ancora  
> 107 D’ esser di là dal centro, ov’ io mi presi  
> 108 Al pel del vermo reo che il mondo fora.  

And he to me: “Thou imaginest that thou art still upon the other side of the centre, where I caught hold on the hair of the evil Worm which pierces through the world.

- *Tu immagini ancora D’ esser* ‘Thou imaginest still to be’ → “Thou imaginest that thou art still”: the infinitive becomes a finite clause introduced by “that”; “still” moves from the verb of imagining to the clause describing where the hearer is.
- *di là dal centro* ‘beyond the centre’ → “upon the other side of the centre”: the compact expression of position is expanded into a phrase with “side”.
- *io mi presi Al pel* ‘I attached myself to the hair’ → “I caught hold on the hair”: the reflexive construction becomes the non-reflexive idiom “caught hold on”.
- *vermo reo* ‘worm wicked’ → “evil Worm”: “evil” renders *reo*, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *che il mondo fora* ‘which the world pierces’ → “which pierces through the world”: the object moves after the verb, and “through” is supplied.

> 109 Di là fosti cotanto, quant’ io scesi:  

Thou wast on that side, so long as I descended:

- *Di là fosti* ‘On that side thou wast’ → “Thou wast on that side”: the expression of position moves after the verb.
- *cotanto, quant’ io scesi* ‘as much as I descended’ → “so long as I descended”: the correlative expression of extent is rendered explicitly as one of duration.

> 110 Quando mi volsi, tu passasti il punto  
> 111 Al qual si traggon d’ ogni parte i pesi:  

when I turned myself, thou then didst pass the point to which all gravities from every part are drawn;

- *Quando mi volsi* ‘When myself I turned’ → “when I turned myself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *tu passasti il punto* ‘thou didst pass the point’ → “thou then didst pass the point”: “then” is supplied.
- *Al qual si traggon d’ ogni parte i pesi* ‘to which are drawn from every part the weights’ → “to which all gravities from every part are drawn”: *pesi* is rendered as “gravities” rather than ‘weights’, and “all” is supplied. The postposed subject moves before the verb, with “from every part” between them; the passive construction with *si* becomes an English passive with “are”.

> 112 E sei or sotto l’ emisperio giunto,  
> 113 Che è opposto a quel, che la gran secca  
> 114 Coverchia, e sotto il cui colmo consunto  
> 115 Fu l’ uom che nacque e visse senza pecca:  

and now thou art arrived beneath the hemisphere opposed to that which canopies the great dry land, and underneath whose summit was consumed the Man, who without sin was born and lived.

- *sei or sotto l’ emisperio giunto* ‘thou art now beneath the hemisphere arrived’ → “now thou art arrived beneath the hemisphere”: “now” moves to the beginning, and the participle moves beside its auxiliary, before the expression of position.
- *Che è opposto a quel* ‘which is opposed to that’ → “opposed to that”: the relative pronoun and finite verb are omitted, leaving a participial phrase.
- *che la gran secca Coverchia* ‘which the great dry [part] covers’ → “which canopies the great dry land”: the Italian object, *la gran secca*, moves after the verb. “Canopies” replaces ‘covers’, and the substantivized adjective *secca* is expressed through the noun phrase “dry land”.
- *consunto Fu* ‘consumed was’ → “was consumed”: the auxiliary moves before the participle.
- *che nacque e visse senza pecca* ‘who was born and lived without sin’ → “who without sin was born and lived”: “without sin” moves before both verbs. The active Italian verb *nacque* is expressed by the English passive form “was born”.

## Lines 116–126 — The Other Side of the Earth

> 116 Tu hai i piedi in su picciola spera,  
> 117 Che l’ altra faccia fa della Giudecca.  

Thou hast thy feet upon a little sphere, which forms the other face of the Judecca.

- *i piedi* ‘the feet’ → “thy feet”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *in su picciola spera* ‘upon little sphere’ → “upon a little sphere”: the indefinite article is supplied.
- *Che l’ altra faccia fa della Giudecca* ‘which the other face makes of the Judecca’ → “which forms the other face of the Judecca”: “forms” renders *fa*; the verb moves before its object, bringing the modifying phrase “of the Judecca” immediately after “face”.

> 118 Qui è da man, quando di là è sera:  

Here it is morn, when it is evening there:

- *Qui è da man* ‘Here it is at morning’ → “Here it is morn”: the prepositional expression *da man* becomes a predicate noun.
- *quando di là è sera* ‘when on that side it is evening’ → “when it is evening there”: *di là* becomes “there” and moves to the end of the clause.

> 119 E questi, che ne fe’ scala col pelo,  
> 120 Fitto è ancora, sì come prima era.  

and this Fiend, who made a ladder for us with his hair, is still fixed as he was before.

- *questi* ‘this one’ → “this Fiend”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a demonstrative with an explanatory noun.
- *ne fe’ scala* ‘for us made ladder’ → “made a ladder for us”: the indirect-object pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase following the object, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *col pelo* ‘with the hair’ → “with his hair”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Fitto è ancora* ‘fixed is still’ → “is still fixed”: the participle moves after the verb and adverb.
- *sì come prima era* ‘just as before he was’ → “as he was before”: *sì* is not separately rendered, and “before” moves after the verb.

> 121 Da questa parte cadde giù dal Cielo:  

On this side fell he down from Heaven;

- *Da questa parte* ‘From this side’ → “On this side”: the preposition is rendered as one of location rather than origin.

> 122 E la terra, che pria di qua si sporse,  
> 123 Per paura di lui fe’ del mar velo,  

and here the land, which erst stood out, through fear of him veiled itself with sea,

- *di qua* ‘on this side’ → “here”: the phrase is shortened and moved from within the relative clause to before “the land”, attaching it to the main clause.
- *si sporse* ‘thrust itself out’ → “stood out”: the pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive expression describing position rather than explicitly expressing movement.
- *fe’ del mar velo* ‘made of the sea a veil’ → “veiled itself with sea”: the noun *velo* becomes the verb “veiled”, absorbing *fe’*; a reflexive object is supplied, “of” becomes “with”, and the article before “sea” is omitted.

> 124 E venne all’ emisperio nostro; e forse  
> 125 Per fuggir lui lasciò qui il luogo voto  
> 126 Quella che appar di qua, e su ricorse.  

and came to our hemisphere: and perhaps, in order to escape from him, that which on this side appears, left here the empty space, and upwards rushed.”

- *all’ emisperio nostro* ‘to the hemisphere our’ → “to our hemisphere”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *Per fuggir lui* ‘To flee him’ → “in order to escape from him”: the purpose construction is expanded to “in order to”, and the transitive *fuggir* becomes “escape” with a prepositional complement.
- *lasciò qui il luogo voto* ‘left here the place empty’ → “left here the empty space”: “space” renders *luogo*, and *voto*, describing the condition in which the place was left, becomes an attributive adjective before the noun.
- *Quella che appar di qua* ‘That which appears on this side’ → “that which on this side appears”: the subject moves from after *lasciò* to before it, and the locative phrase moves before “appears”.
- *ricorse* ‘ran back’ → “rushed”: the translation introduces haste but does not explicitly retain the sense of return.

## Lines 127–139 — Return to the Stars

> 127 Luogo è laggiù da Belzebù rimoto  
> 128 Tanto, quanto la tomba si distende,  
> 129 Che non per vista, ma per suono è noto  

Down there, from Beelzebub as far removed as his tomb extends, is a space, not known by sight but by the sound

- *Luogo è laggiù* ‘a place is down there’ → “Down there,” … “is a space,”: the location comes first and the subject follows the verb; ‘place’ becomes “space”.
- *da Belzebù rimoto Tanto, quanto* ‘from Beelzebub removed as much as’ → “from Beelzebub as far removed as”: the degree expression moves before “removed”, and “far” makes the spatial comparison explicit.
- *la tomba si distende* ‘the tomb extends’ → “his tomb extends”: the definite article becomes a possessive, explicitly connecting the tomb with Beelzebub.
- *Che non per vista, ma per suono è noto* ‘which not by sight, but by sound is known’ → “not known by sight but by the sound”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase; “known” precedes the phrases describing how the space is known, and an article is supplied before “sound”.

> 130 D’ un ruscelletto, che quivi discende  
> 131 Per la buca d’ un sasso, ch’ egli ha roso  
> 132 Col corso ch’ egli avvolge e poco pende.  

of a rivulet descending in it, along the hollow of a rock which it has eaten out with tortuous course and slow declivity.

- *che quivi discende* ‘which descends there’ → “descending in it”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase, and the adverb ‘there’ becomes “in it”.
- *Per la buca d’un sasso* ‘through the hole of a rock’ → “along the hollow of a rock”: “along” replaces ‘through’, and “hollow” renders the hole as a channel.
- *ch’ egli ha roso* ‘which it has gnawed’ → “which it has eaten out”: the verb becomes a phrase explicitly expressing excavation.
- *Col corso ch’ egli avvolge e poco pende* ‘with the course that it winds and slopes little’ → “with tortuous course and slow declivity”: the definite article is omitted, and the relative construction is recast as an adjective modifying “course” followed by a coordinated noun phrase. The winding becomes “tortuous”; the slight downward slope becomes “slow declivity”.

> 133 Lo Duca ed io per quel cammino ascoso  
> 134 Entrammo a ritornar nel chiaro mondo;  

The Guide and I entered by that hidden road, to return into the bright world:

- *Lo Duca* ‘the leader’ → “The Guide”: the chosen noun emphasizes guidance rather than leadership.
- *per quel cammino ascoso Entrammo* ‘by that road hidden we entered’ → “entered by that hidden road,”: the verb moves before the phrase describing the route, and the adjective moves before its noun.

> 135 E senza cura aver d’ alcun riposo  
> 136 Salimmo su, ei primo ed io secondo,  

and, without caring for any rest, we mounted up, he first and I second,

- *senza cura aver d’alcun riposo* ‘without care having for any rest’ → “without caring for any rest”: the inverted noun-and-infinitive construction becomes the single gerund “caring”, with its complement following directly.

> 137 Tanto ch’ io vidi delle cose belle  
> 138 Che porta il Ciel, per un pertugio tondo:  

so far that I distinguished through a round opening the beauteous things which Heaven bears;

- *Tanto ch’ io vidi* ‘so much that I saw’ → “so far that I distinguished”: “far” gives the degree expression an explicitly spatial sense, while “distinguished” makes ‘saw’ more specifically an act of discerning.
- *delle cose belle* ‘some things beautiful’ → “the beauteous things”: the partitive becomes a definite phrase, the adjective precedes the noun, and ‘beautiful’ receives the more literary equivalent “beauteous”.
- *Che porta il Ciel* ‘which bears the Heaven’ → “which Heaven bears”: the subject moves before the verb, and the Italian definite article is omitted.
- *per un pertugio tondo* ‘through an opening round’ → “through a round opening”: the adjective moves before its noun, and the whole phrase moves from after the relative clause to immediately after “distinguished”.

> 139 E quindi uscimmo a riveder le stelle.  

and thence we issued out, again to see the Stars.

- *a riveder* ‘to see again’ → “again to see”: the repetition expressed by the prefix *ri-* becomes a separate adverb, placed before the infinitive rather than after the verb.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Syntax

Carlyle keeps much of the Italian’s vocabulary, imagery, and sequence of thought, but frequently rearranges its syntax. Subjects and objects placed after or before their usual positions in Italian move into more customary English order: *Disse il Maestro mio* becomes “my Master said”, and *che il mondo fora* becomes “which pierces through the world”. Yet the prose also preserves or introduces marked inversions, as in “No plumes had they” and “must we depart”. Its closeness is therefore not consistently word-for-word: it balances direct correspondence with both ordinary and deliberately elevated English phrasing.

Changes of construction recur throughout. Relative clauses become participial phrases, as *un mulin che al vento gira* becomes “a mill, turning with the wind”; *Ch’ avea* is compressed into “with”. Reflexive or pronominal verbs become intransitive verbs, passives, or English idioms: *s’ aggelava* becomes “was frozen”, while *io mi presi Al pel* becomes “I caught hold on the hair”. Impersonal expressions become personal ones, notably *E’ da partir* as “must we depart”. Tense and aspect also shift: *Già era*, ‘Already I was’, becomes “Already I had come”, replacing a state with a completed arrival.

The lexical departures range from contextual clarification to changes of force or register. *Grotta*, ‘cave’, becomes “shed”; *ceffo*, ‘mug’, becomes the more elevated “visage”. “Gushed” strengthens *Gocciava*, ‘dripped’, and “tearing” intensifies *graffiar*, ‘scratching’. Elsewhere the translation specifies an interpretation: *cotali*, ‘such’, becomes “so broad”, and *travagliato*, ‘troubled’, becomes “toil-worn”. Small additions likewise make implications explicit: “in size” identifies the basis of a comparison, “death and life” names the alternatives in *d’ uno e d’ altro*, and “this Fiend” expands *questi*. Conversely, separate locative words disappear when *Là, dove* becomes “where” or *di là, ove* becomes “from where”.

Alongside these departures, many phrases remain closely aligned with the Italian, including “I did not die, and did not remain alive” and “the right seemed between white and yellow”. The opening Latin is retained untranslated, and numerous concrete nouns and comparisons pass into English with little alteration. Two routine grammatical adjustments, generally left unlisted in the comparison, further distinguish the languages: Carlyle supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and omits the Italian definite article before possessives, as *il Maestro mio* becomes “my Master”. These ordinary accommodations underpin a translation that stays close in detail while repeatedly reshaping the means of expression.
