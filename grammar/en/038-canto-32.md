# Inferno 32

## Lines 1–12 — A Plea for Fitting Verse

> 1 S’ io avessi le rime e aspre e chiocce,  
> 2 Come si converrebbe al tristo buco,  
> 3 Sovra il qual pontan tutte l’ altre rocce,  

If I had rhymes both rough and hoarse, as would befit the dismal hole, on which all the other rocky steeps converge and weigh,

- *le rime e aspre e chiocce* ‘the rhymes both rough and hoarse’ → “rhymes both rough and hoarse”: the definite article is omitted.
- *Come si converrebbe al tristo buco* ‘as would be fitting to the sad hole’ → “as would befit the dismal hole”: the impersonal construction becomes the transitive verb “befit”, with the hole as its direct object; *tristo* is rendered as “dismal”.
- *pontan tutte l’ altre rocce* ‘bear down all the other rocks’ → “all the other rocky steeps converge and weigh”: the subject moves before the verb; one verb is expanded into “converge and weigh”, and “rocky steeps” replaces ‘rocks’.

> 4 Io premerei di mio concetto il suco  
> 5 Più pienamente; ma perch’ io non l’ abbo,  
> 6 Non senza tema a dicer mi conduco:  

I should press out the juice of my conception more fully: but since I have them not, not without fear I bring myself to tell thereof;

- *Io premerei* ‘I would press’ → “I should press out”: the conditional is expressed with “should”, and “out” makes the extraction explicit.
- *di mio concetto il suco* ‘of my conception the juice’ → “the juice of my conception”: the modifying phrase follows its noun instead of preceding it.
- *perch’* ‘because’ → “since”: the causal conjunction is expressed by an alternative English conjunction.
- *io non l’ abbo* ‘I not them have’ → “I have them not”: the object pronoun and the negative both move after the verb; the elided Italian pronoun is explicitly rendered as plural “them”.
- *a dicer mi conduco* ‘to speak myself I lead’ → “I bring myself to tell thereof”: the infinitive moves after the main verb and reflexive pronoun; ‘lead’ becomes “bring”, and “thereof” supplies an object of reference absent from the Italian.

> 7 Chè non è impresa da pigliare a gabbo,  
> 8 Descriver fondo a tutto l’ universo,  
> 9 Nè da lingua che chiami mamma e babbo.  

for to describe the bottom of all the Universe is not an enterprise for being taken up in sport, nor for a tongue that cries mamma and papa.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *non è impresa* ‘not is an enterprise’ → “is not an enterprise”: the negative follows the verb instead of preceding it.
- *da pigliare a gabbo* ‘to take as a jest’ → “for being taken up in sport”: the active infinitive becomes a passive gerund construction; “taken up” expresses undertaking, while “in sport” replaces ‘as a jest’.
- *Descriver fondo a tutto l’ universo* ‘to describe bottom to all the universe’ → “to describe the bottom of all the Universe”: the infinitive phrase, which is the subject, moves before the main verb; “the” is supplied before “bottom”, and the Italian relation expressed by *a* becomes “of”.
- *Nè da lingua* ‘nor for tongue’ → “nor for a tongue”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *che chiami* ‘that should call’ → “that cries”: the subjunctive characterizing a possible tongue becomes an English indicative; ‘call’ is rendered as “cries”.

> 10 Ma quelle Donne aiutino il mio verso,  
> 11 Ch’ aiutaro Anfione a chiuder Tebe,  
> 12 Sì che dal fatto il dir non sia diverso.  

But may those Ladies help my verse, who helped Amphion with walls to close in Thebes; so that my words may not be diverse from the fact.

- *Ma quelle Donne aiutino* ‘but those Ladies help’ → “But may those Ladies help”: the Italian subjunctive expressing a wish becomes a construction with “may” placed before the subject.
- *a chiuder Tebe* ‘to enclose Thebes’ → “with walls to close in Thebes”: “with walls” is supplied, and the single verb is expressed by “close in”.
- *dal fatto il dir non sia diverso* ‘from the fact the saying not be different’ → “my words may not be diverse from the fact”: the initial prepositional phrase moves to the end; the substantivized infinitive *il dir* becomes the plural noun “words”, with “my” supplied; and the subjunctive is expressed through “may”, with the negative following that auxiliary.

## Lines 13–30 — The Frozen Lake Beneath the Giant

> 13 Oh sovra tutte mal creata plebe,  
> 14 Che stai nel loco, onde parlare è duro!  
> 15 Me’ foste state qui pecore o zebe.  

O ye, beyond all others, miscreated rabble, who are in the place, to speak of which is hard, better had ye here on earth been sheep or goats!

- *Oh* ‘O’ → “O ye”: a pronoun is added to the exclamation.
- *sovra tutte* ‘above all’ → “beyond all others”: the comparison is expressed with “beyond,” and “others” makes explicit the substantive use of *tutte*.
- *mal creata* ‘badly created’ → “miscreated”: the adverb and participle become a single word.
- *Che stai* ‘who stayest’ → “who are”: the verb of remaining or being situated becomes “are”; the Italian singular, agreeing with the collective *plebe*, becomes plural.
- *onde parlare è duro* ‘of which to speak is hard’ → “to speak of which is hard”: the relative phrase moves after the infinitive.
- *Me’ foste state qui* ‘better you had been here’ → “better had ye here on earth been”: subject and auxiliary are inverted, “here” moves before the participle, and “on earth” is supplied, interpreting *qui* as referring to earthly life.
- *pecore o zebe* ‘sheep or she-goats’ → “sheep or goats”: the specific female designation in *zebe* is not retained.

> 16 Come noi fummo giù nel pozzo scuro  
> 17 Sotto i piè del Gigante, assai più bassi,  
> 18 Ed io mirava ancora all’ alto muro,  

When we were down in the dark pit, under the Giant’s feet, much lower, and I still was gazing at the high wall,

- *Come* ‘as’ → “When”: the temporal sense is made explicit.
- *pozzo scuro* ‘pit dark’ → “dark pit”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *Sotto i piè del Gigante* ‘under the feet of the Giant’ → “under the Giant’s feet”: the phrase with *del* becomes an English possessive construction.
- *io mirava ancora* ‘I was gazing still’ → “I still was gazing”: the adverb moves before the verb phrase; the Italian imperfect is expressed by the English past progressive.

> 19 Dicere udimmi: Guarda come passi;  
> 20 Fa sì, che tu non calchi con le piante  
> 21 Le teste de’ fratei miseri lassi.  

I heard a voice say to me: “Look how thou passest: take care that with thy soles thou tread not on the heads of the weary wretched brothers.”

- *Dicere udimmi* ‘say I heard to me’ → “I heard a voice say to me”: the infinitive moves after the governing verb, “a voice” is supplied as its subject, and the attached dative *mi* becomes the separate phrase “to me.”
- *Fa sì* ‘do so’ → “take care”: the injunction to act in a certain way becomes an injunction to exercise care.
- *tu non calchi* ‘thou tread not’ → “thou tread not on”: “on” is added because English here uses a prepositional construction where *calcare* takes a direct object.
- *con le piante* ‘with the soles’ → “with thy soles”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves before the subject and verb.
- *de’ fratei miseri lassi* ‘of the brothers wretched weary’ → “of the weary wretched brothers”: both adjectives move before the noun, and their order is reversed.

> 22 Per ch’ io mi volsi, e vidimi davante  
> 23 E sotto i piedi un lago, che per gielo  
> 24 Avea di vetro, e non d’ acqua sembiante.  

Whereat I turned myself, and saw before me and beneath my feet a lake, which through frost had the semblance of glass and not of water.

- *Per ch’* ‘because of which’ → “Whereat”: the causal connection becomes a phrase meaning ‘at which.’
- *i piedi* ‘the feet’ → “my feet”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Avea di vetro, e non d’ acqua sembiante* ‘had of glass, and not of water, semblance’ → “had the semblance of glass and not of water”: “semblance” moves before its dependent phrases, and the definite article is supplied.

> 25 Non fece al corso suo sì grosso velo  
> 26 Di verno la Danoia in Austericch,  
> 27 Nè il Tanai là sotto il freddo cielo,  

Never did the Danube of Austria make so thick a veil for his course in winter, nor the Don afar beneath the frigid sky,

- *Non fece* ‘did not make’ → “Never did” … “make”: simple negation becomes the temporally comprehensive “Never,” which prompts inversion of auxiliary and subject; the subject also moves before the main verb.
- *al corso suo* ‘for the course his’ → “for his course”: the possessive moves before the noun, and the whole phrase follows “veil” rather than preceding it.
- *sì grosso velo* ‘so thick veil’ → “so thick a veil”: the indefinite article is supplied.
- *la Danoia in Austericch* ‘the Danube in Austria’ → “the Danube of Austria”: the locative preposition becomes “of,” expressing association rather than location.
- *là* ‘there’ → “afar”: a designation of place becomes an explicit indication of distance.
- *freddo* ‘cold’ → “frigid”: the English adjective gives the cold a stronger expression.

> 28 Com’ era quivi: chè se Tabernicch  
> 29 Vi fosse su caduto, o Pietrapana,  
> 30 Non avria pur dall’ orlo fatto cricch.  

as there was here; for if Tabernicch had fallen on it, or Pietrapana, it would not even at the edge have given a creak.

- *Com’ era quivi* ‘as it was there’ → “as there was here”: the elliptical comparison becomes an existential construction with “there,” while *quivi* is rendered from the speaker’s immediate standpoint as “here.”
- *Vi fosse su caduto* ‘upon it had fallen’ → “had fallen on it”: the locative elements *vi* and *su* are combined into “on it” and moved after the participle.
- *Non avria pur dall’ orlo fatto cricch* ‘it would not have even from the edge made cricch’ → “it would not even at the edge have given a creak”: “even” and the phrase about the edge precede “have”; ‘from’ becomes “at”; and the construction with ‘make’ and an imitated cracking sound becomes “given a creak,” using a noun with an indefinite article.

## Lines 31–39 — Frozen Shades and Chattering Teeth

> 31 E come a gracidar si sta la rana  
> 32 Col muso fuor dell’ acqua, quando sogna  
> 33 Di spigolar sovente la villana;  

And as the frog to croak, sits with his muzzle out of the water, when the villager oft dreams that she is gleaning;

- *a gracidar si sta la rana* ‘to croak stays the frog’ → “the frog to croak, sits”: the subject moves before the infinitive and main verb; *si sta* becomes “sits,” with the pronominal *si* not separately represented.
- *Col muso* ‘with the muzzle’ → “with his muzzle”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *quando sogna Di spigolar sovente la villana* ‘when dreams of gleaning often the peasant woman’ → “when the villager oft dreams that she is gleaning”: the subject moves before the verb, and “oft” moves before “dreams.” The infinitival complement becomes a finite clause in the present progressive. *Villana* becomes the less specific “villager,” though its feminine reference is retained in “she.”

> 34 Livide insin là, dove appar vergogna,  
> 35 Eran l’ ombre dolenti nella ghiaccia,  
> 36 Mettendo i denti in nota di cicogna.  

so, livid, up to where the hue of shame appears, the doleful shades were in the ice, sounding with their teeth like storks.

- *Livide insin là, dove appar vergogna* ‘livid up to there, where appears shame’ → “so, livid, up to where the hue of shame appears”: “so” is supplied to complete the preceding comparison; *là, dove* is compressed into “where.” The subject moves before “appears,” and “the hue of” is supplied, interpreting shame through its visible colour.
- *Eran l’ombre dolenti* ‘were the shades doleful’ → “the doleful shades were”: the subject moves before the verb, and the adjective before its noun.
- *Mettendo i denti in nota di cicogna* ‘putting the teeth into the note of a stork’ → “sounding with their teeth like storks”: putting the teeth into a particular sound becomes sounding by means of them, so the direct object becomes an instrumental phrase. The definite article becomes “their”; the reference to a stork’s note becomes a comparison with storks, changing singular to plural.

> 37 Ognuna in giù tenea volta la faccia:  

Each held his face turned downwards:

- *in giù tenea volta la faccia* ‘downwards held turned the face’ → “held his face turned downwards”: the directional phrase moves to the end, the object moves before “turned,” and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 38 Da bocca il freddo, e dagli occhi il cor tristo  
> 39 Tra lor testimonianza si procaccia.  

by the mouth their cold, and by the eyes the sorrow of their hearts is testified amongst them.

- *Da bocca* ‘from mouth’ → “by the mouth”: the preposition of source becomes one of means, and an article is supplied.
- *il freddo* ‘the cold’ → “their cold”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *dagli occhi* ‘from the eyes’ → “by the eyes”: the preposition of source becomes one of means.
- *il cor tristo* ‘the sad heart’ → “the sorrow of their hearts”: the adjective becomes the abstract noun “sorrow,” while ‘heart’ becomes its possessive complement, changes to the plural, and receives “their.”
- *Tra lor testimonianza si procaccia* ‘among them it procures testimony for itself’ → “is testified amongst them”: the reflexive construction with ‘testimony’ as its object becomes a passive verb; the sense of procuring is not separately expressed. The phrase meaning ‘among them’ moves to the end.

## Lines 40–54 — Frozen Tears and Butting Heads

> 40 Quand’ io ebbi d’ intorno alquanto visto,  
> 41 Volsimi a’ piedi, e vidi due sì stretti,  
> 42 Che il pel del capo aveano insieme misto.  

When I had looked round awhile, I turned towards my feet; and saw two so pressed against each other, that they had the hair of their heads intermixed.

- *ebbi d’ intorno alquanto visto* ‘had around somewhat seen’ → “had looked round awhile”: seeing becomes looking; the intervening adverbials follow the participle, and *alquanto* is interpreted as duration rather than extent.
- *Volsimi a’ piedi* ‘I turned myself to the feet’ → “I turned towards my feet”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive; “towards” replaces ‘to’, and “my” replaces the definite article.
- *due sì stretti* ‘two so close’ → “two so pressed against each other”: closeness is expressed as pressure, with the reciprocal relation made explicit.
- *Che il pel del capo aveano insieme misto* ‘that the hair of the head they had together mixed’ → “that they had the hair of their heads intermixed”: the subject and finite verb precede the object rather than follow it; singular ‘the head’ becomes plural “their heads”; and ‘together mixed’ is condensed into “intermixed”.

> 43 Ditemi voi, che sì stringete i petti,  
> 44 Diss’ io, chi siete? E quei piegaro li colli;  

“Tell me, ye who thus together press your bosoms,” said I, “who are ye?” And they bended their necks;

- *Ditemi* ‘Say to me’ → “Tell me”: *dire* is rendered with “Tell”, which takes the person addressed without ‘to’.
- *sì stringete i petti* ‘thus press the bosoms’ → “thus together press your bosoms”: “together” is supplied, and the definite article becomes the possessive “your”.
- *quei* ‘those’ → “they”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *li colli* ‘the necks’ → “their necks”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 45 E poi ch’ ebber li visi a me eretti,  
> 46 Gli occhi lor, ch’ eran pria pur dentro molli,  
> 47 Gocciar su per le labbra, e il gielo strinse  
> 48 Le lagrime tra essi, e riserrolli.  

and when they had raised their faces towards me, their eyes, which only inwardly were moist before, gushed at the lids, and the frost bound fast the tears between them, and closed them up again:

- *poi ch’* ‘after’ → “when”: the explicit relation of succession becomes a more general temporal connection.
- *ebber li visi a me eretti* ‘they had the faces to me raised’ → “they had raised their faces towards me”: the participle moves immediately after the auxiliary, and the directional phrase follows the object; the definite article becomes “their”, and ‘to’ becomes “towards”.
- *Gli occhi lor* ‘the eyes their’ → “their eyes”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *ch’ eran pria pur dentro molli* ‘which were before only within moist’ → “which only inwardly were moist before”: “only inwardly” moves before the verb, while “before” moves to the end of the clause.
- *Gocciar* ‘dripped’ → “gushed”: the translation gives the flow greater force.
- *su per le labbra* ‘over the lips’ → “at the lids”: the translation takes *labbra* as the rims of the eyes rather than the lips of the mouth; the spatial phrase becomes “at”.
- *strinse* ‘clasped’ → “bound fast”: the verb is expanded into a phrase emphasizing firm restraint.
- *riserrolli* ‘reclosed them’ → “closed them up again”: the repeated action expressed by the prefix is rendered separately as “again”, with “up” also supplied.

> 49 Legno con legno spranga mai non cinse  
> 50 Forte così: ond’ ei, come duo becchi,  
> 51 Cozzaro insieme, tant’ ira li vinse.  

wood with wood no cramp did ever gird so strongly. Wherefore they, like two he-goats, butted one another; such rage came over them.

- *spranga mai non cinse* ‘a clamp never girded’ → “no cramp did ever gird”: the negative moves from the verb phrase to the subject, while ‘never’ becomes “ever” under that negation; the simple past becomes the construction “did” plus infinitive.
- *Forte così* ‘strongly so’ → “so strongly”: the degree word moves before the adverb.
- *ond’* ‘whence’ → “Wherefore”: the connective is rendered explicitly as expressing consequence.
- *Cozzaro insieme* ‘butted together’ → “butted one another”: the adverb becomes a reciprocal object.
- *tant’ ira li vinse* ‘so much anger conquered them’ → “such rage came over them”: quantitative ‘so much’ becomes “such”; conquest becomes the idiom “came over”, and the direct-object pronoun becomes the complement of “over”.

> 52 Ed un, ch’ avea perduti ambo gli orecchi  
> 53 Per la freddura, pur col viso in giue  
> 54 Disse: Perchè cotanto in noi ti specchi?  

And one, who had lost both ears by the cold, with his face still downwards said: “Why art thou looking so much at us?

- *ambo gli orecchi* ‘both the ears’ → “both ears”: the definite article is omitted after “both”.
- *Per la freddura* ‘because of the cold’ → “by the cold”: the causal phrase is rendered with “by”.
- *pur col viso in giue* ‘still with the face downwards’ → “with his face still downwards”: “still” moves after the noun, and the definite article becomes “his”.
- *Perchè cotanto in noi ti specchi* ‘Why so much in us do you mirror yourself?’ → “Why art thou looking so much at us?”: the reflexive mirror-image expression becomes non-reflexive looking, with ‘in us’ changed to “at us”; the simple present becomes progressive, and both the degree phrase and the prepositional complement move after the verb.

## Lines 55–65 — Shades Most Worthy of Ice

> 55 Se vuoi saper chi son cotesti due,  

If thou desirest to know who are these two,

- *vuoi* ‘thou wantest’ → “thou desirest”: wanting is expressed as desiring.
- *cotesti* ‘those near thee’ → “these”: the demonstrative loses its particular reference to proximity to the person addressed.

> 56 La valle, onde Bisenzio si dichina,  
> 57 Del padre loro Alberto e di lor fue.  

the valley whence the Bisenzio descends was theirs and their father Albert’s.

- *Bisenzio* ‘Bisenzio’ → “the Bisenzio”: English supplies the definite article before the river’s name.
- *si dichina* ‘inclines itself downward’ → “descends”: the reflexive construction becomes a non-reflexive verb of downward movement.
- *Del padre loro Alberto e di lor fue* ‘of their father Albert and of them was’ → “was theirs and their father Albert’s”: the verb moves before the possessive complements, and their order is reversed. The constructions with *di* become English possessives, and the possessive following *padre* moves before “father”.

> 58 D’ un corpo usciro: e tutta la Caina  
> 59 Potrai cercare, e non troverai ombra  
> 60 Degna più d’ esser fitta in gelatina:  

They issued from one body; and thou mayest search the whole Caïna, and shalt not find a shade more worthy to be fixed in gelatine:

- *D’ un corpo usciro* ‘from one body they issued’ → “They issued from one body”: the phrase expressing origin moves from before the verb to after it.
- *tutta la Caina Potrai cercare* ‘all Caïna thou wilt be able to search’ → “thou mayest search the whole Caïna”: the object moves after the verb; “the whole” renders *tutta la*. Future ability becomes the present modal “mayest”.
- *ombra* ‘shade’ → “a shade”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *Degna più* ‘worthy more’ → “more worthy”: the comparative modifier moves before the adjective.
- *d’ esser* ‘of being’ → “to be”: the Italian prepositional complement becomes an English infinitive complement.
- *in gelatina* ‘in jelly’ → “in gelatine”: Carlyle uses the cognate “gelatine” rather than the ordinary English ‘jelly’.

> 61 Non quelli a cui fu rotto il petto e l’ ombra  
> 62 Con esso un colpo per la man d’ Artù:  

not him, whose breast and shadow at one blow were pierced by Arthur’s hand;

- *quelli* ‘he’ → “him”: the Italian singular demonstrative is rendered by an English personal pronoun in the objective case.
- *a cui fu rotto il petto e l’ ombra* ‘to whom was broken the breast and the shadow’ → “whose breast and shadow” … “were pierced”: the relative construction with *a cui* becomes possessive “whose”, replacing the articles before the nouns. The compound subject moves before the passive verb; singular *fu* becomes plural “were”; and “pierced” interprets *rotto* more specifically than ‘broken’.
- *Con esso un colpo* ‘with one and the same blow’ → “at one blow”: “at” replaces ‘with’, the emphasis of *esso* is not separately expressed, and the phrase moves before the passive verb.
- *per la man d’ Artù* ‘by the hand of Arthur’ → “by Arthur’s hand”: the possessive construction with *di* becomes a possessive placed before “hand”.

> 63 Non Focaccia; non questi, che m’ ingombra  
> 64 Col capo sì, ch’ io non veggio oltre più,  
> 65 E fu nomato Sassol Mascheroni:  

not Focaccia; not this one, who so obstructs me with his head that I see no farther, and who was named Sassol Mascheroni:

- *che m’ ingombra Col capo sì* ‘who me obstructs with the head so’ → “who so obstructs me with his head”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, while *sì* moves before it. The definite article before *capo* becomes the possessive “his”.
- *ch’ io non veggio oltre più* ‘that I do not see further beyond’ → “that I see no farther”: the verbal negation becomes “no” modifying “farther”, and *oltre più* is compressed into a single comparative.
- *E fu nomato* ‘and was named’ → “and who was named”: English repeats the relative pronoun “who”, which the Italian leaves understood.

## Lines 66–72 — Camiccion and the Frozen Faces

> 66 Se Tosco sei, ben sa’ omai chi fu.  

if thou beest a Tuscan, well knowest thou now who he was.

- *Se Tosco sei* ‘if Tuscan thou art’ → “if thou beest a Tuscan”: the predicate moves after the verb; the adjective becomes a noun with an indefinite article, and the Italian indicative becomes the English subjunctive “beest”.

> 67 E perchè non mi metti in più sermoni,  
> 68 Sappi ch’ io fu’ il Camicion de’ Pazzi,  
> 69 Ed aspetto Carlin che mi scagioni.  

And that thou mayest not put me to further speech, know that I was Camiccion de’ Pazzi, and am waiting for Carlino to excuse me.”

- *perchè non mi metti in più sermoni* ‘so that thou dost not put me into more speeches’ → “that thou mayest not put me to further speech”: the purpose clause is expressed with “mayest” rather than the Italian present indicative; the object pronoun moves after the verb, and “to further speech” replaces ‘into more speeches’, making the plural noun singular.
- *il Camicion de’ Pazzi* ‘the Camicion de’ Pazzi’ → “Camiccion de’ Pazzi”: the definite article before the personal name is omitted.
- *Ed aspetto Carlin* ‘and I await Carlin’ → “and am waiting for Carlino”: the simple present becomes progressive, and the direct object of ‘await’ becomes the object of “for”.
- *che mi scagioni* ‘who may exculpate me’ → “to excuse me”: the relative clause with a subjunctive verb becomes an infinitive clause; the object pronoun moves after the verb, and ‘exculpate’ is rendered by “excuse”.

> 70 Poscia vid’ io mille visi cagnazzi  
> 71 Fatti per freddo: onde mi vien ribrezzo,  
> 72 E verrà sempre, de’ gelati guazzi.  

Afterwards I saw a thousand visages, made doggish by the cold; whence shuddering comes over me, and always will come, when I think of the frozen fords.

- *Poscia vid’ io* ‘Afterwards saw I’ → “Afterwards I saw”: the explicit subject moves before the verb.
- *visi cagnazzi Fatti per freddo* ‘faces doggish made by cold’ → “visages, made doggish by the cold”: the adjective moves after “made”, becoming its resultative complement rather than directly qualifying the noun; an article is supplied before “cold”.
- *onde mi vien ribrezzo* ‘whence to me comes shuddering’ → “whence shuddering comes over me”: the subject moves before the verb, and the dative pronoun becomes the prepositional phrase “over me”.
- *E verrà sempre* ‘and will come always’ → “and always will come”: the adverb moves before the verb phrase.
- *de’ gelati guazzi* ‘of the frozen pools’ → “when I think of the frozen fords”: the prepositional phrase becomes part of a temporal clause through the supplied “when I think”; *guazzi* is interpreted as “fords” rather than pools.

## Lines 73–87 — A Blow and a Question

> 73 E mentre ch’ andavamo in vêr lo mezzo,  
> 74 Al quale ogni gravezza si rauna,  
> 75 Ed io tremava nell’ eterno rezzo,  

And as we were going towards the middle at which all weight unites, and I was shivering in the eternal shade,

- *mentre ch’* ‘while’ → “as”: the temporal conjunction is rendered by an alternative English conjunction.
- *Al quale* ‘to which’ → “at which”: movement towards a point becomes location at that point.
- *ogni gravezza* ‘every weight’ → “all weight”: the distributive expression becomes a collective one.
- *si rauna* ‘gathers itself’ → “unites”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an English intransitive verb.
- *tremava* ‘I trembled’ → “I was shivering”: trembling is specified as shivering, and the imperfect is expressed by the past progressive.
- *rezzo* ‘chill shade’ → “shade”: the suggestion of cold carried by the Italian noun is left implicit.

> 76 Se voler fu, o destino, o fortuna,  
> 77 Non so; ma passeggiando tra le teste,  
> 78 Forte percossi il piè nel viso ad una.  

whether it was will, or destiny or chance, I know not; but, walking amid the heads, I hit my foot violently against the face of one.

- *Se voler fu* ‘whether will it was’ → “whether it was will”: the predicate noun moves after the verb.
- *fortuna* ‘fortune’ → “chance”: the noun is interpreted specifically as chance.
- *Non so* ‘not I know’ → “I know not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *Forte percossi il piè* ‘violently I struck the foot’ → “I hit my foot violently”: the adverb moves from before the verb to after its object, and the definite article identifying a body part becomes a possessive.
- *nel viso ad una* ‘in the face to one’ → “against the face of one”: “against” expresses the impact, and the Italian construction identifying the affected head becomes an English possessive construction with “of”.

> 79 Piangendo mi sgridò: Perchè mi peste?  

Weeping it cried out to me: “Why tramplest thou on me?

- *mi sgridò* ‘me it scolded’ → “it cried out to me”: scolding becomes crying out; the direct object becomes a prepositional recipient and follows the verb.
- *mi peste* ‘me thou tramplest’ → “tramplest thou on me”: the direct object becomes a prepositional complement after the verb, and English question order places the subject after the verb.

> 80 Se tu non vieni a crescer la vendetta  
> 81 Di Mont’ Aperti, perchè mi moleste?  

If thou comest not to increase the vengeance for Montaperti, why dost thou molest me?”

- *tu non vieni* ‘thou not comest’ → “thou comest not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *Di Mont’ Aperti* ‘of Montaperti’ → “for Montaperti”: the relation expressed by *di* is interpreted as the occasion for vengeance.
- *perchè mi moleste* ‘why me thou molestest’ → “why dost thou molest me”: English supplies the interrogative auxiliary “dost” and places the object after the main verb.

> 82 Ed io: Maestro mio, or qui m’ aspetta,  
> 83 Sì ch’ io esca d’ un dubbio per costui:  
> 84 Poi mi farai, quantunque vorrai, fretta.  

And I: “My Master! now wait me here, that I may rid me of a doubt respecting him: then shalt thou, however much thou pleasest, make me haste.”

- *Maestro mio* ‘Master mine’ → “My Master”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *qui m’ aspetta* ‘here me await’ → “wait me here”: the object follows the imperative, and the place adverb moves to the end.
- *Sì ch’* ‘so that’ → “that”: the explicit *sì* has no separate English equivalent.
- *io esca d’ un dubbio* ‘I may come out of a doubt’ → “I may rid me of a doubt”: the intransitive expression of emerging becomes a transitive, reflexive expression of freeing oneself; “may” expresses the Italian subjunctive.
- *per costui* ‘through this man’ → “respecting him”: *per* is interpreted as ‘concerning’ rather than ‘through’, and the demonstrative referring to a person becomes a personal pronoun.
- *mi farai … fretta* ‘to me thou wilt make haste’ → “shalt thou” … “make me haste”: the Italian idiom for hurrying someone becomes a causative construction. The noun *fretta* becomes the verb “haste”, the indirect object becomes the object of “make”, and the subject follows the future auxiliary.
- *quantunque vorrai* ‘however much thou wilt wish’ → “however much thou pleasest”: the future becomes present, and wishing is expressed as being pleased to do something.

> 85 Lo Duca stette; ed io dissi a colui,  
> 86 Che bestemmiava duramente ancora:  
> 87 Qual sei tu, che così rampogni altrui?  

The Master stood. And to that shade, which still kept bitterly reviling, I said: “What art thou, who thus reproachest others?”

- *Lo Duca* ‘the Leader’ → “The Master”: the title of leadership becomes one of authority or instruction.
- *ed io dissi a colui* ‘and I said to that one’ → “And to that shade,” … “I said”: “shade” supplies a noun absent from the Italian demonstrative pronoun. The indirect object, together with its relative clause, moves before the subject and verb.
- *bestemmiava duramente ancora* ‘was blaspheming harshly still’ → “still kept bitterly reviling”: blaspheming or cursing is interpreted as reviling; “kept” makes the continuing action explicit; “bitterly” replaces ‘harshly’; and “still” moves before the verbal expression.

## Lines 88–102 — A Name Refused Under Threat

> 88 Or tu chi sei, che vai per l’ Antenora  
> 89 Percotendo, rispose, altrui le gote,  
> 90 Sì che, se vivo fossi, troppo fora?  

“Nay, who art thou,” he answered, “that through the Antenora goest, smiting the cheeks of others; so that, if thou wert alive, it were too much?”

- *Or* ‘now’ → “Nay”: an introductory particle becomes an expression of objection.
- *tu chi sei … rispose* ‘thou who art … he answered’ → “who art thou,” he answered”: the subject moves after the verb, following English interrogative order; the reporting clause moves earlier, before the relative clause rather than within it.
- *che vai per l’Antenora* ‘that goest through the Antenora’ → “that through the Antenora goest”: the prepositional phrase moves before the verb.
- *Percotendo … altrui le gote* ‘smiting others the cheeks’ → “smiting the cheeks of others”: the possessive relationship expressed by *altrui* becomes an “of” phrase following the noun.
- *se vivo fossi* ‘if alive I were’ → “if thou wert alive”: Carlyle takes the grammatically ambiguous *fossi* as second-person rather than the usual first-person interpretation; the adjective also moves after the verb.
- *troppo fora* ‘too much it would be’ → “it were too much”: the conditional *fora* becomes hypothetical “were”, and the predicate follows the verb.

> 91 Vivo son io; e caro esser ti puote,  
> 92 Fu mia risposta, se domandi fama,  
> 93 Ch’ io metta il nome tuo tra l’ altre note.  

“I am alive,” was my reply; “and if thou seekest fame, it may be precious to thee, that I put thy name among the other notes.”

- *Vivo son io; … Fu mia risposta* ‘alive am I; … was my reply’ → “I am alive,” was my reply”: the predicate-first order becomes ordinary English subject–verb–predicate order; the reporting clause moves forward to follow the declaration of being alive.
- *caro esser ti puote* ‘dear be to thee it can’ → “it may be precious to thee”: the modal precedes the infinitive, ‘dear’ becomes “precious”, and ‘can’ becomes “may”; the whole clause moves after the condition.
- *se domandi fama* ‘if thou askest for fame’ → “if thou seekest fame”: asking for something becomes seeking it.
- *il nome tuo* ‘the name thy’ → “thy name”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 94 Ed egli a me: Del contrario ho io brama:  

And he to me: “The contrary is what I long for.

- *Del contrario ho io brama* ‘of the contrary have I longing’ → “The contrary is what I long for”: the construction with *avere brama*, ‘have longing’, becomes the verb “long for”; its object becomes the subject of an identifying construction with “is what”.

> 95 Levati quinci, e non mi dar più lagna;  
> 96 Chè mal sai lusingar per questa lama.  

Take thyself away! and pester me no more; for thou ill knowest how to flatter on this icy slope.”

- *Levati quinci* ‘remove thyself from here’ → “Take thyself away!”: the command is recast idiomatically, and the explicit reference to ‘here’ becomes the less specific “away”.
- *non mi dar più lagna* ‘give me no more annoyance’ → “pester me no more”: the verb–noun combination becomes the single verb “pester”, and the indirect object becomes its direct object.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating explanatory conjunction.
- *sai lusingar* ‘knowest to flatter’ → “knowest how to flatter”: “how” makes explicit the practical knowledge expressed by *sapere* with an infinitive.
- *per questa lama* ‘through this hollow’ → “on this icy slope”: ‘through’ becomes the locative “on”; *lama*, a hollow or low-lying stretch of ground, is interpreted as “slope”, and “icy” is supplied.

> 97 Allor lo presi per la cuticagna,  
> 98 E dissi: E’ converrà che tu ti nomi,  
> 99 O che capel qui su non ti rimagna.  

Then I seized him by the afterscalp, and said: “It will be necessary that thou name thyself, or that not a hair remain upon thee here!”

- *lo presi* ‘him I took’ → “I seized him”: ‘took’ becomes the more forceful “seized”, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *per la cuticagna* ‘by the nape’ → “by the afterscalp”: “afterscalp” specifies the back of the scalp rather than simply the nape.
- *capel qui su non ti rimagna* ‘hair here above not remain to thee’ → “not a hair remain upon thee here”: verbal negation becomes negation of the indefinite subject, “not a hair”; *qui su* and the possessive dative *ti* are recast as “upon thee here”, without a separate equivalent for ‘above’.

> 100 Ond’ egli a me: Perchè tu mi dischiomi,  
> 101 Nè ti dirò ch’ io sia, nè mostrerolti,  
> 102 Se mille fiate in sul capo mi tomi.  

Whence he to me: “Even if thou unhair me, I will not tell thee who I am; nor shew it thee, though thou fall foul upon my head a thousand times.”

- *Perchè tu mi dischiomi* ‘because thou unhair me’ → “Even if thou unhair me”: *perchè* is interpreted concessively rather than causally; the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Nè ti dirò* ‘nor to thee will I tell’ → “I will not tell thee”: the first *nè* becomes “not” rather than “nor”, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *ch’io sia* ‘who I be’ → “who I am”: the Italian subjunctive in the indirect question becomes an English indicative.
- *nè mostrerolti* ‘nor will I show it to thee’ → “nor shew it thee”: the independently inflected future becomes an infinitive sharing the earlier subject and auxiliary; the recipient is expressed without “to”.
- *Se mille fiate in sul capo mi tomi* ‘if a thousand times upon the head thou tumble to me’ → “though thou fall foul upon my head a thousand times”: “if” becomes explicitly concessive “though”; tumbling or falling becomes the aggressive idiom “fall foul”; the dative *mi* becomes possessive “my”; and the frequency phrase moves to the end, while the verb moves before the phrase naming where the action falls.

## Lines 103–114 — Bocca Exposed and Defiant

> 103 Io avea già i capelli in mano avvolti,  
> 104 E tratto glien avea più d’ una ciocca,  
> 105 Latrando lui con gli occhi in giù raccolti;  

I already had his hair coiled on my hand, and had plucked off more than one tuft of it, he barking and keeping down his eyes,

- *Io avea già* ‘I had already’ → “I already had”: the adverb precedes rather than follows the verb.
- *i capelli* ‘the hairs’ → “his hair”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the Italian plural becomes an English mass noun.
- *in mano avvolti* ‘in hand wrapped’ → “coiled on my hand”: wrapping is rendered as coiling; the participle precedes the prepositional phrase; and the translation changes the preposition and supplies a possessive.
- *tratto glien avea* ‘pulled from him thereof I had’ → “had plucked off” and “of it”: the auxiliary precedes the participle, and pulling becomes plucking off. The partitive pronoun is rendered after the quantity it qualifies; the personal pronoun meaning ‘from him’ is not separately expressed.
- *Latrando lui* ‘barking he’ → “he barking”: the explicit subject precedes the participle.
- *con gli occhi in giù raccolti* ‘with the eyes downward gathered’ → “and keeping down his eyes”: a prepositional phrase describing the eyes becomes a coordinated participial phrase describing his action. Gathering downward becomes keeping down, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 106 Quando un altro gridò: Che hai tu, Bocca?  

when another cried: “What ails thee, Bocca?

- *Che hai tu* ‘what hast thou’ → “What ails thee”: the idiomatic question with ‘have’ becomes a question with “ails”; the Italian subject becomes the English object.

> 107 Non ti basta sonar con le mascelle,  
> 108 Se tu non latri? qual Diavol ti tocca?  

Is it not enough for thee to chatter with thy jaws, but thou must bark too? What Devil is upon thee?”

- *Non ti basta* ‘does it not suffice thee’ → “Is it not enough for thee”: the verb meaning ‘suffice’ becomes a copular construction with “enough,” and the pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *sonar con le mascelle* ‘to sound with the jaws’ → “to chatter with thy jaws”: the general verb for making a sound becomes the more specific “chatter,” and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Se tu non latri* ‘if thou dost not bark’ → “but thou must bark too”: a negative conditional clause becomes an affirmative coordinated clause, with necessity and addition explicitly supplied through “must” and “too.”
- *qual Diavol ti tocca* ‘which Devil touches thee’ → “What Devil is upon thee”: “What” replaces ‘which,’ while touching becomes being upon someone; the direct object becomes the complement of a preposition.

> 109 Omai, diss’ io, non vo’ che tu favelle,  
> 110 Malvagio traditor; ch’ alla tua onta  
> 111 Io porterò di te vere novelle.  

“Now,” said I, “accursed traitor! I do not want thee to speak; for to thy shame I will bear true tidings of thee.”

- *non vo’ che tu favelle* ‘I do not wish that thou speak’ → “I do not want thee to speak”: the finite subordinate clause with a subjunctive becomes an object-and-infinitive construction.
- *Malvagio traditor* ‘wicked traitor’ → “accursed traitor”: moral wickedness becomes a curse or condemnation, and the address is moved before the preceding clause.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *di te vere novelle* ‘of thee true tidings’ → “true tidings of thee”: the prepositional phrase follows rather than precedes the noun phrase.

> 112 Va via, rispose, e ciò che tu vuoi, conta;  

“Go away!” he answered; “and tell what pleases thee.

- *e ciò che tu vuoi, conta* ‘and that which thou wantest, tell’ → “and tell what pleases thee”: the imperative precedes its object, and ‘that which’ is condensed into “what.” Wanting something becomes being pleased by it, turning the Italian subject into the English object.

> 113 Ma non tacer, se tu di qua entro eschi,  
> 114 Di quel ch’ ebbe or così la lingua pronta.  

But be not silent, if thou gettest out from hence, respecting him, who now had his tongue so ready.

- *non tacer* ‘not to keep silent’ → “be not silent”: the Italian prohibitive infinitive becomes an English imperative, with the verb expressed through “be” and an adjective.
- *se tu di qua entro eschi* ‘if thou from here within come out’ → “if thou gettest out from hence”: the verb moves before the phrase indicating departure, and coming out is expressed as getting out. “Hence” renders ‘from here,’ but the explicit indication of being within is lost; the Italian subjunctive becomes an English present indicative.
- *Di quel* ‘of that one’ → “respecting him”: the preposition becomes an explicit expression of reference, and the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *ch’ ebbe or* ‘who had now’ → “who now had”: the adverb moves before the verb.
- *così la lingua pronta* ‘so the tongue ready’ → “his tongue so ready”: the degree adverb moves next to the adjective it qualifies, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

## Lines 115–123 — Traitors Named in the Ice

> 115 Ei piange qui l’ argento de’ Franceschi:  

Here he laments the Frenchmen’s silver.

- *Ei piange qui* ‘He weeps here’ → “Here he laments”: the adverb moves to the beginning, and ‘weeps’ becomes “laments”.
- *l’argento de’ Franceschi* ‘the silver of the Frenchmen’ → “the Frenchmen’s silver”: the Italian possessive phrase becomes an English possessive placed before the noun.

> 116 Io vidi, potrai dir, quel da Duera  
> 117 Là, dove i peccatori stanno freschi.  

‘Him of Duera,’ thou canst say, ‘I saw there, where the sinners stand pinched in ice.’

- *potrai dir* ‘thou wilt be able to say’ → “thou canst say”: future ability becomes present ability.
- *quel da Duera* ‘that one from Duera’ → “Him of Duera”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, “of” replaces ‘from’, and the object moves before “I saw”.
- *stanno freschi* ‘remain cool’ → “stand pinched in ice”: *stanno* is rendered as physical standing, while the adjective ‘cool’ is expanded into a phrase specifying the cold’s effect and adding an explicit reference to ice.

> 118 Se fossi dimandato, altri chi v’ era;  
> 119 Tu hai da lato quel di Beccaria,  
> 120 Di cui segò Fiorenza la gorgiera.  

Shouldst thou be asked who else was there, thou hast beside thee the Beccaria whose gorge was slit by Florence.

- *Se fossi dimandato* ‘If thou wert asked’ → “Shouldst thou be asked”: the conditional conjunction and subjunctive construction become an inverted construction with “Shouldst”.
- *altri chi* ‘others, who’ → “who else”: the indefinite pronoun becomes the adverb “else”, placed after the interrogative.
- *v’era* ‘there was’ → “was there”: the locative adverb moves after the verb.
- *da lato* ‘at the side’ → “beside thee”: the noun phrase becomes a preposition with an explicit personal pronoun.
- *quel di Beccaria* ‘that one of Beccaria’ → “the Beccaria”: the demonstrative and its dependent phrase become a name introduced by the definite article.
- *Di cui segò Fiorenza la gorgiera* ‘of whom Florence sawed the throat’ → “whose gorge was slit by Florence”: the relative possessive phrase becomes “whose”; the active construction becomes passive, making the throat the subject and Florence the agent; and ‘sawed’ becomes “slit”.

> 121 Gianni del Soldanier credo che sia  
> 122 Più là con Ganellone, e Tribaldello  
> 123 Ch’ aprì Faenza quando si dormia.  

Gianni del Soldanier, I think, is farther on, with Ganellone, and Tribaldello who unbarred Faenza when it slept.”

- *credo che sia* ‘I believe that he is’ → “I think” and “is”: the governing verb becomes a parenthetical comment, the conjunction is omitted, and the subordinate subjunctive becomes a main-clause indicative.
- *Più là* ‘farther there’ → “farther on”: the locative ‘there’ becomes the idiomatic directional “on”.
- *aprì* ‘opened’ → “unbarred”: the translation specifies a means of opening that the Italian verb does not itself express.
- *quando si dormia* ‘when one was sleeping’ → “when it slept”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal one referring to Faenza as “it”, and the imperfect is rendered by the English simple past.

## Lines 124–139 — One Frozen Sinner Gnaws Another

> 124 Noi eravam partiti già da ello,  
> 125 Ch’ io vidi duo ghiacciati in una buca  
> 126 Sì, che l’ un capo all’ altro era cappello:  

We had already left him, when I saw two frozen in one hole so closely, that the one head was a cap to the other.

- *eravam partiti già da ello* ‘we had departed already from him’ → “had already left him”: departure expressed with *da* becomes the transitive “left”; “already” moves between the auxiliary and the main verb.
- *Ch’* ‘that’ → “when”: the conjunction is interpreted temporally.
- *Sì* ‘so’ → “so closely”: “closely” is supplied to specify the relationship expressed by “so”.
- *all’ altro era cappello* ‘to the other was cap’ → “was a cap to the other”: the verb and predicate noun precede the prepositional phrase, and English supplies the indefinite article.

> 127 E come il pan per fame si manduca,  
> 128 Così il sovran li denti all’ altro pose  
> 129 Là, ’ve il cervel s’ aggiunge con la nuca.  

And as bread is chewed for hunger, so the uppermost put his teeth into the other there where the brain joins with the nape.

- *il pan* ‘the bread’ → “bread”: the article is omitted for the generic English noun.
- *per fame si manduca* ‘for hunger is eaten’ → “is chewed for hunger”: “chewed” makes the action more specific than eating; the phrase expressing its cause follows the verb. The Italian passive construction with *si* becomes an English passive with “is”.
- *li denti all’ altro pose* ‘the teeth to the other put’ → “put his teeth into the other”: the verb moves before its objects; “his” replaces the definite article, and “into” replaces the less specific ‘to’.
- *s’ aggiunge* ‘joins itself’ → “joins”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes a simple intransitive verb.

> 130 Non altrimenti Tideo si rose  
> 131 Le tempie a Menalippo per disdegno,  
> 132 Che quei faceva il teschio e l’ altre cose.  

Not otherwise did Tydeus gnaw the temples of Menalippus for rage, than he the skull and the other parts.

- *Non altrimenti Tideo si rose* ‘not otherwise Tydeus gnawed for himself’ → “Not otherwise did Tydeus gnaw”: the initial negative expression requires English subject–auxiliary inversion, with “did” carrying the past tense. The reflexive *si*, marking the subject’s involvement in the action, is not separately represented.
- *a Menalippo* ‘to Menalippus’ → “of Menalippus”: the Italian possessive dative becomes an English phrase with “of”.
- *disdegno* ‘disdain’ → “rage”: the translation selects the sense of angry passion rather than contempt.
- *quei faceva* ‘that one did’ → “he”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the substitute verb *faceva* is omitted, leaving the action understood from “gnaw”.
- *cose* ‘things’ → “parts”: the general noun is made specifically anatomical.

> 133 O tu, che mostri per sì bestial segno  
> 134 Odio sovra colui che tu ti mangi,  
> 135 Dimmi il perchè, diss’ io: per tal convegno,  

“O thou! who by such brutal token showest thy hate on him whom thou devourest, tell me why,” I said: “on this condition,

- *mostri per sì bestial segno* ‘showest by so bestial a sign’ → “by such brutal token showest”: the verb moves after the phrase describing the means of showing; “such” replaces the degree expression ‘so’; “brutal” renders *bestial*, and “token” renders *segno* as an outward indication.
- *Odio* ‘hate’ → “thy hate”: the possessive is supplied.
- *colui* ‘that one’ → “him”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *tu ti mangi* ‘thou eatest for thyself’ → “thou devourest”: “devourest” intensifies eating, while the reflexive dative *ti* is not separately expressed.
- *Dimmi il perchè* ‘tell me the why’ → “tell me why”: the substantivized interrogative becomes an ordinary interrogative complement without an article.
- *diss’ io* ‘said I’ → “I said”: subject and verb change places.
- *per tal convegno* ‘by such an agreement’ → “on this condition”: the agreement is expressed as a condition, with the corresponding English preposition; *tal* becomes the demonstrative “this”.

> 136 Che se tu a ragion di lui ti piangi,  
> 137 Sappiendo chi voi siete, e la sua pecca,  
> 138 Nel mondo suso ancor io te ne cangi,  

that if thou with reason complainest of him, I, knowing who ye are and his offence, may yet repay thee in the world above,

- *di lui ti piangi* ‘of him complainest’ → “complainest of him”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb, and the Italian reflexive construction becomes the non-reflexive English “complainest”.
- *Sappiendo chi voi siete, e la sua pecca, Nel mondo suso ancor io te ne cangi* ‘knowing who ye are, and his fault, in the world above yet I thee for it recompense’ → “I, knowing who ye are and his offence, may yet repay thee in the world above”: “offence” gives *pecca* the more specific character of wrongdoing. The phrase “in the world above” moves from before the main clause’s subject and verb to the end of that clause. The subject moves forward to precede the intervening participial phrase beginning “knowing”. The subjunctive *cangi* is rendered with “may”; “yet” follows that modal, and “thee” follows the main verb. The pronoun *ne*, expressing what the recompense is for, is left implicit.

> 139 Se quella, con ch’ io parlo, non si secca.  

if that, wherewith I speak, be not dried up.”

- *con ch’* ‘with which’ → “wherewith”: the preposition and relative pronoun become a single relative adverb.
- *non si secca* ‘does not dry up’ → “be not dried up”: the Italian present indicative becomes an English subjunctive in the condition; the reflexive verb expressing drying becomes a passive construction.

## Summary — Close Texture, Recast Syntax

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s sequence of expressions, imagery, and often unusual vocabulary, while repeatedly rearranging its syntax. Subjects and verbs commonly move before objects or complements placed first in Italian: *di mio concetto il suco* becomes “the juice of my conception”, and *Vivo son io* becomes “I am alive”. Adjectives generally move before their nouns, and possessive phrases become English possessives, as in “Arthur’s hand”. Yet the prose retains marked inversions and compressed expressions, including “wood with wood no cramp did ever gird so strongly” and “than he the skull and the other parts”, rather than consistently adopting ordinary English order.

Changes of construction often make Italian idioms workable in English. *non mi dar più lagna*, ‘give me no more annoyance’, becomes “pester me no more”; *Del contrario ho io brama* becomes “The contrary is what I long for”. Reflexive verbs frequently become non-reflexive, infinitival complements sometimes become finite clauses, and subjunctives are often expressed through “may”. More substantial recasting occurs when *Di cui segò Fiorenza la gorgiera* becomes the passive “whose gorge was slit by Florence”, or when *Se tu non latri* becomes “but thou must bark too”, replacing a negative conditional with an affirmative expression of necessity and addition.

The vocabulary ranges from close, conspicuous equivalents such as “gelatine” and “unhair” to interpretive or more forceful choices. *Gocciar*, ‘dripped’, becomes “gushed”; *aprì*, ‘opened’, becomes “unbarred”; and *cose*, ‘things’, becomes the anatomically specific “parts”. Additions clarify what the Italian leaves implicit: “with walls” specifies the means of enclosing, “the hue of” makes shame visibly perceptible, and “when I think” supplies a mental action absent from *de’ gelati guazzi*. Elsewhere distinctions are reduced: *zebe* loses its specifically female reference in “goats”, and *rezzo* becomes “shade” without separately expressing its suggestion of cold. Some choices settle an ambiguity rather than merely rephrase it, notably the second-person reading in “if thou wert alive” for *se vivo fossi*.

Alongside these recorded departures are routine adjustments generally left out of the comparison. English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits definite articles before possessives. Articles identifying body parts also frequently become possessives, as in “thy soles” and “his head”. Such grammatical accommodation coexists with considerable verbal fidelity: “the juice of my conception”, “mamma and papa”, and “rough and hoarse” preserve distinctive features of the Italian. The result is a translation that usually retains the original’s verbal texture, even while expanding, interpreting, or rebuilding individual expressions.
