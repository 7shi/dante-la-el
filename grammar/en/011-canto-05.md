# Inferno 5

## Lines 1–12 — Minos Judges the Souls

> 1 Così discesi del cerchio primaio  
> 2 Giù nel secondo, che men loco cinghia,  
> 3 E tanto più dolor, che pugne a guaio.  

Thus I descended from the first circle down into the second, which encompasses less space, and so much greater pain, that it stings to wailing.

- *men loco cinghia* ‘less space encompasses’ → “encompasses less space”: the verb is moved before its object.
- *tanto più dolor* ‘so much more pain’ → “so much greater pain”: “greater” replaces the quantitative comparative ‘more’.
- *che pugne a guaio* ‘which stings to wailing’ → “that it stings to wailing”: the English reads the connection as a result clause, rather than a relative clause modifying *dolor*.

> 4 Stavvi Minos orribilmente, e ringhia:  
> 5 Esamina le colpe nell’ entrata,  
> 6 Giudica e manda, secondo che avvinghia.  

There Minos sits horrific, and grins: examines the crimes upon the entrance; judges, and sends according as he girds himself.

- *Stavvi Minos* ‘stands there Minos’ → “There Minos sits”: the locative precedes the subject, the subject precedes the verb, and *sta* is rendered specifically as sitting.
- *orribilmente* ‘horribly’ → “horrific”: an adverb becomes an adjective describing Minos.
- *ringhia* ‘growls’ → “grins”: the audible action is rendered as a facial action or baring of the teeth.
- *le colpe* ‘the faults’ → “the crimes”: the English uses a more specifically juridical term.
- *nell’ entrata* ‘in the entrance’ → “upon the entrance”: the preposition changes, allowing the entrance to be understood as the occasion of examination.
- *avvinghia* ‘encircles’ → “he girds himself”: encircling becomes girding, and the reflexive object, implicit in the Italian, is made explicit.

> 7 Dico, che quando l’ anima mal nata  
> 8 Li vien dinanzi, tutta si confessa;  

I say, that when the ill-born spirit comes before him, it confesses all;

- *l’ anima mal nata* ‘the soul ill-born’ → “the ill-born spirit”: “spirit” replaces ‘soul’, and the descriptive phrase moves before the noun.
- *Li vien dinanzi* ‘to him comes before’ → “comes before him”: the preceding dative pronoun becomes the following complement of “before”.
- *tutta si confessa* ‘wholly confesses itself’ → “it confesses all”: *tutta*, agreeing with *anima* and indicating completeness, becomes the object “all”; the reflexive construction is not retained.

> 9 E quel conoscitor delle peccata  
> 10 Vede qual loco d’ inferno è da essa:  

and that sin-discerner sees what place in hell is for it,

- *quel conoscitor delle peccata* ‘that discerner of the sins’ → “that sin-discerner”: the noun and its dependent phrase become a compound; the plural ‘sins’ becomes singular attributive “sin”, and its article disappears.
- *d’ inferno* ‘of hell’ → “in hell”: the relation expressed by ‘of’ becomes explicitly locative.
- *è da essa* ‘is suited to it’ → “is for it”: the Italian construction with *da*, indicating fitness or appropriateness, is rendered by the simpler “for”.

> 11 Cignesi colla coda tante volte,  
> 12 Quantunque gradi vuol che giù sia messa.  

and with his tail makes as many circles round himself as the degrees he will have it to descend.

- *Cignesi … tante volte* ‘girds himself … so many times’ → “and” … “makes as many circles round himself”: a coordinating conjunction is supplied; the reflexive verb becomes a construction with “makes” and an object, while the repeated actions become countable “circles”. “Round himself” preserves the reflexive relationship.
- *colla coda* ‘with the tail’ → “with his tail”: a possessive replaces the definite article, and the phrase moves before the verb.
- *Quantunque gradi* ‘as many degrees’ → “as the degrees”: the quantitative comparison is expressed by “as”, following the earlier “as many”, and a definite article is supplied.
- *vuol che giù sia messa* ‘he wills that it be put down’ → “he will have it to descend”: the clause with a passive subjunctive becomes an active infinitival construction after “will have”; ‘be put down’ becomes “descend”, which incorporates the downward direction of *giù*.

## Lines 13–27 — Minos’s Warning and the Lamenting

> 13 Sempre dinanzi a lui ne stanno molte:  

Always before him stands a crowd of them.

- *ne stanno molte* ‘of them stand many’ → “stands a crowd of them”: “a crowd” replaces ‘many’, making the verb singular; “of them” moves from before the verb to after the noun.

> 14 Vanno a vicenda ciascuna al giudizio;  
> 15 Dicono e odono, e poi son giù volte.  

They go each in its turn to judgment: they tell, and hear; and then are whirled down.

- *a vicenda ciascuna* ‘in turn each’ → “each in its turn”: “each” moves before the expression of succession, and “its” is supplied.
- *Dicono* ‘they say’ → “they tell”: the verb is rendered as telling rather than saying.
- *son giù volte* ‘are down turned’ → “are whirled down”: “whirled” gives a more forceful motion than ‘turned’, and “down” follows the participle.

> 16 O tu, che vieni al doloroso ospizio,  
> 17 Disse Minos a me, quando mi vide,  
> 18 Lasciando l’ atto di cotanto ufizio,  

“O thou who comest to the abode of pain!” said Minos to me, leaving the act of that great office when he saw me;

- *al doloroso ospizio* ‘to the painful lodging’ → “to the abode of pain”: ‘lodging’ becomes “abode”, and the adjective becomes the noun phrase “of pain”.
- *quando mi vide* ‘when me he saw’ → “when he saw me”: the object follows the verb, and the clause moves after the participial phrase rendering line 18.
- *di cotanto ufizio* ‘of so great an office’ → “of that great office”: the expression of degree becomes a demonstrative followed by an adjective.

> 19 Guarda com’ entri, e di cui tu ti fide:  
> 20 Non t’ inganni l’ ampiezza dell’ entrare.  

“look how thou enterest, and in whom thou trustest. Let not the wideness of the entrance deceive thee.”

- *e di cui tu ti fide* ‘and of whom thou trustest thyself’ → “and in whom thou trustest”: the Italian construction uses *di* and a reflexive pronoun; English uses “in” without a reflexive.
- *Non t’ inganni l’ ampiezza dell’ entrare* ‘may not thee deceive the wideness of the entering’ → “Let not the wideness of the entrance deceive thee”: “Let” expresses the negative exhortation conveyed by the Italian subjunctive; the subject moves before “deceive” and the object after it. The substantivized infinitive ‘entering’ becomes “entrance”, shifting from the act of entering to the opening itself.

> 21 E il Duca mio a lui: Perchè pur gride?  

And my guide to him: “Why criest thou too?

- *il Duca mio* ‘the leader my’ → “my guide”: “guide” replaces ‘leader’, and the possessive precedes the noun.
- *pur* ‘still’ → “too”: the translation takes the adverb additively rather than as expressing persistence.

> 22 Non impedir lo suo fatale andare:  

Hinder not his fated going.

- *Non impedir* ‘not to hinder’ → “Hinder not”: the infinitive used for a negative command becomes an English imperative, with the negative following the verb.

> 23 Vuolsi così colà, dove si puote  
> 24 Ciò che si vuole, e più non dimandare.  

Thus it is willed there where what is willed can be done: and ask no more.”

- *Vuolsi così* ‘it is willed thus’ → “Thus it is willed”: the adverb moves before the verb.
- *dove si puote / Ciò che si vuole* ‘where one can [do] that which one wills’ → “where what is willed can be done”: the impersonal constructions are rendered passively; “done” supplies the action implicit in *puote*. “What” replaces ‘that which’, and its clause precedes “can be done” rather than following *si puote*.
- *più non dimandare* ‘more not to ask’ → “ask no more”: the infinitive used for a negative command becomes an imperative; the preceding adverb and negative become the following phrase “no more”.

> 25 Ora incomincian le dolenti note  
> 26 A farmisi sentire: or son venuto  
> 27 Là dove molto pianto mi percuote.  

Now begin the doleful notes to reach me; now am I come where much lamenting strikes me.

- *A farmisi sentire* ‘to make themselves heard to me’ → “to reach me”: the reflexive causative construction becomes a simple verb of arrival, leaving hearing implicit.
- *Là dove* ‘there where’ → “where”: the demonstrative adverb is omitted.
- *molto pianto* ‘much weeping’ → “much lamenting”: the translation emphasizes expressions of grief rather than weeping specifically.
- *mi percuote* ‘me strikes’ → “strikes me”: the object pronoun follows the verb.

## Lines 28–39 — The Unceasing Infernal Storm

> 28 Io venni in loco d’ ogni luce muto,  
> 29 Che mugghia, come fa mar per tempesta,  
> 30 Se da contrari venti è combattuto.  

I came into a place void of all light, which bellows like the sea in tempest, when it is combated by warring winds.

- *in loco* ‘in place’ → “into a place”: the preposition explicitly expresses movement into somewhere, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *d’ ogni luce muto* ‘of every light mute’ → “void of all light”: “void” replaces the metaphorical ‘mute’ and precedes its complement; ‘every’ becomes “all”.
- *come fa mar* ‘as does sea’ → “like the sea”: the comparison becomes a prepositional phrase, the verb is omitted, and a definite article is supplied.
- *per tempesta* ‘because of a tempest’ → “in tempest”: the tempest is expressed as a condition rather than a cause.
- *Se* ‘if’ → “when”: the conditional conjunction becomes temporal.
- *da contrari venti è combattuto* ‘by opposing winds it is combated’ → “it is combated by warring winds”: the passive verb precedes its agent rather than following it; “warring” renders opposition as active conflict.

> 31 La bufera infernal, che mai non resta,  
> 32 Mena gli spirti con la sua rapina;  

The hellish storm, which never rests, leads the spirits with its sweep;

- *La bufera infernal* ‘the storm infernal’ → “The hellish storm”: the adjective precedes the noun, and ‘infernal’ is rendered by “hellish”.
- *mai non resta* ‘never ceases’ → “never rests”: cessation is expressed as rest; the Italian negative combination *mai non* is represented by “never” alone.
- *rapina* ‘violent seizure’ → “sweep”: a noun denoting forcible seizure becomes one denoting sweeping movement.

> 33 Voltando e percotendo li molesta.  

whirling, and smiting it vexes them.

- *Voltando* ‘turning’ → “whirling”: the English verb specifies a more vigorous revolving movement.
- *li molesta* ‘them it vexes’ → “it vexes them”: the object pronoun follows the verb rather than preceding it.

> 34 Quando giungon davanti alla ruina,  
> 35 Quivi le strida, il compianto e il lamento;  

When they arrive before the ruin, there the shrieks, the moanings, and the lamentation;

- *il compianto* ‘the mourning’ → “the moanings”: the singular noun becomes plural, and mourning is rendered specifically as vocal expression.

> 36 Bestemmian quivi la virtù divina.  

there they blaspheme the divine power.

- *Bestemmian quivi* ‘they blaspheme there’ → “there they blaspheme”: the adverb moves before the subject and verb.
- *la virtù divina* ‘the power divine’ → “the divine power”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 37 Intesi, che a così fatto tormento  
> 38 Eran dannati i peccator carnali,  
> 39 Che la ragion sommettono al talento.  

I learnt that to such torment were doomed the carnal sinners, who subject reason to lust.

- *Intesi* ‘I understood’ → “I learnt”: coming to understand is expressed as acquiring knowledge.
- *così fatto* ‘so made’ → “such”: the two-word description is compressed into a determiner.
- *eran dannati* ‘were condemned’ → “were doomed”: condemnation is expressed as being consigned to a fate.
- *i peccator carnali* ‘the sinners carnal’ → “the carnal sinners”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *Che la ragion sommettono al talento* ‘who reason subject to desire’ → “who subject reason to lust”: the object follows the verb rather than preceding it; the Italian articles before the abstract nouns are omitted, and the broader ‘desire’ is rendered specifically as “lust”.

## Lines 40–54 — Shadows in the Black Air

> 40 E come gli stornei ne portan l’ ali,  
> 41 Nel freddo tempo, a schiera larga e piena;  
> 42 Così quel fiato gli spiriti mali:  

And as their wings bear along the starlings, at the cold season, in large and crowded troop; so that blast, the evil spirits.

- *gli stornei ne portan l’ ali* ‘the starlings the wings bear along’ → “their wings bear along the starlings”: the Italian places the object before the verb and its subject, *l’ali*; English puts the subject first and the object last, and supplies “their”.
- *Nel freddo tempo* ‘in the cold time’ → “at the cold season”: “at” replaces ‘in’, and “season” specifies the sense of *tempo*.
- *a schiera larga e piena* ‘in troop broad and full’ → “in large and crowded troop”: the adjectives move before the noun; “large” replaces ‘broad’, and “crowded” interprets ‘full’.
- *fiato* ‘breath’ → “blast”: the translation uses a stronger term for moving air.
- *gli spiriti mali* ‘the spirits evil’ → “the evil spirits”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 43 Di qua, di là, di giù, di su gli mena.  

Hither, thither, down, up, it leads them.

- *Di qua, di là, di giù, di su* ‘this way, that way, downward, upward’ → “Hither, thither, down, up”: the Italian directional phrases become single English adverbs.
- *gli mena* ‘them leads’ → “it leads them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the subject implicit in the verb is supplied.

> 44 Nulla speranza gli conforta mai,  
> 45 Non che di posa, ma di minor pena.  

No hope ever comforts them, not of rest but even of less pain.

- *gli conforta mai* ‘them comforts ever’ → “ever comforts them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, while the adverb moves before it.
- *Non che di posa, ma di minor pena* ‘not to speak of rest, but of lesser pain’ → “not of rest but even of less pain”: the idiomatic *non che* is reduced to “not”, while “even” is supplied in the second member to convey the force of the contrast.

> 46 E come i gru van cantando lor lai,  
> 47 Facendo in aer di sè lunga riga;  
> 48 Così vid’ io venir, traendo guai,  
> 49 Ombre portate dalla detta briga:  

And as the cranes go chanting their lays, making a long streak of themselves in the air; so I saw the shadows come, uttering wails, borne by that strife of winds.

- *in aer* ‘in air’ → “in the air”: the definite article is supplied, and the phrase moves to the end of the participial clause.
- *di sè lunga riga* ‘of themselves long line’ → “a long streak of themselves”: the noun phrase moves before ‘of themselves’, an indefinite article is supplied, and “streak” replaces ‘line’.
- *vid’ io venir … Ombre* ‘saw I come … shadows’ → “I saw the shadows come”: the subject moves before the verb; ‘shadows’ moves before ‘come’ rather than following the intervening participial phrase, and receives a definite article.
- *traendo guai* ‘drawing forth laments’ → “uttering wails”: the Italian expression is rendered with a verb explicitly denoting vocal expression.
- *dalla detta briga* ‘by the said strife’ → “by that strife of winds”: “that” replaces ‘the said’, and “of winds” is supplied to specify the nature of the strife.

> 50 Per ch’ io dissi: Maestro, chi son quelle  
> 51 Genti, che l’ aer nero sì gastiga?  

Whereat I said: “Master, who are those people, whom the black air thus lashes?”

- *Per ch’* ‘for which’ → “Whereat”: the connective becomes a single relative adverb, presenting the speech as occurring ‘at which’ rather than explicitly ‘because of which’.
- *l’ aer nero* ‘the air black’ → “the black air”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *gastiga* ‘chastises’ → “lashes”: the translation substitutes a specific physical action for the more general verb of punishment.

> 52 La prima di color, di cui novelle  
> 53 Tu vuoi saper, mi disse quegli allotta,  
> 54 Fu imperatrice di molte favelle.  

“The first of these concerning whom thou seekest to know,” he then replied, “was Empress of many tongues.

- *di color* ‘of those’ → “of these”: the demonstrative changes from distal to proximal.
- *di cui novelle* ‘of whom news’ → “concerning whom”: *novelle*, the object of ‘know’, is omitted; the remaining phrase expresses the subject about whom knowledge is sought.
- *Tu vuoi saper* ‘thou wishest to know’ → “thou seekest to know”: seeking replaces wishing.
- *mi disse quegli allotta* ‘to me said he then’ → “he then replied”: ‘to me’ is omitted, ‘said’ becomes “replied”, and both the subject and the adverb move before the verb.

## Lines 55–63 — Semiramis and Other Lustful Queens

> 55 A vizio di lussuria fu sì rotta,  
> 56 Che libito fe’ licito in sua legge  
> 57 Per torre il biasmo, in che era condotta.  

With the vice of luxury she was so broken, that she made lust and law alike in her decree, to take away the blame she had incurred.

- *A vizio* ‘to vice’ → “With the vice”: the preposition changes, and a definite article is supplied.
- *lussuria* ‘lust’ → “luxury”: the translation uses “luxury” in its older sense of sexual indulgence.
- *libito fe’ licito* ‘desire she made lawful’ → “she made lust and law alike”: the object moves after the verb; the adjective ‘lawful’ becomes the noun “law,” coordinated with “lust,” and “alike” expresses the equivalence.
- *in sua legge* ‘in her law’ → “in her decree”: “decree” replaces ‘law.’
- *in che era condotta* ‘into which she was brought’ → “she had incurred”: the passive construction becomes active, and the imperfect becomes a pluperfect; the prepositional relative becomes an unexpressed direct-object relative.

> 58 Ell’ è Semiramis, di cui si legge,  
> 59 Che succedette a Nino, e fu sua sposa:  

She is Semiramis, of whom we read that she succeeded Ninus, and was his spouse.

- *si legge* ‘one reads’ → “we read”: the impersonal construction becomes a first-person plural.
- *succedette a Nino* ‘succeeded to Ninus’ → “she succeeded Ninus”: the Italian prepositional complement becomes a direct object in English.

> 60 Tenne la terra, che il Soldan corregge.  

She held the land which the Soldan rules.

- Literal.

> 61 L’ altra è colei, che s’ ancise amorosa,  
> 62 E ruppe fede al cener di Sicheo;  

That other is she who slew herself in love, and broke faith to the ashes of Sichæus.

- *L’altra* ‘the other’ → “That other”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative.
- *colei* ‘that woman’ → “she”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *s’ancise* ‘herself slew’ → “slew herself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *amorosa* ‘enamoured’ → “in love”: an adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *al cener* ‘to the ash’ → “to the ashes”: the singular noun becomes plural.

> 63 Poi è Cleopatras lussuriosa.  

Next comes luxurious Cleopatra.”

- *è* ‘is’ → “comes”: a verb of motion replaces the verb ‘to be.’
- *Cleopatras lussuriosa* ‘Cleopatra lustful’ → “luxurious Cleopatra”: the adjective moves before the name; “luxurious” is used in its older sense of ‘lustful.’

## Lines 64–78 — Named Shades and a Request

> 64 Elena vidi, per cui tanto reo  
> 65 Tempo si volse; e vidi il grande Achille,  
> 66 Che con amore al fine combatteo.  

Helena I saw, for whom so long a time of ill revolved; and I saw the great Achilles, who fought at last with love.

- *tanto reo Tempo* ‘so much evil time’ → “so long a time of ill”: quantity becomes duration in “so long”; an indefinite article is supplied; and the adjective *reo* becomes the noun phrase “of ill”, following “time”.
- *si volse* ‘turned’ → “revolved”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes a plain intransitive verb.
- *Che con amore al fine combatteo* ‘who with love at the end fought’ → “who fought at last with love”: the verb moves before both adverbial phrases, whose order is reversed; “at last” replaces ‘at the end’.

> 67 Vidi Paris, Tristano; e più di mille  
> 68 Ombre mostrommi, e nominolle a dito,  
> 69 Ch’ amor di nostra vita dipartille.  

I saw Paris, Tristan. And more than a thousand shades he shewed to me, and pointing with his finger, named them, whom love had parted from our life.

- *più di mille* ‘more than thousand’ → “more than a thousand”: English supplies the indefinite article before “thousand”.
- *mostrommi* ‘showed me’ → “he shewed to me”: the attached indirect-object pronoun becomes the prepositional phrase “to me”.
- *e nominolle a dito* ‘and named them by a finger’ → “and pointing with his finger, named them”: *a dito* is expanded into a participial phrase, supplying the act of pointing and the possessive “his”; this phrase is placed before the naming rather than after it.
- *Ch’ amor di nostra vita dipartille* ‘whom love from our life parted them’ → “whom love had parted from our life”: the resumptive object pronoun *-le*, repeating the relative object, is omitted; the simple past becomes past perfect; and “from our life” moves after the verb.

> 70 Poscia ch’ io ebbi il mio Dottore udito  
> 71 Nomar le donne antiche e i cavalieri,  
> 72 Pietà mi vinse, e fui quasi smarrito.  

After I had heard my teacher name the olden dames and cavaliers, pity conquered me, and I was as if bewildered.

- *io ebbi il mio Dottore udito Nomar* ‘I had my teacher heard name’ → “I had heard my teacher name”: the object, placed between auxiliary and participle in Italian, follows the complete compound verb in English.
- *le donne antiche* ‘the women ancient’ → “the olden dames”: the adjective precedes the noun; “olden dames” gives an archaizing English equivalent of ‘ancient women’.
- *Pietà mi vinse* ‘pity me conquered’ → “pity conquered me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *e fui quasi smarrito* ‘and I was almost lost’ → “and I was as if bewildered”: *quasi* becomes “as if” rather than ‘almost’, while *smarrito* is rendered in its psychological sense of bewilderment.

> 73 Io cominciai: Poeta, volentieri  
> 74 Parlerei a que’ duo, che insieme vanno,  
> 75 E paion sì al vento esser leggieri.  

I began: “Poet, willingly would I speak with those two that go together, and seem so light upon the wind.”

- *Parlerei a* ‘I would speak to’ → “would I speak with”: “with” replaces ‘to’, presenting the speaking as conversation.
- *che insieme vanno* ‘that together go’ → “that go together”: the adverb moves after the verb.
- *E paion sì al vento esser leggieri* ‘and seem so to the wind to be light’ → “and seem so light upon the wind”: the explicit infinitive *esser* is omitted; “light” moves beside “so”; and *al vento* becomes “upon the wind”, following the adjective.

> 76 Ed egli a me: Vedrai, quando saranno  
> 77 Più presso a noi; e tu allor li prega  
> 78 Per quell’ amor che i mena; e quei verranno.  

And he to me: “Thou shalt see when they are nearer to us: and do thou then entreat them by that love, which leads them; and they will come.”

- *quando saranno* ‘when they will be’ → “when they are”: the Italian future becomes an English present in a future-time clause introduced by “when”.
- *e tu allor li prega* ‘and thou then them entreat’ → “and do thou then entreat them”: English supplies the imperative auxiliary “do” before the expressed subject; the object pronoun moves after the main verb.
- *che i mena* ‘which them leads’ → “which leads them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *e quei verranno* ‘and those will come’ → “and they will come”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.

## Lines 79–90 — A Call to Troubled Souls

> 79 Sì tosto come il vento a noi li piega,  
> 80 Muovo la voce: O anime affannate,  
> 81 Venite a noi parlar, s’ altri nol niega.  

Soon as the wind bends them to us, I raise my voice: “O wearied souls! come to speak with us, if none denies it.”

- *Sì tosto come* ‘As soon as’ → “Soon as”: the initial comparative “as” is omitted.
- *il vento a noi li piega* ‘the wind to us them bends’ → “the wind bends them to us”: the verb precedes its objects, and the destination follows the direct object.
- *Muovo la voce* ‘I move the voice’ → “I raise my voice”: the verb is changed to an English expression for beginning to speak, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *anime affannate* ‘souls distressed’ → “wearied souls”: distress is rendered as weariness, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *Venite a noi parlar* ‘Come to us to speak’ → “come to speak with us”: the infinitive precedes the phrase identifying the people addressed, and “with” replaces ‘to’.
- *s’altri nol niega* ‘if another does not deny it’ → “if none denies it”: the negative is transferred from the verb to the pronoun; ‘another’ under negation becomes “none”.

> 82 Quali colombe, dal disio chiamate,  
> 83 Con l’ ali aperte e ferme al dolce nido  
> 84 Volan per l’ aer dal voler portate:  

As doves called by desire, with open and steady wings fly through the air to their loved nest, borne by their will;

- *dal disio chiamate* ‘by desire called’ → “called by desire”: the participle precedes the phrase naming its agent.
- *Con l’ali aperte e ferme* ‘with the wings open and still’ → “with open and steady wings”: the definite article is omitted, the adjectives precede the noun, and “steady” renders *ferme*.
- *al dolce nido / Volan per l’aer* ‘to the sweet nest fly through the air’ → “fly through the air to their loved nest”: the destination follows the verb and the phrase describing passage; ‘sweet’ becomes “loved”, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *dal voler portate* ‘by will borne’ → “borne by their will”: the participle precedes its agent phrase, and “their” is supplied.

> 85 Cotali uscir della schiera ov’ è Dido,  
> 86 A noi venendo per l’ aer maligno,  

so those spirits issued from the band where Dido is, coming to us through the malignant air.

- *Cotali uscir* ‘Such came out’ → “so those spirits issued”: the comparative *Cotali* becomes the adverb “so”, with “those spirits” supplied as the explicit subject; ‘came out’ is expressed by “issued”.
- *ov’è Dido* ‘where is Dido’ → “where Dido is”: the subject is placed before the verb.
- *A noi venendo* ‘to us coming’ → “coming to us”: the participle precedes the destination phrase.
- *per l’aer maligno* ‘through the air malignant’ → “through the malignant air”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 87 Sì forte fu l’ affettuoso grido.  

Such was the force of my affectuous cry.

- *Sì forte fu* ‘So strong was’ → “Such was the force of”: the adjective expressing strength becomes the noun “force”, and the construction changes from describing the cry to identifying its force.
- *l’affettuoso grido* ‘the affectuous cry’ → “my affectuous cry”: the definite article becomes a possessive not explicitly present in the Italian.

> 88 O animal grazioso e benigno,  
> 89 Che visitando vai per l’ aer perso  
> 90 Noi che tignemmo il mondo di sanguigno:  

“O living creature, gracious and benign! that goest through the black air, visiting us who stained the earth with blood.

- *animal* ‘animal’ → “living creature”: the single noun is expanded into a phrase retaining its broader sense of an animate being.
- *visitando vai … Noi* ‘visiting goest … us’ → “goest” … “visiting us”: the verb-plus-gerund construction is recast as a verb of motion followed by a participial phrase; the object is brought directly after “visiting”.
- *per l’aer perso* ‘through the air dark-purple’ → “through the black air”: the specific dark hue becomes “black”, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *tignemmo … di sanguigno* ‘dyed … blood-red’ → “stained” … “with blood”: dyeing becomes staining, and the colour expression is rendered as the substance producing the stain.
- *il mondo* ‘the world’ → “the earth”: “earth” replaces the broader noun ‘world’.

## Lines 91–105 — Pity, Birthplace, and Love

> 91 Se fosse amico il Re dell’ universo,  
> 92 Noi pregheremmo lui per la tua pace,  
> 93 Poi che hai pietà del nostro mal perverso.  

If the King of the Universe were our friend, we would pray him for thy peace; seeing that thou hast pity of our perverse misfortune.

- *Se fosse amico il Re dell’ universo* ‘If were friend the King of the Universe’ → “If the King of the Universe were our friend”: the subject precedes the verb rather than following its complement; “our” is supplied.
- *Poi che* ‘since’ → “seeing that”: the causal conjunction becomes an explanatory phrase.
- *mal perverso* ‘evil perverse’ → “perverse misfortune”: *mal* is rendered as “misfortune,” and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 94 Di quel che udire e che parlar ti piace  
> 95 Noi udiremo e parleremo a vui,  
> 96 Mentrechè il vento, come fa, si tace.  

Of that which it pleases thee to hear and to speak, we will hear and speak with you, whilst the wind, as now, is silent.

- *che udire e che parlar ti piace* ‘which to hear and which to speak pleases thee’ → “which it pleases thee to hear and to speak”: anticipatory “it” is supplied, the governing verb precedes the infinitives, and the repeated relative *che* is omitted.
- *a vui* ‘to you’ → “with you”: the preposition makes the exchange reciprocal rather than directional.
- *come fa* ‘as it does’ → “as now”: the verb referring back to the wind’s silence is replaced by a temporal adverb.
- *si tace* ‘keeps silent’ → “is silent”: the pronominal verb becomes a construction with “is” and an adjective.

> 97 Siede la terra, dove nata fui,  
> 98 Su la marina dove il Po discende  
> 99 Per aver pace co’ seguaci sui.  

“The town, where I was born, sits on the shore, where Po descends to rest with his attendant streams.

- *Siede la terra, dove nata fui* ‘Sits the town, where born I was’ → “The town, where I was born, sits”: the subject and its relative clause move before the main verb; within the relative clause, the auxiliary precedes the participle.
- *il Po* ‘the Po’ → “Po”: the article before the river’s name is omitted.
- *Per aver pace* ‘to have peace’ → “to rest”: a verb with a noun object becomes a single verb.
- *co’ seguaci sui* ‘with followers his’ → “with his attendant streams”: “streams” makes explicit what the Italian leaves unnamed; the noun *seguaci* becomes the adjective “attendant,” and the possessive moves before the noun.

> 100 Amor, che al cor gentil ratto s’ apprende,  
> 101 Prese costui della bella persona  
> 102 Che mi fu tolta, e il modo ancor m’ offende.  

Love, which is quickly caught in gentle heart, took him with the fair body of which I was bereft; and the manner still afflicts me.

- *al cor gentil* ‘to the heart gentle’ → “in gentle heart”: “in” replaces ‘to,’ the article is omitted, the adjective precedes the noun, and the phrase moves after the verb.
- *ratto s’ apprende* ‘quickly attaches itself’ → “is quickly caught”: the pronominal verb becomes passive, presenting attachment as being caught.
- *costui* ‘this man’ → “him”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *della bella persona* ‘of the fair person’ → “with the fair body”: *di* is rendered instrumentally by “with,” and *persona* is understood specifically as the body.
- *Che mi fu tolta* ‘which from me was taken’ → “of which I was bereft”: the relative pronoun, originally the subject of the passive verb, becomes a prepositional complement; the person deprived becomes the subject, and ‘was taken’ becomes “was bereft.”
- *m’ offende* ‘me hurts’ → “afflicts me”: “afflicts” renders the verb, and the object pronoun follows it rather than preceding it.

> 103 Amor, che a nullo amato amar perdona,  
> 104 Mi prese del costui piacer sì forte,  
> 105 Che, come vedi, ancor non m’ abbandona.  

Love, which to no loved one permits excuse for loving, took me so strongly with delight in him, that, as thou seest, even now it leaves me not.

- *amato* ‘loved’ → “loved one”: the substantival participle is expanded with “one.”
- *amar perdona* ‘loving pardons’ → “permits excuse for loving”: *perdona* is paraphrased as permitting an excuse; the infinitive preceding the verb becomes an *-ing* form following the supplied preposition “for.”
- *Mi prese* ‘me took’ → “took me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *del costui piacer sì forte* ‘by this man’s charm so strongly’ → “so strongly with delight in him”: the man’s attractiveness is rendered as delight experienced in him; the possessive relationship becomes “in him,” and the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun; “so strongly” moves before, rather than after, the phrase describing what captivated her.
- *ancor* ‘still’ → “even now”: continued duration is expressed through a more emphatic temporal phrase.
- *non m’ abbandona* ‘not me abandons’ → “it leaves me not”: “leaves” replaces ‘abandons’; the object follows the verb, and the negative moves to the end.

## Lines 106–117 — Love, Death, and Pity

> 106 Amor condusse noi ad una morte:  

Love led us to one death.

- Literal.

> 107 Caina attende chi vita ci spense.  

Caïna waits for him who quenched our life.”

- *attende chi* ‘awaits whoever’ → “waits for him who”: the transitive verb becomes a verb with a preposition, and the fused relative becomes an explicit antecedent followed by a relative clause.
- *vita ci spense* ‘life to us quenched’ → “quenched our life”: the object moves after the verb, and the dative pronoun expressing the persons affected becomes the possessive “our”.

> 108 Queste parole da lor ci fur porte.  

These words from them were offered to us.

- *ci fur porte* ‘to us were offered’ → “were offered to us”: the indirect object moves after the passive verb.

> 109 Da che io intesi quelle anime offense,  
> 110 Chinai il viso, e tanto il tenni basso,  
> 111 Finchè il Poeta mi disse: Che pense?  

After I had heard those wounded souls, I bowed my face, and held it low until the Poet said to me: “What art thou thinking of?”

- *Da che* ‘From when’ → “After”: the temporal expression becomes a conjunction marking a preceding event.
- *io intesi* ‘I heard’ → “I had heard”: the simple past becomes a past perfect.
- *quelle anime offense* ‘those souls wounded’ → “those wounded souls”: the participial adjective moves before the noun.
- *Chinai il viso* ‘I bowed the face’ → “I bowed my face”: the definite article marking a body part becomes a possessive.
- *e tanto il tenni basso* ‘and so long it I held low’ → “and held it low”: ‘so long’ is omitted, leaving “until” alone to express the duration; the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *Che pense* ‘What thinkest thou?’ → “What art thou thinking of?”: the simple present becomes a progressive, and “of” is supplied.

> 112 Quando risposi, cominciai: O lasso!  

When I answered, I began: “Ah me!

- *O lasso* ‘Oh, wretched!’ → “Ah me!”: the adjective describing the speaker becomes an idiomatic exclamation with a personal pronoun.

> 113 Quanti dolci pensier, quanto disio  
> 114 Menò costoro al doloroso passo!  

what sweet thoughts, what longing led them to the woful pass!”

- *Quanti* ‘How many’ → “what”: the exclamation no longer explicitly expresses number.
- *quanto* ‘how much’ → “what”: the exclamation no longer explicitly expresses degree or quantity.
- *costoro* ‘these people’ → “them”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *passo* ‘step’ → “pass”: the translation takes the noun as a difficult situation rather than retaining the image of a step.

> 115 Poi mi rivolsi a loro, e parlai io,  
> 116 E cominciai: Francesca, i tuoi martiri  
> 117 A lagrimar mi fanno tristo e pio.  

Then I turned again to them; and I spoke, and began: “Francesca, thy torments make me weep with grief and pity.

- *mi rivolsi* ‘myself I turned again’ → “I turned again”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive one, without an object pronoun.
- *e parlai io* ‘and spoke I’ → “and I spoke”: the explicit subject moves before the verb.
- *A lagrimar mi fanno tristo e pio* ‘to weep they make me sad and compassionate’ → “make me weep with grief and pity”: the opening infinitive moves after the verb and object, becoming the bare infinitive required after “make”; the adjectives describing the speaker become abstract nouns in a phrase describing his weeping.

## Lines 118–131 — The First Root of Love

> 118 Ma dimmi: al tempo de’ dolci sospiri,  
> 119 A che, e come concedette amore,  
> 120 Che conosceste i dubbiosi desiri?  

But tell me: in the time of the sweet sighs, by what and how love granted you to know the dubious desires?”

- *al tempo* ‘at the time’ → “in the time”: the temporal preposition changes.
- *A che* ‘at what’ → “by what”: the English interprets the question as asking by what means.
- *concedette amore, Che conosceste* ‘granted love that you came to know’ → “love granted you to know”: the subject moves before the verb; the finite clause introduced by *che* becomes an infinitive construction, with “you” placed after “granted”.

> 121 Ed ella a me: Nessun maggior dolore,  
> 122 Che ricordarsi del tempo felice  
> 123 Nella miseria; e ciò sa il tuo dottore.  

And she to me: “There is no greater pain than to recall a happy time in wretchedness; and this thy teacher knows.

- *Nessun maggior dolore* ‘no greater pain’ → “There is no greater pain”: the English supplies an existential construction where the Italian leaves the verb understood.
- *ricordarsi del tempo felice* ‘to remember the happy time’ → “to recall a happy time”: the pronominal verb with *di* becomes the transitive “recall”; the definite article becomes indefinite, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Nella miseria* ‘in the misery’ → “in wretchedness”: “wretchedness” renders *miseria*, and the definite article is omitted.
- *ciò sa il tuo dottore* ‘this knows thy teacher’ → “this thy teacher knows”: the subject moves before the verb, while the object remains at the beginning.

> 124 Ma se a conoscer la prima radice  
> 125 Del nostro amor tu hai cotanto affetto,  
> 126 Farò come colui che piange e dice.  

But if thou hast such desire to learn the first root of our love, I will do like one who weeps and tells.

- *a conoscer la prima radice Del nostro amor* ‘to know the first root of our love’ → “to learn the first root of our love”: “learn” emphasizes acquiring knowledge; the infinitive phrase moves from before *tu hai cotanto affetto* to after its English rendering.
- *tu hai cotanto affetto* ‘thou hast so much desire’ → “thou hast such desire”: the expression of degree changes from ‘so much’ to “such”.
- *come colui* ‘as that one’ → “like one”: the demonstrative becomes an indefinite pronoun, and the comparison is expressed with “like”.
- *dice* ‘says’ → “tells”: the English chooses a verb of recounting rather than the more general verb of saying.

> 127 Noi leggevamo un giorno per diletto  
> 128 Di Lancillotto, come amor lo strinse:  

“One day, for pastime, we read of Lancelot, how love constrained him.

- *Noi leggevamo* ‘we were reading’ → “we read”: the Italian imperfect becomes an English simple past, without explicitly marking the reading as ongoing.
- *un giorno per diletto* ‘one day for pleasure’ → “One day, for pastime”: both phrases move before the subject and verb; “pastime” renders *diletto* as recreation rather than pleasure.
- *lo strinse* ‘him gripped’ → “constrained him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the image of gripping becomes an expression of constraint.

> 129 Soli eravamo e senza alcun sospetto.  

We were alone, and without all suspicion.

- *Soli eravamo* ‘alone we were’ → “We were alone”: the predicate adjective moves after the verb.
- *alcun sospetto* ‘any suspicion’ → “all suspicion”: “all” replaces ‘any’ within the construction with “without”.

> 130 Per più fiate gli occhi ci sospinse  
> 131 Quella lettura, e scolorocci il viso:  

Several times that reading urged our eyes to meet, and changed the colour of our faces.

- *Per più fiate* ‘for several times’ → “Several times”: the English expresses frequency without the Italian preposition.
- *gli occhi ci sospinse Quella lettura* ‘the eyes for us impelled that reading’ → “that reading urged our eyes to meet”: the subject moves from after the verb to before it, and the object moves after the verb; the dative *ci* becomes possessive “our”. “Urged” renders *sospinse*, and “to meet” supplies the direction of the eyes’ movement, which the Italian leaves implicit.
- *e scolorocci il viso* ‘and made pale for us the face’ → “and changed the colour of our faces”: the single verb becomes a phrase that describes a change of colour without explicitly specifying pallor; the enclitic dative *ci* becomes possessive “our”, and the singular *viso* becomes plural “faces”.

## Lines 132–142 — A Kiss and a Faint

> 132 Ma solo un punto fu quel che ci vinse.  

But one moment alone it was that overcame us.

- *solo un punto* ‘only one moment’ → “one moment alone”: the limiting word moves from before the noun phrase to after it, and ‘only’ becomes “alone”.
- *fu quel che* ‘was that which’ → “it was that”: the demonstrative construction is recast as an English cleft construction, supplying “it”.
- *ci vinse* ‘us overcame’ → “overcame us”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 133 Quando leggemmo il disiato riso  
> 134 Esser baciato da cotanto amante,  

When we read how the fond smile was kissed by such a lover,

- *leggemmo il disiato riso / Esser baciato* ‘we read the desired smile to be kissed’ → “we read how the fond smile was kissed”: the noun phrase and passive infinitive become a finite clause introduced by “how”. “Fond” replaces ‘desired’ or ‘longed-for’, making the smile affectionate rather than the object of longing.
- *da cotanto amante* ‘by so great a lover’ → “by such a lover”: “such” replaces the explicit degree expressed by *cotanto*.

> 135 Questi, che mai da me non fia diviso,  
> 136 La bocca mi baciò tutto tremante:  

he, who shall never be divided from me, kissed my mouth all trembling.

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *che mai da me non fia diviso* ‘who never from me shall be divided’ → “who shall never be divided from me”: the negative adverb moves after the auxiliary, and the phrase expressing separation moves after the participle. Italian *mai … non* expresses the single negative rendered by “never”.
- *La bocca mi baciò* ‘the mouth to me kissed’ → “kissed my mouth”: the possessive dative becomes the possessive determiner “my”, replacing the definite article; the object moves after the verb.

> 137 Galeotto fu il libro, e chi lo scrisse:  

The book, and he who wrote it, was a Galeotto.

- *Galeotto fu il libro, e chi lo scrisse* ‘Galeotto was the book, and whoever it wrote’ → “The book, and he who wrote it, was a Galeotto.”: the predicate moves from the beginning to the end, and both coordinated subjects precede the verb. “a” is supplied before the predicate name; the independent relative *chi* is expanded into “he who”; and the object pronoun moves after “wrote”.

> 138 Quel giorno più non vi leggemmo avante.  

That day we read in it no farther.”

- *più non … avante* ‘no more … forward’ → “no farther”: the negative expression and the adverb of onward movement are combined into a negative comparative.
- *vi leggemmo* ‘in it we read’ → “we read in it”: the pronominal adverb becomes the prepositional phrase “in it” and moves after the verb.

> 139 Mentre che l’ uno spirto questo disse,  
> 140 L’ altro piangeva sì, che di pietade  
> 141 Io venni men così com’ io morisse;  

Whilst the one spirit thus spake, the other wept so, that I fainted with pity, as if I had been dying;

- *questo disse* ‘said this’ → “thus spake”: the demonstrative object becomes an adverb of manner, and the transitive ‘said’ becomes the intransitive “spake”.
- *piangeva* ‘was weeping’ → “wept”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past rather than an explicitly progressive construction.
- *di pietade / Io venni men* ‘from pity I came less’ → “I fainted with pity”: the idiom *venni men* is rendered by the single verb “fainted”; the causal phrase moves after the verb, and ‘from’ becomes “with”.
- *così com’ io morisse* ‘just as if I were dying’ → “as if I had been dying”: the reinforcing *così* is not separately expressed, and the imperfect subjunctive becomes a past-perfect progressive construction rather than ‘were dying’.

> 142 E caddi, come corpo morto cade.  

and fell, as a dead body falls.

- *come corpo morto cade* ‘as body dead falls’ → “as a dead body falls”: the indefinite article is supplied, and the adjective moves before the noun.

## Summary — Close Rendering and English Recasting

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s sequence of statements, repetitions, and much of its vocabulary, but regularly rearranges words to suit English syntax. Object pronouns generally follow rather than precede their verbs, adjectives move before nouns, and subjects often move before verbs. Longer phrases also change position: *al dolce nido* follows the verb and the phrase describing passage in “fly through the air to their loved nest”. Yet the translation sometimes preserves marked Italian ordering, as in “Helena I saw” and “to such torment were doomed the carnal sinners”.

Changes of vocabulary range from contextual equivalents to appreciable shifts in emphasis or imagery. *Talento*, ‘desire’, becomes the more specific “lust”; *ringhia*, ‘growls’, becomes “grins”; and *d’ ogni luce muto*, ‘mute of all light’, becomes “void of all light”, losing the metaphor of muteness. Elsewhere the English strengthens movement: *Voltando*, ‘turning’, becomes “whirling”, while *gastiga*, ‘chastises’, becomes “lashes”. Some apparent differences reflect older English usage rather than a substantial departure in meaning, notably “luxury” and “luxurious” for *lussuria* and *lussuriosa*.

Constructions are frequently recast even where the sense remains close. *Conoscitor delle peccata* becomes the compound “sin-discerner”; *Per aver pace*, ‘to have peace’, becomes “to rest”; and *tristo e pio*, ‘sad and compassionate’, becomes “with grief and pity”. Reflexive, impersonal, and passive constructions do not always retain their original form: *tutta si confessa* becomes “it confesses all”, *si legge* becomes “we read”, and *Che mi fu tolta*, ‘which was taken from me’, becomes “of which I was bereft”. Infinitival and finite clauses also exchange roles, while some Italian simple pasts become English past perfects and imperfects become simple pasts.

The English supplies words to clarify what the Italian leaves implicit: “of winds” specifies *briga*, “streams” identifies *seguaci*, and “to meet” explains the movement of the eyes. Conversely, *novelle* disappears from the question about the shades, *Là dove* is reduced to “where”, and the duration expressed by *tanto* in *tanto il tenni basso* is left to the following “until”. Throughout, English also supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending, and omits the Italian definite article before possessives, as in *il mio Dottore*, “my teacher”. These routine adjustments accompany many nearly word-for-word passages: “Love led us to one death”, for example, preserves the Italian’s vocabulary and construction with little alteration.
