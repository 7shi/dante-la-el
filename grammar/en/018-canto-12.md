# Inferno 12

## Lines 1–10 — The Rugged Descent

> 1 Era lo loco, ove a scender la riva  
> 2 Venimmo, alpestro; e, per quel ch’ ivi er’ anco,  
> 3 Tal, ch’ ogni vista ne sarebbe schiva.  

The place to which we came, in order to descend the bank, was alpine, and such, from what was there besides, that every eye would shun it.

- *Era lo loco* ‘Was the place’ → “The place” … “was”: the subject precedes the verb, and the intervening relative clause postpones the verb further.
- *ove a scender la riva / Venimmo* ‘where to descend the bank we came’ → “to which we came, in order to descend the bank”: “to which” makes the direction of movement explicit; the verb comes before the purpose clause, and “in order to” expands the purpose-marking *a*.
- *alpestro* ‘rugged’ → “alpine”: the English uses a mountain-associated adjective rather than directly describing roughness.
- *e, per quel ch’ ivi er’ anco, / Tal* ‘and, because of that which there was also, such’ → “and such, from what was there besides”: “such” moves before the explanatory phrase; “from” renders the causal *per*, “what” contracts ‘that which’, and “there” follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *ch’ ogni vista ne sarebbe schiva* ‘that every sight would be averse to it’ → “that every eye would shun it”: “eye” replaces the faculty of sight; the adjective-and-copula construction becomes the active verb “shun”, with *ne* rendered as its direct object “it”.

> 4 Qual è quella ruina, che nel fianco  
> 5 Di qua da Trento l’ Adice percosse  
> 6 O per tremuoto o per sostegno manco;  

As is the ruin, which struck the Adige in its flank, on this side Trent, caused by earthquake or by defective prop;

- *quella* ‘that’ → “the”: the demonstrative becomes a definite article.
- *che nel fianco / Di qua da Trento l’ Adice percosse* ‘which in the flank on this side of Trent the Adige struck’ → “which struck the Adige in its flank, on this side Trent”: the verb moves before its object, and both locative phrases follow them; “its” replaces the definite article before *fianco*, and the relation expressed by *da* is conveyed without ‘of’ before “Trent”.
- *O per tremuoto o per* ‘either through earthquake or through’ → “caused by earthquake or by”: “caused” is supplied to make the causal relation explicit, and the first member of the paired conjunction ‘either … or’ is omitted.
- *sostegno manco* ‘support lacking’ → “defective prop”: “prop” gives *sostegno* a concrete form, “defective” renders the insufficiency expressed by *manco*, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 7 Che da cima del monte, onde si mosse,  
> 8 Al piano, è sì la roccia discoscesa,  
> 9 Ch’ alcuna via darebbe a chi su fosse:  

for from the summit of the mountain, whence it moved, to the plain, the rock is shattered so, that it might give some passage to one that were above:

- *Che* ‘that’ → “for”: the connective is interpreted as explanatory and rendered by a coordinating conjunction.
- *da cima* ‘from summit’ → “from the summit”: English supplies a definite article.
- *onde si mosse* ‘whence it moved itself’ → “whence it moved”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive verb without a reflexive pronoun.
- *è sì la roccia discoscesa* ‘is so the rock rugged’ → “the rock is shattered so”: the subject moves before the verb, and the degree adverb follows the predicate adjective; “shattered” interprets *discoscesa* as describing broken rock rather than simply rugged or precipitous rock.
- *alcuna via* ‘some way’ → “some passage”: “passage” presents *via* as a means of getting through rather than simply a way or path.
- *darebbe* ‘would give’ → “it might give”: the conditional is rendered with “might”, emphasizing possibility.
- *a chi su fosse* ‘to whoever above were’ → “to one that were above”: the fused relative *chi* is expanded into an antecedent and relative clause, and the adverb moves after the verb.

> 10 Cotal di quel burrato era la scesa.  

such of that rocky steep was the descent.

- *burrato* ‘ravine’ → “rocky steep”: a single noun becomes an adjective-and-noun phrase, describing a steep rocky face rather than naming the hollow or chasm.

## Lines 11–24 — The Monster’s Rage and Rebuff

> 11 E in su la punta della rotta lacca  
> 12 L’ infamia di Creti era distesa,  
> 13 Che fu concetta nella falsa vacca:  

And on the top of the broken cleft lay spread the infamy of Crete, which was conceived in the false cow.

- *punta* ‘point’ → “top”: the projecting point is rendered as the uppermost part.
- *lacca* ‘hollow’ → “cleft”: a more specific word for a split or opening replaces the Italian term for a hollow or depression.
- *L’ infamia di Creti era distesa* ‘the infamy of Crete was stretched out’ → “lay spread the infamy of Crete”: “lay” replaces the copula, making posture explicit, and the subject is moved after the verb.

> 14 E quando vide noi, se stesso morse,  
> 15 Sì come quei cui l’ ira dentro fiacca.  

And when he saw us he gnawed himself, like one whom anger inwardly consumes.

- *se stesso morse* ‘himself he bit’ → “he gnawed himself”: “gnawed” replaces ‘bit’, and the reflexive object moves after the verb.
- *Sì come quei* ‘just as that one’ → “like one”: the comparison is expressed with “like”, without a separate equivalent for *sì*, and the demonstrative becomes indefinite.
- *cui l’ ira dentro fiacca* ‘whom the anger within weakens’ → “whom anger inwardly consumes”: the article before the abstract noun is omitted; “inwardly” renders the adverb of internal location, and “consumes” gives a stronger, more figurative sense than ‘weakens’.

> 16 Lo Savio mio in vêr lui gridò: Forse  
> 17 Tu credi che qui sia il Duca d’ Atene,  
> 18 Che su nel mondo la morte ti porse?  

My Sage cried towards him: “Perhaps thou thinkest the Duke of Athens may be here, who, in the world above, gave thee thy death?

- *Lo Savio mio in vêr lui gridò* ‘the Sage mine towards him cried’ → “My Sage cried towards him”: the possessive moves before the noun, without the Italian article, and the directional phrase moves after the verb.
- *Forse Tu credi che qui sia il Duca d’ Atene* ‘Perhaps thou thinkest that here is the Duke of Athens’ → “Perhaps thou thinkest the Duke of Athens may be here”: the conjunction *che* is omitted; the subordinate subject moves before its verb and “here” after it; the subjunctive *sia* is rendered with the modal “may”.
- *su nel mondo* ‘above in the world’ → “in the world above”: the indication of height moves from before the prepositional phrase to after “world”.
- *la morte ti porse* ‘death to thee offered’ → “gave thee thy death”: ‘offered’ becomes “gave”; the indirect object follows the verb and precedes the direct object; the definite article is replaced by the supplied possessive “thy”.

> 19 Partiti, bestia, chè questi non viene  
> 20 Ammaestrato dalla tua sorella,  
> 21 Ma vassi per veder le vostre pene.  

Get thee gone, Monster! For this one comes not, instructed by thy sister; but passes on to see your punishments.”

- *Partiti* ‘depart’ → “Get thee gone”: the imperative is expanded into an English idiom.
- *bestia* ‘beast’ → “Monster”: a more strongly characterizing noun replaces the ordinary word for an animal.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *non viene* ‘not comes’ → “comes not”: the negative moves after the finite verb.
- *vassi* ‘goes his way’ → “passes on”: the pronominal verb is rendered by an English verb and particle, with ‘going’ expressed as onward passage.

> 22 Qual è quel toro, che si slaccia in quella  
> 23 Che ha ricevuto già ’l colpo mortale,  
> 24 Che gir non sa, ma qua e là saltella;  

As a bull, that breaks loose, in the moment when he has received the fatal stroke, and cannot go, but plunges hither and thither;

- *Qual è quel toro* ‘As is that bull’ → “As a bull”: the copula is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes an indefinite article.
- *che si slaccia* ‘that unbinds itself’ → “that breaks loose”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive English idiom.
- *in quella Che* ‘in that [moment] in which’ → “in the moment when”: the noun understood with *quella* is supplied, the demonstrative becomes a definite article, and the temporal connection is expressed by “when”.
- *ha ricevuto già* ‘has received already’ → “he has received”: *già*, ‘already’, is omitted.
- *’l colpo mortale* ‘the blow mortal’ → “the fatal stroke”: “stroke” replaces ‘blow’, ‘mortal’ is rendered as “fatal”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Che gir non sa* ‘which does not know how to go’ → “and cannot go”: the relative clause becomes a coordinated clause, and not knowing how to move is rendered as inability to move.
- *qua e là* ‘here and there’ → “hither and thither”: adverbs of location become explicitly directional, and the phrase moves after the verb.
- *saltella* ‘hops about’ → “plunges”: the repeated little leaps suggested by the Italian verb become forceful, uncontrolled movements.

## Lines 25–36 — The Descent Over the Fallen Rocks

> 25 Vid’ io lo Minotauro far cotale.  

so I saw the Minotaur do.

- *Vid’ io lo Minotauro far cotale* ‘Saw I the Minotaur do thus’ → “so I saw the Minotaur do”: the subject moves before the verb, and the expression of manner, *cotale*, becomes “so” at the beginning rather than following “do”.

> 26 E quegli accorto gridò: Corri al varco;  
> 27 Mentre ch’ è in furia, è buon che tu ti cale.  

And my wary Guide cried: “Run to the passage. Whilst he is in fury, it is good that thou descend.”

- *quegli accorto* ‘that discerning one’ → “my wary Guide”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes the explicit noun “Guide”, “my” is supplied, and *accorto* is rendered as “wary”, preceding the noun.
- *ti cale* ‘lower thyself’ → “descend”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes a simple intransitive verb.

> 28 Così prendemmo via giù per lo scarco  
> 29 Di quelle pietre, che spesso moviensi  
> 30 Sotto i miei piedi per lo nuovo carco.  

Thus we took our way downwards on the ruin of those stones, which often moved beneath my feet, from the unusual weight.

- *prendemmo via* ‘we took way’ → “we took our way”: the possessive “our” is supplied.
- *per lo scarco* ‘through the heap’ → “on the ruin”: *per* becomes “on”, presenting the stones as the surface traversed; *scarco*, the fallen accumulation, is rendered as “ruin”.
- *moviensi* ‘moved themselves’ → “moved”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes a simple English intransitive verb.
- *lo nuovo carco* ‘the new load’ → “the unusual weight”: “unusual” interprets *nuovo* rather than reproducing ‘new’, and “weight” replaces ‘load’.

> 31 Io già pensando; e quei disse: Tu pensi  
> 32 Forse a questa rovina, ch’ è guardata  
> 33 Da quell’ ira bestial, ch’ io ora spensi.  

I went musing, and he said: “Perhaps thou art thinking of this fallen mass, guarded by that bestial rage, which I quelled just now.

- *Io già pensando* ‘I already thinking’ → “I went musing”: the elliptical Italian construction receives the finite verb “went”; *già* is omitted, and “musing” renders *pensando*.
- *Tu pensi Forse* ‘Thou thinkest perhaps’ → “Perhaps thou art thinking”: the adverb moves to the beginning, and the simple present becomes an English present progressive.
- *questa rovina* ‘this ruin’ → “this fallen mass”: the noun is expanded into an adjective and noun, making its concrete sense explicit.
- *ch’ è guardata* ‘which is guarded’ → “guarded”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase, without the relative pronoun and auxiliary.
- *quell’ ira bestial* ‘that rage bestial’ → “that bestial rage”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *ch’ io ora spensi* ‘which I now extinguished’ → “which I quelled just now”: the metaphorical ‘extinguished’ becomes “quelled”; *ora* becomes “just now” and moves after the verb.

> 34 Or vo’ che sappi, che l’ altra fiata  
> 35 Ch’ io discesi quaggiù nel basso Inferno,  
> 36 Questa roccia non era ancor cascata.  

I would have thee know, that, when I went the other time, down here to the deep Hell, this rock had not yet fallen.

- *Or vo’ che sappi* ‘Now I want that thou know’ → “I would have thee know”: *Or* is omitted; the present-tense expression of desire becomes “would have”, and the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object followed by a bare infinitive.
- *l’ altra fiata Ch’ io discesi quaggiù* ‘the other time that I descended down here’ → “when I went the other time, down here”: the temporal noun phrase and its relative clause become a clause introduced by “when”, with “the other time” following the verb. *Discesi* becomes the more general “went”, while “down here” retains the downward direction expressed by *quaggiù*.
- *nel basso Inferno* ‘in the low Hell’ → “to the deep Hell”: “to” expresses the destination of the movement, and “deep” renders *basso* rather than ‘low’.
- *non era ancor cascata* ‘was not yet fallen’ → “had not yet fallen”: Italian forms this pluperfect with ‘be’; English uses “had”, preserving the tense.

## Lines 37–51 — The Tremor and the River of Blood

> 37 Ma certo, poco pria, se ben discerno,  
> 38 Che venisse Colui, che la gran preda  
> 39 Levò a Dite del cerchio superno,  

But certainly, if I distinguish rightly, short while before He came, who took from Dis the great prey of the upmost circle,

- *poco pria* ‘a little before’ → “short while before”: the adverbial measure *poco* becomes an adjective modifying the supplied noun “while”.
- *se ben discerno* ‘if rightly I discern’ → “if I distinguish rightly”: “distinguish” replaces ‘discern’, the adverb follows the verb, and the whole clause moves before “short while before”.
- *Che venisse Colui* ‘that He should come’ → “He came”: the conjunction introducing the clause after *pria* is omitted; the imperfect subjunctive becomes an English past indicative, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *la gran preda Levò a Dite* ‘the great prey took away from Dis’ → “took from Dis the great prey”: the object moves from before the verb to after the verb and its prepositional complement; *levò* is rendered by “took” without a separate equivalent for ‘away’.
- *cerchio superno* ‘circle uppermost’ → “upmost circle”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 40 Da tutte parti l’ alta valle feda  
> 41 Tremò sì, ch’ io pensai che l’ universo  
> 42 Sentisse amor, per lo quale è chi creda  
> 43 Più volte il mondo in caos converso:  

on all sides the deep loathsome valley trembled so, that I thought the universe felt love, whereby, as some believe, the world has oft-times been converted into chaos.

- *Da tutte parti* ‘from all parts’ → “on all sides”: “on” replaces ‘from’, and “sides” replaces ‘parts’.
- *l’ alta valle feda* ‘the deep valley loathsome’ → “the deep loathsome valley”: *feda* moves before the noun.
- *Sentisse amor* ‘should feel love’ → “felt love”: the imperfect subjunctive dependent on *pensai* becomes an English past indicative.
- *per lo quale* ‘through which’ → “whereby”: the preposition and relative pronoun become a single relative adverb.
- *è chi creda* ‘there is someone who believes’ → “as some believe”: the existential construction becomes a parenthetical clause introduced by the supplied “as”; the indefinite singular becomes plural, and the subjunctive becomes indicative.
- *Più volte il mondo in caos converso* ‘several times the world into chaos converted’ → “the world has oft-times been converted into chaos”: the compressed construction dependent on *creda* becomes a finite present-perfect passive clause, with “has” and “been” supplied. “Oft-times” expresses frequency rather than a number of occurrences and moves after the first auxiliary; “into chaos” moves after the participle.

> 44 E in quel punto questa vecchia roccia  
> 45 Qui, e altrove, tal fece riverso.  

And in that moment, here, and elsewhere, this ancient rock made such downfall.

- *in quel punto* ‘at that point’ → “in that moment”: the temporal sense of *punto* is made explicit through “moment”, with a corresponding change of preposition.
- *questa vecchia roccia Qui, e altrove* ‘this old rock here, and elsewhere’ → “here, and elsewhere, this ancient rock”: “ancient” replaces ‘old’, and “here, and elsewhere” moves before the subject rather than following it.
- *tal fece riverso* ‘such made an overturning’ → “made such downfall”: the verb moves before “such”, and *riverso* is rendered as “downfall”.

> 46 Ma ficca gli occhi a valle; chè s’ approccia  
> 47 La riviera del sangue, in la qual bolle  
> 48 Qual che per violenza in altrui noccia.  

“But fix thy eyes upon the valley; for the river of blood draws nigh, in which boils every one who by violence injures others.

- *ficca gli occhi* ‘plant the eyes’ → “fix thy eyes”: “fix” replaces the more forceful *ficca*, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *a valle* ‘downward’ → “upon the valley”: an expression of downward direction becomes a prepositional phrase naming the object of the gaze.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *s’ approccia La riviera del sangue* ‘approaches the river of the blood’ → “the river of blood draws nigh”: the subject moves before the verb; “draws nigh” replaces the pronominal verb *s’approccia*, and the article before *sangue* is omitted.
- *Qual che* ‘whoever’ → “every one who”: the indefinite relative becomes an explicit antecedent followed by a relative pronoun.
- *in altrui noccia* ‘to others should do harm’ → “injures others”: the construction with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object, and the subjunctive in the indefinite relative clause becomes an English indicative.

> 49 O cieca cupidigia, o ira folle,  
> 50 Che sì ci sproni nella vita corta,  
> 51 E nell’ eterna poi sì mal c’ immolle!  

O blind cupidity! O foolish anger! which so incites us in the short life; and then, in the eternal, steeps us so bitterly.”

- *ira folle* ‘anger foolish’ → “foolish anger”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *sì ci sproni* ‘so us thou spurrest’ → “so incites us”: the metaphorical ‘spur’ becomes “incites”, the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the second-person singular address becomes third-person singular.
- *nella vita corta* ‘in the life short’ → “in the short life”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *E nell’ eterna poi* ‘and in the eternal then’ → “and then, in the eternal,”: the adverb moves before the phrase “in the eternal”.
- *sì mal c’ immolle* ‘so ill us thou steepest’ → “steeps us so bitterly”: the verb moves before its object and adverbial modifiers; ‘ill’ becomes “bitterly”, and the second-person singular address becomes third-person singular.

## Lines 52–66 — The Centaurs Challenge the Travellers

> 52 Io vidi un’ ampia fossa in arco torta,  
> 53 Come quella che tutto il piano abbraccia,  
> 54 Secondo ch’ avea detto la mia scorta:  

I saw a wide fosse bent arcwise, as embracing all the plain, according to what my Guide had told me.

- *in arco torta* ‘in an arc bent’ → “bent arcwise”: the participle comes first, and the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *Come quella che tutto il piano abbraccia* ‘like one that all the plain embraces’ → “as embracing all the plain”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun disappear, the finite verb becomes a participle, and its object follows rather than precedes it.
- *Secondo ch’* ‘according as’ → “according to what”: the conjunction becomes a preposition followed by a relative construction.
- *avea detto la mia scorta* ‘had said my escort’ → “my Guide had told me”: the subject moves before the verb; “Guide” renders *scorta* by function; and “had told me” replaces ‘had said’, supplying the recipient.

> 55 E tra il piè della ripa ed essa, in traccia  
> 56 Correan Centauri armati di saette,  
> 57 Come solean nel mondo andare a caccia.  

And between it and the foot of the bank were Centaurs, running one behind the other, armed with arrows, as they were wont on earth to go in hunting.

- *tra il piè della ripa ed essa* ‘between the foot of the bank and it’ → “between it and the foot of the bank”: the two terms governed by ‘between’ exchange places.
- *in traccia* ‘in a file’ → “one behind the other”: a compact phrase is expanded into an explicit description of the arrangement and moved after “running”.
- *Correan Centauri* ‘ran Centaurs’ → “were Centaurs, running”: the finite verb of motion becomes a participle attached to a construction introducing the Centaurs with “were”.
- *nel mondo* ‘in the world’ → “on earth”: both the noun and the preposition change.
- *a caccia* ‘to the hunt’ → “in hunting”: the Italian idiom for going hunting is rendered with “in” and an *-ing* form.

> 58 Vedendoci calar ciascun ristette,  
> 59 E della schiera tre si dipartiro  
> 60 Con archi e asticciuole prima elette:  

Perceiving us descend, they all stood still; and from the band three came forth with bows and javelins chosen first.

- *Vedendoci* ‘seeing us’ → “Perceiving us”: direct seeing is expressed by the broader verb of perception.
- *ciascun ristette* ‘each stopped’ → “they all stood still”: the distributive singular becomes a collective plural, and stopping is expressed as standing still.
- *si dipartiro* ‘departed’ → “came forth”: the pronominal verb of departure becomes a phrase presenting the movement as emergence.
- *asticciuole* ‘little shafts’ → “javelins”: the translation identifies the weapons as javelins rather than small shafts or arrows, without expressing the diminutive.
- *prima elette* ‘beforehand chosen’ → “chosen first”: the participle precedes the adverb, and “first” renders the prior selection expressed by ‘beforehand’.

> 61 E l’ un gridò da lungi: A qual martiro  
> 62 Venite voi, che scendete la costa?  
> 63 Ditel costinci, se non, l’ arco tiro.  

And one of them cried from far: “To what torment come ye, ye that descend the coast? Tell from thence: if not, I draw the bow.”

- *l’ un* ‘the one’ → “one of them”: the definite expression becomes an indefinite one with an explicit partitive complement.
- *Venite voi, che* ‘come you, who’ → “come ye, ye that”: the translation repeats the personal pronoun before the relative clause.
- *la costa* ‘the slope’ → “the coast”: “coast” is used in its older sense of a hillside or slope.
- *Ditel* ‘say it’ → “Tell”: ‘say’ becomes “Tell”, and the attached object pronoun is omitted.
- *l’ arco tiro* ‘the bow I draw’ → “I draw the bow”: the object moves from before the verb to after it.

> 64 Lo mio Maestro disse: La risposta  
> 65 Farem noi a Chiron costà di presso:  
> 66 Mal fu la voglia tua sempre sì tosta.  

My Master said: “Our answer we will make to Chiron, there near at hand. Unhappily thy will was always thus rash.”

- *La risposta* ‘the answer’ → “Our answer”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Farem noi* ‘will make we’ → “we will make”: the expressed subject moves before the verb.
- *Mal* ‘ill’ → “Unhappily”: the translation interprets the adverb as marking an unfortunate circumstance.
- *fu la voglia tua* ‘was the will thy’ → “thy will was”: the subject moves before the verb, and the possessive moves before its noun.
- *sì* ‘so’ → “thus”: an expression of degree becomes one of manner.
- *tosta* ‘hard’ → “rash”: the adjective’s figurative force is rendered as rashness rather than hardness or stubbornness.

## Lines 67–81 — The Centaurs Notice Dante

> 67 Poi mi tentò, e disse: Quegli è Nesso,  
> 68 Che morì per la bella Deianira,  
> 69 E fe’ di sè la vendetta egli stesso.  

Then he touched me and said: “That is Nessus, who died for the fair Dejanira, and of himself took vengeance for himself.

- *Poi mi tentò* ‘then me he touched’ → “Then he touched me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *fe’ di sè la vendetta egli stesso* ‘made of himself the vengeance he himself’ → “of himself took vengeance for himself”: *fare vendetta* becomes “took vengeance,” without the article; *di sè* becomes “for himself” and follows “vengeance”; the emphatic subject *egli stesso* becomes the adverbial “of himself,” placed before the verb.

> 70 E quel di mezzo, che al petto si mira,  
> 71 E’ il gran Chirone, il qual nudrio Achille:  

He in the middle, who is looking down upon his breast, is the great Chiron, he who nursed Achilles.

- *E quel di mezzo* ‘and that one in the middle’ → “He in the middle”: the conjunction is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *al petto si mira* ‘at the breast he looks at himself’ → “is looking down upon his breast”: the simple present becomes progressive; “down” is supplied; the reflexive construction becomes a possessive expression, “his breast”; and the phrase naming the breast moves after the verb.
- *il qual* ‘who’ → “he who”: “he” is added before the relative pronoun.
- *nudrio* ‘nourished’ → “nursed”: nourishment or upbringing is expressed through the more particular verb “nursed.”

> 72 Quell’ altro è Folo, che fu sì pien d’ ira.  

That other is Pholus, who was so full of rage.

- Literal.

> 73 Dintorno al fosso vanno a mille a mille,  
> 74 Saettando qual’ anima si svelle  
> 75 Del sangue più che sua colpa sortille.  

Around the fosse they go by thousands, piercing with their arrows whatever spirit wrenches itself out of the blood farther than its guilt has allotted for it.”

- *a mille a mille* ‘a thousand at a thousand’ → “by thousands”: the repeated singular numeral expressing successive groups becomes a plural expression of number.
- *Saettando* ‘shooting arrows at’ → “piercing with their arrows”: the verb becomes a phrase naming the weapon, with “their” supplied; “piercing” makes the arrows’ penetration explicit rather than merely their discharge.
- *anima* ‘soul’ → “spirit”: a different noun renders the Italian.
- *si svelle Del sangue* ‘itself wrenches from the blood’ → “wrenches itself out of the blood”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb, and “out of” expresses emergence more explicitly than ‘from.’
- *più* ‘more’ → “farther”: the comparative is made specifically spatial.
- *sortille* ‘allotted to it’ → “has allotted for it”: the simple past becomes present perfect, and the attached indirect-object pronoun is rendered by “for it” rather than ‘to it.’

> 76 Noi ci appressammo a quelle fiere snelle:  

We drew near those rapid beasts.

- *ci appressammo a* ‘ourselves drew near to’ → “drew near”: the Italian pronominal construction has no expressed reflexive counterpart, and the preposition before the object is omitted.
- *fiere snelle* ‘beasts nimble’ → “rapid beasts”: the adjective moves before the noun, and nimbleness is rendered as speed.

> 77 Chiron prese uno strale, e con la cocca  
> 78 Fece la barba indietro alle mascelle.  

Chiron took an arrow, and with the notch put back his beard upon his jaws.

- *cocca* ‘nock’ → “notch”: the technical name for the arrow’s notched end becomes the more general name for its indentation.
- *Fece la barba indietro alle mascelle* ‘made the beard back to the jaws’ → “put back his beard upon his jaws”: *fece … indietro* becomes the idiomatic “put back,” with “back” moved before the object; the articles before both body parts become possessives; and ‘to’ becomes “upon.”

> 79 Quando s’ ebbe scoperta la gran bocca,  
> 80 Disse ai compagni: Siete voi accorti,  
> 81 Che quel di rietro move ciò ch’ ei tocca?  

When he had uncovered his great mouth, he said to his companions: “Have ye perceived that the one behind moves what he touches?

- *s’ ebbe scoperta la gran bocca* ‘had uncovered for himself the great mouth’ → “he had uncovered his great mouth”: the reflexive construction is conveyed through the possessive “his,” replacing the article before “mouth.”
- *ai compagni* ‘to the companions’ → “to his companions”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Siete voi accorti* ‘are you aware’ → “Have ye perceived”: a present state of awareness becomes a present-perfect act of perception.
- *quel* ‘that one’ → “the one”: the demonstrative becomes a definite description.
- *ciò ch’* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun become a single fused relative.

## Lines 82–96 — A Living Traveller Needs a Guide

> 82 Così non soglion fare i piè de’ morti.  

The feet of the dead are not wont to do so.”

- *Così non soglion fare i piè de’ morti* ‘So are not wont to do the feet of the dead’ → “The feet of the dead are not wont to do so”: the postverbal subject moves to the beginning, and the opening adverb moves to the end.

> 83 E il mio buon Duca, che già gli era al petto,  
> 84 Ove le due nature son consorti,  

And my good Guide, who was already at the breast of him, where the two natures are consorted,

- *Duca* ‘leader’ → “Guide”: leadership is expressed more specifically as guidance.
- *già gli era al petto* ‘already was at his breast’ → “was already at the breast of him”: the adverb follows the verb, and the possessive relationship expressed by the Italian dative pronoun becomes an explicit “of him” phrase.
- *son consorti* ‘are consorts’ → “are consorted”: a description of partnership becomes a participial expression of union.

> 85 Rispose: Ben è vivo; e sì soletto  
> 86 Mostrarli mi convien la valle buia:  

replied: “Indeed he is alive, and solitary thus have I to shew him the dark valley.

- *e sì soletto* ‘and so all alone’ → “and solitary thus”: the adjective precedes the adverb, and the emphatic diminutive in *soletto* is not separately expressed.
- *Mostrarli mi convien* ‘to show him is necessary for me’ → “have I to shew him”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal expression of obligation, with inverted subject and auxiliary.
- *la valle buia* ‘the valley dark’ → “the dark valley”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 87 Necessità il c’ induce, e non diletto.  

Necessity brings him to it, and not sport.

- *Necessità il c’ induce* ‘Necessity him there brings’ → “Necessity brings him to it”: the object follows the verb, and the locative pronoun becomes “to it” rather than ‘there’.
- *diletto* ‘pleasure’ → “sport”: enjoyment is rendered as recreation or amusement.

> 88 Tal si partì da cantare alleluia,  
> 89 Che mi commise quest’ uficio nuovo;  
> 90 Non è ladron, nè io anima fuia.  

From singing Alleluiah, came She who gave me this new office. He is no robber, nor I a thievish spirit.

- *Tal si partì da cantare alleluia* ‘Such a one departed from singing alleluia’ → “From singing Alleluiah, came She”: the source phrase moves to the beginning; departure becomes arrival; and the indefinite, grammatically ungendered designation becomes an explicitly feminine pronoun.
- *mi commise* ‘to me entrusted’ → “gave me”: entrusting becomes giving, and the recipient follows the verb.
- *quest’ uficio nuovo* ‘this office new’ → “this new office”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Non è ladron* ‘He is not a robber’ → “He is no robber”: negation of the verb becomes a negative determiner before the noun.
- *anima* ‘soul’ → “spirit”: a different noun renders the designation of the immaterial being.

> 91 Ma per quella virtù, per cui io muovo  
> 92 Li passi miei per sì selvaggia strada,  
> 93 Danne un de’ tuoi, a cui noi siamo a pruovo,  

But by that virtue through which I move my steps on such a woody way, give us some one of thine whom we may follow,

- *per cui* ‘by which’ → “through which”: the preposition expressing agency or means is changed.
- *Li passi miei* ‘the steps mine’ → “my steps”: the possessive moves before the noun, with the article omitted.
- *per sì selvaggia strada* ‘along so wild a road’ → “on such a woody way”: the preposition of movement becomes “on”; the degree construction becomes “such a”; and ‘wild’ is interpreted more narrowly as “woody”.
- *un de’ tuoi* ‘one of yours’ → “some one of thine”: “some” makes the indefinite selection explicit.
- *a cui noi siamo a pruovo* ‘to whom we may be close’ → “whom we may follow”: being near someone is interpreted as following him; the prepositional relative becomes the direct object of the new verb.

> 94 Che ne dimostri là ove si guada,  
> 95 E che porti costui in su la groppa,  
> 96 Chè non è spirto che per l’ aer vada.  

that he may shew us where the ford is, and carry over him upon his back, for he is not a spirit to go through the air.”

- *Che ne dimostri* ‘who may show us’ → “that he may shew us”: the relative clause becomes an explicit purpose clause.
- *là ove si guada* ‘there where one fords’ → “where the ford is”: the separate ‘there’ disappears, and an impersonal verb describing an action becomes a noun with a verb expressing location.
- *E che porti* ‘and who may carry’ → “and carry over”: the repeated relative construction becomes a coordinated verb sharing the preceding subject and modal; “over” is supplied to specify crossing.
- *costui* ‘this one’ → “him”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *in su la groppa* ‘upon the rump’ → “upon his back”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the specific anatomical term is generalized.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *che per l’ aer vada* ‘that through the air may go’ → “to go through the air”: the subjunctive relative clause becomes an infinitive phrase, and the phrase expressing the path moves after the verb.

## Lines 97–108 — Tyrants in the Boiling Blood

> 97 Chiron si volse in sulla destra poppa,  
> 98 E disse a Nesso: Torna, e sì li guida;  
> 99 E fa cansar, s’ altra schiera v’ intoppa.  

Chiron bent round on his right breast, and said to Nessus: “Turn, and guide them then; and if another troop encounter you, keep it off.”

- *si volse* ‘turned himself’ → “bent round”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb with an adverb, and turning is expressed as bending round.
- *in sulla destra poppa* ‘on the right breast’ → “on his right breast”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Torna* ‘Turn back’ → “Turn”: the sense of returning is not separately expressed.
- *e sì li guida* ‘and thus them guide’ → “and guide them then”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, while *sì* becomes “then” rather than ‘thus’ and moves to the end.
- *E fa cansar, s’ altra schiera v’ intoppa* ‘And make withdraw, if another troop encounters you’ → “and if another troop encounter you, keep it off”: the conditional clause moves before the command, and its object follows its verb. The causative command becomes “keep it off,” expressing prevention of approach rather than withdrawal and supplying an explicit object. “Encounter” uses the English subjunctive where the Italian has an indicative.

> 100 Noi ci movemmo colla scorta fida  
> 101 Lungo la proda del bollor vermiglio,  
> 102 Ove i bolliti faceano alte strida.  

We moved onwards with our trusty guide, along the border of the purple boiling, wherein the boiled were making loud shrieks.

- *Noi ci movemmo* ‘We moved ourselves’ → “We moved onwards”: the reflexive pronoun disappears, and “onwards” makes forward movement explicit.
- *colla scorta fida* ‘with the escort faithful’ → “with our trusty guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive; ‘escort’ becomes “guide,” and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *bollor vermiglio* ‘boiling vermilion’ → “purple boiling”: the colour changes from vermilion to purple, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Ove* ‘where’ → “wherein”: the English explicitly expresses being within the boiling.
- *faceano alte strida* ‘made high shrieks’ → “were making loud shrieks”: the imperfect becomes an English past progressive; ‘high’ is rendered as “loud,” and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 103 Io vidi gente sotto infino al ciglio;  

I saw people down in it even to the eyebrows;

- *sotto* ‘below’ → “down in it”: the English supplies an explicit reference to the substance containing the people.
- *infino al ciglio* ‘as far as the eyebrow’ → “even to the eyebrows”: the limit is expressed with “even to,” and the singular becomes plural.

> 104 E il gran Centauro disse: Ei son tiranni,  
> 105 Che dier nel sangue e nell’ aver di piglio.  

and the great Centaur said: “These are tyrants who took to blood and plunder.

- *Ei* ‘They’ → “These”: the personal pronoun becomes a demonstrative.
- *dier nel sangue e nell’ aver di piglio* ‘gave a clutch at blood and at possessions’ → “took to blood and plunder”: the Italian idiom for laying hold of something becomes “took to,” suggesting habitual engagement. Possessions become “plunder,” shifting from property to its violent acquisition; the repeated preposition and articles are not retained.

> 106 Quivi si piangon li spietati danni:  

Here they lament their merciless offences.

- *si piangon* ‘are lamented’ → “they lament”: the passive construction becomes active, with the lamenters supplied as its subject and the things lamented becoming its object.
- *li spietati danni* ‘the merciless injuries’ → “their merciless offences”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the injuries inflicted are recast as offences committed.

> 107 Quivi è Alessandro, e Dionisio fero,  
> 108 Che fe’ Cicilia aver dolorosi anni:  

Here is Alexander; and fierce Dionysius, who made Sicily have years of woe.

- *Dionisio fero* ‘Dionysius fierce’ → “fierce Dionysius”: the adjective moves before the name.
- *dolorosi anni* ‘sorrowful years’ → “years of woe”: the adjective becomes a noun in a following prepositional phrase.

## Lines 109–114 — Azzolino, Obizzo, and the Guide

> 109 E quella fronte che ha il pel così nero  
> 110 E’ Azzolino; e quell’ altro, ch’ è biondo,  
> 111 E’ Obizzo da Esti, il qual per vero  
> 112 Fu spento dal figliastro su nel mondo.  

And that brow which has the hair so black is Azzolino; and that other, who is blonde, is Obizzo of Este, who in verity was quenched by his step-son up in the world.”

- *per vero* ‘for true’ → “in verity”: the Italian adverbial idiom becomes an English prepositional phrase with the noun “verity”.
- *dal figliastro* ‘by the stepson’ → “by his step-son”: the definite article is replaced by a possessive.

> 113 Allor mi volsi al Poeta; e quei disse:  

Then I turned me to the Poet, and he said:

- *mi volsi* ‘myself I turned’ → “I turned me”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb and takes the archaic reflexive form “me”.
- *quei* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.

> 114 Questi ti sia or primo, ed io secondo.  

“Let him be chief guide to thee now, and me second.”

- *Questi ti sia or primo* ‘this one to thee be now first’ → “Let him be chief guide to thee now”: the subjunctive expressing an injunction becomes a construction with “Let”; the demonstrative becomes the personal pronoun “him”, in the object form required by that construction. ‘first’ is interpreted as “chief guide”, supplying the noun; “to thee” moves after the complement, and “now” moves to the end of the clause.
- *ed io secondo* ‘and I second’ → “and me second”: the subject pronoun becomes the object form “me”, continuing the construction with “Let” understood.

## Lines 115–126 — Passage Through the Shallowing Blood

> 115 Poco più oltre il Centauro s’ affisse  
> 116 Sovra una gente, che infino alla gola  
> 117 Parea che di quel bulicame uscisse.  

A little farther on, the Centaur paused beside a people which, as far as the throat, seemed to issue from that boiling stream.

- *s’ affisse* ‘fixed himself’ → “paused”: the reflexive expression becomes an intransitive verb of stopping.
- *Sovra* ‘above’ → “beside”: the spatial relation is changed.
- *che infino alla gola Parea che di quel bulicame uscisse* ‘which, up to the throat, it seemed was issuing from that boiling pool’ → “which, as far as the throat, seemed to issue from that boiling stream”: the construction with *parea che* and a finite subjunctive becomes “seemed” followed by an infinitive; the phrase identifying where the people issue from moves after the verb. *Bulicame* is rendered as a “boiling stream” rather than a boiling pool.

> 118 Mostrocci un’ ombra dall’ un canto sola,  
> 119 Dicendo: Colui fesse, in grembo a Dio,  
> 120 Lo cuor che in sul Tamigi ancor si cola.  

He shewed us a spirit by itself apart, saying: “That one, in God’s bosom, pierced the heart which still is venerated on the Thames.”

- *un’ ombra* ‘a shade’ → “a spirit”: a different term is used for the disembodied figure.
- *dall’ un canto sola* ‘on one side alone’ → “by itself apart”: “by itself” renders *sola* before the spatial expression; “apart” replaces the explicit reference to one side.
- *fesse* ‘cleft’ → “pierced”: splitting or cleaving is rendered as piercing.
- *in grembo a Dio* ‘in the lap of God’ → “in God’s bosom”: “bosom” replaces ‘lap’, the relation to God becomes an English possessive, and the phrase moves before the verb.
- *che in sul Tamigi ancor si cola* ‘which on the Thames still is revered’ → “which still is venerated on the Thames”: the Thames phrase moves after the verb. The translation takes *si cola* as a passive expression of reverence, rather than as an expression of flowing or dripping.

> 121 Poi vidi genti, che di fuor del rio  
> 122 Tenean la testa e ancor tutto il casso:  
> 123 E di costoro assai riconobb’ io.  

Then some I saw, who kept the head and likewise all the chest out of the river; and of these I recognised many.

- *vidi genti* ‘I saw people’ → “some I saw”: the noun becomes an indefinite pronoun, and the object moves before the subject and verb.
- *di fuor del rio* ‘outside the river’ → “out of the river”: the spatial phrase moves from before the verb to after its objects.
- *Tenean* ‘held’ → “kept”: the verb is rendered as maintaining a position.
- *assai riconobb’ io* ‘many recognised I’ → “I recognised many”: object–verb–subject order becomes subject–verb–object.

> 124 Così a più a più si facea basso  
> 125 Quel sangue sì, che copria pur li piedi:  
> 126 E quivi fu del fosso il nostro passo.  

Thus more and more that blood grew shallow, until it covered the feet only: and here was our passage through the fosse.

- *si facea basso Quel sangue* ‘made itself low that blood’ → “that blood grew shallow”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive expression of change; ‘low’ becomes “shallow”, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *sì, che* ‘so that’ → “until”: an expression of degree and consequence becomes one marking an endpoint.
- *pur li piedi* ‘only the feet’ → “the feet only”: the limiting word moves after the noun.
- *quivi* ‘there’ → “here”: the spatial reference shifts to the travellers’ immediate position.
- *del fosso il nostro passo* ‘of the fosse our passage’ → “our passage through the fosse”: the modifying phrase moves after the noun, and *di* is rendered as “through”, making the relation explicitly one of traversal.

## Lines 127–139 — The Stream’s Depth and Torments

> 127 Sì come tu da questa parte vedi  
> 128 Lo bulicame che sempre si scema,  
> 129 Disse il Centauro, voglio che tu credi,  

“As thou seest the boiling stream, on this side, continually diminish,” said the Centaur, “so I would have thee to believe

- *Sì come tu da questa parte vedi / Lo bulicame* ‘As thou on this side seest the boiling pool’ → “As thou seest the boiling stream, on this side” … “so”: the comparison is expanded into an explicit English correlative construction; the phrase indicating place moves from before the verb of seeing to after its object, and “stream” replaces the Italian noun’s sense of a bubbling or boiling pool.
- *che sempre si scema* ‘which always diminishes itself’ → “continually diminish”: the finite relative clause becomes a bare infinitive after “seest”; the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, and “continually” replaces ‘always’.
- *voglio che tu credi* ‘I want that thou believe’ → “I would have thee to believe”: the direct expression of wanting becomes “would have,” and the finite subordinate clause becomes an object pronoun followed by an infinitive.

> 130 Che da quest’ altra a più a più giù prema  
> 131 Lo fondo suo, infin che si raggiunge  
> 132 Ove la tirannia convien che gema.  

that, on this other, it lowers its bottom more and more, till it comes again to where tyranny is doomed to mourn.

- *a più a più giù prema / Lo fondo suo* ‘more and more downward presses its bottom’ → “it lowers its bottom more and more”: the phrase indicating degree moves from before the verb to after its object. The Italian makes the bottom the subject of downward movement; the English makes the stream the subject and its bottom the object. “Lowers” combines the downward direction with the verb.
- *infin che si raggiunge / Ove* ‘until it joins up where’ → “till it comes again to where”: the verb is rendered by a phrase of motion, with “again” explicitly expressing return; the Italian reflexive construction is not retained.
- *la tirannia* ‘the tyranny’ → “tyranny”: the article before the abstract noun is omitted.
- *convien che gema* ‘it is necessary that [it] groan’ → “is doomed to mourn”: an impersonal expression of necessity followed by a finite clause becomes a personal passive followed by an infinitive; ‘groan’ becomes “mourn”.

> 133 La divina giustizia di qua punge  
> 134 Quell’ Attila che fu flagello in terra,  

Divine Justice here torments that Attila, who was a scourge on earth;

- *La divina giustizia* ‘the divine justice’ → “Divine Justice”: the definite article is omitted.
- *di qua* ‘on this side’ → “here”: the reference to a particular side becomes a general indication of place.
- *punge* ‘stings’ → “torments”: the specific action is replaced by a broader term for causing suffering.
- *flagello* ‘scourge’ → “a scourge”: English supplies an indefinite article before the singular predicate noun.

> 135 E Pirro, e Sesto; ed in eterno munge  
> 136 Le lagrime, che col bollor disserra,  
> 137 A Rinier da Corneto, a Rinier Pazzo,  
> 138 Che fecero alle strade tanta guerra.  

and Pyrrhus and Sextus; and to eternity milks tears, which by the boiling it unlocks, from Rinier of Corneto, from Rinier Pazzo, who on the highways made so much war.”

- *in eterno* ‘eternally’ → “to eternity”: the adverbial expression is rendered as a prepositional phrase.
- *Le lagrime* ‘the tears’ → “tears”: the definite article is omitted.
- *col bollor* ‘with the boiling’ → “by the boiling”: “by” makes the instrumental force of *con* explicit.
- *A Rinier da Corneto, a Rinier Pazzo* ‘to Rinier from Corneto, to Rinier Pazzo’ → “from Rinier of Corneto, from Rinier Pazzo”: the Italian datives become English phrases marking the source of what is milked; *da* in the first name becomes “of” rather than ‘from’.
- *alle strade* ‘to the roads’ → “on the highways”: the Italian construction presents the roads as the target of warfare, whereas the English presents them as its location. “Highways” specifies the kind of roads, and the phrase moves from after the verb to before it.

> 139 Poi si rivolse, e ripassossi il guazzo.  

Then he turned back, and by himself repassed the ford.

- *si rivolse* ‘turned himself back’ → “he turned back”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive English verb.
- *ripassossi* ‘recrossed’ → “by himself repassed”: the attached reflexive *si* is interpreted as indicating that the crossing was made alone and is given the separate phrase “by himself”.

## Summary — Close Wording, Reshaped Syntax

Carlyle keeps much of the Italian vocabulary and imagery while frequently rearranging its syntax for English prose. Subjects and objects move into more usual English positions: *Era lo loco* becomes “The place” … “was”, and *l’arco tiro* becomes “I draw the bow”. Adjectives generally move before their nouns, as in *ira folle*, “foolish anger”. Yet the translation also preserves conspicuously Italian arrangements, including “such of that rocky steep was the descent”, as well as compact figurative expressions such as “bestial rage” and “milks tears”. Its closeness is therefore selective rather than uniformly word-for-word.

Changes of construction recur throughout. Italian pronominal verbs often become simple English intransitives: *si rivolse* becomes “he turned back”. Finite clauses may be compressed into participles or infinitives, as *ch’è guardata* becomes “guarded” and *che per l’aer vada* becomes “to go through the air”. Elsewhere Carlyle expands compressed wording: *è chi creda*, ‘there is someone who believes’, becomes “as some believe”, while the following clause receives the explicit auxiliaries “has” and “been”. Impersonal necessity becomes personal obligation in *Mostrarli mi convien*, “have I to shew him”; the passive *si piangon* becomes the active “they lament”. Italian subjunctives are variously represented by English indicatives, modals, or infinitival constructions rather than by one consistent grammatical equivalent.

Lexical choices range from close correspondences to interpretation and shifts of emphasis. *Alpestro*, ‘rugged’, becomes “alpine”; *nuovo carco*, ‘new load’, becomes “unusual weight”; and *selvaggia*, ‘wild’, becomes the narrower “woody”. Some choices intensify or specify an action: *morse*, ‘bit’, becomes “gnawed”, while *saettando*, ‘shooting arrows at’, becomes “piercing with their arrows”. Others alter the description more noticeably, as *vermiglio* becomes “purple”. Expansions can identify what the Italian leaves implicit: *quegli accorto* becomes “my wary Guide”, and *primo* becomes “chief guide”.

Small omissions and additions also help shape the prose. Carlyle sometimes leaves out *già* or *or*, supplies causal clarification through “caused by”, and makes direction explicit with words such as “onwards” and “over”. Definite articles may become possessives, especially with body parts, as *gli occhi* becomes “thy eyes”. Beyond the changes individually recorded in the comparison, English regularly supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending alone, and omits Italian articles before possessives: *Lo mio Maestro* becomes “My Master”. Taken together, these adjustments produce a translation that remains closely tied to the Italian’s phrasing and figures, but repeatedly clarifies, expands, and reorganizes them into English prose.
