# Inferno 9

## Lines 1–9 — Waiting for Help

> 1 Quel color che viltà di fuor mi pinse,  
> 2 Veggendo il Duca mio tornare in volta,  
> 3 Più tosto dentro il suo nuovo ristrinse.  

That colour which cowardice painted on my face, when I saw my Guide turn back, repressed in him more quickly his new colour.

- *di fuor mi pinse* ‘on the outside painted me’ → “painted on my face”: the outward appearance is specified as the face, *mi* becomes possessive “my”, and the phrase describing where the colour appears follows the verb.
- *Veggendo* ‘seeing’ → “when I saw”: the participle becomes a finite past-tense clause with an explicit temporal conjunction.
- *il Duca mio* ‘my leader’ → “my Guide”: “Guide” replaces ‘leader’, and the possessive, which follows the Italian noun, precedes the English noun.
- *tornare in volta* ‘turn in retreat’ → “turn back”: the Italian expression is condensed into an English phrasal verb.
- *Più tosto dentro il suo nuovo ristrinse* ‘more quickly within his new [one] drew in’ → “repressed in him more quickly his new colour”: the adverbial phrase moves from before the verb to after it. The translation explicitly supplies the person within whom the change occurs. The noun understood in Italian is supplied. The verb is rendered as suppression rather than drawing inward, and moves from after its object to before it.

> 4 Attento si fermò, com’ uom che ascolta;  
> 5 Chè l’ occhio nol potea menare a lunga  
> 6 Per l’ aer nero, e per la nebbia folta.  

He stopped attentive, like one who listens; for his eye could not lead him far, through the black air and the dense fog.

- *Attento si fermò* ‘attentive he stopped himself’ → “He stopped attentive”: the pronominal verb becomes intransitive “stopped”, and the adjective moves after the verb.
- *com’ uom che ascolta* ‘like a man who listens’ → “like one who listens”: the noun ‘man’ becomes the indefinite pronoun “one”.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes coordinating “for”.
- *l’ occhio* ‘the eye’ → “his eye”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Per l’ aer nero, e per la nebbia folta* ‘through the air black, and through the fog dense’ → “through the black air and the dense fog”: both adjectives move before their nouns, and the repeated preposition is omitted.

> 7 Pure a noi converrà vincer la punga,  
> 8 Cominciò ei: se non . . . tal ne s’ offerse.  

“Yet it behoves us to gain this battle,” he began. “If not . . . . such help was offered to us.

- *a noi converrà* ‘to us it will be necessary’ → “it behoves us”: the future becomes present, the impersonal construction is recast with “behoves”, and the prepositional phrase becomes the verb’s object.
- *vincer la punga* ‘to win the battle’ → “to gain this battle”: “gain” renders ‘win’, and the definite article becomes the demonstrative “this”.
- *Cominciò ei* ‘began he’ → “he began”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *tal ne s’ offerse* ‘such a one offered himself to us’ → “such help was offered to us”: Carlyle interprets *tal* as unspecified help rather than a person, supplying “help”, and renders *s’ offerse* passively rather than reflexively.

> 9 Oh quanto tarda a me ch’ altri qui giunga!  

Oh! how long to me it seems till some one come!”

- *quanto tarda a me* ‘how much it delays to me’ → “how long to me it seems”: the verb expressing delay becomes a construction expressing apparent duration, with “seems” supplied.
- *ch’ altri qui giunga* ‘that someone here should arrive’ → “till some one come”: the clause introduced by ‘that’ becomes a temporal clause introduced by “till”, and *qui*, ‘here’, is omitted.

## Lines 10–21 — Fear and a Question About Descent

> 10 Io vidi ben, sì com’ ei ricoperse  
> 11 Lo cominciar con l’ altro, che poi venne,  
> 12 Che fur parole alle prime diverse.  

I saw well how he covered the beginning with the other that came after, which were words differing from the first.

- *sì com’* ‘just as’ → “how”: the manner construction is recast, without a separate equivalent for the intensifying *sì*.
- *che poi venne* ‘that afterwards came’ → “that came after”: the temporal adverb follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *parole alle prime diverse* ‘words from the first different’ → “words differing from the first”: the adjective becomes a participle and precedes its complement rather than following it.

> 13 Ma nondimen paura il suo dir dienne,  

But not the less his language gave me fear;

- *paura il suo dir dienne* ‘fear his speaking gave us’ → “his language gave me fear”: the object moves from the beginning to the end of the clause; the substantivized infinitive becomes “language”; and the enclitic *ne* is interpreted in the singular sense, “me”.

> 14 Perch’ io traeva la parola tronca  
> 15 Forse a peggior sentenzia, ch’ ei non tenne.  

for perhaps I drew his broken speech to a worse meaning than he held.

- *Perch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *io traeva la parola tronca Forse* ‘I was drawing the word broken off perhaps’ → “perhaps I drew his broken speech”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past; the definite article becomes a possessive, “speech” replaces ‘word’, and the adjective precedes the noun; the adverb moves from after the object to before the subject.
- *a peggior sentenzia* ‘to worse meaning’ → “to a worse meaning”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *ch’ ei non tenne* ‘than he did not hold’ → “than he held”: the Italian comparative construction’s non-negative, pleonastic *non* is omitted.

> 16 In questo fondo della trista conca  
> 17 Discende mai alcun del primo grado,  
> 18 Che sol per pena ha la speranza cionca?  

“Into this bottom of the dreary shell, does any ever descend from the first degree, whose only punishment is hope cut off?”

- *In questo fondo* ‘in this bottom’ → “Into this bottom”: English makes the direction of movement explicit.
- *conca* ‘basin’ → “shell”: a different noun renders the concave shape.
- *Discende mai alcun* ‘descends ever anyone’ → “does any ever descend”: English supplies interrogative “does”, puts the subject before the main verb, and places “ever” between them.
- *del primo grado* ‘of the first degree’ → “from the first degree”: affiliation is expressed as a point of departure.
- *Che sol per pena ha la speranza cionca* ‘who only as punishment has the hope cut off’ → “whose only punishment is hope cut off”: the subject relative becomes possessive; ‘has … as punishment’ becomes “punishment is”; “only” directly modifies “punishment”; and the article before ‘hope’ is omitted.

> 19 Questa question fec’ io; e quei: Di rado  
> 20 Incontra, mi rispose, che di nui  
> 21 Faccia il cammino alcun per quale io vado.  

This question I made, and he replied to me: “Rarely it occurs that any of us makes this journey on which I go.

- *Questa question fec’ io* ‘this question made I’ → “This question I made”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *e quei … mi rispose* ‘and that one … to me replied’ → “and he replied to me”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun, the reporting clause moves before the quoted speech, and the indirect object follows the verb.
- *Di rado Incontra* ‘rarely occurs’ → “Rarely it occurs”: English supplies anticipatory “it”, referring forward to the following clause.
- *di nui … alcun* ‘of us … anyone’ → “any of us”: the separated words are brought together, their order is reversed, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *Faccia il cammino* ‘make the journey’ → “makes this journey”: the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative, and the definite article becomes a demonstrative.
- *per quale io vado* ‘by which I go’ → “on which I go”: the preposition expressing the route is rendered by English “on”.

## Lines 22–34 — Virgil’s Earlier Descent

> 22 Vero è che altra fiata quaggiù fui  
> 23 Congiurato da quella Eriton cruda,  
> 24 Che richiamava l’ ombre a’ corpi sui.  

It is true, that once before I was down here, conjured by that fell Erichtho, who recalled the shadows to their bodies.

- *Vero è* ‘true it is’ → “It is true”: the predicate adjective moves after the verb, and an impersonal subject is supplied.
- *altra fiata* ‘another time’ → “once before”: the indefinite expression becomes an explicit reference to one earlier occasion.
- *quaggiù fui* ‘down here I was’ → “I was down here”: the adverb follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *quella Eriton cruda* ‘that Erichtho cruel’ → “that fell Erichtho”: “fell” renders *cruda*, and the adjective moves before the name.
- *a’ corpi sui* ‘to the bodies their’ → “to their bodies”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 25 Di poco era di me la carne nuda,  
> 26 Ch’ ella mi fece entrar dentro a quel muro,  
> 27 Per trarne un spirto del cerchio di Giuda.  

My flesh had been but short time divested of me, when she made me enter within that wall, to draw out a spirit from the Circle of Judas.

- *Di poco* ‘for a little time’ → “but short time”: the preposition is omitted, the implicit noun “time” is supplied, and “but” explicitly marks the shortness of the interval.
- *era* ‘was’ → “had been”: the imperfect becomes a pluperfect, marking anteriority to the following action.
- *di me … nuda* ‘of me … bare’ → “divested of me”: an adjective becomes a participle, and its complement follows it rather than preceding it.
- *la carne* ‘the flesh’ → “My flesh”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Ch’* ‘that’ → “when”: the temporal relation expressed by *che* after the preceding duration is made explicit.
- *ella mi fece entrar* ‘she me made enter’ → “she made me enter”: the object pronoun moves after the causative verb.
- *Per trarne* ‘to draw from there’ → “to draw out”: the locative pronoun *ne* becomes the directional particle “out”, leaving ‘there’ unexpressed.
- *del cerchio di Giuda* ‘of the Circle of Judas’ → “from the Circle of Judas”: the phrase is rendered as the source of the drawing-out rather than as the spirit’s affiliation.

> 28 Quell’ è il più basso loco, e il più oscuro,  
> 29 E il più lontan dal Ciel che tutto gira:  

That is the lowest place, and the most dark, and farthest from the Heaven, which encircles all.

- *il più lontan* ‘the farthest’ → “farthest”: the repeated definite article is omitted.
- *che tutto gira* ‘which turns all’ → “which encircles all”: *gira* is interpreted spatially as encompassing rather than causing rotation, and the object moves after the verb.

> 30 Ben so il cammin; però ti fa securo.  

Well do I know the way; so reassure thyself.

- *Ben so* ‘well I know’ → “Well do I know”: emphatic “do” is supplied, with inversion after the initial adverb.
- *ti fa securo* ‘yourself make secure’ → “reassure thyself”: the imperative with an adjective complement becomes a single verb, and the reflexive pronoun follows it.

> 31 Questa palude, che il gran puzzo spira,  
> 32 Cinge d’ intorno la città dolente,  
> 33 U’ non potemo entrare omai senz’ ira.  

This marsh, which breathes the mighty stench, all round begirds the doleful city, where we cannot now enter without anger.”

- *che il gran puzzo spira* ‘which the great stench breathes’ → “which breathes the mighty stench”: the object moves after the verb, and “mighty” renders *gran*.
- *Cinge d’ intorno* ‘girds around’ → “all round begirds”: the adverbial phrase moves before the verb, “all” is supplied, and “begirds” renders *Cinge*.
- *la città dolente* ‘the city sorrowful’ → “the doleful city”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *non potemo entrare omai* ‘we cannot enter now’ → “we cannot now enter”: the temporal adverb moves before the infinitive.

> 34 Ed altro disse, ma non l’ ho a mente;  

And more he said: but I have it not in memory;

- *altro* ‘other things’ → “more”: the indefinite pronoun becomes a quantitative expression.
- *non l’ ho a mente* ‘I do not have it in mind’ → “I have it not in memory”: the negative follows the verb and object rather than preceding them, and “memory” specifies the recollective sense of *mente*.

## Lines 35–48 — The Three Infernal Furies

> 35 Perocchè l’ occhio m’ avea tutto tratto  
> 36 Vêr l’ alta torre alla cima rovente,  

for my eye had drawn me wholly to the high tower with glowing summit,

- *Perocchè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *m’ avea tutto tratto* ‘me had whole drawn’ → “had drawn me wholly”: the object pronoun follows the verb; *tutto*, describing the speaker as wholly absorbed, becomes the adverb “wholly” and follows the object.
- *Vêr* ‘toward’ → “to”: direction toward something is expressed as movement to it.
- *alla cima rovente* ‘at the summit glowing’ → “with glowing summit”: the locative phrase becomes a descriptive phrase introduced by “with”; the article is omitted, and the adjective precedes its noun.

> 37 Ove in un punto vidi dritte ratto  
> 38 Tre furie infernal di sangue tinte,  

where all at once I saw erect three Hellish Furies, stained with blood;

- *in un punto … ratto* ‘in an instant … swiftly’ → “all at once”: the two adverbial expressions are compressed into one phrase before the verb; *ratto* has no separate equivalent.
- *Tre furie infernal* ‘three Furies infernal’ → “three Hellish Furies”: the adjective precedes its noun.
- *di sangue tinte* ‘with blood dyed’ → “stained with blood”: “stained” replaces ‘dyed’, and the participle precedes the phrase expressing what stains them.

> 39 Che membra femminili aveano, ed atto;  
> 40 E con idre verdissime eran cinte:  

who had the limbs and attitude of women, and were girt with greenest hydras.

- *membra femminili aveano, ed atto* ‘limbs female they had, and bearing’ → “had the limbs and attitude of women”: the verb comes before both coordinated objects rather than between them; “the” is supplied, and the adjective *femminili* becomes the shared postmodifier “of women”.
- *con idre verdissime eran cinte* ‘with hydras very green they were girt’ → “were girt with greenest hydras”: the verb precedes the prepositional phrase, and the adjective precedes its noun. The Italian intensifying superlative is represented by the English superlative “greenest”.

> 41 Serpentelli e ceraste avean per crine,  
> 42 Onde le fiere tempie eran avvinte.  

For hair, they had little serpents and cerastes, wherewith their horrid temples were bound.

- *avean per crine* ‘they had as hair’ → “For hair, they had”: the phrase expressing function is moved to the beginning, and the verb consequently precedes its objects rather than following them.
- *le fiere tempie* ‘the fierce temples’ → “their horrid temples”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘fierce’ is rendered as “horrid”.

> 43 E quei, che ben conobbe le meschine  
> 44 Della Regina dell’ eterno pianto,  
> 45 Guarda, mi disse, le feroci Erine.  

And he, knowing well the handmaids of the Queen of everlasting lamentation, said to me: “Mark the fierce Erynnis!

- *quei, che ben conobbe* ‘that one, who well knew’ → “he, knowing well”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun; the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase, losing its explicit past tense, and the adverb follows the participle.
- *dell’ eterno pianto* ‘of the eternal weeping’ → “of everlasting lamentation”: the article before the abstract noun is omitted, and “lamentation” renders the more specifically tearful ‘weeping’.
- *Guarda, mi disse* ‘Look, to me he said’ → “said to me” and “Mark”: the reporting clause is moved before the direct speech, its indirect object follows the verb, and ‘Look’ becomes the transitive imperative “Mark”.

> 46 Questa è Megera dal sinistro canto:  

This is Megæra on the left hand;

- *dal sinistro canto* ‘from the left side’ → “on the left hand”: *da* is interpreted locatively rather than as expressing origin, and ‘side’ becomes the idiomatic “hand”.

> 47 Quella, che piange dal destro, è Aletto:  

she, that weeps upon the right, is Alecto.

- *Quella* ‘that one’ → “she”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *dal destro* ‘from the right’ → “upon the right”: *da* is again rendered locatively rather than as expressing origin.

> 48 Tesifone è nel mezzo: e tacque a tanto.  

Tesiphone is in the middle.” And therewith he was silent.

- *e tacque a tanto* ‘and fell silent at that’ → “And therewith he was silent.”: the transition into silence is expressed as a state; ‘at that’ becomes “therewith” and moves before the subject and verb.

## Lines 49–60 — Medusa’s Threat and the Master’s Protection

> 49 Coll’ unghie si fendea ciascuna il petto;  

With her claws each was rending her breast;

- *Coll’ unghie* ‘with the nails’ → “With her claws”: “claws” replaces ‘nails’, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *si fendea ciascuna il petto* ‘was cleaving herself each the breast’ → “each was rending her breast”: the subject moves before the verb; the reflexive indicating whose breast is being wounded becomes “her”; and ‘cleaving’ is rendered as “rending”.

> 50 Batteansi a palme, e gridavan sì alto,  
> 51 Ch’ io mi strinsi al Poeta per sospetto.  

they were smiting themselves with their palms, and crying so loudly, that I pressed close to the Poet for fear.

- *a palme* ‘with palms’ → “with their palms”: the possessive “their” is supplied.
- *e gridavan* ‘and they were crying’ → “and crying”: the finite verb becomes a participle sharing the preceding subject and auxiliary.
- *sì alto* ‘so high’ → “so loudly”: height is rendered explicitly as loudness.
- *io mi strinsi* ‘I drew myself tight’ → “I pressed close”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb with an adverb.
- *per sospetto* ‘for apprehension’ → “for fear”: “fear” gives the contextual sense of *sospetto*, rather than suspicion or distrust.

> 52 Venga Medusa, sì il farem di smalto:  
> 53 Gridavan tutte riguardando in giuso:  

“Let Medusa come, that we may change him into stone,” they all cried, looking downwards.

- *Venga Medusa* ‘may Medusa come’ → “Let Medusa come”: the independent subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”, and Medusa moves before the verb expressing her coming.
- *sì il farem* ‘thus we shall make him’ → “that we may change him”: the future statement becomes a purpose clause with “may”; the object moves after the verb; and ‘make’ becomes “change”.
- *di smalto* ‘of enamel’ → “into stone”: “stone” renders the contextual sense of a hard, stony substance rather than enamel; “into” replaces ‘of’ to suit “change”.
- *Gridavan tutte* ‘cried all’ → “they all cried”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *riguardando in giuso* ‘looking down below’ → “looking downwards”: the phrase *in giuso* is condensed into a directional adverb.

> 54 Mal noi vengiammo in Teseo l’ assalto.  

“Badly did we avenge the assault of Theseus.”

- *Mal noi vengiammo* ‘badly we avenged’ → “Badly did we avenge”: the translation introduces “did” and places it before the subject after the initial adverb.
- *in Teseo l’ assalto* ‘upon Theseus the assault’ → “the assault of Theseus”: Theseus changes from the person upon whom vengeance was taken to the possessor identifying whose assault it was; the phrase naming him moves after “the assault”.

> 55 Volgiti indietro, e tien lo viso chiuso;  
> 56 Chè se il Gorgon si mostra, e tu il vedessi,  
> 57 Nulla sarebbe del tornar mai suso.  

“Turn thee backwards, and keep thy eyes closed; for if the Gorgon shew herself, and thou shouldst see her, there would be no returning up again.”

- *lo viso chiuso* ‘the face closed’ → “thy eyes closed”: *viso* is interpreted as the eyes or sight rather than the face; the singular noun becomes plural, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *si mostra* ‘shows herself’ → “shew herself”: the Italian present indicative becomes an English present subjunctive in the conditional clause.
- *e tu il vedessi* ‘and thou sawest him’ → “and thou shouldst see her”: the imperfect subjunctive is expressed with “shouldst”; the masculine object pronoun, agreeing with Italian *Gorgon*, becomes feminine in English.
- *Nulla sarebbe del tornar* ‘nothing would be of returning’ → “there would be no returning”: the Italian construction becomes an existential clause; the preposition disappears, and the nominal infinitive is rendered by an English gerund.
- *mai suso* ‘ever up’ → “up again”: the order is reversed, and “again” replaces ‘ever’, emphasizing renewed upward movement rather than return at any future time.

> 58 Così disse il Maestro; ed egli stessi  
> 59 Mi volse, e non si tenne alle mie mani,  
> 60 Che con le sue ancor non mi chiudessi.  

Thus said the Master, and he himself turned me, and trusted not to my hands, but closed me also with his own.

- *Mi volse* ‘me turned’ → “turned me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *e non si tenne alle mie mani* ‘and did not confine himself to my hands’ → “and trusted not to my hands”: the reflexive expression for limiting himself to something becomes the non-reflexive “trusted”.
- *Che con le sue ancor non mi chiudessi* ‘so that with his also he should not close me’ → “but closed me also with his own”: the negative subordinate clause, completing the preceding negative construction, becomes an affirmative clause coordinated with “but”; the imperfect subjunctive becomes a simple past. The phrase naming his hands moves to the end, “also” moves after the object, and “own” strengthens the substantival possessive.

## Lines 61–70 — A Veiled Doctrine and a Fearful Crash

> 61 O voi, che avete gl’ intelletti sani,  
> 62 Mirate la dottrina, che s’ asconde  
> 63 Sotto il velame degli versi strani.  

O ye, who have sane intellects, mark the doctrine, which conceals itself beneath the veil of the strange verses!

- *gl’ intelletti sani* ‘the intellects sane’ → “sane intellects”: the article is omitted, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Mirate* ‘look at’ → “mark”: the command to look becomes a command to heed or notice.
- *degli versi strani* ‘of the verses strange’ → “of the strange verses”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 64 E già venìa su per le torbid’ onde  
> 65 Un fracasso d’ un suon pien di spavento,  
> 66 Per cui tremavano ambedue le sponde;  

And now there came, upon the turbid waves, a crash of fearful sound, at which the shores both trembled:

- *E già venìa* ‘And already was coming’ → “And now there came”: “now” replaces ‘already’; the imperfect becomes a simple past, and introductory “there” is supplied.
- *su per* ‘up along’ → “upon”: the directional phrase becomes a preposition expressing position.
- *d’ un suon pien di spavento* ‘of a sound full of fright’ → “of fearful sound”: the indefinite article is omitted, and the phrase describing the sound’s fullness becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *Per cui* ‘through which’ → “at which”: the causal connection is expressed as a reaction to the sound.
- *tremavano ambedue le sponde* ‘trembled both the shores’ → “the shores both trembled”: the subject moves before the verb, and “both” follows rather than precedes “the shores”.

> 67 Non altrimenti fatto che d’ un vento  
> 68 Impetuoso per gli avversi ardori,  
> 69 Che fier la selva senza alcun rattento;  

a sound as of a wind, impetuous for the adverse heats, which smites the forest without any stay;

- *Non altrimenti fatto che d’ un vento* ‘not otherwise made than of a wind’ → “a sound as of a wind”: the negative comparison becomes a positive comparison with “as”; “a sound” is supplied to restate what is being compared, and *fatto* is not separately expressed.

> 70 Li rami schianta, abbatte, e porta fuori:  

shatters off the boughs, beats down, and sweeps away:

- *Li rami schianta* ‘the boughs shatters’ → “shatters off the boughs”: the object moves after the verb, and “off” makes the separation explicit.
- *porta fuori* ‘carries out’ → “sweeps away”: carrying becomes sweeping, and outward movement is expressed as movement away.

## Lines 71–84 — Flight Before the Unwetted Stranger

> 71 Dinanzi polveroso va superbo,  
> 72 E fa fuggir le fiere e li pastori.  

dusty in front, it goes superb, and makes the wild beasts and the shepherds flee.

- *Dinanzi polveroso* ‘in front dusty’ → “dusty in front”: the order of the adjective and the adverbial phrase is reversed.
- *E fa fuggir le fiere e li pastori* ‘and makes flee the wild beasts and the shepherds’ → “and makes the wild beasts and the shepherds flee”: English places those made to flee between “makes” and “flee”.

> 73 Gli occhi mi sciolse, e disse: Or drizza il nerbo  
> 74 Del viso su per quella schiuma antica,  
> 75 Per indi ove quel fummo è più acerbo.  

He loosed my eyes, and said: “Now turn thy nerve of vision on that ancient foam, there where the smoke is harshest.”

- *Gli occhi mi sciolse* ‘the eyes to me he loosed’ → “He loosed my eyes”: the object moves after the verb, and the personal dative becomes the possessive “my”.
- *drizza il nerbo Del viso* ‘direct the nerve of sight’ → “turn thy nerve of vision”: “turn” replaces ‘direct’, and the definite article becomes the possessive “thy”.
- *su per* ‘up over’ → “on”: the directional expression becomes a preposition indicating the object of attention, without a separate equivalent for ‘up’.
- *quella schiuma antica* ‘that foam ancient’ → “that ancient foam”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Per indi* ‘through there’ → “there”: the preposition is not separately represented.
- *quel fummo* ‘that smoke’ → “the smoke”: the demonstrative becomes a definite article.
- *più acerbo* ‘more harsh’ → “harshest”: the comparative expression is interpreted as a superlative.

> 76 Come le rane innanzi alla nimica  
> 77 Biscia per l’ acqua si dileguan tutte,  
> 78 Fin ch’ alla terra ciascuna s’ abbica;  

As frogs, before their enemy the serpent, run all asunder through the water, till each squats upon the bottom;

- *le rane* ‘the frogs’ → “frogs”: the definite article is omitted in the general comparison.
- *innanzi alla nimica Biscia* ‘before the hostile serpent’ → “before their enemy the serpent”: the adjective becomes the noun “enemy”, “their” is supplied, and “the serpent” becomes an apposition.
- *per l’acqua si dileguan tutte* ‘through the water they scatter, all’ → “run all asunder through the water”: the pronominal verb is expanded into “run” and “asunder”; the water phrase moves after the verb, and “all” is placed within the expanded verbal expression.
- *alla terra* ‘on the ground’ → “upon the bottom”: ‘ground’ is interpreted as the bottom of the water, and the phrase moves after the subject and verb.
- *ciascuna s’abbica* ‘each heaps herself up’ → “each squats”: the figurative pronominal verb becomes an ordinary intransitive verb describing posture.

> 79 Vid’ io più di mille anime distrutte  
> 80 Fuggir così dinanzi ad un, che al passo  
> 81 Passava Stige colle piante asciutte.  

so I saw more than a thousand ruined spirits flee before one, who passed the Stygian ferry with soles unwet.

- *Vid’ io* ‘saw I’ → “I saw”: subject and verb exchange places.
- *anime distrutte* ‘souls destroyed’ → “ruined spirits”: “spirits” replaces ‘souls’, “ruined” renders ‘destroyed’, and the participial adjective moves before the noun.
- *Fuggir così* ‘flee thus’ → “so” … “flee”: the comparison word moves from after the infinitive to the beginning of the main clause.
- *al passo Passava Stige* ‘at a walk was crossing Styx’ → “passed the Stygian ferry”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the river’s name becomes an adjective qualifying the supplied noun “ferry”. This recasts the crossing as passing a ferry rather than explicitly expressing the walking pace; “ferry” may reflect an interpretation of *passo* as a crossing.
- *colle piante asciutte* ‘with the soles dry’ → “with soles unwet”: the definite article is omitted, and the positive adjective ‘dry’ becomes the negative “unwet”.

> 82 Dal volto rimovea quell’ aer grasso,  
> 83 Menando la sinistra innanzi spesso;  
> 84 E sol di quell’ angoscia parea lasso.  

He waved that gross air from his countenance, often moving his left hand before him; and only of that trouble seemed he weary.

- *Dal volto* ‘from the face’ → “from his countenance”: the definite article becomes a possessive, “countenance” replaces ‘face’, and the phrase moves after the verb and its object.
- *rimovea* ‘was removing’ → “He waved”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and removal is expressed through the particular motion used to accomplish it.
- *quell’ aer grasso* ‘that air thick’ → “that gross air”: “gross” renders the air’s thickness, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Menando la sinistra innanzi spesso* ‘moving the left in front often’ → “often moving his left hand before him”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “hand”, implicit in the Italian feminine adjective, is supplied; “him” explicitly identifies the reference point left implicit in the Italian; the adverb moves from the end of the participial clause to its beginning.
- *di quell’ angoscia* ‘from that distress’ → “of that trouble”: the stronger ‘distress’ becomes “trouble”, and the preposition is adapted to the English construction with “weary”.

## Lines 85–97 — The Heavenly Messenger Opens the Gate

> 85 Ben m’ accorsi ch’ egli era del Ciel messo,  
> 86 E volsimi al Maestro: e quei fe’ segno,  
> 87 Ch’ io stessi cheto, ed inchinassi ad esso.  

Well did I perceive that he was a Messenger of Heaven; and I turned to the Master. And he made a sign that I should stand quiet, and bow down to him.

- *Ben m’ accorsi* ‘Well I became aware’ → “Well did I perceive”: the pronominal verb becomes the transitive “perceive”; emphatic “did” is supplied and precedes the subject.
- *del Ciel messo* ‘of Heaven messenger’ → “a Messenger of Heaven”: the modifying phrase follows the noun, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *E volsimi al Maestro* ‘and turned myself to the Master’ → “and I turned to the Master”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive one.
- *e quei* ‘and that one’ → “And he”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *stessi cheto* ‘should stay quiet’ → “should stand quiet”: “stand” gives *stare* a more specifically physical sense.
- *ed inchinassi ad esso* ‘and should bow to him’ → “and bow down to him”: “down” is supplied, and the preceding “should” also governs this verb.

> 88 Ahi quanto mi parea pien di disdegno!  

Ah, how full he seemed to me of indignation!

- *quanto mi parea pien* ‘how to me he seemed full’ → “how full he seemed to me”: “full” moves forward beside “how,” while the indirect object follows the verb.
- *disdegno* ‘disdain’ → “indignation”: the translation chooses the sense of offended anger rather than contempt.

> 89 Giunse alla porta, e con una verghetta  
> 90 L’ aperse, chè non v’ ebbe alcun ritegno.  

He reached the gate, and with a wand opened it; for in it there was no resistance.

- *Giunse alla porta* ‘He arrived at the gate’ → “He reached the gate”: a verb with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object.
- *con una verghetta* ‘with a little rod’ → “with a wand”: “wand” replaces the diminutive noun, without separately expressing its diminutive force.
- *L’ aperse* ‘it he opened’ → “opened it”: the object pronoun follows the verb instead of preceding it.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *non v’ ebbe alcun ritegno* ‘there was not any hindrance there’ → “in it there was no resistance”: the locative pronoun is interpreted as referring to the gate and placed first as “in it”; ‘not any’ becomes “no,” and ‘hindrance’ becomes “resistance.”

> 91 O cacciati del ciel, gente dispetta,  
> 92 Cominciò egli in su l’ orribil soglia,  
> 93 Ond’ esta oltracotanza in voi s’ alletta?  

“O outcasts of Heaven! race despised!” began he, upon the horrid threshold. “Why dwells this insolence in you?

- *cacciati del ciel* ‘those driven out of Heaven’ → “outcasts of Heaven”: a substantivized participle becomes a noun, and the relation of expulsion from Heaven is expressed by “of.”
- *gente* ‘people’ → “race”: the collective noun is rendered with a term suggesting a distinct class or kind.
- *Ond’* ‘Whence’ → “Why”: a question about origin becomes a question about cause.
- *esta oltracotanza in voi s’ alletta* ‘this presumptuousness in you lodges itself’ → “dwells this insolence in you”: “insolence” renders presumptuous arrogance; the reflexive verb becomes intransitive “dwells,” the subject follows the verb, and the locative phrase moves to the end.

> 94 Perchè ricalcitrate a quella voglia,  
> 95 A cui non puote il fin mai esser mozzo,  
> 96 E che più volte v’ ha cresciuta doglia?  
> 97 Che giova nelle Fata dar di cozzo?  

Why spurn ye at that Will, whose object never can be frustrated, and which often has increased your pain?

- *ricalcitrate a* ‘do you kick against’ → “spurn ye at”: the image of kicking against something is rendered by “spurn,” with “at” replacing ‘against’; the archaic question construction places the subject after the main verb without auxiliary ‘do.’
- *A cui non puote il fin mai esser mozzo* ‘to which the end can never be cut off’ → “whose object never can be frustrated”: the relative phrase becomes possessive “whose”; *fin* is interpreted as an intended “object,” and physical cutting off becomes frustration of that object. The subject moves before the modal verb, and the negative expression becomes “never,” placed before “can.”
- *più volte* ‘several times’ → “often”: a phrase expressing repeated occasions becomes an adverb of frequency.
- *v’ ha cresciuta doglia* ‘has increased pain for you’ → “has increased your pain”: the dative pronoun becomes a possessive modifier of “pain.”
- *Che giova nelle Fata dar di cozzo?* ‘What use is it to butt against the Fates?’ → ∅: line 97 has no counterpart in the supplied English; the translation provided stops with line 96.

## Lines 98–111 — The Messenger Departs; The Poets Enter

> 98 Cerbero vostro, se ben vi ricorda,  
> 99 Ne porta ancor pelato il mento e il gozzo.  

What profits it to butt against the Fates? Your Cerberus, if ye remember, still bears his chin and his throat peeled for doing so.”

- *Cerbero vostro* ‘Cerberus your’ → “Your Cerberus”: the possessive precedes the noun. The preceding sentence, “What profits it to butt against the Fates?”, has no counterpart in the supplied Italian: it renders the line before this excerpt.
- *se ben vi ricorda* ‘if well it comes to your memory’ → “if ye remember”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal verb with “ye” as subject; *ben* is omitted.
- *Ne* ‘from it’ → “for doing so”: the pronominal reference becomes an explicit phrase expressing cause, moved to the end of the sentence.
- *porta ancor* ‘bears still’ → “still bears”: the adverb precedes the verb.
- *pelato il mento e il gozzo* ‘peeled the chin and the gullet’ → “his chin and his throat peeled”: “peeled” follows the two nouns rather than preceding them; the definite articles become possessives, and *gozzo* is rendered by the broader “throat”.

> 100 Poi si rivolse per la strada lorda,  
> 101 E non fe’ motto a noi: ma fe’ sembiante  
> 102 D’ uomo, cui altra cura stringa e morda,  
> 103 Che quella di colui che gli è davante.  

Then he returned by the filthy way, and spake no word to us; but looked like one whom other care urges and incites than that of those who stand before him.

- *Poi si rivolse* ‘then he turned back’ → “Then he returned”: the reflexive verb of turning becomes an intransitive verb of returning.
- *per la strada lorda* ‘along the road filthy’ → “by the filthy way”: “by” renders the route expressed by *per*, “way” replaces ‘road’, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *E non fe’ motto a noi* ‘and made no utterance to us’ → “and spake no word to us”: the construction with ‘make’ and a noun becomes a verb of speaking with “word” as its object.
- *ma fe’ sembiante D’ uomo* ‘but made the appearance of a man’ → “but looked like one”: the noun construction becomes “looked like”, and “one” replaces ‘a man’.
- *cui altra cura stringa e morda* ‘whom another care may grip and bite’ → “whom other care urges and incites”: the physical verbs become verbs of prompting; the Italian subjunctives become English indicatives.
- *Che quella di colui che gli è davante* ‘than that of the one who is before him’ → “than that of those who stand before him”: singular *colui* becomes plural “those”, and ‘is’ becomes “stand”.

> 104 E noi movemmo i piedi in vêr la terra,  
> 105 Sicuri appresso le parole sante.  

And we moved our feet towards the city, secure after the sacred words.

- *movemmo i piedi* ‘moved the feet’ → “moved our feet”: the definite article becomes a possessive identifying whose feet they are.
- *la terra* ‘the town’ → “the city”: “city” renders *terra* in its sense of an inhabited settlement, rather than ‘earth’ or ‘land’.
- *le parole sante* ‘the words holy’ → “the sacred words”: “sacred” renders ‘holy’, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 106 Dentro v’ entrammo senza alcuna guerra:  

We entered into it without any strife.

- *Dentro v’ entrammo* ‘within, there we entered’ → “We entered into it”: the two locative elements, *dentro* and *vi*, are combined into “into it” and placed after the verb.
- *guerra* ‘war’ → “strife”: a broader term for conflict replaces ‘war’.

> 107 Ed io, ch’ avea di riguardar disio  
> 108 La condizion che tal fortezza serra,  
> 109 Com’ io fui dentro, l’ occhio intorno invio;  

And I, who was desirous to behold the condition which such a fortress encloses, as soon as I was in, sent my eyes around;

- *ch’ avea di riguardar disio* ‘who had of looking desire’ → “who was desirous to behold”: ‘had desire’ becomes “was desirous”, the infinitive follows rather than precedes the expression of desire, and “behold” renders ‘look at’.
- *tal fortezza* ‘such fortress’ → “such a fortress”: English supplies the indefinite article.
- *Com’ io fui dentro* ‘as I was inside’ → “as soon as I was in”: “as soon as” makes the immediate temporal relationship explicit.
- *l’ occhio intorno invio* ‘the eye around I send’ → “sent my eyes around”: the present becomes past, singular ‘eye’ becomes plural “eyes”, and the definite article becomes a possessive; the verb precedes its object, and “around” follows it.

> 110 E veggio ad ogni man grande campagna  
> 111 Piena di duolo e di tormento rio.  

and saw, on either hand, a spacious plain full of sorrow and of evil torment.

- *veggio* ‘see’ → “saw”: the present tense becomes past.
- *ad ogni man* ‘at every hand’ → “on either hand”: “on” replaces ‘at’, and “either” presents the surroundings as two sides rather than using ‘every’.
- *grande campagna* ‘great plain’ → “a spacious plain”: “spacious” specifies extent, and English supplies an indefinite article.
- *tormento rio* ‘torment evil’ → “evil torment”: the adjective precedes the noun.

## Lines 112–123 — The Burning Tombs

> 112 Sì come ad Arli, ove il Rodano stagna,  
> 113 Sì com’ a Pola presso del Quarnaro,  
> 114 Che Italia chiude e i suoi termini bagna,  

As at Arles, where the Rhone stagnates, as at Pola near the Quarnaro gulf, which shuts up Italy and bathes its confines,

- *Sì come* ‘just as’ → “As”: the reinforcing *Sì* is omitted.
- *Sì com’* ‘just as’ → “as”: the reinforcing *Sì* is again omitted.
- *presso del Quarnaro* ‘near the Quarnaro’ → “near the Quarnaro gulf”: “gulf” is supplied.
- *Che Italia chiude* ‘which Italy encloses’ → “which shuts up Italy”: the object, *Italia*, moves from before the verb to after it.
- *e i suoi termini bagna* ‘and its confines bathes’ → “and bathes its confines”: the object moves from before the verb to after it.

> 115 Fanno i sepolcri tutto il loco varo:  

the sepulchres make all the place uneven;

- *Fanno i sepolcri* ‘make the sepulchres’ → “the sepulchres make”: the subject follows the verb in Italian but precedes it in English.
- *varo* ‘variegated’ → “uneven”: the translation interprets the variation as physical unevenness rather than a varied appearance.

> 116 Così facevan quivi d’ ogni parte,  
> 117 Salvo che il modo v’ era più amaro;  

so did they here on every side, only the manner here was bitterer.

- *Così facevan* ‘so they were making’ → “so did they”: “did” substitutes for the previously expressed verb ‘make’; the supplied subject follows it, and the imperfect is rendered by a simple past.
- *quivi* ‘there’ → “here”: the spatial standpoint changes.
- *d’ ogni parte* ‘from every side’ → “on every side”: the preposition expresses position rather than direction.
- *Salvo che* ‘except that’ → “only”: the exceptive clause is introduced by an adverb rather than an explicit subordinating phrase.
- *v’* ‘there’ → “here”: the spatial standpoint again changes.
- *più amaro* ‘more bitter’ → “bitterer”: the two-word comparative becomes an inflected comparative.

> 118 Chè tra gli avelli fiamme erano sparte,  
> 119 Per le quali eran sì del tutto accesi,  
> 120 Che ferro più non chiede verun’ arte.  

For amongst the tombs were scattered flames, whereby they were made all over so glowing-hot, that iron more hot no craft requires.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *tra gli avelli fiamme erano sparte* ‘amongst the tombs flames were scattered’ → “amongst the tombs were scattered flames”: the subject moves after the passive verb.
- *Per le quali* ‘through which’ → “whereby”: the preposition and relative pronoun become a single relative adverb.
- *eran sì del tutto accesi* ‘were so entirely kindled’ → “they were made all over so glowing-hot”: *accesi* becomes a description of intense heat rather than being kindled, expressed through “made” and an adjective. *Del tutto* is rendered spatially as “all over,” and its position relative to “so” is reversed.
- *ferro più non chiede verun’ arte* ‘no craft asks for iron more [heated]’ → “iron more hot no craft requires”: “hot” makes explicit what *più* leaves understood, and *chiede* is rendered as “requires” rather than ‘asks for’. The Italian places the subject *verun’ arte* after the verb; English places “no craft” before it while retaining “iron more hot” at the front. The negative moves from the verb to the subject determiner.

> 121 Tutti gli lor coperchi eran sospesi,  

Their covers were all raised up;

- *Tutti gli lor coperchi eran sospesi* ‘all their covers were suspended’ → “Their covers were all raised up”: “all” moves from the beginning of the subject phrase to after “were”; *sospesi* is rendered as “raised up,” expressing elevation rather than suspension.

> 122 E fuor n’ uscivan sì duri lamenti,  
> 123 Che ben parean di miseri e d’ offesi.  

and out of them proceeded moans so grievous, that they seemed indeed the moans of spirits sad and wounded.

- *fuor n’ uscivan* ‘out of them came out’ → “out of them proceeded”: the outward motion expressed both by *fuor* and by *uscivan* is expressed only once; “proceeded” replaces the verb ‘came out’.
- *sì duri lamenti* ‘so harsh laments’ → “moans so grievous”: the adjectival phrase moves after the noun; “moans” gives *lamenti* a specifically vocal form, while “grievous” renders *duri* as distressing rather than harsh.
- *ben parean* ‘indeed they seemed’ → “they seemed indeed”: the emphatic adverb moves after the verb.
- *di miseri e d’ offesi* ‘of wretched ones and of wounded ones’ → “the moans of spirits sad and wounded”: “the moans” is supplied to complete the elliptical predicate. “Spirits” supplies a noun for the substantivized adjectives, which become adjectives modifying it; *miseri* becomes “sad” rather than ‘wretched’. The second *di* is not repeated.

## Lines 124–133 — The Heretics’ Tombs

> 124 Ed io: Maestro, quai son quelle genti,  
> 125 Che seppellite dentro da quell’ arche  
> 126 Si fan sentir coi sospiri dolenti?  

And I: “Master, what are these people who, buried within those chests, make themselves heard by their painful sighs?”

- *quai son quelle genti* ‘which are those people’ → “what are these people”: the interrogative “what” replaces ‘which’, and the demonstrative changes from ‘those’ to “these”.
- *arche* ‘tombs’ → “chests”: the translation uses the word’s sense of box-like receptacles rather than its contextual sense of tombs.
- *Si fan sentir* ‘themselves make hear’ → “make themselves heard”: the reflexive pronoun follows the causative verb, and the infinitive ‘hear’ becomes the passive participle “heard”.
- *coi sospiri dolenti* ‘with the sighs sorrowful’ → “by their painful sighs”: ‘with’ becomes instrumental “by”, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the adjective precedes the noun; ‘sorrowful’ is rendered as “painful”.

> 127 Ed egli a me: Qui son gli eresiarche  
> 128 Co’ lor seguaci d’ ogni setta, e molto  
> 129 Più che non credi, son le tombe carche.  

And he to me: “These are the Arch-heretics with their followers of every sect; and much more, than thou thinkest, the tombs are laden.

- *Qui* ‘Here’ → “These”: the locative adverb becomes a demonstrative pronoun serving as the subject.
- *gli eresiarche* ‘the heresiarchs’ → “the Arch-heretics”: the Italian noun is rendered by an explanatory English compound.
- *che non credi* ‘than thou dost not believe’ → “than thou thinkest”: the expletive *non*, which does not negate the comparison, is omitted; ‘believe’ becomes “thinkest”.
- *son le tombe carche* ‘are the tombs laden’ → “the tombs are laden”: the subject is moved before the verb.

> 130 Simile qui con simile è sepolto:  

Like with like is buried here;

- *Simile qui con simile è sepolto* ‘Like here with like is buried’ → “Like with like is buried here”: the adverb ‘here’ is moved to the end of the clause.

> 131 E i monimenti son più, e men caldi.  

and the monuments are more and less hot.”

- Literal.

> 132 E poi ch’ alla man destra si fu vôlto,  
> 133 Passammo tra i martìri e gli alti spaldi.  

Then, after turning to the right hand, we passed between the tortures and the high battlements.

- *E* ‘And’ → “Then”: the coordinating conjunction becomes an adverb marking sequence.
- *poi ch’ … si fu vôlto* ‘after he had turned himself’ → “after turning”: the finite reflexive pluperfect becomes a nonfinite construction without an expressed reflexive pronoun. This also removes the Italian’s grammatical distinction between the singular subject of turning and the plural subject of passing.
- *alla man destra* ‘to the hand right’ → “to the right hand”: the adjective is placed before the noun.

## Summary — Close Fidelity, Adjusted Expression

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s vocabulary, sequence, and imagery, often retaining expressions that sound unusual in English, such as “thy nerve of vision” for *il nerbo del viso* and “the monuments are more and less hot” for *i monimenti son più, e men caldi*. His departures from word-for-word rendering nevertheless recur throughout the canto. Subjects and objects commonly move before and after their verbs respectively, adjectives precede their nouns, and separated phrases are brought together: *di nui … alcun* becomes “any of us”. Yet the translation also preserves marked arrangements, including “This question I made” and “iron more hot no craft requires”.

Changes of construction make some Italian expressions more explicit or more readily usable in English. *Veggendo* becomes “when I saw”, while *che ben conobbe* becomes “knowing well”: finite and nonfinite constructions can thus replace one another in either direction. Reflexive verbs frequently become ordinary intransitives, as *si fermò* becomes “He stopped”; elsewhere, *tal ne s’ offerse*, ‘such a one offered himself to us’, becomes the passive “such help was offered to us”. Tenses also shift: *converrà* loses its future reference in “it behoves us”, and the narrative presents *invio* and *veggio* become “sent” and “saw”.

Word choices range from close equivalents to interpretive substitutions. *Conca*, ‘basin’, becomes “shell”; *disdegno* becomes “indignation”; and *stringa e morda*, ‘grip and bite’, becomes “urges and incites”, replacing physical imagery with an account of its force. Carlyle supplies nouns left understood in Italian, including “colour” after *il suo nuovo* and “hand” for *la sinistra*. Definite articles often become possessives, as *l’ occhio* becomes “his eye”. Conversely, repeated prepositions, some intensifiers, and occasional locative words disappear: *qui* has no equivalent in “till some one come”. Comparative *non* is left untranslated where it carries no negative force.

Two broader adjustments operate throughout, beyond the individual changes recorded in the comparison. English regularly supplies subject pronouns where Italian identifies the subject through the verb ending alone, and omits Italian articles before possessives, as *a’ corpi sui* becomes “to their bodies”. These grammatical accommodations coexist with considerable literalness. Carlyle generally preserves the original’s repetitions, comparisons, and concrete terms, while rearranging syntax, supplying understood elements, and occasionally choosing an explanatory sense over the Italian expression’s more immediate wording.
