# Inferno 29

## Lines 1–9 — Lingering Over the Wounded

> 1 La molta gente e le diverse piaghe  
> 2 Avean le luci mie sì inebriate,  
> 3 Che dello stare a piangere eran vaghe.  

The many people and the diverse wounds had made my eyes so drunken that they longed to stay and weep.

- *La molta gente* ‘the great multitude’ → “The many people”: the singular collective noun becomes a plural noun, with “many” expressing quantity.
- *Avean le luci mie sì inebriate* ‘had the lights mine so intoxicated’ → “had made my eyes so drunken”: the metaphorical *luci* becomes “eyes”; the possessive moves before its noun; and the transitive verb becomes “made” with an adjective describing the object.
- *dello stare a piangere eran vaghe* ‘of staying to weep were desirous’ → “they longed to stay and weep”: the adjective expressing desire becomes the verb “longed,” and its complement follows rather than precedes it. The nominal infinitive becomes “to stay,” while *stare a piangere* becomes coordinated “stay and weep.”

> 4 Ma Virgilio mi disse: Che pur guate?  

But Virgil said to me: “Why art thou gazing still?

- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *pur guate* ‘still dost thou gaze’ → “art thou gazing still”: the simple present becomes a progressive construction, and “still” moves after the verb.

> 5 Perchè la vista tua pur si soffolge  
> 6 Laggiù tra l’ ombre triste smozzicate?  

Wherefore does thy sight still rest, down there, among the dismal mutilated shadows?

- *la vista tua pur si soffolge* ‘the sight thy still supports itself’ → “does thy sight still rest”: the possessive moves before its noun, and English introduces the interrogative auxiliary “does.” The reflexive *si soffolge* is interpreted as resting or lingering and rendered by the non-reflexive “rest.”
- *ombre triste smozzicate* ‘shadows sad, chopped to pieces’ → “dismal mutilated shadows”: both adjectives move before the noun; “dismal” renders *triste*, while “mutilated” gives a less specific expression than ‘chopped to pieces.’

> 7 Tu non hai fatto sì all’ altre bolge:  

Thou hast not done so at the other chasms.

- *non hai fatto* ‘not hast done’ → “hast not done”: the negative moves after the auxiliary.
- *bolge* ‘pouches’ → “chasms”: the word is rendered by a term for geographical hollows rather than its literal sense.

> 8 Pensa, se tu annoverar le credi,  
> 9 Che miglia ventiduo la valle volge;  

Consider, if thou thinkest to number them, that the valley goes round two-and-twenty miles;

- *annoverar le credi* ‘to number them believest’ → “thinkest to number them”: the governing verb moves before its infinitive, and *credi* is rendered as “thinkest” rather than ‘believest.’
- *miglia ventiduo* ‘miles twenty-two’ → “two-and-twenty miles”: the numeral moves before the noun, and the phrase expressing distance moves from before the subject to after the verb.
- *la valle volge* ‘the valley turns’ → “the valley goes round”: the single verb is rendered by the phrase “goes round.”

## Lines 10–23 — Concern for a Kindred Spirit

> 10 E già la Luna è sotto i nostri piedi:  

and the Moon already is beneath our feet.

- *E già la Luna è* ‘and already the Moon is’ → “and the Moon already is”: the adverb moves after the subject.

> 11 Lo tempo è poco omai, che n’ è concesso;  

The time is now short, that is conceded to us;

- *è poco omai* ‘is little now’ → “is now short”: the quantity of time becomes its short duration, and the adverb moves before the adjective.
- *che n’ è concesso* ‘that to us is conceded’ → “that is conceded to us”: the pronoun preceding the verb becomes a prepositional phrase following it.

> 12 Ed altro è da veder, che tu non vedi.  

and far other things are to be seen than thou dost see.”

- *altro* ‘something else’ → “far other things”: the substantival singular becomes a plural noun phrase, and “far” adds emphasis.
- *è da veder* ‘is to see’ → “are to be seen”: the infinitive becomes explicitly passive, and the finite verb becomes plural.
- *che tu non vedi* ‘which thou dost not see’ → “than thou dost see”: Carlyle takes the clause comparatively rather than as a negative relative clause, rendering *che* as “than” and leaving *non* without a separate English equivalent.

> 13 Se tu avessi, rispos’ io appresso.  
> 14 Atteso alla cagion per ch’ io guardava,  
> 15 Forse m’ avresti ancor lo star dimesso.  

“Hadst thou,” I thereupon replied, “attended to the cause for which I looked, perhaps thou mightest have vouchsafed me yet to stay.”

- *Se tu avessi … Atteso* ‘If thou hadst … attended’ → “Hadst thou,” … “attended”: the conditional conjunction is omitted, and inversion of auxiliary and subject marks the condition instead.
- *rispos’ io appresso* ‘replied I afterwards’ → “I thereupon replied”: the subject moves before the verb, and the following adverb moves between them; “thereupon” presents the reply as immediately consequent.
- *io guardava* ‘I was looking’ → “I looked”: the imperfect becomes a simple past rather than a progressive.
- *m’ avresti ancor lo star dimesso* ‘to me thou wouldst have yet permitted the staying’ → “thou mightest have vouchsafed me yet to stay”: the conditional becomes “mightest have”; “vouchsafed” gives permission a deferential colouring; the nominal infinitive becomes “to stay”; and the pronoun and adverb move after the main verb.

> 16 Parte sen gìa, ed io retro gli andava,  
> 17 Lo Duca, già facendo la risposta,  

Meantime the Guide was going on; and I went behind him, now making my reply,

- *sen gìa … Lo Duca* ‘was going away … the Guide’ → “the Guide was going on”: the postponed subject moves before its verb, and the pronominal expression of departure becomes continued movement, “going on”.
- *ed io retro gli andava* ‘and I behind him was going’ → “and I went behind him”: the phrase expressing position moves after the verb, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *già facendo la risposta* ‘already making the answer’ → “now making my reply”: ‘already’ becomes “now”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 18 E soggiungendo: Dentro a quella cava,  
> 19 Dov’ io teneva gli occhi sì a posta,  
> 20 Credo ch’ un spirto del mio sangue pianga  
> 21 La colpa, che laggiù cotanto costa.  

and adding: “Within that cavern where I kept my eyes so fixed, I believe that a spirit, of my own blood, laments the guilt which costs so much down there.”

- *io teneva gli occhi sì a posta* ‘I held the eyes so deliberately’ → “I kept my eyes so fixed”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the adverbial expression of deliberate attention becomes an adjective describing the eyes as fixed.
- *del mio sangue* ‘of my blood’ → “of my own blood”: “own” is supplied for emphasis.
- *pianga* ‘weep over’ → “laments”: weeping is expressed as lamentation, and the Italian subjunctive dependent on *Credo* becomes an English indicative.
- *laggiù cotanto costa* ‘down there so much costs’ → “costs so much down there”: the verb moves before both adverbial expressions, and the expression of place moves to the end.

> 22 Allor disse il Maestro: Non si franga  
> 23 Lo tuo pensier da qui innanzi sov’ ello:  

Then the Master said: “Let not thy thought henceforth distract itself on him.

- *disse il Maestro* ‘said the Master’ → “the Master said”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *Non si franga Lo tuo pensier* ‘Let not break itself thy thought’ → “Let not thy thought” … “distract itself”: the subject moves before the main verb, the reflexive pronoun follows it, and the metaphor of breaking becomes mental distraction.
- *da qui innanzi* ‘from here onward’ → “henceforth”: the temporal phrase is condensed into one adverb.

## Lines 24–35 — Geri’s Threat and Unavenged Death

> 24 Attendi ad altro, ed ei là si rimanga;  

Attend to somewhat else, and let him stay there;

- *ei là si rimanga* ‘he there remain’ → “let him stay there”: the third-person subjunctive expressing a command becomes an English construction with “let”; the subject becomes “him,” and the adverb follows the verb. The pronominal *si* has no separate English equivalent.

> 25 Ch’ io vidi lui a piè del ponticello  
> 26 Mostrarti, e minacciar forte col dito,  
> 27 Ed udîl nominar Geri del Bello.  

for I saw him, at the foot of the little bridge, point to thee, and vehemently threaten with his finger; and heard them call him Geri del Bello.

- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *a piè* ‘at foot’ → “at the foot”: English supplies the definite article.
- *Mostrarti* ‘indicate thee’ → “point to thee”: the verb is rendered by a phrase specifying the gesture, and its direct object becomes the complement of “to.”
- *minacciar forte* ‘threaten strongly’ → “vehemently threaten”: the adverb precedes rather than follows the verb, and “vehemently” expresses the force of the threatening.
- *col dito* ‘with the finger’ → “with his finger”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *udîl nominar* ‘I heard him named’ → “heard them call him”: the construction is recast with an explicit, indefinite “them” performing the naming. The clitic referring to the person named becomes the object of “call,” and “heard” shares the earlier subject “I.”

> 28 Tu eri allor sì del tutto impedito  
> 29 Sovra colui che già tenne Altaforte,  
> 30 Che non guardasti in là; sì fu partito.  

Thou wast then so totally entangled upon him who once held Altaforte, that thou didst not look that way; so he departed.”

- *impedito Sovra colui* ‘hindered over that one’ → “entangled upon him”: “entangled” gives a figurative rendering of being occupied or absorbed; the demonstrative *colui* becomes the personal pronoun “him.”
- *già* ‘already’ → “once”: the translation takes the adverb as referring to a former time.
- *non guardasti* ‘looked not’ → “didst not look”: English introduces the auxiliary “didst” to form the negative.
- *in là* ‘towards there’ → “that way”: the prepositional expression becomes an English adverbial phrase.
- *sì fu partito* ‘so he had departed’ → “so he departed”: the compound past with *essere* becomes a simple past, without explicitly marking anteriority.

> 31 O Duca mio! la violenta morte  
> 32 Che non gli è vendicata ancor, diss’ io,  
> 33 Per alcun che dell’ onta sia consorte,  

“O my Guide! his violent death, which is not yet avenged for him,” said I, “by any that is a partner of his shame,

- *Duca mio* ‘leader my’ → “my Guide”: “Guide” renders *Duca*, and the possessive precedes rather than follows the noun.
- *la violenta morte* ‘the violent death’ → “his violent death”: the definite article becomes a possessive, making the possessor explicit.
- *Che non gli è vendicata ancor* ‘which not for him is avenged yet’ → “which is not yet avenged for him”: the negative follows the auxiliary, “yet” moves before the participle, and the indirect object follows it.
- *che dell’ onta sia consorte* ‘that of the shame be partner’ → “that is a partner of his shame”: the subjunctive becomes indicative; the phrase qualifying “partner” moves after that noun. English supplies “a,” and the definite article before *onta* becomes “his.”

> 34 Fece lui disdegnoso; onde sen gìo  
> 35 Senza parlarmi, sì com’ io stimo;  

made him indignant: therefore, as I suppose, he went away without speaking to me;

- *Fece lui disdegnoso* ‘made him disdainful’ → “made him indignant”: the translation takes *disdegnoso* as expressing indignation rather than contempt.
- *onde* ‘whence’ → “therefore”: the connective is rendered explicitly as expressing consequence.
- *sen gìo* ‘himself thence he went’ → “he went away”: the pronominal construction becomes “went away”; *ne* is represented by “away,” while *si* has no separate equivalent.
- *Senza parlarmi* ‘without to speak to me’ → “without speaking to me”: the Italian infinitive becomes an English gerund, and the attached pronoun becomes the separate phrase “to me.”
- *sì com’ io stimo* ‘so as I suppose’ → “as I suppose”: *sì* has no separate equivalent, and the clause moves from the end to a position before “he went away.”

## Lines 36–45 — Pity and Piercing Lamentations

> 36 E in ciò m’ ha fatto egli a sè più pio.  

and in that has made me pity him the more.”

- *m’ ha fatto egli a sè più pio* ‘me has made he toward himself more compassionate’ → “has made me pity him the more”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the explicit subject *egli* is omitted. The adjective becomes the verb “pity”; the reflexive phrase ‘toward himself’ becomes its object “him”; and “the” is added before the comparative.

> 37 Così parlammo insino al luogo primo,  
> 38 Che dello scoglio l’ altra valle mostra,  
> 39 Se più lume vi fosse, tutto ad imo.  

Thus we spake, up to the first place of the cliff, which shews the other valley, if more light were there, quite to the bottom.

- *al luogo primo* ‘to the place first’ → “to the first place”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *dello scoglio* ‘of/from the rock’ → “of the cliff”: “cliff” gives a more particular sense to *scoglio*. The translation takes the phrase as qualifying “place” and moves it before “which”, rather than retaining its position within the relative clause.
- *l’ altra valle mostra* ‘the other valley shows’ → “shews the other valley”: the object moves after the verb.
- *Se più lume vi fosse* ‘if more light there were’ → “if more light were there”: the locative “there” moves after the verb.

> 40 Quando noi fummo in su l’ ultima chiostra  
> 41 Di Malebolge, sì che i suoi conversi  
> 42 Potean parere alla veduta nostra,  

When we were above the last cloister of Malebolge, so that its lay-brethren could appear to our view,

- *in su* ‘upon’ → “above”: the translation expresses position above rather than position on.
- *alla veduta nostra* ‘to the view our’ → “to our view”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 43 Lamenti saettaron me diversi,  
> 44 Che di pietà ferrati avean gli strali;  

lamentations pierced me, manifold, which had their arrows barbed with pity;

- *saettaron* ‘shot arrows at’ → “pierced”: the act of shooting is rendered as its penetrating effect.
- *di pietà ferrati avean gli strali* ‘with pity iron-tipped had the arrows’ → “had their arrows barbed with pity”: the verb and object precede the participial phrase, and “with pity” follows rather than precedes the participle. “Barbed” replaces ‘iron-tipped’, and the definite article becomes the possessive “their”.

> 45 Ond’ io gli orecchi con le man copersi.  

whereat I covered my ears with my hands.

- *Ond’* ‘wherefore’ → “whereat”: a connective expressing consequence becomes one presenting the action as a response.
- *gli orecchi con le man copersi* ‘the ears with the hands covered’ → “covered my ears with my hands”: the verb moves before the object and the accompanying prepositional phrase; both definite articles become possessive “my”.

## Lines 46–57 — Pain and Stench Among the Falsifiers

> 46 Qual dolor fora, se degli spedali  
> 47 Di Valdichiana tra il luglio e il settembre,  
> 48 E di Maremma, e di Sardigna i mali  
> 49 Fossere in una fossa tutti insembre,  

Such grief as there would be, if the diseases in the hospitals of Valdichiana, between July and September, and of Maremma and Sardinia, were all together in one ditch:

- *Qual dolor fora* ‘What pain would be’ → “Such grief as there would be”: the comparative *qual* is expanded into “Such … as”; “there” supplies an existential construction, and *dolor* is rendered as “grief” rather than ‘pain’.
- *degli spedali … i mali* ‘of the hospitals … the ailments’ → “the diseases in the hospitals”: the subject is moved before the phrase modifying it; ‘of’ becomes “in”, and the broader *mali* is rendered specifically as “diseases”.
- *tra il luglio e il settembre* ‘between the July and the September’ → “between July and September”: the articles before the months are omitted.
- *e di Sardigna* ‘and of Sardinia’ → “and Sardinia”: the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *Fossere in una fossa tutti insembre* ‘were in a ditch all together’ → “were all together in one ditch”: “all together” moves before the prepositional phrase, and *una* is rendered explicitly as the numeral “one”.

> 50 Tal era quivi; e tal puzzo n’ usciva,  
> 51 Qual suole uscir dalle marcite membre.  

such was there here; and such stench issued thence, as is wont to issue from putrid limbs.

- *Tal era quivi* ‘Such was there’ → “such was there here”: “there” is supplied to form an existential construction, while the Italian locative *quivi*, ‘there’, becomes “here”.
- *n’ usciva* ‘from it was coming out’ → “issued thence”: the preceding pronominal expression becomes the following adverb “thence”; the imperfect is rendered by a simple past rather than an explicitly ongoing construction.
- *dalle marcite membre* ‘from the rotted limbs’ → “from putrid limbs”: the definite article is omitted, and the participle *marcite* becomes the adjective “putrid”.

> 52 Noi discendemmo in su l’ ultima riva  
> 53 Del lungo scoglio, pur da man sinistra,  

We descended on the last bank of the long cliff, again to the left hand;

- *in su* ‘onto’ → “on”: the compound preposition is reduced to “on”, leaving the movement to be expressed by “descended”.
- *pur* ‘still’ → “again”: continued direction is expressed as repetition.
- *da man sinistra* ‘on hand left’ → “to the left hand”: the preposition becomes “to”, an article is supplied, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 54 Ed allor fu la mia vista più viva  

and then my sight was more vivid,

- *fu la mia vista* ‘was my sight’ → “my sight was”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *più viva* ‘more lively’ → “more vivid”: the adjective is rendered in terms of visual distinctness rather than liveliness.

> 55 Giù vêr lo fondo, dove la ministra  
> 56 Dell’ alto Sire, infallibil Giustizia,  
> 57 Punisce i falsator che qui registra.  

down towards the depth in which the ministress of the Great Sire, infallible Justice, punishes the falsifiers that she here registers.

- *lo fondo* ‘the bottom’ → “the depth”: “depth” replaces the more concrete designation of the bottom.
- *dove* ‘where’ → “in which”: the relative adverb becomes a preposition and relative pronoun.
- *alto* ‘high’ → “Great”: the adjective of height or exalted rank is rendered as one of greatness.

## Lines 58–72 — The Spirits Languishing in Heaps

> 58 Non credo ch’ a veder maggior tristizia  
> 59 Fosse in Egina il popol tutto infermo,  

I do not think it was a greater sorrow to see the people in Ægina all infirm;

- *Non credo ch’ a veder maggior tristizia Fosse* ‘I believe not that to see greater sadness was’ → “I do not think it was a greater sorrow to see”: ‘believe’ becomes “think”; English supplies the negative auxiliary, omits ‘that’, and supplies an impersonal “it” and the article “a”. The verb precedes its complement rather than following it, and the infinitive moves from before ‘greater sadness’ to after “a greater sorrow”.
- *in Egina il popol tutto infermo* ‘in Ægina the people all infirm’ → “the people in Ægina all infirm”: the place phrase follows “the people” rather than preceding it.

> 60 Quando fu l’ aer sì pien di malizia,  

when the air was so malignant,

- *fu l’ aer* ‘was the air’ → “the air was”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *sì pien di malizia* ‘so full of malignity’ → “so malignant”: the construction expressing fullness becomes a direct adjectival description.

> 61 Che gli animali, infino al picciol vermo,  
> 62 Cascaron tutti, e poi le genti antiche,  
> 63 Secondo che i poeti hanno per fermo,  
> 64 Si ristorar di seme di formiche;  

that every animal, even to the little worm, dropt down—and afterwards, as Poets hold for sure, the ancient peoples were restored from seed of ants—

- *gli animali ... tutti* ‘the animals ... all’ → “every animal”: the plural becomes singular, and the following quantifier becomes a preceding determiner.
- *infino al* ‘as far as the’ → “even to the”: the expression of extent becomes one emphasizing inclusion.
- *Cascaron* ‘fell’ → “dropt down”: the single verb becomes a verb with a directional adverb.
- *le genti antiche* ‘the peoples ancient’ → “the ancient peoples”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Secondo che i poeti hanno per fermo* ‘according as the poets have for certain’ → “as Poets hold for sure”: ‘according as’ is shortened to “as”, the article is omitted, and ‘have for certain’ becomes “hold for sure”. The clause moves before the subject “the ancient peoples”.
- *Si ristorar* ‘restored themselves’ → “were restored”: the reflexive construction is rendered as a passive.

> 65 Ch’ era a veder per quella oscura valle  
> 66 Languir gli spirti per diverse biche.  

than it was to see, through that dim valley, the spirits languishing in diverse heaps.

- *Ch’ era* ‘than was’ → “than it was”: English supplies an impersonal “it” to complete the comparison.
- *oscura* ‘dark’ → “dim”: the adjective is rendered with a word suggesting reduced light rather than darkness.
- *Languir gli spirti* ‘languish the spirits’ → “the spirits languishing”: the subject of the infinitive moves before it, and the infinitive becomes a participle.

> 67 Qual sovra il ventre, e qual sovra le spalle  
> 68 L’ un dell’ altro giacea; e qual carpone  
> 69 Si trasmutava per lo tristo calle.  

This upon the belly, and that upon the shoulders of the other lay; and some were crawling on along the dismal path.

- *Qual ... e qual ... e qual* ‘One ... and another ... and another’ → “This”, “and that”, “and some”: the distributive pronouns become demonstratives in the first two instances and an indefinite plural in the third.
- *L’ un dell’ altro giacea* ‘the one of the other lay’ → “of the other lay”: ‘the one’, which resumes the preceding distributive construction, is omitted.
- *carpone Si trasmutava* ‘on all fours shifted himself’ → “were crawling on”: the adverbial description of posture is absorbed into “crawling”; the reflexive verb becomes an intransitive progressive construction with “on”. The singular becomes plural to agree with “some”.
- *per lo tristo calle* ‘through the sad path’ → “along the dismal path”: “along” replaces ‘through’, and “dismal” renders ‘sad’.

> 70 Passo passo andavam senza sermone,  
> 71 Guardando ed ascoltando gli ammalati,  
> 72 Che non potean levar le lor persone.  

Step by step we went, without speech, looking at and listening to the sick who could not raise their bodies.

- *Passo passo* ‘step step’ → “Step by step”: English supplies “by” between the repeated nouns.
- *andavam* ‘we were going’ → “we went”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past.
- *Guardando ed ascoltando* ‘watching and listening’ → “looking at and listening to”: “looking at” replaces the transitive verb ‘watching’, and English supplies prepositions before the shared object.
- *persone* ‘persons’ → “bodies”: the translation makes the physical sense of the noun explicit.

## Lines 73–81 — Two Sufferers Scratching Their Scabs

> 73 Io vidi duo sedere a sè appoggiati,  

I saw two sit leaning on each other,

- *a sè appoggiati* ‘leant against themselves’ → “leaning on each other”: the reflexive expression is interpreted reciprocally; the past participle becomes an English present participle describing their posture.

> 74 Come a scaldar, s’ appoggia tegghia a tegghia,  

as pan is leant on pan to warm:

- *Come a scaldar, s’appoggia tegghia a tegghia* ‘as to warm, one leans pan against pan’ → “as pan is leant on pan to warm”: the purpose phrase moves from before the verb to the end of the clause; the Italian *si* construction becomes an explicit English passive, with “pan” placed before the verb; the relation expressed by *a* is rendered as “on”.

> 75 Dal capo ai piè di schianze maculati:  

from head to foot spotted with scabs.

- *Dal capo ai piè* ‘from the head to the feet’ → “from head to foot”: the articles disappear in the English idiom, and plural *piè* becomes singular “foot”.
- *di schianze maculati* ‘with scabs spotted’ → “spotted with scabs”: the participle moves before its complement.

> 76 E non vidi giammai menare stregghia  
> 77 A ragazzo aspettato dal signorso,  
> 78 Nè a colui che mal volentier vegghia;  

And never did I see currycomb plied by stable-boy for whom his master waits, nor by one who stays unwillingly awake;

- *E non vidi giammai* ‘And I did not ever see’ → “And never did I see”: the Italian negative construction becomes “never”, placed before the verb and triggering English auxiliary–subject inversion.
- *menare stregghia* ‘ply currycomb’ → “currycomb plied”: the active infinitive becomes a passive participle, and the noun precedes rather than follows it.
- *A ragazzo* ‘by a boy’ → “by stable-boy”: “stable-boy” makes the boy’s occupation explicit.
- *aspettato dal signorso* ‘awaited by his master’ → “for whom his master waits”: the passive participial modifier becomes an active relative clause, with the master as its subject.
- *Nè a colui* ‘nor by that one’ → “nor by one”: the demonstrative becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *mal volentier vegghia* ‘unwillingly stays awake’ → “stays unwillingly awake”: the adverb moves from before the verb to within the English verb phrase.

> 79 Come ciascun menava spesso il morso  
> 80 Dell’ unghie sovra sè per la gran rabbia  
> 81 Del pizzicor, che non ha più soccorso:  

as each of these plied thick the clawing of his nails upon himself, for the great fury of their itch which has no other succour.

- *ciascun* ‘each’ → “each of these”: “of these” is supplied to identify the persons referred to.
- *spesso* ‘often’ → “thick”: frequency is expressed through an adverb suggesting densely successive strokes.
- *il morso Dell’ unghie* ‘the bite of the nails’ → “the clawing of his nails”: the metaphorical ‘bite’ becomes the action “clawing”, and the definite article before *unghie* becomes a possessive.
- *Del pizzicor* ‘of the itch’ → “of their itch”: the definite article becomes a plural possessive.
- *più* ‘more’ → “other”: the translation interprets the unavailable succour as an alternative rather than further succour.

## Lines 82–96 — Scratching and Questioning

> 82 E si traevan giù l’ unghie la scabbia,  
> 83 Come coltel di scardova le scaglie,  
> 84 O d’ altro pesce che più larghe l’ abbia.  

And so the nails drew down the scurf, as does a knife the scales from bream or other fish that has them larger.

- *traevan giù l’unghie* ‘drew down the nails’ → “the nails drew down”: the postposed subject moves before the verb; the Italian imperfect becomes an English simple past.
- *la scabbia* ‘the scab’ → “the scurf”: the crust is rendered as flaky material.
- *Come coltel* ‘as a knife’ → “as does a knife”: “does” supplies the verb left understood in Italian.
- *di scardova le scaglie* ‘of bream the scales’ → “the scales from bream”: the noun precedes its modifying phrase, and “from” replaces ‘of’.
- *d’altro pesce* ‘of other fish’ → “other fish”: the repeated preposition is omitted; the preceding “from” governs both kinds of fish.
- *più larghe l’abbia* ‘larger them may have’ → “has them larger”: the verb precedes the object and adjective, and the subjunctive becomes an indicative.

> 85 O tu, che colle dita ti dismaglie,  
> 86 Cominciò il Duca mio a un di loro,  
> 87 E che fai d’ esse tal volta tanaglie;  

“O thou!” began my Guide to one of them, “who with thy fingers dismailest thyself, and sometimes makest pincers of them;

- *colle dita* ‘with the fingers’ → “with thy fingers”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Cominciò il Duca mio a un di loro* ‘began the Leader my to one of them’ → “began my Guide to one of them”: the reporting clause moves forward, interrupting the address before its first relative clause rather than after it; ‘Leader’ becomes “Guide”, and the possessive precedes its noun.
- *E che fai d’esse tal volta tanaglie* ‘and who makest of them sometimes pincers’ → “and sometimes makest pincers of them”: the repeated relative pronoun is omitted, the adverb moves before the verb, and “pincers” precedes “of them”.

> 88 Dimmi s’ alcun Latino è tra costoro  
> 89 Che son quinc’ entro, se l’ unghia ti basti  
> 90 Eternalmente a cotesto lavoro.  

tell me if there be any Latian among these who are here within: so may thy nails eternally suffice thee for that work.”

- *s’alcun Latino è* ‘if any Latian is’ → “if there be any Latian”: existential “there” is supplied, the subject follows the verb, and the indicative becomes the subjunctive “be”.
- *se l’unghia ti basti Eternalmente* ‘if the nail suffice thee eternally’ → “so may thy nails eternally suffice thee”: the Italian formula introducing a wish becomes an explicit English wish with “so may”; the singular noun becomes plural, the article becomes a possessive, the adverb moves before the verb, and the object pronoun follows it.

> 91 Latin sem noi, che tu vedi sì guasti  
> 92 Qui ambodue, rispose l’ un piangendo;  
> 93 Ma tu chi sei, che di noi dimandasti?  

“Latians are we, whom thou seest so disfigured here, both of us,” replied the one weeping; “but who art thou that hast inquired of us?”

- *sì guasti* ‘so ruined’ → “so disfigured”: the broader adjective is rendered by one specifically describing damaged appearance.
- *ambodue* ‘both’ → “both of us”: “of us” makes the reference explicit.
- *Ma tu chi sei* ‘but thou who art’ → “but who art thou”: the subject moves after the interrogative and verb.
- *che di noi dimandasti* ‘that of us didst inquire’ → “that hast inquired of us”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb, and the Italian simple past becomes an English present perfect.

> 94 E il Duca disse: Io son un, che discendo  
> 95 Con questo vivo giù di balzo in balzo,  
> 96 E di mostrar l’ Inferno a lui intendo.  

And the Guide said: “I am one, who with this living man descend from steep to steep, and mean to shew him Hell.”

- *Duca* ‘Leader’ → “Guide”: a different designation is used.
- *che discendo Con questo vivo* ‘who descend with this living one’ → “who with this living man descend”: the accompanying phrase moves before the verb, and the substantival adjective is expanded with the noun “man”.
- *giù di balzo in balzo* ‘down from ledge to ledge’ → “from steep to steep”: ‘down’ is omitted, and ‘ledge’ is rendered as “steep”.
- *E di mostrar l’Inferno a lui intendo* ‘and to show Hell to him I intend’ → “and mean to shew him Hell”: the governing verb moves before its infinitive, ‘intend’ becomes “mean”, and the prepositional phrase ‘to him’ becomes an indirect object before “Hell”.

## Lines 97–108 — An Appeal for Their Names

> 97 Allor si ruppe lo comun rincalzo,  
> 98 E tremando ciascuno a me si volse  
> 99 Con altri che l’ udiron di rimbalzo.  

Then they sprang asunder, and each turned trembling towards me, with others that by echo heard him.

- *si ruppe lo comun rincalzo* ‘the mutual support broke’ → “they sprang asunder”: the breaking of a shared support becomes an action performed by the people supporting one another; the singular noun-subject is replaced by “they”, and “sprang” adds a suggestion of sudden movement.
- *tremando ciascuno a me si volse* ‘trembling, each towards me turned himself’ → “each turned trembling towards me”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive “turned”; “trembling” moves after the verb, and “towards me” moves from before it to the end.
- *di rimbalzo* ‘by rebound’ → “by echo”: the metaphor of rebounding is expressed specifically as reflected sound; the phrase moves before “heard him”.

> 100 Lo buon Maestro a me tutto s’ accolse,  
> 101 Dicendo: Di’ a lor ciò che tu vuoli.  

The kind Master to me directed himself wholly, saying: “Tell them what thou wishest.”

- *buon* ‘good’ → “kind”: general goodness is rendered specifically as kindness.
- *tutto s’accolse* ‘wholly gathered himself’ → “directed himself wholly”: the reflexive expression for gathering or concentrating oneself becomes one of directing oneself; “wholly” moves after the verb.
- *Di’ a lor* ‘Say to them’ → “Tell them”: “Tell” replaces ‘Say’, and its indirect object requires no preposition.
- *ciò che* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun are combined into a single relative expression.

> 102 Ed io incominciai, poscia ch’ ei volse:  

And I began, as he desired:

- *poscia ch’ei volse* ‘after he willed it’ → “as he desired”: the temporal construction ‘after’ becomes one of conformity, “as”; the translation presents the beginning as following his wishes rather than explicitly following them in time.

> 103 Se la vostra memoria non s’imboli  
> 104 Nel primo mondo dall’ umane menti,  
> 105 Ma s’ ella viva sotto molti soli;  

“So may your memory not fade away from human minds in the first world, but may it live under many suns:

- *Se* ‘if’ → “So may”: the conditional form introducing a wish is rendered as an explicit English wish with “may”.
- *non s’imboli* ‘not be stolen away’ → “not fade away”: the image of memory being stolen or slipping away becomes one of gradual fading.
- *Nel primo mondo dall’umane menti* ‘in the first world from the human minds’ → “from human minds in the first world”: the two prepositional phrases exchange places, and the definite article before ‘human minds’ is omitted.
- *Ma s’ella viva* ‘but if it live’ → “but may it live”: the repeated conditional form with a subjunctive is again rendered as an explicit wish, replacing ‘if’ with “may”.

> 106 Ditemi chi voi siete, e di che genti:  

tell me who ye are, and of what people.

- *di che genti* ‘of what peoples’ → “of what people”: the Italian plural is rendered by singular collective “people”.

> 107 La vostra sconcia e fastidiosa pena  
> 108 Di palesarvi a me non vi spaventi.  

Let not your ugly and disgusting punishment frighten you from revealing yourselves to me.”

- *La vostra sconcia e fastidiosa pena Di palesarvi a me non vi spaventi* ‘your ugly and disgusting punishment, from revealing yourselves to me, may not frighten you’ → “Let not your ugly and disgusting punishment frighten you from revealing yourselves to me”: the negative subjunctive injunction becomes an English construction with “Let”; the object pronoun moves after “frighten”, the injunction’s opening moves before the noun-subject, and the phrase ‘from revealing yourselves to me’ moves from before the main verb to after it.

## Lines 109–123 — Alchemy and Sienese Vanity

> 109 Io fui d’ Arezzo, e Albero da Siena,  
> 110 Rispose l’ un, mi fe’ mettere al fuoco;  

“I was of Arezzo,” replied the one, “and Albert of Siena had me burned;

- *e Albero da Siena, Rispose l’un* ‘and Albert of Siena, replied the one’ → “replied the one, “and Albert of Siena”: the reporting clause is moved before the second coordinated statement, rather than interrupting it after its subject.
- *mi fe’ mettere al fuoco* ‘made me be put to the fire’ → “had me burned”: the causative construction is recast with “had”, and being put to the fire becomes being burned.

> 111 Ma quel, per ch’ io mori’, qui non mi mena.  

but what I died for does not bring me here.

- *quel, per ch’ io mori’* ‘that for which I died’ → “what I died for”: the demonstrative and relative become the single relative “what”, and the preposition moves after the verb.
- *qui non mi mena* ‘here does not lead me’ → “does not bring me here”: ‘lead’ becomes “bring”; the place adverb moves to the end, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 112 Ver è, ch’ io dissi a lui, parlando a giuoco:  
> 113 Io mi saprei levar per l’ aere a volo:  

’Tis true, I said to him, speaking in jest: ‘I could raise myself through the air in flight.’

- *Ver è, ch’* ‘true it is that’ → “’Tis true,”: the predicate adjective follows the verb rather than preceding it, and the conjunction ‘that’ is omitted.
- *Io mi saprei levar* ‘I myself would know how to raise’ → “I could raise myself”: conditional ‘know how to’ becomes the modal “could”, and the reflexive pronoun moves after “raise”.

> 114 E quei che avea vaghezza e senno poco,  
> 115 Volle ch’ io gli mostrassi l’ arte; e solo  
> 116 Perch’ io nol feci Dedalo, mi fece  
> 117 Ardere a tal, che l’ avea per figliuolo.  

And he, who had a fond desire and little wit, willed that I should shew him the art; and only because I made him not a Dædalus, he made me be burned by one who had him for a son.

- *vaghezza* ‘desire’ → “a fond desire”: an indefinite article and the qualifying adjective “fond” are supplied.
- *senno poco* ‘wit little’ → “little wit”: the modifier is placed before the noun.
- *ch’ io gli mostrassi l’arte* ‘that I to him should show the art’ → “that I should shew him the art”: the indirect-object pronoun moves after the verb; the Italian imperfect subjunctive is expressed by “should” and an infinitive.
- *Perch’ io nol feci Dedalo* ‘because I did not make him Daedalus’ → “because I made him not a Dædalus”: the negative follows the verb and object rather than preceding them; the article “a” makes the proper name designate a person of that kind.
- *mi fece Ardere* ‘made me burn’ → “he made me be burned”: the Italian causative with an active infinitive becomes a causative with a passive infinitive.
- *a tal* ‘by such a one’ → “by one”: the indefinite expression is simplified, leaving ‘such’ unexpressed.

> 118 Ma nell’ ultima bolgia delle diece  
> 119 Me per alchimia, che nel mondo usai,  
> 120 Dannò Minos, a cui fallir non lece.  

But to the last budget of the ten, for the alchemy that I practised in the world, Minos, who may not err, condemned me.”

- *nell’ultima bolgia delle diece* ‘in the last pouch of the ten’ → “to the last budget of the ten”: “to” replaces ‘in’, expressing the destination of the condemnation.
- *Me … Dannò Minos* ‘me … condemned Minos’ → “Minos, who may not err, condemned me”: the subject moves before the verb, carrying its relative clause with it, while the object moves from its emphatic earlier position to follow the verb.
- *per alchimia* ‘for alchemy’ → “for the alchemy”: the definite article is supplied.
- *che nel mondo usai* ‘that in the world I practised’ → “that I practised in the world”: the place phrase moves after the verb.
- *a cui fallir non lece* ‘to whom to err is not permitted’ → “who may not err”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal modal construction, with the relative pronoun as subject rather than the complement of a preposition.

> 121 Ed io dissi al Poeta: Or fu giammai  
> 122 Gente sì vana come la Sanese?  
> 123 Certo non la Francesca sì d’ assai.  

And I said to the Poet: “Now was there ever people so vain as the Sienese? Certainly the French not so by far.”

- *fu giammai* ‘was ever’ → “was there ever”: existential “there” is supplied.
- *Certo non la Francesca* ‘certainly not the French’ → “Certainly the French not”: the negative moves after the expression designating the people.
- *d’assai* ‘by much’ → “by far”: the expression of degree is rendered by the English idiom “by far”.

## Lines 124–129 — Stricca’s Moderation and Niccolò’s Cloves

> 124 Onde l’ altro lebbroso, che m’ intese,  
> 125 Rispose al detto mio: Tranne lo Stricca,  
> 126 Che seppe far le temperate spese;  

Whereat the other leper, who heard me, responded to my words: “Except the Stricca who contrived to spend so moderately;

- *Onde* ‘whence’ → “Whereat”: the connective is expressed in terms of occasion rather than origin.
- *m’ intese* ‘me heard’ → “heard me”: the object pronoun moves from before the verb to after it.
- *al detto mio* ‘to the saying of mine’ → “to my words”: the singular substantivized participle *detto* becomes the plural noun “words”, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *seppe far le temperate spese* ‘knew how to make the moderate expenditures’ → “contrived to spend so moderately”: *seppe* is interpreted as successful management rather than knowledge; the verb-and-noun construction becomes “spend”, the adjective becomes the adverb “moderately”, and “so” is supplied.

> 127 E Niccolò, che la costuma ricca  
> 128 Del garofano prima discoperse  
> 129 Nell’ orto, dove tal seme s’ appicca;  

and Niccolò, who first discovered the costly usage of the clove, in the garden where such seed takes root;

- *la costuma ricca Del garofano* ‘the usage rich of the clove’ → “the costly usage of the clove”: “costly” renders *ricca*; the adjective moves before its noun, and the whole object moves from before “first discovered” to after it.
- *s’ appicca* ‘attaches itself’ → “takes root”: the reflexive verb is rendered by a nonreflexive idiom specifying how the seed establishes itself.

## Lines 130–139 — Sienese Extravagance and Capocchio’s Identity

> 130 E tranne la brigata, in che disperse  
> 131 Caccia d’ Ascian la vigna e la gran fronda,  
> 132 E l’ Abbagliato il suo senno proferse.  

and except the company in which Caccia of Asciano squandered his vineyard and his great forest, and the Abbagliato shewed his wit.

- *disperse Caccia d’ Ascian* ‘scattered Caccia of Asciano’ → “Caccia of Asciano squandered”: the subject moves before the verb, and the verb is rendered specifically as wasteful expenditure.
- *la vigna* ‘the vineyard’ → “his vineyard”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *la gran fronda* ‘the great foliage’ → “his great forest”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “forest” interprets *fronda*, literally ‘foliage’, as woodland.
- *il suo senno proferse* ‘his judgement brought forth’ → “shewed his wit”: the verb moves before its object; ‘brought forth’ becomes “shewed”, and ‘judgement’ becomes “wit”.

> 133 Ma perchè sappi chi sì ti seconda  
> 134 Contra i Sanesi, aguzza vêr me l’ occhio,  
> 135 Sì che la faccia mia ben ti risponda;  

But that thou mayest know who thus seconds thee against the Sienese, sharpen thine eye towards me, that my face may give thee right response;

- *perchè sappi* ‘so that you know’ → “that thou mayest know”: the purpose conjunction is rendered by “that”, and the Italian subjunctive is expressed through “mayest”.
- *ti seconda* ‘thee seconds’ → “seconds thee”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *aguzza vêr me l’ occhio* ‘sharpen towards me the eye’ → “sharpen thine eye towards me”: the direct object moves before the directional phrase, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Sì che* ‘so that’ → “that”: *sì* has no separate equivalent.
- *la faccia mia* ‘the face mine’ → “my face”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *ben ti risponda* ‘well to thee respond’ → “may give thee right response”: the subjunctive is expressed through “may”; the verb ‘respond’ becomes “give” with the noun “response”, and the adverb ‘well’ becomes the adjective “right”.

> 136 Sì vedrai ch’ io son l’ ombra di Capocchio,  
> 137 Che falsai li metalli con alchimia,  

so shalt thou see I am the shadow of Capocchio, who falsified the metals by alchemy.

- *Sì vedrai* ‘thus you will see’ → “so shalt thou see”: the future is expressed with “shalt”, with the subject placed after the auxiliary.
- *ch’* ‘that’ → “”: the conjunction introducing the clause dependent on ‘see’ is omitted.
- *con alchimia* ‘with alchemy’ → “by alchemy”: “by” replaces ‘with’ to express the means employed.

> 138 E ten dee ricordar, se ben t’ adocchio,  
> 139 Com’ io fui di natura buona scimia.  

And thou must recollect, if I rightly eye thee, how good an ape I was of Nature.”

- *ten dee ricordar* ‘to thee it must come back to mind thereof’ → “thou must recollect”: the construction with recollection coming to someone becomes a personal construction in which that person recollects; *ne*, ‘thereof’, has no separate equivalent.
- *se ben t’ adocchio* ‘if well thee I eye’ → “if I rightly eye thee”: ‘well’ becomes “rightly”, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Com’ io fui di natura buona scimia* ‘how I was of nature good ape’ → “how good an ape I was of Nature”: the predicate is brought forward, with “how” directly modifying “good”; the indefinite article is supplied, and ‘of nature’ moves after the noun it qualifies.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Recasting

Carlyle keeps much of the Italian’s vocabulary, imagery, and rhetorical shape, while regularly adjusting its order to English usage. Subjects and verbs move before objects or complements placed earlier in Italian; adjectives and possessives generally move before their nouns. Thus *miglia ventiduo* becomes “two-and-twenty miles,” and *di schianze maculati* becomes “spotted with scabs.” More extensive rearrangement brings a governing verb before its infinitive or relocates a reporting clause. Throughout, English also supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending, and omits the Italian articles before possessives.

Changes of construction often make the English more idiomatic or explicit. *Eran vaghe* becomes “they longed,” replacing an adjective with a verb; *più pio* becomes “pity him the more,” turning a quality into an action. Italian subjunctives expressing commands or wishes become constructions with “let” or “may,” while *Se tu avessi* becomes “Hadst thou,” marking the condition by inversion rather than a conjunction. Reflexive and impersonal constructions are variously recast as intransitives, passives, or personal statements: *si soffolge* becomes “rest,” and *a cui fallir non lece* becomes “who may not err.” Italian imperfects frequently appear as English simple pasts, although progressive forms are also used.

Word choice ranges from close verbal echoes to explanatory interpretation. *Luci* becomes “eyes,” *persone* becomes “bodies,” and *ragazzo* becomes “stable-boy,” making a contextual meaning explicit. Elsewhere the image changes: *il morso Dell’ unghie* becomes “the clawing of his nails,” and *non s’imboli* becomes “not fade away.” Additions such as “own” in “of my own blood” and “of these” after “each” add emphasis or clarify reference. Repeated prepositions, relative pronouns, and conjunctions are sometimes omitted. A more substantial adjustment occurs when *che tu non vedi* becomes “than thou dost see”: a negative relative clause is rendered comparatively, without a separate equivalent for *non*.

Nevertheless, the translation often preserves conspicuous features of the original rather than smoothing them away. “Latians are we” retains the inverted presentation of *Latin sem noi*, while “dismailest thyself” closely follows the unusual metaphor of *ti dismaglie*. Expressions such as “under many suns” and “an ape I was of Nature” likewise retain the Italian’s figurative terms. The recurring departures therefore coexist with considerable verbal closeness: Carlyle reorganizes grammar and clarifies reference, but frequently allows the Italian’s distinctive phrasing and images to remain audible in his English.
