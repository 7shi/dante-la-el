# Inferno 6

## Lines 1–15 — Rain and Cerberus

> 1 Al tornar della mente, che si chiuse  
> 2 Dinanzi alla pietà de’ duo cognati,  
> 3 Che di tristizia tutto mi confuse,  

On sense returning, which closed itself before the misery of the two kinsfolk that stunned me all with sadness,

- *Al tornar della mente* ‘at the returning of the mind’ → “On sense returning”: the nominal infinitive becomes a participial construction, and “sense” renders *mente* as consciousness rather than mind.
- *pietà* ‘pity’ → “misery”: the translation takes the word as the pitiable condition that evokes compassion, rather than compassion itself.
- *de’ duo cognati* ‘of the two relatives by marriage’ → “of the two kinsfolk”: “kinsfolk” leaves the specifically marital relationship unexpressed.
- *Che di tristizia tutto mi confuse* ‘which with sadness wholly confounded me’ → “that stunned me all with sadness”: “stunned” replaces ‘confounded’; the phrase expressing the cause moves after the verb and object, and *tutto* becomes “all” after “me”.

> 4 Nuovi tormenti e nuovi tormentati  
> 5 Mi veggio intorno, come ch’ io mi muova,  
> 6 E come ch’ io mi volga, e ch’ io mi guati.  

I discern new torments, and new tormented souls, whithersoever I move, and turn, and gaze.

- *Nuovi tormenti e nuovi tormentati* ‘new torments and new tormented ones’ → “new torments, and new tormented souls”: the objects move from before the verb to after it; the substantival participle *tormentati* is expanded with the supplied noun “souls”.
- *Mi veggio intorno* ‘I see around me’ → “I discern”: “discern” replaces ‘see’, and the surrounding position expressed by *mi … intorno* is omitted.
- *come ch’ io mi muova, / E come ch’ io mi volga, e ch’ io mi guati* ‘however I move, and however I turn, and however I gaze’ → “whithersoever I move, and turn, and gaze”: the indefinite clauses receive an explicitly spatial rendering. The repeated conjunctions and explicit subjects are reduced to one introductory “whithersoever I”, and the pronominal Italian verbs become simple English verbs.

> 7 Io sono al terzo cerchio della piova  
> 8 Eterna, maledetta, fredda e greve:  

I am in the Third Circle, that of the eternal, accursed, cold, and heavy rain.

- *al* ‘at the’ → “in the”: the preposition expresses location within rather than at.
- *della piova / Eterna, maledetta, fredda e greve* ‘of the rain eternal, accursed, cold and heavy’ → “that of the eternal, accursed, cold, and heavy rain”: “that” is supplied to introduce an explanatory apposition, and the adjectives move before the noun.

> 9 Regola e qualità mai non l’ è nova.  

Its course and quality is never new:

- *Regola e qualità mai non l’ è nova* ‘rule and quality never is new to it’ → “Its course and quality is never new”: “course” interprets *regola* as the rain’s regular operation. The dative pronoun *l’* becomes the possessive “Its”; the negative combination *mai non* becomes “never”, placed after rather than before the verb.

> 10 Grandine grossa, e acqua tinta, e neve  
> 11 Per l’ aer tenebroso si riversa:  

large hail, and turbid water, and snow, it pours down through the darksome air.

- *Grandine grossa* ‘hail large’ → “large hail”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *acqua tinta* ‘water coloured’ → “turbid water”: the adjective moves before the noun, and “turbid” interprets the colouring as murkiness.
- *Per l’ aer tenebroso* ‘through the air dark’ → “through the darksome air”: the adjective moves before the noun, and the prepositional phrase moves from before the verb to after it.
- *si riversa* ‘pours itself out’ → “it pours down”: the pronominal construction becomes an intransitive verb with a supplied resumptive “it” referring to the preceding list; “down” makes the direction explicit.

> 12 Pute la terra che questo riceve.  

The ground, on which it falls, emits a putrid smell.

- *Pute* ‘stinks’ → “emits a putrid smell”: a single verb becomes a verb-and-object phrase, moved from before the subject to after it.
- *che questo riceve* ‘which receives this’ → “on which it falls”: the receiving ground becomes the location of falling, while the demonstrative object *questo* becomes the subject “it”.

> 13 Cerbero, fiera crudele e diversa,  
> 14 Con tre gole caninamente latra  
> 15 Sovra la gente che quivi è sommersa.  

Cerberus, a monster fierce and strange, with three throats, barks dog-like over those that are immersed in it.

- *fiera crudele e diversa* ‘beast cruel and different’ → “a monster fierce and strange”: an indefinite article is supplied; “monster” replaces ‘beast’, “fierce” replaces ‘cruel’, and “strange” interprets *diversa* as extraordinary rather than merely different.
- *caninamente latra* ‘dog-like barks’ → “barks dog-like”: the adverbial expression moves after the verb.
- *Sovra la gente che quivi è sommersa* ‘over the people that there is submerged’ → “over those that are immersed in it”: the collective singular *gente* becomes the plural pronoun “those”, with corresponding plural “are”. The locative adverb *quivi*, ‘there’, becomes “in it”, explicitly referring back to the rain.

## Lines 16–27 — Cerberus and the Tormented Spirits

> 16 Gli occhi ha vermigli, e la barba unta ed atra,  
> 17 E il ventre largo, e unghiate le mani;  

His eyes are red, his beard gory and black, his belly wide, and clawed his hands.

- *Gli occhi ha vermigli* ‘The eyes he has vermilion’ → “His eyes are red”: the construction with ‘has’ becomes one with “are,” making the eyes the subject; the article becomes a possessive, and ‘vermilion’ becomes the less specific “red”.
- *la barba unta ed atra* ‘the beard greasy and black’ → “his beard gory and black”: the article becomes a possessive; “gory” introduces blood where *unta* means greasy or oily.
- *E il ventre largo* ‘And the belly broad’ → “his belly wide”: the conjunction is omitted, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *e unghiate le mani* ‘and clawed the hands’ → “and clawed his hands”: the article becomes a possessive.

> 18 Graffia gli spirti, gli scuoia, ed isquatra.  

He clutches the spirits, flays, and piecemeal rends them.

- *Graffia* ‘scratches’ → “He clutches”: scratching or clawing becomes grasping.
- *gli scuoia, ed isquatra* ‘them flays, and quarters’ → “flays, and piecemeal rends them”: the object pronoun moves after the coordinated verbs; ‘quarters’ is expanded into “piecemeal rends,” without specifying four parts.

> 19 Urlar gli fa la pioggia come cani:  

The rain makes them howl like dogs.

- *Urlar gli fa la pioggia* ‘Howl them makes the rain’ → “The rain makes them howl”: the subject moves from the end to the beginning, and the infinitive follows the causative verb and its object, as English requires.

> 20 Dell’ un de lati fanno all’ altro schermo;  

With one side they screen the other;

- *Dell’ un de lati* ‘Of the one of the sides’ → “With one side”: ‘of’ becomes instrumental “With,” and the partitive expression is compressed into “one side”.
- *fanno all’ altro schermo* ‘make for the other a screen’ → “they screen the other”: the noun *schermo* becomes the verb “screen,” replacing ‘make a screen’; ‘for the other’ becomes a direct object.

> 21 Volgonsi spesso i miseri profani.  

they often turn themselves, the impious wretches.

- *Volgonsi spesso* ‘Turn themselves often’ → “they often turn themselves”: the adverb moves before the verb.
- *i miseri profani* ‘the wretched profane ones’ → “the impious wretches”: the adjective *miseri* becomes the noun “wretches,” while the substantival *profani* becomes the adjective “impious”. The Italian’s postposed subject becomes an appositional phrase after the supplied “they”.

> 22 Quando ci scorse Cerbero, il gran vermo,  
> 23 Le bocche aperse, e mostrocci le sanne:  
> 24 Non avea membro che tenesse fermo.  

When Cerberus, the great Worm, perceived us, he opened his mouths and shewed his tusks: no limb of him kept still.

- *Quando ci scorse Cerbero, il gran vermo* ‘When us perceived Cerberus, the great worm’ → “When Cerberus, the great Worm, perceived us”: the subject and its apposition move before the verb, and the object pronoun follows it.
- *Le bocche aperse* ‘The mouths he opened’ → “he opened his mouths”: the object moves after the verb, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *e mostrocci le sanne* ‘and showed us the tusks’ → “and shewed his tusks”: the enclitic *-ci*, ‘us,’ is omitted, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *Non avea membro che tenesse fermo* ‘He had no limb that stayed still’ → “no limb of him kept still”: the negative construction with ‘had’ becomes a statement whose subject is “no limb”; possession is expressed by “of him”. The relative clause, with its subjunctive verb, becomes the main clause.

> 25 E il Duca mio distese le sue spanne;  
> 26 Prese la terra, e con piene le pugna  
> 27 La gittò dentro alle bramose canne.  

My guide, spreading his palms, took up earth; and, with full fists, cast it into his ravening gullets.

- *E il Duca mio* ‘And the guide my’ → “My guide”: the initial conjunction is omitted, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *distese le sue spanne* ‘stretched out his spans’ → “spreading his palms”: the finite past-tense verb becomes a present participle, subordinating this action to the taking of earth. *Spanne*, denoting outspread hands, is rendered more specifically as “palms”.
- *Prese la terra* ‘took the earth’ → “took up earth”: “up” is supplied, and the definite article is omitted.
- *con piene le pugna* ‘with full the fists’ → “with full fists”: the definite article is omitted.
- *La gittò* ‘It cast’ → “cast it”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *dentro alle* ‘inside to the’ → “into his”: the directional expression is compressed into one preposition, and the article becomes a possessive.
- *bramose canne* ‘craving pipes’ → “ravening gullets”: “ravening” specifies voracious hunger, while “gullets” makes the anatomical reference of *canne* explicit.

## Lines 28–42 — Cerberus Quieted and a Shade Raised

> 28 Qual è quel cane che abbaiando agugna,  
> 29 E si racqueta poi che il pasto morde,  
> 30 Chè solo a divorarlo intende e pugna;  

As the dog, that barking craves, and grows quiet when he bites his food, for he strains and battles only to devour it;

- *Qual è quel cane* ‘as is that dog’ → “As the dog”: the verb is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes the definite article.
- *si racqueta* ‘quiets himself’ → “grows quiet”: the reflexive verb becomes an intransitive construction with an adjective.
- *poi che* ‘after’ → “when”: the explicit indication of succession becomes a more general temporal connection.
- *il pasto morde* ‘the meal he bites’ → “he bites his food”: the object moves after the verb, the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘meal’ becomes “food”.
- *Chè solo a divorarlo intende e pugna* ‘because only to devour it he applies himself and fights’ → “for he strains and battles only to devour it”: the subordinating conjunction becomes coordinating “for”; *intende* is rendered as exertion rather than application of attention or purpose; and the infinitive phrase moves after the two verbs.

> 31 Cotai se fecer quelle facce lorde  
> 32 Dello demonio Cerbero che introna  
> 33 L’ anime sì, ch’ esser vorrebber sorde.  

so did those squalid visages of Cerberus the Demon, who thunders on the spirits so, that they would fain be deaf.

- *Cotai se fecer quelle facce lorde* ‘such became those filthy faces’ → “so did those squalid visages”: the construction describing a resulting condition becomes “so did”, referring back to the dog's behaviour; ‘filthy faces’ becomes “squalid visages”, with the adjective moved before the noun.
- *Dello demonio Cerbero* ‘of the demon Cerberus’ → “of Cerberus the Demon”: the name and its identifying noun exchange positions.
- *introna* ‘deafens’ → “thunders on”: the transitive verb becomes a verb with a preposition, expressing the noise rather than its deafening effect.
- *L’ anime* ‘the souls’ → “the spirits”: a different designation is used.
- *ch’ esser vorrebber sorde* ‘that to be they would wish deaf’ → “that they would fain be deaf”: the infinitive moves after the expression of wishing, and the conditional verb of desire becomes “would fain”.

> 34 Noi passavam su per l’ ombre, che adona  
> 35 La greve pioggia, e ponevam le piante  
> 36 Sopra lor vanità, che par persona.  

We passed over the shadows whom the heavy rain subdues; and placed our soles upon their emptiness, which seems a body.

- *Noi passavam* ‘we were passing’ → “We passed”: the imperfect's ongoing action is rendered by the English simple past.
- *su per* ‘on over’ → “over”: the combined prepositions are reduced to one.
- *che adona La greve pioggia* ‘whom subdues the heavy rain’ → “whom the heavy rain subdues”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *ponevam le piante* ‘we were placing the soles’ → “placed our soles”: the imperfect becomes the simple past, and the definite article becomes a possessive identifying whose soles they are.
- *persona* ‘a person’ → “a body”: the translation selects the bodily sense rather than rendering the word as ‘person’.

> 37 Elle giacean per terra tutte quante,  
> 38 Fuor d’ una che a seder si levò, ratto  
> 39 Ch’ ella ci vide passarsi davante.  

They all were lying on the ground save one, who sat up forthwith when he saw us pass before him.

- *Elle giacean per terra tutte quante* ‘they were lying on the ground, all of them’ → “They all were lying on the ground”: the concluding expression of totality moves beside the subject and is compressed into “all”.
- *che a seder si levò* ‘who to sit raised herself’ → “who sat up”: the reflexive verb and infinitive of purpose become a single idiomatic verbal phrase; the pronoun no longer marks the grammatical feminine of the Italian.
- *ratto Ch’* ‘as soon as’ → “forthwith when”: the temporal expression becomes an adverb attached to the preceding action followed by a temporal conjunction.
- *ella ci vide passarsi davante* ‘she saw us pass before herself’ → “he saw us pass before him”: feminine agreement with the Italian word for ‘shadow’ becomes masculine reference to the individual; the reflexive *si* is rendered by the non-reflexive pronoun “him” after “before”.

> 40 O tu, che se’ per questo inferno tratto,  
> 41 Mi disse, riconoscimi, se sai:  
> 42 Tu fosti, prima ch’ io disfatto, fatto.  

“O thou, who through this Hell art led,” he said to me; “recognise me if thou mayest: thou wast made before I was unmade.”

- *che se’ per questo inferno tratto* ‘who art through this hell drawn’ → “who through this Hell art led”: the prepositional phrase moves before the auxiliary, and ‘drawn’ becomes “led”.
- *Mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “he said to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *se sai* ‘if thou knowest how’ → “if thou mayest”: a verb of knowing becomes a modal expressing ability.
- *Tu fosti, prima ch’ io disfatto, fatto* ‘thou wast, before I unmade, made’ → “thou wast made before I was unmade”: “made” moves beside its auxiliary, before the intervening temporal clause, and “was” supplies the verb left implicit in that clause.

## Lines 43–54 — Ciacco Names Himself and His Sin

> 43 Ed io a lei; L’ angoscia che tu hai  
> 44 Forse ti tira fuor della mia mente,  
> 45 Sì che non par ch’ io ti vedessi mai.  

And I to him: “The anguish which thou hast, perhaps withdraws thee from my memory, so that it seems not as if I ever saw thee.

- *lei* ‘her’ → “him”: the feminine pronoun is rendered as masculine.
- *ti tira fuor della mia mente* ‘thee pulls out of my mind’ → “withdraws thee from my memory”: the object pronoun moves after the verb; ‘pulls out’ becomes “withdraws”, and ‘mind’ is rendered more specifically as “memory”.
- *non par ch’ io ti vedessi mai* ‘it does not seem that I ever saw thee’ → “it seems not as if I ever saw thee”: the negative follows the verb, and the subordinate statement introduced by ‘that’ becomes an “as if” construction. The Italian imperfect subjunctive is rendered by the English simple past.

> 46 Ma dimmi chi tu se’, che in sì dolente  
> 47 Luogo se’ messa, ed a sì fatta pena,  
> 48 Che s’ altra è maggior, nulla è sì spiacente.  

But tell me who art thou, that art put in such a doleful place, and in such punishment; that, though other may be greater, none is so displeasing.”

- *chi tu se’* ‘who thou art’ → “who art thou”: the subject and verb are inverted within the indirect question.
- *in sì dolente Luogo se’ messa* ‘in so doleful a place art put’ → “art put in such a doleful place”: the verb precedes the place phrase, and ‘so doleful a’ becomes “such a doleful”.
- *a sì fatta pena* ‘to punishment of such a kind’ → “in such punishment”: *a* becomes “in”, while *sì fatta* is compressed into “such”.
- *s’ altra è maggior* ‘if another is greater’ → “though other may be greater”: the conditional is interpreted concessively; the indicative ‘is’ becomes “may be”, and ‘another’ is rendered as “other”.

> 49 Ed egli a me: La tua città, ch’ è piena  
> 50 D’ invidia sì, che già trabocca il sacco,  
> 51 Seco mi tenne in la vita serena.  

And he to me: “Thy city, which is so full of envy that the sack already overflows, contained me in the clear life.

- *è piena D’ invidia sì* ‘is full of envy so’ → “is so full of envy”: the degree word moves before the adjective it qualifies.
- *già trabocca il sacco* ‘already overflows the sack’ → “the sack already overflows”: the subject moves from after the verb to before it.
- *Seco mi tenne* ‘with itself it kept me’ → “contained me”: the verb “contained” absorbs the sense of *Seco*, ‘with itself’, and the object pronoun follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *vita serena* ‘life serene’ → “clear life”: ‘serene’ is rendered as “clear”, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 52 Voi, cittadini, mi chiamaste Ciacco:  

You, citizens, called me Ciacco:

- *mi chiamaste* ‘me you called’ → “called me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 53 Per la dannosa colpa della gola,  
> 54 Come tu vedi, alla pioggia mi fiacco;  

for the baneful crime of gluttony, as thou seest, I languish in the rain.

- *colpa* ‘fault’ → “crime”: moral fault is expressed through a more explicitly juridical noun.
- *gola* ‘throat’ → “gluttony”: the bodily term is rendered by the vice it denotes metonymically.
- *alla pioggia mi fiacco* ‘at the rain I weaken myself’ → “I languish in the rain”: the reflexive verb becomes the intransitive “languish”; the rain phrase moves after the verb, and *a* is rendered as “in”.

## Lines 55–66 — Ciacco’s Distress and Civic Discord

> 55 Ed io anima trista non son sola,  
> 56 Chè tutte queste a simil pena stanno  
> 57 Per simil colpa: e più non fe’ parola.  

And I, wretched spirit, am not alone; since all these for like crime are in like punishment:” and more he said not.

- *anima trista* ‘spirit sorrowful’ → “wretched spirit”: sorrow becomes wretchedness, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *tutte queste a simil pena stanno / Per simil colpa* ‘all these stand in like punishment for like fault’ → “all these for like crime are in like punishment”: “are” replaces ‘stand’; “crime” gives *colpa* a stronger sense than ‘fault’; and the phrase expressing the cause precedes the phrase expressing the punishment.
- *e più non fe’ parola* ‘and more he made not word’ → “and more he said not”: the construction ‘make word’ becomes the single verb “said”.

> 58 Io gli risposi: Ciacco, il tuo affanno  
> 59 Mi pesa sì, ch’ a lagrimar m’ invita:  

I answered him: “Ciacco, thy sore distress weighs upon me so, that it bids me weep.

- *Io gli risposi* ‘I to him answered’ → “I answered him”: the indirect-object pronoun before the verb becomes an object following it.
- *il tuo affanno* ‘thy distress’ → “thy sore distress”: “sore” is supplied, intensifying the noun.
- *Mi pesa sì* ‘upon me weighs so’ → “weighs upon me so”: the phrase expressing the person affected follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *ch’ a lagrimar m’ invita* ‘that to weep me it invites’ → “that it bids me weep”: “bids” replaces ‘invites’; the object and infinitive follow the verb, and the infinitive loses ‘to’ after “bids”.

> 60 Ma dimmi, se tu sai, a che verranno  
> 61 Li cittadin della città partita:  
> 62 S’ alcun v’ è giusto: e dimmi la cagione,  
> 63 Perchè l’ ha tanta discordia assalita.  

But tell me, if thou canst, what the citizens of the divided city shall come to: if any one in it be just. And tell me the reason why such discord has assailed it.”

- *se tu sai* ‘if thou knowest’ → “if thou canst”: knowledge is recast as ability, with the action of telling understood after “canst”.
- *a che verranno / Li cittadin della città partita* ‘to what shall come the citizens of the city divided’ → “what the citizens of the divided city shall come to”: the subject precedes the verb; the preposition moves from before the interrogative to the end of the clause; and “divided” precedes its noun.
- *S’ alcun v’ è giusto* ‘if any one there is just’ → “if any one in it be just”: the locative pronoun becomes “in it”, explicitly referring to the city, and the indicative ‘is’ becomes the subjunctive “be”.
- *Perchè l’ ha tanta discordia assalita* ‘why it has so much discord assailed’ → “why such discord has assailed it”: the subject moves before the auxiliary, the object moves after the participle, and ‘so much’ becomes “such”.

> 64 Ed egli a me: Dopo lunga tenzone  
> 65 Verranno al sangue, e la parte selvaggia  
> 66 Caccerà l’ altra con molta offensione.  

And he to me: “After long contention, they shall come to blood, and the savage party shall expel the other with much offence.

- *la parte selvaggia* ‘the party savage’ → “the savage party”: the adjective precedes the noun rather than following it.

## Lines 67–75 — Reversal, Injustice, and Burning Vices

> 67 Poi appresso convien che questa caggia  
> 68 Infra tre soli, e che l’ altra sormonti  
> 69 Con la forza di tal, che testè piaggia.  

Then it behoves this to fall within three suns, and the other to prevail through the force of one who now keeps tacking.

- *Poi appresso* ‘then afterwards’ → “Then”: the two expressions of succession are reduced to one.
- *convien che questa caggia* ‘it behoves that this fall’ → “it behoves this to fall”: the clause introduced by *che*, with a subjunctive verb, becomes an infinitive construction.
- *e che l’ altra sormonti* ‘and that the other rise above’ → “and the other to prevail”: the second subjunctive clause likewise becomes an infinitive construction; rising above is rendered as prevailing.
- *Con* ‘with’ → “through”: accompaniment or instrument is expressed as agency.
- *di tal* ‘of such a one’ → “of one”: the demonstrative force of *tal* is not explicitly retained.
- *piaggia* ‘coasts’ → “keeps tacking”: the image of following the shore becomes one of changing course; “keeps” explicitly marks continued action.

> 70 Alto terrà lungo tempo le fronti,  
> 71 Tenendo l’ altra sotto gravi pesi,  
> 72 Come che di ciò pianga, e che ne adonti.  

It shall carry its front high for a long time, keeping the other under heavy burdens, however it may weep thereat and be ashamed.

- *Alto terrà lungo tempo le fronti* ‘high it will hold a long time the brows’ → “It shall carry its front high for a long time”: “carry” replaces ‘hold’; the plural ‘brows’ becomes the singular “front”, and the definite article becomes a possessive. “high” moves from the beginning to follow the object, while the expression of duration moves to the end and gains “for a”.
- *di ciò pianga* ‘at this it weep’ → “it may weep thereat”: the subjunctive is expressed with “may”; the phrase preceding the verb becomes the adverb “thereat” following it.
- *e che ne adonti* ‘and that it take offence at it’ → “and be ashamed”: the repeated *che* is omitted, and the verb shares the preceding subject and modal. *Ne* (‘at it’) is not separately rendered; taking offence is interpreted as feeling shame.

> 73 Giusti son duo, ma non vi sono intesi:  

Two are just; but are not listened to there.

- *Giusti son duo* ‘just are two’ → “Two are just”: the subject moves before the verb, and the predicate adjective follows it.
- *non vi sono intesi* ‘not there are understood’ → “are not listened to there”: “not” follows the auxiliary, and “there” moves to the end. *Intesi* is interpreted as ‘heeded’ rather than simply ‘understood’.

> 74 Superbia, invidia ed avarizia sono  
> 75 Le tre faville ch’ hanno i cuori accesi.  

Pride, Envy, and Avarice are the three sparks which have set the hearts of all on fire.”

- *hanno i cuori accesi* ‘have the hearts kindled’ → “have set the hearts of all on fire”: the Italian past participle follows the object; English instead uses “set” before the object and “on fire” after it. “of all” is supplied, making the scope of “the hearts” explicit.

## Lines 76–87 — Asking Where the Worthy Are

> 76 Qui pose fine al lacrimabil suono.  

Here he ended the lamentable sound.

- *pose fine al* ‘put an end to the’ → “ended the”: the phrase built around the noun *fine* becomes a single transitive verb.

> 77 Ed io a lui: Ancor vo’ che m’ insegni,  
> 78 E che di più parlar mi facci dono.  

And I to him: “Still I wish thee to instruct me, and to bestow a little farther speech on me.

- *Ancor vo’ che m’ insegni* ‘still I wish that thou instruct me’ → “Still I wish thee to instruct me”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction; the object pronoun corresponding to *m’* follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *E che di più parlar mi facci dono* ‘and that of more speaking thou make me a gift’ → “and to bestow a little farther speech on me”: the second subjunctive clause likewise becomes an infinitive. ‘Make a gift’ becomes “bestow”; the substantival infinitive *parlar* becomes “speech”, now the direct object rather than a phrase dependent on *dono*. *Più* becomes “a little farther”, adding a qualification of quantity, and the recipient pronoun becomes the following prepositional phrase “on me”.

> 79 Farinata e il Tegghiaio, che fur sì degni,  
> 80 Jacopo Rusticucci, Arrigo e il Mosca,  
> 81 E gli altri, che a ben far poser gl’ ingegni,  
> 82 Dimmi ove sono, e fa ch’ io li conosca;  

Farinata and the Tegghiaio, who were so worthy; Jacopo Rusticucci, Arrigo and Mosca, and the rest who set their minds on doing good: tell me where they are, and give me to know them;

- *il Mosca* ‘the Mosca’ → “Mosca”: the definite article is omitted.
- *gli altri* ‘the others’ → “the rest”: the plural pronoun becomes a collective expression.
- *che a ben far poser gl’ ingegni* ‘who on doing well set their talents’ → “who set their minds on doing good”: the phrase expressing the object of their efforts moves after the verb and its direct object. ‘Doing well’ becomes “doing good”, with an adverb rendered as a noun object; *ingegni* becomes “minds” rather than ‘talents’ or ‘intellectual powers’, and the Italian definite article is represented by a possessive.
- *fa ch’ io li conosca* ‘make that I know them’ → “give me to know them”: the causative imperative with a finite subjunctive clause becomes “give” followed by a recipient and an infinitive; the object pronoun moves after “know”.

> 83 Chè gran desio mi stringe di sapere,  
> 84 Se il Ciel gli addolcia o l’ Inferno gli attosca.  

for great desire urges me to learn whether Heaven soothes or Hell empoisons them.”

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *mi stringe* ‘me presses’ → “urges me”: physical pressure becomes urging, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *di sapere* ‘to know’ → “to learn”: knowledge is expressed as its acquisition.
- *il Ciel gli addolcia o l’ Inferno gli attosca* ‘the Heaven them sweetens or the Hell them poisons’ → “Heaven soothes or Hell empoisons them”: both definite articles are omitted; ‘sweetens’ becomes “soothes”. The two preverbal object pronouns become a single shared object placed after the second verb.

> 85 E quegli: Ei son tra le anime più nere;  

And he to me: “They are amongst the blackest spirits.

- *E quegli* ‘and that one’ → “And he to me”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and “to me” is supplied.
- *le anime più nere* ‘the souls most black’ → “the blackest spirits”: ‘souls’ becomes “spirits”, and the superlative adjective precedes the noun rather than following it.

> 86 Diversa colpa giù gli aggrava al fondo:  

A different crime weighs them downwards to the bottom.

- *Diversa colpa* ‘different fault’ → “A different crime”: an indefinite article is supplied, and ‘fault’ is rendered by the stronger “crime”.
- *giù gli aggrava* ‘downwards them weighs down’ → “weighs them downwards”: the verb moves before the object pronoun, and the directional adverb moves after it.

> 87 Se tanto, scendi, gli potrai vedere.  

Shouldst thou descend so far, thou mayest see them.

- *Se tanto, scendi* ‘if so far thou descendest’ → “Shouldst thou descend so far”: the explicit conditional conjunction and present indicative become an inverted construction with “Shouldst”; the phrase of extent moves after the verb.
- *gli potrai vedere* ‘them thou wilt be able to see’ → “thou mayest see them”: the future of *potere* becomes the present modal “mayest”, expressing possibility rather than explicitly future ability; the object pronoun moves after “see”.

## Lines 88–102 — Remembrance and the Final Awakening

> 88 Ma se tu torni mai nel dolce mondo,  
> 89 Pregoti che alla mente altrui mi rechi:  

But if ever thou return to the sweet world, I pray thee recall me to the memory of men.

- *tu torni mai* ‘thou returnest ever’ → “ever thou return”: the adverb moves before the subject, and the Italian present indicative is rendered by the English subjunctive form.
- *nel* ‘in the’ → “to the”: the preposition expresses the destination of the return.
- *Pregoti che* ‘I pray thee that’ → “I pray thee”: the conjunction introducing the request is omitted.
- *alla mente altrui mi rechi* ‘to the mind of others thou bring me’ → “recall me to the memory of men”: the verb and its object precede the complement; ‘bring’ becomes “recall,” ‘mind’ becomes “memory,” and ‘others’ becomes “men.” The Italian subordinate subjunctive becomes a bare verb expressing the request.

> 90 Più non ti dico, e più non ti rispondo.  

More I tell thee not, and more I answer not.”

- *Più non ti dico, e più non ti rispondo* ‘More not to thee I say, and more not to thee I answer’ → “More I tell thee not, and more I answer not”: both negatives move after their verbs. ‘Say to thee’ becomes “tell thee,” with the pronoun following the verb; the second *ti*, ‘to thee,’ is omitted.

> 91 Gli diritti occhi torse allora in biechi:  

Therewith he writhed his straight eyes asquint;

- *Gli diritti occhi* ‘the straight eyes’ → “his straight eyes”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the object moves from before the verb to after it.
- *torse* ‘turned’ → “writhed”: Carlyle chooses a more forceful verb of twisting.
- *allora* ‘then’ → “Therewith”: the temporal adverb becomes an expression of accompanying action and moves to the beginning.
- *in biechi* ‘into squinting ones’ → “asquint”: a prepositional phrase with an adjective referring to the eyes becomes an adverb.

> 92 Guardommi un poco; e poi chinò la testa:  
> 93 Cadde con essa a par degli altri ciechi.  

looked at me a little; then bent his head, and fell down with it like his blind companions.

- *Guardommi* ‘regarded me’ → “looked at me”: the Italian direct object becomes the object of the English preposition “at.”
- *e poi* ‘and then’ → “then”: the conjunction is omitted.
- *la testa* ‘the head’ → “his head”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Cadde* ‘fell’ → “and fell down”: a conjunction links the action to the preceding clause, and “down” is supplied.
- *a par degli altri ciechi* ‘on a level with the other blind ones’ → “like his blind companions”: an expression of equal position becomes a comparison; ‘the other blind ones’ becomes “his blind companions,” supplying both possession and companionship.

> 94 E il Duca disse a me: Più non si desta  
> 95 Di qua dal suon dell’ angelica tromba:  

And my Guide said to me: “He wakes no more until the angel’s trumpet sounds.

- *il Duca* ‘the leader’ → “my Guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘leader’ is rendered as “Guide.”
- *Più non si desta* ‘No more does he awaken’ → “He wakes no more”: the negative expression moves after the verb, and the Italian pronominal verb becomes the English intransitive “wakes.”
- *Di qua dal suon dell’ angelica tromba* ‘on this side of the sound of the angelic trumpet’ → “until the angel’s trumpet sounds”: the spatially phrased limit becomes a temporal clause; the noun ‘sound’ becomes the verb “sounds,” and the adjective ‘angelic’ becomes the possessive “angel’s.”

> 96 Quando verrà la nimica podesta,  

When the adverse Power shall come,

- *verrà la nimica podesta* ‘shall come the adverse power’ → “the adverse Power shall come”: the subject moves before the verb.

> 97 Ciascun ritroverà la trista tomba,  

each shall revisit his sad grave;

- *ritroverà* ‘shall find again’ → “shall revisit”: finding again is rendered as returning to a place.
- *la trista tomba* ‘the sad grave’ → “his sad grave”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 98 Ripiglierà sua carne e sua figura.  

shall resume his flesh and form;

- *Ripiglierà* ‘shall take back’ → “shall resume”: taking back is expressed as resuming possession.
- *sua figura* ‘his form’ → “form”: the repeated possessive is omitted.

> 99 Udirà quel che in eterno rimbomba.  

shall hear that which resounds to all eternity.”

- *in eterno rimbomba* ‘for ever resounds’ → “resounds to all eternity”: the temporal phrase moves after the verb, and ‘for ever’ is expanded into “to all eternity.”

> 100 Sì trapassammo per sozza mistura  
> 101 Dell’ ombre e della pioggia, a passi lenti,  
> 102 Toccando un poco la vita futura.  

Thus passed we through the filthy mixture of the shadows and the rain, with paces slow, touching a little on the future life.

- *sozza mistura* ‘filthy mixture’ → “the filthy mixture”: the definite article is supplied.
- *e della pioggia* ‘and of the rain’ → “and the rain”: the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *a passi lenti* ‘at paces slow’ → “with paces slow”: “with” replaces the Italian preposition introducing manner.
- *Toccando un poco la vita futura* ‘touching a little the life future’ → “touching a little on the future life”: “on” is supplied, turning the Italian transitive construction into the English expression for briefly discussing something; the adjective moves before “life.”

## Lines 103–115 — Torments After the Great Sentence

> 103 Perch’ io dissi: Maestro, esti tormenti  
> 104 Cresceranno ei dopo la gran sentenza,  
> 105 O fien minori, o saran sì cocenti?  

Wherefore I said: “Master, shall these torments increase after the great Sentence, or grow less, or remain as burning?”

- *esti tormenti Cresceranno ei* ‘these torments, will they increase’ → “shall these torments increase”: the future auxiliary precedes the subject in the English question; the resumptive pronoun *ei*, repeating *esti tormenti*, is omitted.
- *O fien minori* ‘or will they be less’ → “or grow less”: being less becomes growing less; the future auxiliary is shared with the preceding clause rather than repeated.
- *o saran sì cocenti* ‘or will they be so burning’ → “or remain as burning”: being becomes remaining, and *sì* is interpreted as expressing an unchanged degree. The future auxiliary is again shared rather than repeated.

> 106 Ed egli a me: Ritorna a tua scienza,  
> 107 Che vuol, quanto la cosa è più perfetta,  
> 108 Più senta il bene, e così la doglienza.  

And he to me: “Return to thy science, which has it, that the more a thing is perfect, the more it feels pleasure and likewise pain.

- *Che vuol* ‘which wills’ → “which has it”: the verb of willing becomes an expression meaning that something maintains or lays down a proposition.
- *quanto la cosa è più perfetta, Più senta* ‘in proportion as the thing is more perfect, it feels more’ → “that the more a thing is perfect, the more it feels”: “that” explicitly introduces the proposition; the proportional construction becomes the English correlative construction. The first comparative modifier moves before the subject, the definite *la cosa* becomes indefinite, and the subjunctive *senta* becomes indicative.
- *il bene* ‘the good’ → “pleasure”: the broader noun is rendered as a particular kind of good, and its article is omitted.
- *e così la doglienza* ‘and so the pain’ → “and likewise pain”: *così* is rendered as an expression of parallel application, and the article before the abstract noun is omitted.

> 109 Tuttochè questa gente maledetta  
> 110 In vera perfezion giammai non vada,  
> 111 Di là, più che di qua, essere aspetta.  

Though these accursed people never attain to true perfection, yet shall they be nearer to it after than before.”

- *questa gente maledetta* ‘this accursed people’ → “these accursed people”: the grammatically singular collective becomes plural, with a corresponding change in the demonstrative; the adjective moves before the noun.
- *In vera perfezion giammai non vada* ‘into true perfection it never goes’ → “never attain to true perfection”: going into becomes attaining to; the destination moves after the verb. The concessive subjunctive becomes indicative, and the Italian negative combination *giammai non* is expressed by the single “never”.
- *Di là, più che di qua, essere aspetta* ‘on that side, more than on this side, it expects to be’ → “yet shall they be nearer to it after than before”: “yet” is supplied to mark the contrast. Expectation becomes a statement of futurity; being more is interpreted as being nearer to perfection, with “nearer to it” supplying that relation explicitly. The spatial expressions become temporal ones, and the comparison moves after the verb.

> 112 Noi aggirammo a tondo quella strada,  
> 113 Parlando più assai ch’ io non ridico:  

We went round along that road, speaking much more than I repeat.

- *Noi aggirammo a tondo quella strada* ‘we circled round that road’ → “We went round along that road”: the verb and the phrase *a tondo* are combined in “went round”; the direct object becomes a prepositional phrase introduced by the supplied “along”.
- *Parlando più assai* ‘speaking more much’ → “speaking much more”: the intensifier follows the comparative in Italian but precedes it in English.
- *ch’ io non ridico* ‘than I do not repeat’ → “than I repeat”: the expletive *non* following the comparison is omitted; it does not negate the Italian verb here.

> 114 Venimmo al punto dove si digrada:  

We reached the point where the descent begins.

- *Venimmo al punto* ‘we came to the point’ → “We reached the point”: coming to becomes reaching, and the prepositional complement becomes a direct object.
- *dove si digrada* ‘where one descends’ → “where the descent begins”: the impersonal verbal construction becomes a clause with “the descent” as its subject; “begins” makes the commencement explicit.

> 115 Quivi trovammo Pluto il gran nemico.  

Here found we Plutus, the great enemy.

- *Quivi trovammo* ‘there we found’ → “Here found we”: the place adverb changes from ‘there’ to “Here”, and the English subject follows the verb.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Recasting

Carlyle generally preserves the Italian’s sequence of statements, images, and repeated terms, while rearranging words into English prose order. Subjects and objects placed after or before their usual positions in Italian are commonly relocated: *Giusti son duo* becomes “Two are just”, and *Urlar gli fa la pioggia* becomes “The rain makes them howl”. Adjectives usually move before their nouns, though phrases such as “a monster fierce and strange” and “with paces slow” retain the Italian order. Elsewhere, “Here found we” introduces an English inversion. The translation remains especially close in such expressions as “new torments, and new tormented souls” and the sequence “eternal, accursed, cold, and heavy rain”.

Choices of vocabulary range from close equivalents to interpretation or expansion. *Mente* becomes “sense” or “memory”, according to its context; *gola*, literally ‘throat’, becomes “gluttony”; and *bene*, ‘good’, becomes “pleasure”. Some choices shift the emphasis more noticeably: *unta*, ‘greasy’, is rendered “gory”, *graffia*, ‘scratches’, becomes “clutches”, and *colpa* repeatedly becomes “crime”. Conversely, “three suns”, “the great Worm”, and “their emptiness, which seems a body” closely preserve the Italian’s distinctive expressions rather than replacing them with explanatory paraphrases.

Changes of construction often make the English more direct or idiomatic. *Pute*, ‘stinks’, expands into “emits a putrid smell”, while *pose fine*, ‘put an end’, contracts into “ended”. Finite clauses become infinitive constructions, as in “it behoves this to fall”; *distese*, ‘stretched out’, becomes the participle “spreading”; and the nominal expression *dal suon dell’ angelica tromba* is recast as “until the angel’s trumpet sounds”. Other changes replace possession with a description—*Gli occhi ha vermigli* becomes “His eyes are red”—or turn spatial language into temporal language, notably “after than before”.

Carlyle also supplies or omits words to clarify reference and reduce repetition. He adds “souls” to *tormentati*, “sore” to the rendering of *affanno*, and “of all” after “the hearts”; “in it” makes the reference of *quivi* explicit. Repeated conjunctions, pronouns, and possessives are sometimes reduced, while *intorno*, ‘around’, and the recipient expressed by *-ci* in *mostrocci* are left unexpressed. Alongside these individually noted changes are routine grammatical adjustments: English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits Italian articles before possessives. Articles attached to body parts also frequently become possessives. These adjustments give the prose an English grammatical framework without generally disturbing its close verbal correspondence with the original.
