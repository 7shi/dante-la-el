# Inferno 33

## Lines 1–15 — Ugolino Begins His Account

> 1 La bocca sollevò dal fiero pasto  
> 2 Quel peccator, forbendola a’ capelli  
> 3 Del capo ch’ egli avea diretro guasto.  

From the fell repast that sinner raised his mouth, wiping it upon the hair of the head he had laid waste behind.

- *La bocca sollevò dal fiero pasto Quel peccator* ‘The mouth raised from the fierce repast that sinner’ → “From the fell repast that sinner raised his mouth”: the phrase naming the repast moves to the beginning; the subject precedes the verb, and the object follows it. The definite article before *bocca* becomes the possessive “his”; *fiero* is rendered by the archaic “fell”.
- *forbendola a’ capelli* ‘wiping it on the hairs’ → “wiping it upon the hair”: the plural *capelli* becomes the English collective singular “hair”.
- *ch’ egli avea diretro guasto* ‘that he had behind ruined’ → “he had laid waste behind”: the relative pronoun is omitted, *guasto* becomes the phrase “laid waste”, and “behind” follows rather than precedes the participial expression.

> 4 Poi cominciò: Tu vuoi ch’ io rinnovelli  
> 5 Disperato dolor, che il cor mi preme,  
> 6 Già pur pensando, pria ch’ io ne favelli.  

Then he began: “Thou willest that I renew desperate grief, which wrings my heart, even at the very thought, before I tell thereof.

- *che il cor mi preme* ‘which presses the heart for me’ → “which wrings my heart”: “wrings” gives *preme* a more specific figurative force. The object follows the verb instead of preceding it, and the personal pronoun *mi* becomes the possessive “my”.
- *Già pur pensando* ‘already merely thinking’ → “even at the very thought”: the gerund becomes a noun within a prepositional phrase. “Even” replaces the temporal ‘already’, while “very” gives emphasis rather than directly reproducing the restrictive force of *pur*.
- *favelli* ‘speak’ → “tell”: the verb of speaking is rendered as a verb of telling.

> 7 Ma se le mie parole esser den seme,  
> 8 Che frutti infamia al traditor ch’ io rodo,  
> 9 Parlare e lagrimar vedrai insieme.  

But if my words are to be a seed, that may bear fruit of infamy to the traitor whom I gnaw, thou shalt see me speak and weep at the same time.

- *esser den seme* ‘be must seed’ → “are to be a seed”: the contracted *den*, meaning ‘must’ or ‘are destined to’, becomes “are to”; the modal expression precedes the infinitive rather than following it. An indefinite article is supplied before “seed”.
- *Che frutti infamia* ‘that may yield infamy’ → “that may bear fruit of infamy”: the single verb *frutti* expands into “bear fruit”, and its direct object becomes a phrase introduced by “of”. The subjunctive is expressed through “may”.
- *Parlare e lagrimar vedrai insieme* ‘speak and weep thou wilt see together’ → “thou shalt see me speak and weep at the same time”: the infinitives move after the verb of seeing, and “me” is supplied as their understood subject. ‘Together’ becomes the explicitly temporal phrase “at the same time”.

> 10 Io non so chi tu sie, nè per che modo  
> 11 Venuto sei quaggiù; ma Fiorentino  
> 12 Mi sembri veramente, quand’ io t’ odo.  

I know not who thou mayest be, nor by what mode thou hast come down here; but, when I hear thee, in truth thou seemest to me a Florentine.

- *chi tu sie* ‘who thou art’ → “who thou mayest be”: the Italian subjunctive becomes an English construction with “mayest”, explicitly marking uncertainty.
- *Venuto sei* ‘come thou art’ → “thou hast come”: the participle moves after the auxiliary, and Italian *essere* becomes English “hast” in the compound past tense.
- *Fiorentino Mi sembri veramente* ‘Florentine to me thou seemest truly’ → “in truth thou seemest to me a Florentine”: the predicate “Florentine” and the pronoun rendered “to me” move after the verb. The adverb becomes the prepositional phrase “in truth” and moves before the subject; an indefinite article is supplied before “Florentine”.
- *quand’ io t’ odo* ‘when I thee hear’ → “when I hear thee”: the object pronoun follows the verb instead of preceding it, and the whole subordinate clause moves before the main clause.

> 13 Tu dei saper ch’ io fui il Conte Ugolino,  
> 14 E questi l’ Arcivescovo Ruggieri:  

Thou hast to know that I was Count Ugolino, and this the Archbishop Ruggieri:

- *dei saper* ‘must know’ → “hast to know”: the modal verb becomes the English construction “hast to” followed by an infinitive.
- *il Conte Ugolino* ‘the Count Ugolino’ → “Count Ugolino”: the definite article before the title is omitted.

> 15 Or ti dirò perch’ io son tal vicino.  

now I will tell thee why I am such a neighbour to him.

- *Or ti dirò* ‘now to thee I shall tell’ → “now I will tell thee”: the Italian future ending is expressed by “will”, and the object pronoun follows the verb instead of preceding it.
- *tal vicino* ‘such neighbour’ → “such a neighbour to him”: an indefinite article is supplied, and “to him” makes explicit a relationship left unexpressed in the Italian phrase.

## Lines 16–21 — The Cruelty of My Death

> 16 Che per l’ effetto de’ suoi mal pensieri,  
> 17 Fidandomi di lui, io fossi preso  
> 18 E poscia morto, dir non è mestieri.  

That by the effect of his ill devices I, confiding in him, was taken and thereafter put to death, it is not necessary to say:

- *pensieri* ‘thoughts’ → “devices”: the translation interprets the thoughts as schemes or designs.
- *Fidandomi di lui, io* ‘trusting in him, I’ → “I, confiding in him,”: the subject moves before the participial phrase; the Italian pronominal verb is rendered by the non-reflexive “confiding”.
- *fossi preso* ‘were taken’ → “was taken”: the Italian imperfect subjunctive becomes an English indicative past, retaining the passive construction.
- *morto* ‘killed’ → “put to death”: a single participle becomes a verbal phrase.
- *dir non è mestieri* ‘to say is not a necessity’ → “it is not necessary to say”: the infinitive moves to the end, with anticipatory “it” supplied; the noun *mestieri* is rendered by the adjective “necessary”.

> 19 Però quel che non puoi avere inteso,  
> 20 Cioè, come la morte mia fu cruda,  
> 21 Udirai; e saprai se m’ ha offeso.  

but that which thou canst not have learnt, that is, how cruel was my death, thou shalt hear—and know if he has offended me.

- *Però* ‘therefore’ → “but”: the translation gives the connective an adversative rather than a consequential force.
- *inteso* ‘heard’ → “learnt”: hearing is expressed more broadly as acquiring knowledge.
- *come la morte mia fu cruda* ‘how my death was cruel’ → “how cruel was my death”: “cruel” moves immediately after “how”, and the verb precedes the subject; the possessive also moves from after the noun to before it.
- *saprai* ‘thou shalt know’ → “know”: the future verb shares the preceding “thou shalt” instead of receiving a separate English future construction.
- *m’ha offeso* ‘me he has offended’ → “he has offended me”: the object pronoun moves from before the auxiliary to after the participle.

## Lines 22–33 — Captivity and an Ominous Dream

> 22 Breve pertugio dentro dalla muda,  
> 23 La qual per me ha il titol della fame,  
> 24 E in che conviene ancor ch’ altri si chiuda,  

“A narrow hole within the mew, which from me has the title of Famine, and in which others yet must be shut up,

- *Breve pertugio* ‘a small opening’ → “A narrow hole”: general smallness becomes narrowness, and ‘opening’ becomes “hole”.
- *La qual* ‘the which’ → “which”: the article-bearing Italian relative becomes a simple English relative.
- *per me* ‘because of me’ → “from me”: the causal expression becomes an expression of source.
- *della fame* ‘of the hunger’ → “of Famine”: ‘hunger’ is rendered as “Famine”, and the article before the abstract noun is omitted.
- *conviene ancor ch’ altri si chiuda* ‘it is necessary still that someone else shut himself up’ → “others yet must be shut up”: the impersonal construction with a subjunctive clause becomes a personal construction with “must”; singular *altri* becomes plural “others”, and the reflexive construction is rendered as a passive.

> 25 M’ avea mostrato per lo suo forame  
> 26 Più lune già, quand’ io feci il mal sonno,  
> 27 Che del futuro mi squarciò il velame.  

had through its opening already shewn me several moons, when I slept the evil sleep that rent for me the curtain of the future.

- *M’ avea mostrato per lo suo forame Più lune già* ‘to me had shown through its opening more moons already’ → “had through its opening already shewn me several moons”: the object pronoun moves after the participle; the prepositional phrase moves between auxiliary and participle; and *già* moves from after the object to before the participle. *Più* is interpreted as an indefinite plurality, “several”, rather than comparative ‘more’.
- *quand’ io feci il mal sonno* ‘when I made the evil sleep’ → “when I slept the evil sleep”: ‘made’ becomes “slept”, producing an English verb with a cognate object.
- *Che del futuro mi squarciò il velame* ‘that of the future tore for me the veil’ → “that rent for me the curtain of the future”: ‘of the future’ moves after the noun it qualifies, and the pronoun preceding the verb becomes “for me” after it. *Velame* is rendered as “curtain” rather than ‘veil’.

> 28 Questi pareva a me maestro e donno,  
> 29 Cacciando il lupo e i lupicini al monte,  
> 30 Per che i Pisan veder Lucca non ponno.  

This man seemed to me lord and master, chasing the wolf and his whelps, upon the mountain for which the Pisans cannot see Lucca.

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “This man”: the demonstrative pronoun is expanded with the noun “man”.
- *maestro e donno* ‘master and lord’ → “lord and master”: the two nouns exchange places.
- *Cacciando* ‘hunting’ → “chasing”: the verb is rendered specifically as pursuit.
- *il lupo e i lupicini* ‘the wolf and the little wolves’ → “the wolf and his whelps”: the diminutive becomes a noun denoting young animals, and the definite article becomes the possessive “his”.
- *al monte* ‘to the mountain’ → “upon the mountain”: the preposition is interpreted as indicating location rather than direction.
- *i Pisan veder Lucca non ponno* ‘the Pisans see Lucca cannot’ → “the Pisans cannot see Lucca”: the negative modal moves before the infinitive and its object.

> 31 Con cagne magre, studïose e conte,  
> 32 Gualandi con Sismondi, e con Lanfranchi  
> 33 S’ avea messi dinanzi dalla fronte.  

With hounds meagre, keen, and dextrous, he had put in front of him Gualandi with Sismondi, and with Lanfranchi.

- *cagne* ‘female hounds’ → “hounds”: the Italian noun’s specification of sex is not retained.
- *studïose* ‘eager’ → “keen”: eagerness is expressed through the English adjective “keen”.
- *conte* ‘skilled’ → “dextrous”: skill is expressed more specifically as dexterity.
- *Gualandi con Sismondi, e con Lanfranchi S’ avea messi dinanzi dalla fronte* ‘Gualandi with Sismondi, and with Lanfranchi he had placed for himself ahead of the front’ → “he had put in front of him Gualandi with Sismondi, and with Lanfranchi”: the named objects move from before the verb to after the verb and its locative phrase. The reflexive pronoun and the fuller spatial expression are combined into “in front of him”.

## Lines 34–49 — The Dream and the Locked Tower

> 34 In picciol corso mi pareano stanchi  
> 35 Lo padre e i figli, e con l’ agute scane  
> 36 Mi parea lor veder fender li fianchi.  

After short course, the father and his sons seemed to me weary; and methought I saw their flanks torn by the sharp teeth.

- *In picciol corso* ‘in a little course’ → “After short course”: “After” makes the relation temporal rather than retaining ‘in’; ‘little’ becomes “short”.
- *mi pareano stanchi Lo padre e i figli* ‘to me seemed weary the father and the sons’ → “the father and his sons seemed to me weary”: the subject moves before the verb, and “his” replaces the article before ‘sons’.
- *con l’ agute scane* ‘with the sharp fangs’ → “by the sharp teeth”: the instrumental phrase becomes a “by” phrase following the passive “torn”; ‘fangs’ is generalized to “teeth”.
- *Mi parea lor veder fender li fianchi* ‘it seemed to me to see their flanks being rent’ → “methought I saw their flanks torn”: the impersonal construction becomes “methought” followed by a finite clause; the infinitive *veder* becomes “I saw”, and *fender* becomes the passive participle “torn”. The possessive dative *lor*, literally ‘to them’, is rendered by “their” before “flanks”.

> 37 Quando fui desto innanzi la dimane,  
> 38 Pianger senti’ fra ’l sonno i miei figliuoli,  
> 39 Ch’ erano meco, e dimandar del pane.  

When I awoke before the dawn, I heard my sons asking for bread.

- *fui desto* ‘I was awake’ → “I awoke”: a construction describing the awakened state becomes an intransitive verb expressing the event.
- *innanzi la dimane* ‘before the morning’ → “before the dawn”: “dawn” narrows ‘morning’ to its beginning.
- *Pianger senti’ fra ’l sonno i miei figliuoli, Ch’ erano meco, e dimandar del pane* ‘weep I heard amid sleep my sons, who were with me, and ask for some bread’ → “I heard my sons asking for bread”: the translation omits their weeping, their being asleep, and the relative clause stating that they were with the speaker. The coordinating ‘and’ consequently disappears. “I heard my sons” precedes the remaining complement, whose infinitive becomes the participle “asking”; the partitive *del* is represented by the bare noun “bread”.

> 40 Ben sei crudel, se tu già non ti duoli,  
> 41 Pensando ciò ch’ il mio cor s’ annunziava:  
> 42 E se non piangi, di che pianger suoli?  

Thou art right cruel, if thou dost not grieve already at the thought of what my heart foreboded; and if thou weepest not, at what art thou used to weep?

- *Ben* ‘indeed’ → “right”: an affirmation of the statement becomes an intensifier immediately before “cruel”.
- *se tu già non ti duoli* ‘if you already do not grieve’ → “if thou dost not grieve already”: “already” moves after the verb, and the Italian reflexive construction becomes the English intransitive “grieve”.
- *Pensando* ‘thinking’ → “at the thought of”: the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase containing a noun.
- *ciò ch’ il mio cor s’ annunziava* ‘what my heart announced to itself’ → “what my heart foreboded”: “foreboded” gives the announcement an explicitly ominous, anticipatory sense, without separately expressing the reflexive ‘to itself’.
- *di che pianger suoli* ‘for what to weep are you accustomed’ → “at what art thou used to weep”: “at” replaces ‘for’, and the infinitive moves after the expression of accustomed action.

> 43 Già eran desti, e l’ ora s’ appressava  
> 44 Che il cibo ne soleva essere addotto,  
> 45 E per suo sogno ciascun dubitava,  

They were now awake, and the hour approaching at which our food used to be brought us, and each was anxious from his dream,

- *Già eran desti* ‘already they were awake’ → “They were now awake”: ‘already’ becomes “now” and moves after the finite verb.
- *e l’ ora s’ appressava* ‘and the hour was drawing near’ → “and the hour approaching”: the finite reflexive verb becomes an English participle with its auxiliary left unexpressed.
- *Che* ‘that’ → “at which”: the temporal relative is rendered with an explicit preposition.
- *il cibo ne soleva essere addotto* ‘the food to us used to be brought’ → “our food used to be brought us”: “our” replaces the definite article, while *ne* is still rendered separately as “us”; that pronoun moves from before the verbal construction to after “brought”.
- *E per suo sogno ciascun dubitava* ‘and because of his dream each doubted’ → “and each was anxious from his dream”: the causal phrase moves after the predicate, with “from” replacing ‘because of’. “Was anxious” interprets *dubitava* as fearful uncertainty rather than simply doubt, replacing the verb with a copula and adjective.

> 46 Ed io sentii chiavar l’ uscio di sotto  
> 47 All’ orribile torre: ond’ io guardai  
> 48 Nel viso a’ miei figliuoi senza far motto.  

and below I heard the outlet of the horrible tower locked up: whereat I looked into the faces of my sons, without uttering a word.

- *Ed io sentii chiavar l’ uscio di sotto All’ orribile torre* ‘and I heard nail shut the door below at the horrible tower’ → “and below I heard the outlet of the horrible tower locked up”: “below” moves before “I heard”; the active infinitive becomes the passive participial expression “locked up”, following its object. *Chiavar* is interpreted as locking rather than nailing shut, and ‘door’ becomes “outlet”. The tower is attached to the outlet with “of”, rather than retaining the Italian preposition *a*.
- *ond’* ‘whence’ → “whereat”: the connective is rendered as a response to the preceding event rather than literally as ‘from which’.
- *Nel viso a’ miei figliuoi* ‘in the face to my sons’ → “into the faces of my sons”: “into” replaces ‘in’; the distributive singular ‘face’ becomes plural; and the Italian possessive dative becomes an “of” phrase.
- *senza far motto* ‘without making a word’ → “without uttering a word”: the Italian idiom with ‘make’ is rendered with the English verb “uttering”.

> 49 Io non piangeva, sì dentro impietrai:  

I did not weep: so stony grew I within.

- *Io non piangeva* ‘I was not weeping’ → “I did not weep”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past rather than an explicitly ongoing construction.
- *sì dentro impietrai* ‘so within I turned to stone’ → “so stony grew I within”: the verb ‘turned to stone’ becomes “grew” with the adjective “stony”; “within” moves to the end, while the adjective is placed before the verb and the expressed subject follows it.

## Lines 50–66 — A Father’s Grief and His Children’s Offer

> 50 Piangevan elli; e Anselmuccio mio  
> 51 Disse: Tu guardi sì! Padre, che hai?  

They wept; and my little Anselm said: ‘Thou lookest so! Father, what ails thee?’

- *Piangevan elli* ‘were weeping they’ → “They wept”: the subject moves before the verb, and the Italian imperfect becomes an English simple past.
- *Anselmuccio mio Disse* ‘little Anselm my said’ → “my little Anselm said”: the possessive moves before the name, and the affectionate diminutive ending is expressed separately by “little”.
- *che hai* ‘what hast thou’ → “what ails thee”: possession becomes an idiomatic expression of distress; the Italian subject becomes the object “thee”.

> 52 Però non lagrimai, nè rispos’ io  
> 53 Tutto quel giorno, nè la notte appresso,  
> 54 Infin che l’ altro sol nel mondo uscío.  

But I shed no tear, nor answered all that day, nor the next night, till another sun came forth upon the world.

- *Però* ‘therefore’ → “But”: Carlyle gives the connective an adversative rather than a consequential force.
- *non lagrimai* ‘I did not weep’ → “I shed no tear”: the single verb becomes a verb-and-object expression, with the negation attached to the singular noun “tear”.
- *nè rispos’ io* ‘nor answered I’ → “nor answered”: the explicitly stated, postposed subject is omitted; the English shares the preceding “I”.
- *nè la notte appresso* ‘nor the night following’ → “nor the next night”: the postposed modifier becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *l’ altro sol* ‘the other sun’ → “another sun”: the definite expression becomes an indefinite one.
- *nel mondo uscío* ‘into the world came out’ → “came forth upon the world”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase; “forth” replaces ‘out’, and “upon” replaces ‘into’.

> 55 Come un poco di raggio si fu messo  
> 56 Nel doloroso carcere, ed io scorsi  
> 57 Per quattro visi il mio aspetto stesso,  

When a small ray was sent into the doleful prison, and I discerned in their four faces the aspect of my own,

- *Come* ‘as soon as’ → “When”: the temporal connection is retained without explicitly expressing immediacy.
- *un poco di raggio* ‘a little of a ray’ → “a small ray”: the partitive expression becomes a noun qualified by an adjective.
- *si fu messo* ‘had placed itself’ → “was sent”: the pronominal construction becomes passive, and the Italian past anterior becomes an English simple past.
- *Per quattro visi* ‘across four faces’ → “in their four faces”: the distributive or spatial *Per* becomes “in”, and the possessive “their” is supplied.
- *il mio aspetto stesso* ‘my same aspect’ → “the aspect of my own”: the directly possessed noun becomes an *of*-construction; “my own” elliptically supplies the comparison with the speaker’s face rather than separately rendering ‘same’.

> 58 Ambo le mani per dolor mi morsi;  

I bit on both my hands for grief;

- *Ambo le mani per dolor mi morsi* ‘both the hands for grief I bit myself’ → “I bit on both my hands for grief”: the fronted object moves after the verb, the reflexive is represented by the possessive “my”, and “bit” takes “on” rather than a direct object; the phrase ‘for grief’ moves from before the verb to the end of the clause.

> 59 E quei, pensando ch’ io il fessi per voglia  
> 60 Di manicar, di subito levorsi,  

and they, thinking that I did it from desire of eating, of a sudden rose up,

- *quei* ‘those’ → “they”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *pensando ch’ io il fessi* ‘thinking that I it did’ → “thinking that I did it”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the Italian imperfect subjunctive becomes an English indicative past.
- *per voglia Di manicar* ‘from desire to eat’ → “from desire of eating”: the infinitive complement becomes an *of*-phrase containing a gerund.
- *di subito levorsi* ‘suddenly they raised themselves’ → “of a sudden rose up”: the adverbial expression becomes “of a sudden”, and the reflexive verb becomes the intransitive phrasal verb “rose up”.

> 61 E disser: Padre, assai ci fia men doglia,  
> 62 Se tu mangi di noi: tu ne vestisti  
> 63 Queste misere carni, e tu le spoglia.  

and said: ‘Father, it will give us much less pain, if thou wilt eat of us: thou didst put upon us this miserable flesh, and do thou strip it off.’

- *assai ci fia men doglia* ‘much less pain will there be for us’ → “it will give us much less pain”: the construction with ‘be’ and a dative becomes one with “give”, an added subject “it”, and “us” as indirect object; *assai* moves beside “less”.
- *Se tu mangi di noi* ‘if thou eatest of us’ → “if thou wilt eat of us”: the present indicative becomes a construction with “wilt”, introducing futurity or willingness.
- *tu ne vestisti Queste misere carni* ‘thou didst clothe us in these miserable fleshes’ → “thou didst put upon us this miserable flesh”: ‘clothe’ becomes “put upon”, with the flesh made the direct object; the plural noun and demonstrative become singular.
- *e tu le spoglia* ‘and thou strip them off’ → “and do thou strip it off”: the imperative gains “do”, the object pronoun follows the verb, and plural *le* becomes singular “it”, agreeing with “flesh”.

> 64 Quetaimi allor, per non farli più tristi:  

Then I calmed myself, in order not to make them more unhappy.

- *Quetaimi allor* ‘I quieted myself then’ → “Then I calmed myself”: the temporal adverb moves to the beginning of the sentence.
- *per non farli* ‘not to make them’ → “in order not to make them”: the purpose construction is expanded with “in order”.

> 65 Quel dì, e l’ altro stemmo tutti muti:  

That day and the next we all were mute.

- *Quel dì, e l’ altro* ‘that day and the other’ → “That day and the next”: “next” makes the succession explicit.
- *stemmo tutti muti* ‘we remained all mute’ → “we all were mute”: ‘remained’ becomes “were”, and “all” moves before the verb.

> 66 Ahi dura terra, perchè non t’ apristi?  

Ah, hard earth! why didst thou not open?

- *perchè non t’ apristi* ‘why didst thou not open thyself’ → “why didst thou not open”: the Italian reflexive becomes an English intransitive verb without a reflexive pronoun.

## Lines 67–80 — Starvation and Renewed Rage

> 67 Poscia che fummo al quarto dì venuti,  
> 68 Gaddo mi si gittò disteso a’ piedi,  
> 69 Dicendo: Padre mio, che non m’ aiuti?  

When we had come to the fourth day, Gaddo threw himself stretched out at my feet, saying: ‘My father! why don’t you help me?’

- *Poscia che* ‘after that’ → “When”: explicit succession becomes a more general temporal relation.
- *fummo al quarto dì venuti* ‘we had to the fourth day come’ → “we had come to the fourth day”: the participle moves beside its auxiliary, before the phrase specifying the day.
- *mi … a’ piedi* ‘to me … at the feet’ → “at my feet”: the personal pronoun marking the person affected becomes a possessive modifying “feet”.
- *Padre mio* ‘father my’ → “My father”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *non m’ aiuti* ‘not me you help’ → “don’t you help me”: English supplies the auxiliary for the negative question and places the object pronoun after the main verb.

> 70 Quivi morì; e come tu mi vedi,  
> 71 Vid’ io cascar li tre ad uno ad uno,  

There he died; and even as thou seest me, saw I the three fall one by one,

- *e come* ‘and as’ → “and even as”: “even” is added for emphasis.
- *tu mi vedi* ‘thou me seest’ → “thou seest me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Vid’ io cascar li tre* ‘saw I fall the three’ → “saw I the three fall”: the subject of the infinitive moves before it; the inversion of the main verb and its subject is retained.
- *ad uno ad uno* ‘one at a time’ → “one by one”: the repeated Italian prepositional construction becomes the corresponding English idiom with “by”.

> 72 Tra il quinto dì e il sesto, ond’ io mi diedi  
> 73 Già cieco a brancolar sovra ciascuno;  
> 74 E tre dì li chiamai, poi ch’ ei fur morti:  

between the fifth day and the sixth, whence I betook me, already blind, to groping over each; and for three days called them, after they were dead.

- *io mi diedi* ‘I gave myself’ → “I betook me”: the reflexive expression for setting oneself to an activity is rendered with a different verb and the archaic reflexive use of “me”.
- *a brancolar* ‘to grope’ → “to groping”: the Italian infinitive becomes a gerund after the preposition required by “betook”.
- *tre dì li chiamai* ‘three days them I called’ → “for three days called them”: “for” is supplied to mark duration, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 75 Poscia, più che il dolor, potè il digiuno.  

Then fasting had more power than grief.”

- *più che il dolor, potè il digiuno* ‘more than grief, fasting could’ → “fasting had more power than grief”: the final subject moves to the front, and the comparison follows the predicate. The absolute use of *potè*, expressing power or efficacy without a following infinitive, is expanded into “had more power”.

> 76 Quand’ ebbe detto ciò, con gli occhi torti  
> 77 Riprese il teschio misero co’ denti,  
> 78 Che furo all’ osso, come d’ un can, forti.  

When he had spoken this, with eyes distorted he seized the miserable skull again with his teeth, which as a dog’s were strong upon the bone.

- *ebbe detto ciò* ‘had said this’ → “he had spoken this”: *dire* is rendered with “spoken” rather than ‘said’.
- *con gli occhi torti* ‘with the eyes twisted’ → “with eyes distorted”: the definite article is omitted, and “distorted” renders the physical sense of *torti*.
- *Riprese* ‘took again’ → “seized” and “again”: the verb is rendered more forcefully, and its repeated-action element becomes a separate adverb placed after the object.
- *il teschio misero* ‘the skull miserable’ → “the miserable skull”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *co’ denti* ‘with the teeth’ → “with his teeth”: the definite article becomes an explicit possessive.
- *Che furo all’ osso, come d’ un can, forti* ‘which were to the bone, as of a dog, strong’ → “which as a dog’s were strong upon the bone”: the comparison moves before the verb, while “strong” moves beside it. The Italian possessive phrase becomes an English possessive, and the relation expressed by *all’ osso* is rendered as contact “upon the bone”.

> 79 Ahi Pisa, vituperio delle genti  
> 80 Del bel paese là dove il sì suona;  

Ah, Pisa! scandal to the people of the beauteous land where “Sì” is heard!

- *vituperio delle genti* ‘disgrace of the peoples’ → “scandal to the people”: “scandal” renders *vituperio*, the relation expressed by ‘of’ becomes “to”, and plural *genti* becomes the collective “people”.
- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “where”: the separate locative adverb is omitted.
- *il sì suona* ‘the yes sounds’ → ““Sì” is heard”: the definite article is omitted, the affirmative word is retained in Italian, and an active verb describing its sounding becomes a passive expression describing its being heard.

## Lines 81–90 — A Curse on Pisa

> 81 Poi che i vicini a te punir son lenti,  

Since thy neighbours are slow to punish thee,

- *i vicini* ‘the neighbours’ → “thy neighbours”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *a te punir son lenti* ‘to thee punish are slow’ → “are slow to punish thee”: the infinitive phrase moves after “slow,” and its object follows the infinitive.

> 82 Muovasi la Capraia e la Gorgona,  
> 83 E faccian siepe ad Arno in su la foce,  
> 84 Sì ch’ egli annieghi in te ogni persona.  

let the Capraia and Gorgona move, and hedge up the Arno at its mouth, that it may drown in thee every living soul.

- *Muovasi la Capraia e la Gorgona* ‘may the Capraia and the Gorgona move’ → “let the Capraia and Gorgona move”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a “let” construction, placing the named subjects before “move”; the Italian reflexive form becomes an English intransitive verb, and the second definite article is omitted.
- *faccian siepe ad Arno* ‘may they make a hedge to Arno’ → “hedge up the Arno”: the verb-and-noun construction becomes a phrasal verb, with Arno as its direct object; the article is supplied, and the exhortation continues under the preceding “let.”
- *in su la foce* ‘upon the mouth’ → “at its mouth”: the preposition changes, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Sì ch’ egli annieghi* ‘so that it drown’ → “that it may drown”: ‘so’ is omitted, and the subjunctive is expressed with “may.”
- *ogni persona* ‘every person’ → “every living soul”: ‘person’ becomes “soul,” with “living” supplied.

> 85 Chè se il Conte Ugolino aveva voce  
> 86 D’ aver tradita te delle castella,  
> 87 Non dovei tu i figliuoi porre a tal croce.  

For if Count Ugolino had the fame of having betrayed thee in thy castles, thou oughtest not to have put his sons into such torture.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *il Conte Ugolino* ‘the Count Ugolino’ → “Count Ugolino”: the article before the title is omitted.
- *aveva voce* ‘had voice’ → “had the fame”: the Italian idiom is rendered as having a reputation, with a definite article supplied.
- *delle castella* ‘concerning the castles’ → “in thy castles”: the relation expressed by *di* becomes “in,” and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Non dovei tu i figliuoi porre* ‘not shouldst thou the sons put’ → “thou oughtest not to have put his sons”: the subject moves before the modal, the negative follows it, and the object follows the infinitive. The past reference of *dovei* is conveyed through the perfect infinitive “to have put”; the definite article before *figliuoi* becomes “his.”
- *a tal croce* ‘to such a cross’ → “into such torture”: the concrete ‘cross’ is rendered by the suffering it signifies, and “to” becomes “into.”

> 88 Innocenti facea l’ età novella,  
> 89 Novella Tebe! Uguccione e il Brigata,  
> 90 E gli altri duo che il canto suso appella.  

Their youthful age, thou modern Thebes! made innocent Uguccione and Brigata, and the other two whom my song above has named.

- *Innocenti facea* ‘innocent made’ → “made innocent”: the predicative adjective moves after the verb.
- *l’ età novella* ‘the young age’ → “Their youthful age”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and this subject moves before the verb.
- *Novella Tebe!* ‘new Thebes!’ → “thou modern Thebes!”: ‘new’ becomes “modern,” and “thou” is supplied to mark the direct address.
- *il Brigata* ‘the Brigata’ → “Brigata”: the article is omitted.
- *che il canto suso appella* ‘whom the song above calls’ → “whom my song above has named”: the definite article becomes “my,” ‘calls’ becomes “named,” and the Italian present becomes an English present perfect.

## Lines 91–108 — Frozen Tears and an Unexplained Wind

> 91 Noi passamm’ oltre, dove la gelata  
> 92 Ruvidamente un’ altra gente fascia,  
> 93 Non volta in giù, ma tutta riversata.  

We went farther on, where the frost ruggedly inwraps another people, not bent forwards, but all reversed.

- *Noi passamm’ oltre* ‘we passed beyond’ → “We went farther on”: passing beyond is expressed as continuing farther.
- *dove la gelata Ruvidamente un’ altra gente fascia* ‘where the frost roughly another people swathes’ → “where the frost ruggedly inwraps another people”: the verb moves before its object; the image of binding or swathing becomes “inwraps”.
- *Non volta in giù* ‘not turned downward’ → “not bent forwards”: turning becomes bending, and the downward direction becomes a forward inclination.
- *ma tutta riversata* ‘but wholly turned on its back’ → “but all reversed”: “reversed” gives a less anatomically specific rendering of *riversata*.

> 95 E il duol, che truova in su gli occhi rintoppo,  
> 96 Si volve in entro a far crescer l’ ambascia;  

The very weeping there allows them not to weep; and the grief, which finds impediment upon their eyes, turns inward to increase the agony:

- *Lo pianto stesso* ‘the weeping itself’ → “The very weeping”: the postposed emphasis becomes an adjective before the noun.
- *pianger non lascia* ‘does not allow to weep’ → “allows them not to weep”: “them” is supplied as the object of “allows”; the infinitive follows the finite verb, and the negative is placed before the infinitive rather than before the finite verb.
- *che truova in su gli occhi rintoppo* ‘which finds upon the eyes impediment’ → “which finds impediment upon their eyes”: the object moves before the prepositional phrase, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Si volve in entro* ‘turns itself inward’ → “turns inward”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an English intransitive verb.
- *a far crescer l’ ambascia* ‘to make the anguish grow’ → “to increase the agony”: the causative construction with two verbs becomes a single transitive verb.

> 97 Chè le lacrime prime fanno groppo,  
> 98 E, sì come visiere di cristallo,  
> 99 Riempion sotto il ciglio tutto il coppo.  

for their first tears form a knot, and, like crystal vizors, fill up all the cavity beneath their eyebrows.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *le lacrime prime* ‘the tears first’ → “their first tears”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *fanno groppo* ‘make a knot’ → “form a knot”: “form” replaces the more general verb ‘make’.
- *sì come* ‘just as’ → “like”: the comparison is expressed by a preposition.
- *visiere di cristallo* ‘vizors of crystal’ → “crystal vizors”: the phrase specifying material becomes a noun used attributively before “vizors”.
- *Riempion* ‘fill’ → “fill up”: the English adds the completive particle “up”.
- *sotto il ciglio tutto il coppo* ‘beneath the brow the whole cup’ → “all the cavity beneath their eyebrows”: the object moves before the locative phrase; the figurative ‘cup’ becomes the anatomical “cavity”; the singular ‘brow’ becomes plural “eyebrows”, and its definite article becomes a possessive.

> 100 E avvegna che, sì come d’ un callo,  
> 101 Per la freddura ciascun sentimento  
> 102 Cessato avesse del mio viso stallo,  
> 103 Già mi parea sentire alquanto vento;  

And although, as from a callous, through the cold all feeling had departed from my face, it now seemed to me as if I felt some wind.

- *ciascun sentimento* ‘every sensation’ → “all feeling”: a distributive expression becomes a collective mass-noun expression.
- *Cessato avesse del mio viso stallo* ‘had ceased its residence in my face’ → “had departed from my face”: the construction involving *stallo*, ‘residence’ or ‘abode’, becomes a verb of departure, with the face expressed as the point of departure. The auxiliary moves before the participle, and the Italian pluperfect subjunctive becomes an English past perfect without a distinct subjunctive form.
- *Già mi parea sentire* ‘already it seemed to me to feel’ → “it now seemed to me as if I felt”: *già* is interpreted as “now” rather than ‘already’; the pronoun represented by “to me” follows the verb; and the infinitive becomes a finite clause introduced by the supplied “as if”.

> 104 Per ch’ io: Maestro mio, questo chi muove?  
> 105 Non è quaggiuso ogni vapore spento?  

Whereat I: “Master, who moves this? Is not all heat extinguished here below?”

- *Per ch’* ‘for which reason’ → “Whereat”: the connective phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *Maestro mio* ‘my master’ → “Master”: the possessive is omitted.
- *questo chi muove* ‘this who moves’ → “who moves this”: the fronted object moves after the verb.
- *Non è quaggiuso ogni vapore spento* ‘is not here below every vapour extinguished’ → “Is not all heat extinguished here below”: the locative moves to the end; *vapore* is interpreted as heat rather than vapour, and distributive ‘every’ becomes collective “all”.

> 106 Ond’ egli a me: Avaccio sarai dove  
> 107 Di ciò ti farà l’ occhio la risposta,  
> 108 Veggendo la cagion che il fiato piove.  

Whence he to me: “Soon shalt thou be where thine eye itself, seeing the cause which rains the blast, shall answer thee in this.”

- *Avaccio sarai* ‘soon you will be’ → “Soon shalt thou be”: the English future auxiliary precedes the subject after the initial adverb.
- *Di ciò ti farà l’ occhio la risposta, Veggendo la cagion* ‘about this the eye will make you the answer, seeing the cause’ → “thine eye itself, seeing the cause” and “shall answer thee in this”: the subject moves before the verb; its definite article becomes a possessive, and “itself” is supplied for emphasis. Making an answer becomes the single verb “answer”; the recipient follows that verb instead of preceding it; and the initial phrase ‘about this’ becomes the final “in this”. The participial clause moves from after the main clause to a position between its subject and verb.
- *che il fiato piove* ‘which the breath rains’ → “which rains the blast”: the object moves after the verb, and *fiato*, ‘breath’, is rendered as “blast”.

## Lines 109–117 — A Plea for Relief

> 109 E un de’ tristi della fredda crosta  
> 110 Gridò a noi: O anime crudeli

And one of the wretched shadows of the icy crust cried out to us: “O souls, so cruel that the last post of all is given to you!

- *tristi* ‘wretched ones’ → “wretched shadows”: the substantival adjective is rendered with the supplied noun “shadows”.
- *fredda* ‘cold’ → “icy”: coldness is rendered more specifically as iciness.
- *Gridò* ‘cried’ → “cried out”: English adds the idiomatic particle “out”.
- *crudeli Tanto* ‘cruel so’ → “so cruel”: the degree word moves before the adjective.
- *data v’ è l’ ultima posta* ‘given to you is the last post’ → “the last post of all is given to you”: the passive subject moves before the verb, and “of all” makes the superlative’s scope explicit.

> 113 Sì ch’ io sfoghi il dolor che il cor m’ impregna,  
> 114 Un poco pria che il pianto si raggeli.

- *Levatemi dal viso i duri veli* ‘Remove for me from the face the hard veils’ → “Remove the hard veils from my face”: the direct object moves before the prepositional phrase, and the dative *mi* becomes the possessive “my” modifying “face”.
- *Sì ch’ io sfoghi* ‘so that I vent’ → “that I may vent”: “that” alone renders the purpose conjunction *Sì che*, and the Italian subjunctive is expressed with “may”.
- *che il cor m’ impregna* ‘which the heart for me saturates’ → “which stuffs my heart”: the object moves after the verb, the dative *m’* becomes the possessive “my”, and saturation is rendered as stuffing.
- *si raggeli* ‘congeals’ → “freeze again”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive verb; “again” explicitly interprets the freezing as repeated.

> 115 Per ch’ io a lui: Se vuoi ch’ io ti sovvegna,  
> 116 Dimmi chi sei, e s’ io non ti disbrigo,  
> 117 Al fondo della ghiaccia ir mi convegna.

- *Se vuoi ch’ io ti sovvegna* ‘If thou wishest that I help thee’ → “If thou wouldst have me aid thee”: the present-tense expression of wishing becomes “wouldst have”, and the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object-plus-infinitive construction, with “me” as the subject of “aid”.
- *io non ti disbrigo* ‘I not thee extricate’ → “I do not extricate thee”: English supplies “do” to form the negative, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Al fondo della ghiaccia ir mi convegna* ‘To the bottom of the ice to go may it be necessary for me’ → “may I have to go to the bottom of the ice”: the impersonal construction with *mi* becomes the personal “I have to”; “may” expresses the optative subjunctive, and the destination moves from the beginning to the end.

## Summary — Close Fidelity with Syntactic Reshaping

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s vocabulary, imagery, and sequence of expression, but repeatedly rearranges its syntax. Subjects and objects placed after or before their usual positions in Italian move into more customary English order; pronouns preceding verbs generally follow them in translation. Thus *questo chi muove* becomes “who moves this”, while *maestro e donno* becomes the familiar English pairing “lord and master”. Yet the prose also preserves marked inversions, including “saw I” and “found I”, and retains compact expressions such as “falls rushing” and “winters here behind me”. Its archaic vocabulary and pronouns help maintain this closeness without making the rendering consistently word for word.

Changes of construction often accompany these rearrangements. Italian reflexive verbs become English intransitives, as *si volve* becomes “turns”; personal datives become possessives, as *Aprimi gli occhi* becomes “open my eyes”. Gerunds may become noun phrases: *Pensando* is rendered as “at the thought of”. Conversely, a noun-and-verb expression may contract into a single verb, as *mossa le dea* becomes “impels it”, or a single verb may expand, as *potè* becomes “had more power”. Subjunctives are sometimes represented by “may” or “mayest”, sometimes by an indicative; tense and aspect likewise receive English rather than mechanically equivalent forms. Throughout, Carlyle also supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and omits the Italian articles normally accompanying possessives.

Word choice ranges from close lexical correspondence to interpretive substitution. *Fiero* becomes the archaic “fell”, *pensieri* becomes “devices”, and *vapore* becomes “heat”. Concrete or figurative terms are sometimes translated through their implied meaning: *croce*, ‘cross’, becomes “torture”, and *coppo*, ‘cup’, becomes “cavity”. Connectives can shift the relation between clauses: *Però* is twice rendered as “but” or “But” rather than ‘therefore’. Elsewhere the Italian image remains conspicuous, as in “bear fruit of infamy”, “crystal vizors”, and “rains the blast”.

Additions usually clarify relationships or complete English constructions: *tal vicino* becomes “such a neighbour to him”, and *suso* is expanded to “on the earth above”. Articles frequently become possessives, making ownership or bodily reference explicit; singulars and plurals also change where English idiom favours another form. Omissions are generally small, including *oramai* and the possessive in *Maestro mio*, but the rendering of lines 37–39 notably leaves out the weeping, the sleep, and the statement that the sons were with the speaker. Overall, the translation combines substantial verbal fidelity with recurrent syntactic adjustment, explanatory additions, and occasional compression or interpretation.
