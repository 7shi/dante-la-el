# Inferno 1

## Lines 1–9 — Lost in a Dark Wood

> 1 Nel mezzo del cammin di nostra vita  
> 2 Mi ritrovai per una selva oscura,  
> 3 Chè la diritta via era smarrita.  

In the middle of the journey of our life, I found myself in a dark wood; for the straight way was lost.

- *Mi ritrovai* ‘myself I found’ → “I found myself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb, and the subject implicit in the verb ending is expressed.
- *per una selva oscura* ‘through a wood dark’ → “in a dark wood”: *per* is rendered by a preposition of location rather than movement through or about; the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.

> 4 Ahi quanto a dir qual era è cosa dura  
> 5 Questa selva selvaggia ed aspra e forte,  
> 6 Che nel pensier rinnova la paura!  

Ah! how hard a thing it is to tell what a wild, and rough, and stubborn wood this was, which in my thought renews the fear:

- *quanto a dir … è cosa dura* ‘how much to tell … is thing hard’ → “how hard a thing it is to tell”: the degree expressed by *quanto* is attached directly to “hard”; the adjective moves before the noun, and an indefinite article is supplied. The infinitive, which functions as the Italian subject, moves after the predicate, with anticipatory “it” taking its place.
- *qual era … Questa selva selvaggia ed aspra e forte* ‘what was … this wood wild and rough and strong’ → “what a wild, and rough, and stubborn wood this was”: the delayed Italian subject and its adjectives are recast as “what a” followed by adjectives and noun, with “this” becoming the subject of “was”. The adjectives precede the noun, an indefinite article is supplied, and *forte* is interpreted as “stubborn” rather than simply ‘strong’.
- *nel pensier* ‘in the thought’ → “in my thought”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 7 Tanto è amara, che poco è più morte:  

so bitter is it, that scarcely more is death.

- *Tanto è amara* ‘so is bitter’ → “so bitter is it”: the adjective moves before the verb, and the implicit subject is expressed after it.
- *poco è più morte* ‘little is more death’ → “scarcely more is death”: *poco* becomes “scarcely” rather than ‘little’, and “more” moves before the verb to join it.

> 8 Ma per trattar del ben ch’ivi trovai,  
> 9 Dirò dell’ altre cose, ch’io v’ ho scorte.  

But to treat of the good that I there found, I will relate the other things that I discerned.

- *Dirò dell’ altre cose* ‘I will speak of the other things’ → “I will relate the other things”: speaking ‘of’ something becomes relating it directly; the prepositional complement becomes a direct object.
- *ch’io v’ ho scorte* ‘that I there have discerned’ → “that I discerned”: the locative *v’* is omitted, and the Italian compound perfect becomes an English simple past.

## Lines 10–21 — Fear Eases at the Hill

> 10 Io non so ben ridir com’ io v’ entrai;  
> 11 Tant’ era pien di sonno in su quel punto,  
> 12 Che la verace via abbandonai.  

I cannot rightly tell how I entered it, so full of sleep was I about the moment that I left the true way.

- *Io non so ben ridir* ‘I do not know how to recount well’ → “I cannot rightly tell”: knowing how to recount becomes the ability to tell; “rightly” precedes the infinitive.
- *com’ io v’ entrai* ‘how I entered there’ → “how I entered it”: the locative pronoun *v’* becomes the direct object “it”.
- *Tant’ era pien di sonno* ‘So much was I full of sleep’ → “so full of sleep was I”: the degree word directly modifies “full”, and the whole adjective phrase precedes the verb and subject.
- *in su quel punto* ‘at that moment’ → “about the moment”: “about” replaces the more exact temporal preposition, and the demonstrative becomes a definite article.
- *la verace via abbandonai* ‘the true way I abandoned’ → “I left the true way”: the object moves after the verb, and “left” replaces ‘abandoned’.

> 13 Ma poi ch’ io fui al piè d’un colle giunto,  
> 14 Là ove terminava quella valle,  
> 15 Che m’ avea di paura il cor compunto,  

But after I had reached the foot of a Hill there, where that valley ended, which had pierced my heart with fear,

- *io fui al piè d’un colle giunto* ‘I had at the foot of a hill arrived’ → “I had reached the foot of a Hill”: the participle moves beside its auxiliary; ‘arrived at’ becomes the transitive “reached”.
- *Là ove terminava quella valle* ‘there where ended that valley’ → “there, where that valley ended”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *Che m’ avea di paura il cor compunto* ‘which had for me with fear the heart pierced’ → “which had pierced my heart with fear”: the dative *m’* becomes the possessive “my”; the participle moves beside its auxiliary, and “with fear” follows the object.

> 16 Guardai in alto, e vidi le sue spalle  
> 17 Vestite già de’ raggi del pianeta,  
> 18 Che mena dritto altrui per ogni calle.  

I looked up and saw its shoulders already clothed with the rays of the Planet that leads men straight on every road.

- *Guardai in alto* ‘I looked on high’ → “I looked up”: the prepositional phrase becomes the directional adverb “up”.
- *Vestite già de’ raggi* ‘clothed already in the rays’ → “already clothed with the rays”: “already” moves before the participle, and the clothing complement is introduced by “with” rather than ‘in’.
- *mena dritto altrui* ‘leads straight others’ → “leads men straight”: the indefinite pronoun *altrui* becomes the generic noun “men”, and the object moves before “straight”.
- *per ogni calle* ‘along every road’ → “on every road”: the preposition expressing a route becomes “on”.

> 19 Allor fu la paura un poco queta,  
> 20 Che nel lago del cor m’ era durata  
> 21 La notte, ch’ io passai con tanta pieta.  

Then the fear was somewhat calmed, which had continued in the lake of my heart the night that I passed so piteously.

- *fu la paura un poco queta* ‘was the fear a little quiet’ → “the fear was somewhat calmed”: the subject moves before the verb; ‘a little’ becomes “somewhat”, and the adjective ‘quiet’ becomes the participle “calmed”.
- *nel lago del cor m’ era durata* ‘in the lake of the heart had for me lasted’ → “had continued in the lake of my heart”: the verb precedes the locative phrase; ‘lasted’ becomes “continued”, and the dative *m’* becomes the possessive “my”.
- *con tanta pieta* ‘with so much distress’ → “so piteously”: the prepositional phrase containing a noun becomes an adverbial expression, with quantity recast as degree.

## Lines 22–33 — Looking Back and Meeting a Leopard

> 22 E come quei, che con lena affannata  
> 23 Uscito fuor del pelago alla riva,  
> 24 Si volge all’ acqua perigliosa, e guata;  

And as he, who with panting breath has escaped from the deep sea to the shore, turns to the dangerous water and gazes;

- *che ... Uscito fuor* ‘who ... having come out’ → “who” ... “has escaped”: the participial phrase becomes a finite present-perfect verb, and ‘coming out’ becomes “escaped”. The relative pronoun consequently governs this verb rather than the later verb of turning.
- *con lena affannata* ‘with breath laboured’ → “with panting breath”: the adjective precedes the noun, and “panting” gives a more specific description than ‘laboured’.
- *del pelago* ‘from the sea’ → “from the deep sea”: “deep sea” expands the noun, expressing its association with the open sea.
- *Si volge* ‘turns himself’ → “turns”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an intransitive English verb.
- *all’ acqua perigliosa* ‘to the water dangerous’ → “to the dangerous water”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 25 Così l’ animo mio, che ancor fuggiva,  
> 26 Si volse indietro a rimirar lo passo,  
> 27 Che non lasciò giammai persona viva.  

so my mind, which still was fleeing, turned back to see the pass that no one ever left alive.

- *l’ animo mio* ‘the mind my’ → “my mind”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *che ancor fuggiva* ‘which still fled’ → “which still was fleeing”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English past progressive, emphasizing the continuing action.
- *Si volse indietro* ‘turned itself back’ → “turned back”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *a rimirar* ‘to gaze at’ → “to see”: the verb of deliberate looking becomes the more general verb of seeing.
- *Che non lasciò giammai persona viva* ‘which never left a person alive’ → “that no one ever left alive”: the grammatical roles are reversed. The Italian makes the pass the subject of ‘left’ and ‘a person’ its object; the translation makes “no one” the subject and the pass the object. The negative also moves into “no one”. This adopts the interpretation that nobody emerged alive, rather than reproducing the construction in which the pass left nobody alive.

> 28 Poi ch’ ebbi riposato il corpo lasso,  

After I had rested my wearied body,

- *il corpo lasso* ‘the body weary’ → “my wearied body”: the definite article becomes a possessive, the adjective moves before the noun, and ‘weary’ is expressed by the participial adjective “wearied”.

> 29 Ripresi via per la piaggia diserta,  
> 30 Sì che il piè fermo sempre era il più basso.  

I took the way again along the desert strand, so that the firm foot always was the lower.

- *Ripresi via* ‘I resumed way’ → “I took the way again”: the single verb is unpacked into “took” and “again”, and an article is supplied before “way”.
- *per la piaggia diserta* ‘along the slope deserted’ → “along the desert strand”: “strand” takes *piaggia* in its shoreline sense rather than its sense of sloping ground; the adjective moves before the noun.
- *il piè fermo* ‘the foot firm’ → “the firm foot”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *il più basso* ‘the lowest’ → “the lower”: the Italian relative-superlative form becomes an English comparative, appropriate to a comparison between two feet.

> 31 Ed ecco, quasi al cominciar dell’ erta,  
> 32 Una lonza leggiera e presta molto,  
> 33 Che di pel maculato era coverta.  

And behold, almost at the commencement of the steep, a Leopard, light and very nimble, which was covered with a spotted skin:

- *al cominciar* ‘at beginning’ → “at the commencement”: the substantivized infinitive is rendered by an ordinary noun with an article.
- *presta molto* ‘nimble very’ → “very nimble”: the degree adverb moves before the adjective.
- *Che di pel maculato era coverta* ‘which with fur spotted was covered’ → “which was covered with a spotted skin”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb, ‘fur’ becomes “skin”, an indefinite article is supplied, and the adjective precedes its noun.

## Lines 34–47 — Hope and Renewed Fear

> 34 E non mi si partia dinanzi al volto;  

and it went not from before my face;

- *non mi si partia dinanzi al volto* ‘did not withdraw from me, from before the face’ → “it went not from before my face”: the pronominal *partirsi* becomes plain “went”; the personal reference in *mi* becomes the possessive “my” before “face”. The negative follows rather than precedes the verb.

> 35 Anzi impediva tanto il mio cammino,  
> 36 Ch’io fui per ritornar più volte vôlto.  

nay, so impeded my way, that I had often turned to go back.

- *Anzi* ‘rather’ → “nay”: the corrective conjunction becomes an emphatic English connective.
- *impediva tanto il mio cammino* ‘impeded so much my way’ → “so impeded my way”: *tanto* is reduced to “so” and placed before the verb.
- *Ch’io fui per ritornar più volte vôlto* ‘that I was several times turned to return’ → “that I had often turned to go back”: the passive or resultative *fui … vôlto* becomes the active pluperfect “had … turned”; *più volte* becomes “often” rather than ‘several times’. The purpose phrase *per ritornar* becomes “to go back” and moves after “turned”.

> 37 Tempo era dal principio del mattino;  

The time was at the beginning of the morning;

- *Tempo era dal principio* ‘Time was from the beginning’ → “The time was at the beginning”: “The” is supplied, and *dal* is rendered “at the” rather than ‘from the’.

> 38 E il Sol montava in su con quelle stelle  
> 39 Ch’eran con lui, quando l’ Amor Divino  
> 40 Mosse da prima quelle cose belle;  

and the Sun was mounting up with those stars, which were with him when Divine Love first moved those fair things:

- *l’Amor Divino* ‘the Love Divine’ → “Divine Love”: the article is omitted, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *da prima* ‘at first’ → “first”: the prepositional expression becomes a single adverb and moves before “moved”.
- *quelle cose belle* ‘those things beautiful’ → “those fair things”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 41 Sì che a bene sperar m’ eran cagione  
> 42 Di quella fera la gaietta pelle,  
> 43 L’ ora del tempo, e la dolce stagione:  

so that the gay skin of that animal, the hour of time, and the sweet season, were causes to me of good hope;

- *a bene sperar m’eran cagione* ‘for hoping well were to me a cause’ → “were causes to me of good hope”: the infinitive and adverb become the noun phrase “good hope”, introduced by “of” rather than ‘for’. Singular *cagione* becomes plural “causes”; the verb and predicate are placed after their subjects, and “to me” follows “causes”.
- *Di quella fera la gaietta pelle* ‘of that wild beast the mottled skin’ → “the gay skin of that animal”: the possessive phrase moves after “skin”; *fera* becomes the more general “animal”. “gay” selects the bright or pretty sense of *gaietta* rather than explicitly describing mottling. Carlyle takes the skin as one of the causes of hope.
- *L’ora del tempo* ‘the hour of the time’ → “the hour of time”: the article contained in *del* is omitted.

> 44 Ma non sì, che paura non mi desse  
> 45 La vista, che m’ apparve, d’un leone.  

yet not so, but that I feared at the sight, which appeared to me, of a Lion.

- *Ma non sì, che paura non mi desse La vista* ‘But not so that the sight did not give me fear’ → “yet not so, but that I feared at the sight”: “yet” replaces ‘but’, and the negative subordinate construction becomes “but that” followed by an affirmative clause. ‘Give me fear’ becomes “I feared”, changing the sight from the grammatical subject to a complement introduced by “at”. The imperfect subjunctive *desse* becomes an indicative past-tense verb.
- *che m’apparve* ‘which to me appeared’ → “which appeared to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.

> 46 Questi parea, che contra me venesse  
> 47 Con la testa alta, e con rabbiosa fame;  

He seemed coming upon me with head erect, and furious hunger;

- *Questi parea, che contra me venesse* ‘This one seemed that he was coming against me’ → “He seemed coming upon me”: the demonstrative becomes “He”; the finite subordinate clause, with its imperfect subjunctive, becomes the participial complement “coming”. *Contra me* becomes “upon me” and moves after the verb of motion.
- *Con la testa alta* ‘with the head high’ → “with head erect”: the article is omitted, and “erect” expresses posture rather than simply height.
- *e con rabbiosa fame* ‘and with furious hunger’ → “and furious hunger”: the repeated preposition *con* is omitted.

## Lines 48–60 — The She-Wolf Drives Dante Back

> 48 Sì che parea che l’aer ne tremesse.  

so that the air seemed to quake thereat.

- *parea che l’aer ne tremesse* ‘it seemed that the air trembled at it’ → “the air seemed to quake thereat”: the subordinate clause becomes an infinitive construction, with “the air” made the subject of “seemed”; the Italian subjunctive becomes “to quake”.

> 49 Ed una lupa, che di tutte brame  
> 50 Sembiava carca nella sua magrezza,  
> 51 E molte genti fe’ già viver grame.  

And a She-wolf, that looked full of all cravings in her leanness; and has ere now made many live in sorrow:

- *di tutte brame Sembiava carca* ‘with all cravings seemed laden’ → “looked full of all cravings”: seeming is rendered as looking, and being laden as being full; the phrase describing the cravings moves after the predicate, with “of” replacing ‘with’.
- *molte genti* ‘many people’ → “many”: the noun is omitted, and the object moves from before the causative verb to after it.
- *fe’ già* ‘made already’ → “has ere now made”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, and *già* becomes “ere now”, placed before the main verb.
- *viver grame* ‘live sorrowful’ → “live in sorrow”: the adjective describing the people becomes a prepositional phrase describing how they live.

> 52 Questa mi porse tanto di gravezza  
> 53 Con la paura, che uscia di sua vista,  
> 54 Ch’ io perdei la speranza dell’altezza.  

She brought such heaviness upon me with the terror of her aspect, that I lost the hope of ascending.

- *Questa* ‘this one’ → “She”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *mi porse tanto di gravezza* ‘to me offered so much heaviness’ → “brought such heaviness upon me”: offering becomes bringing; the indirect object becomes “upon me” and moves after the direct object; the quantitative ‘so much’ becomes “such”.
- *la paura* ‘the fear’ → “the terror”: the English uses a stronger term.
- *che uscia di sua vista* ‘which came out of her appearance’ → “of her aspect”: the relative clause expressing the fear’s source is compressed into a prepositional phrase, with the verb omitted.
- *dell’altezza* ‘of the height’ → “of ascending”: the noun naming the height becomes a gerund naming the action of rising towards it.

> 55 E quale è quei, che volentieri acquista,  
> 56 E giugne il tempo che perder lo face,  
> 57 Che in tutti i suoi pensier piange e s’ attrista;  

And as one who is eager in gaining, and, when the time arrives that makes him lose, weeps and afflicts himself in all his thoughts;

- *E quale è quei* ‘And as is he’ → “And as one”: the verb ‘is’ is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes the indefinite “one”.
- *che volentieri acquista* ‘who willingly gains’ → “who is eager in gaining”: the adverb becomes the predicative adjective “eager”, while the finite verb becomes a gerund governed by “in”.
- *E giugne il tempo* ‘and arrives the time’ → “and, when the time arrives”: “when” is supplied, making the clause explicitly temporal, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *che perder lo face* ‘that to lose him makes’ → “that makes him lose”: the causative verb moves before its object and the dependent infinitive.
- *Che in tutti i suoi pensier piange e s’attrista* ‘who in all his thoughts weeps and saddens himself’ → “weeps and afflicts himself in all his thoughts”: the repeated relative pronoun is not separately represented; the phrase about his thoughts moves after both verbs, and saddening himself becomes afflicting himself.

> 58 Tal mi fece la bestia senza pace,  
> 59 Che, venendomi incontro, a poco a poco  
> 60 Mi ripingeva là dove il Sol tace.  

such that restless beast made me, which coming against me, by little and little drove me back to where the Sun is silent.

- *Tal mi fece la bestia senza pace* ‘Such me made the beast without peace’ → “such that restless beast made me”: the subject moves before the verb and the object pronoun after it; the definite article becomes the demonstrative “that”; “restless”, placed before the noun, replaces the following phrase ‘without peace’.
- *venendomi incontro* ‘coming towards me’ → “coming against me”: “against” gives the approach an explicitly opposing force, and the attached pronoun becomes the object of an English preposition.
- *a poco a poco* ‘little by little’ → “by little and little”: the repeated Italian prepositional construction becomes an English phrase joining the repetitions with “and”.
- *Mi ripingeva* ‘me was pushing back’ → “drove me back”: pushing becomes driving, the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “to where”: the locative ‘there’ is replaced by a preposition explicitly marking direction.

## Lines 61–72 — The Stranger Identifies Himself

> 61 Mentre ch’ io rovinava in basso loco,  
> 62 Dinanzi agli occhi mi si fu offerto  
> 63 Chi per lungo silenzio parea fioco.  

Whilst I was rushing downwards, there appeared before my eyes one who seemed hoarse from long silence.

- *rovinava* ‘was falling headlong’ → “was rushing”: the verb of falling is rendered as rapid movement.
- *in basso loco* ‘into a low place’ → “downwards”: the phrase naming a destination becomes an adverb of direction.
- *Dinanzi agli occhi mi si fu offerto* ‘Before the eyes presented himself to me’ → “there appeared before my eyes”: presentation becomes appearance, with introductory “there”; the phrase locating the appearance moves after the verb, and *mi* is represented by the possessive “my”.
- *Chi* ‘who’ → “one who”: the substantive relative is expanded with “one”.
- *per lungo silenzio parea fioco* ‘from long silence seemed faint’ → “seemed hoarse from long silence”: the causal phrase moves after the adjective; “hoarse” interprets *fioco* specifically as a quality of the voice.

> 64 Quando vidi costui nel gran diserto,  
> 65 Miserere di me, gridai a lui,  
> 66 Qual che tu sie, od ombra, od uomo certo.  

When I saw him in the great desert, I cried: “Have pity on me, whate’er thou be, whether shade or veritable man!”

- *costui* ‘this one’ → “him”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *Miserere di me* ‘Have mercy on me’ → “Have pity on me”: mercy is rendered as pity.
- *gridai a lui* ‘I cried to him’ → “I cried”: the reporting clause moves before the direct speech, and ‘to him’ is omitted.
- *od … od* ‘or … or’ → “whether” and “or”: the repeated disjunction becomes the English construction introducing alternatives.

> 67 Risposemi: Non uomo, uomo già fui,  

He answered me: “Not man, a man I once was;

- *uomo già fui* ‘man formerly I was’ → “a man I once was”: an indefinite article is supplied before the predicate noun, and the explicit subject precedes the adverb.

> 68 E li parenti miei furon Lombardi,  
> 69 E Mantovani per patria ambedui.  

and my parents were Lombards, and both of Mantua by country.

- *li parenti miei* ‘the parents my’ → “my parents”: the possessive moves before the noun, and its accompanying article is omitted.
- *E Mantovani per patria ambedui* ‘and Mantuans by homeland both’ → “and both of Mantua by country”: the designation ‘Mantuans’ becomes the prepositional phrase “of Mantua”, and “both” moves from the end to the beginning of the predicate.

> 70 Nacqui sub Julio, ancorchè fosse tardi,  

I was born sub Julio, though it was late;

- *ancorchè fosse tardi* ‘although it were late’ → “though it was late”: the Italian subjunctive in the concessive clause becomes an English indicative.

> 71 E vissi a Roma sotto il buono Augusto,  
> 72 Al tempo degli Dei falsi e bugiardi.  

and lived at Rome under the good Augustus, in the time of the false and lying gods.

- *Al tempo* ‘At the time’ → “in the time”: the temporal preposition changes.
- *degli Dei falsi e bugiardi* ‘of the gods false and lying’ → “of the false and lying gods”: both adjectives move before the noun.

## Lines 73–85 — Virgil’s Poetry and Dante’s Praise

> 73 Poeta fui, e cantai di quel giusto  
> 74 Figliuol d’ Anchise, che venne da Troia,  
> 75 Poi che il superbo Ilion fu combusto.  

A Poet I was; and sang of that just son of Anchises, who came from Troy after proud Ilium was burnt.

- *Poeta* ‘poet’ → “A Poet”: English supplies an indefinite article before the predicate noun.

> 76 Ma tu, perchè ritorni a tanta noia?  

But thou, why returnest thou to such disquiet?

- *tu, perchè ritorni* ‘thou, why returnest’ → “thou, why returnest thou”: the emphatic initial pronoun is retained and then repeated after the verb in English question order.
- *tanta* ‘so much’ → “such”: an expression of quantity or degree becomes one of kind or quality.
- *noia* ‘distress’ → “disquiet”: the English emphasizes uneasiness rather than suffering or trouble.

> 77 Perchè non sali il dilettoso monte,  
> 78 Ch’ è principio e cagion di tutta gioia?  

why ascendest not the delectable mountain, which is the beginning and the cause of all gladness?”

- *non sali* ‘not ascendest’ → “ascendest not”: the negative follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *principio e cagion* ‘beginning and cause’ → “the beginning and the cause”: English supplies a definite article before each noun.

> 79 Or se’ tu quel Virgilio, e quella fonte,  
> 80 Che spande di parlar sì largo fiume?  
> 81 Risposi lui con vergognosa fronte.  

“Art thou then that Virgil, and that fountain which pours abroad so rich a stream of speech?” I answered him, with bashful front.

- *Or* ‘now’ → “then”: the connective is rendered as inferential rather than temporal and moved after the subject.
- *spande* ‘spreads’ → “pours abroad”: a single verb becomes a verb with an adverb, explicitly expressing the spreading as a pouring.
- *di parlar* ‘of speaking’ → “of speech”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun, and the phrase moves from before the object to after it.
- *sì largo fiume* ‘so broad river’ → “so rich a stream”: breadth becomes richness, ‘river’ becomes “stream”, and English supplies an indefinite article.
- *Risposi lui* ‘replied to him’ → “I answered him”: the Italian recipient, expressed by an unprepositioned dative pronoun, becomes the direct object of “answered”.
- *vergognosa* ‘ashamed’ → “bashful”: the English selects the nuance of shyness or modest embarrassment rather than shame.

> 82 O degli altri poeti onore e lume,  

“O glory, and light of other poets!

- *degli altri poeti* ‘of the other poets’ → “of other poets”: the definite article is omitted, and the phrase moves from before the nouns it qualifies to after them.
- *onore* ‘honour’ → “glory”: the English substitutes a word emphasizing distinction or renown.

> 83 Vagliami il lungo studio e il grande amore,  
> 84 Che m’ han fatto cercar lo tuo volume.  

May the long zeal avail me, and the great love, that made me search thy volume.

- *Vagliami* ‘may it avail me’ → “May” … “avail me”: the optative subjunctive becomes a construction with a modal auxiliary; the attached pronoun becomes a separate word, and the main verb follows the first subject phrase.
- *studio* ‘study’ → “zeal”: the rendering emphasizes earnest application rather than study itself.
- *m’ han fatto* ‘me have made’ → “made me”: the present perfect becomes a simple past, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *cercar* ‘seek’ → “search”: the translation takes the verb in the sense of examining or searching through, rather than looking for.

> 85 Tu se’ lo mio maestro, e il mio autore:  

Thou art my master and my author.

- Literal.

## Lines 86–100 — A Plea for Help and Another Road

> 86 Tu se’ solo colui, da cui io tolsi  
> 87 Lo bello stile, che m’ ha fatto onore.  

Thou alone art he from whom I took the good style that hath done me honour.

- *Tu se’ solo colui* ‘Thou art the only one’ → “Thou alone art he”: exclusiveness is attached to the subject, “Thou alone”, rather than expressed in the predicate ‘the only one’.
- *Lo bello stile* ‘the beautiful style’ → “the good style”: aesthetic praise becomes the broader judgment “good”.
- *m’ ha fatto onore* ‘to me hath done honour’ → “hath done me honour”: the indirect-object pronoun moves from before the verb to after it.

> 88 Vedi la bestia, per cui io mi volsi:  

See the beast from which I turned back.

- *per cui io mi volsi* ‘because of which I turned myself’ → “from which I turned back”: the causal relation becomes one of movement away; the reflexive construction is replaced by “turned back”, making the reversal explicit.

> 89 Aiutami da lei, famoso saggio,  
> 90 Ch’ ella mi fa tremar le vene e i polsi.  

Help me from her, thou famous sage; for she makes my veins and pulses tremble.”

- *famoso saggio* ‘famous sage’ → “thou famous sage”: “thou” is added to the vocative.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *mi fa tremar le vene e i polsi* ‘to me makes tremble the veins and the pulses’ → “makes my veins and pulses tremble”: the personal dative becomes the possessive “my”; the infinitive moves after the things made to tremble, and the repeated article is not retained.

> 91 A te convien tenere altro viaggio,  
> 92 Rispose, poi che lagrimar mi vide,  
> 93 Se vuoi campar d’ esto loco selvaggio:  

“Thou must take another road,” he answered, when he saw me weeping, “if thou desirest to escape from this wild place;

- *A te convien tenere altro viaggio* ‘For thee it is necessary to hold another journey’ → “Thou must take another road”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal subject with “must”; ‘hold another journey’ is recast idiomatically as “take another road”, with the journey expressed as a route.
- *poi che* ‘after that’ → “when”: explicit temporal succession becomes a more general temporal relation.
- *lagrimar mi vide* ‘weep me he saw’ → “he saw me weeping”: the infinitive becomes a participle, and the order changes from infinitive–object–verb to subject–verb–object–participle.
- *vuoi campar* ‘thou wishest to survive’ → “thou desirest to escape”: volition is expressed as desire, and *campare* is taken in its sense of escaping rather than simply remaining alive.
- *d’ esto loco selvaggio* ‘from this place wild’ → “from this wild place”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 94 Chè questa bestia, per la qual tu gride,  
> 95 Non lascia altrui passar per la sua via,  
> 96 Ma tanto lo impedisce, che l’ uccide:  

because this beast, for which thou criest, lets not men pass her way, but so entangles that she slays them;

- *Non lascia* ‘not lets’ → “lets not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *altrui* ‘another’ → “men”: an indefinite singular pronoun becomes a generic plural noun.
- *per la sua via* ‘along her way’ → “her way”: the preposition is omitted, leaving the route directly after “pass”.
- *tanto lo impedisce* ‘so much him hinders’ → “so entangles”: the object pronoun is omitted, and the general verb ‘hinders’ becomes the more specific “entangles”.
- *l’ uccide* ‘him she kills’ → “she slays them”: the singular object becomes plural to agree with “men”, and moves after the verb.

> 97 E ha natura sì malvagia e ria,  
> 98 Che mai non empie la bramosa voglia,  
> 99 E dopo il pasto ha più fame che pria.  

and has a nature so perverse and vicious, that she never satiates her craving appetite; and after feeding, she is hungrier than before.

- *ha natura* ‘has nature’ → “has a nature”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *sì malvagia e ria* ‘so wicked and evil’ → “so perverse and vicious”: the two broadly condemnatory adjectives are rendered with the more specific senses of perverseness and viciousness.
- *mai non* ‘never not’ → “never”: Italian negative concord is expressed by a single English negative.
- *empie* ‘fills’ → “satiates”: figurative filling is rendered explicitly as satisfying an appetite.
- *la bramosa voglia* ‘the craving desire’ → “her craving appetite”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the broader ‘desire’ becomes “appetite”.
- *dopo il pasto* ‘after the meal’ → “after feeding”: a definite noun becomes an articleless gerund denoting the activity.
- *ha più fame* ‘has more hunger’ → “she is hungrier”: possession of a greater quantity of hunger becomes a copular construction with a comparative adjective.

> 100 Molti son gli animali, a cui s’ ammoglia,  

The animals to which she weds herself are many;

- *Molti son gli animali, a cui s’ ammoglia* ‘Many are the animals to which she weds herself’ → “The animals to which she weds herself are many”: the subject and its relative clause move before the copula, while the initially placed predicate adjective moves to the end.

## Lines 101–105 — The Coming of the Greyhound

> 101 E più saranno ancora, infin che il Veltro  
> 102 Verrà, che la farà morir di doglia.  

and will yet be more, until the Greyhound comes, that will make her die with pain.

- *più saranno ancora* ‘more will be yet’ → “will yet be more”: “more” moves after the verb, while “yet” comes between the auxiliary and “be”.
- *infin che il Veltro Verrà* ‘until the Greyhound will come’ → “until the Greyhound comes”: the Italian future becomes an English present, as usual in a future-referring clause introduced by “until”.
- *che la farà morir* ‘that her will make die’ → “that will make her die”: the object pronoun moves from before the causative verb to after “make”.
- *di doglia* ‘of pain’ → “with pain”: the causal preposition ‘of’ becomes “with”.

> 103 Questi non ciberà terra nè peltro,  
> 104 Ma sapienza, e amore, e virtute;  

He will not feed on land or pelf, but on wisdom, and love, and manfulness;

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “He”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *non ciberà* ‘will not eat’ → “will not feed on”: the transitive verb becomes “feed” followed by a preposition.
- *nè* ‘nor’ → “or”: English uses “or” within the scope of the preceding “not”, rather than repeating the negative through ‘nor’.
- *peltro* ‘pewter’ → “pelf”: the metal-name is interpreted as wealth and rendered with a word for money or riches.
- *Ma* ‘but’ → “but on”: the preposition required by “feed” is repeated before the contrasting objects.
- *virtute* ‘virtue’ → “manfulness”: the broader term for virtue or excellence is given the more specific sense of manly courage or vigour.

> 105 E sua nazion sarà tra Feltro e Feltro.  

and his nation shall be between Feltro and Feltro.

- *nazion* ‘birth’ or ‘birthplace’ → “nation”: the translation uses a word ordinarily denoting a people or country rather than directly expressing birth or origin.

## Lines 106–120 — Italy’s Salvation and Dante’s Guide

> 106 Di quell’ umile Italia fia salute,  
> 107 Per cui morì la vergine Cammilla,  
> 108 Eurialo, e Turno, e Niso di ferute:  

He shall be the salvation of that low Italy, for which Camilla the virgin, Euryalus, and Turnus, and Nisus, died of wounds.

- *Di quell’ umile Italia* ‘of that humble Italy’ → “of that low Italy”: the opening prepositional phrase moves after the predicate; *umile* is rendered as “low” rather than ‘humble’.
- *fia salute* ‘shall be salvation’ → “He shall be the salvation”: English supplies the subject and adds the definite article before “salvation”.
- *morì la vergine Cammilla, Eurialo, e Turno, e Niso* ‘died the virgin Camilla, Euryalus, and Turnus, and Nisus’ → “Camilla the virgin, Euryalus, and Turnus, and Nisus, died”: the verb moves from before the enumerated subjects to after them; the designation ‘the virgin’ moves after Camilla’s name.

> 109 Questi la caccerà per ogni villa,  
> 110 Fin che l’ avrà rimessa nell’ Inferno,  
> 111 Là onde invidia prima dipartilla.  

He shall chase her through every city, till he have put her into Hell again; from which envy first set her loose.

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “He”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *la caccerà* ‘her shall chase’ → “shall chase her”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *per ogni villa* ‘through every town’ → “through every city”: “city” renders *villa*, a settlement or town.
- *l’ avrà rimessa nell’ Inferno* ‘he will have put her back into Hell’ → “he have put her into Hell again”: the Italian future perfect becomes an English perfect subjunctive after “till”; the restorative sense of *rimessa* is expressed by “again”, placed after “into Hell”.
- *Là onde* ‘there whence’ → “from which”: the separate locative ‘there’ disappears, and the relative adverb becomes a preposition with a relative pronoun referring to Hell.
- *dipartilla* ‘sent her away’ → “set her loose”: departure or separation is rendered as release.

> 112 Ond’ io per lo tuo me’ penso e discerno,  
> 113 Che tu mi segui, ed io sarò tua guida,  

Wherefore I think and discern this for thy best, that thou follow me. And I will be thy guide,

- *Ond’* ‘whence’ → “Wherefore”: the connective’s inferential force is expressed directly, rather than through locative wording.
- *per lo tuo me’* ‘for thy better’ → “for thy best”: the comparative *me’*, shortened from *meglio*, becomes the English superlative “best”; the phrase moves from before the verbs to after them.
- *penso e discerno* ‘think and discern’ → “think and discern this”: “this” is supplied as an object anticipating the following clause.
- *tu mi segui* ‘thou me follow’ → “thou follow me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 114 E trarrotti di qui per luogo eterno,  

and lead thee hence through an eternal place,

- *trarrotti* ‘I shall draw thee’ → “lead thee”: ‘draw’ becomes “lead”; the Italian finite future becomes a coordinated verb sharing the subject and future auxiliary of the preceding English clause.
- *di qui* ‘from here’ → “hence”: the prepositional phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *per luogo eterno* ‘through a place eternal’ → “through an eternal place”: English adds an indefinite article and places the adjective before the noun.

> 115 Ove udirai le disperate strida,  
> 116 Vedrai gli antichi spiriti dolenti,  
> 117 Che la seconda morte ciascun grida:  

where thou shalt hear the hopeless shrieks, shalt see the ancient spirits in pain; so that each calls for second death.

- *dolenti* ‘suffering’ → “in pain”: an adjective becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *Che* ‘who’ → “so that”: Carlyle takes the connective as introducing a consequence rather than a relative clause referring to the spirits.
- *la seconda morte* ‘the second death’ → “second death”: the definite article is omitted, and the Italian’s fronted object moves after the verb.
- *ciascun grida* ‘each cries’ → “each calls for”: “calls for” interprets the crying as a demand or appeal; English introduces the object with “for”.

> 118 E vederai color, che son contenti  
> 119 Nel fuoco, perchè speran di venire,  
> 120 Quando che sia, alle beate genti:  

And thou shalt see those who are contented in the fire; for they hope to come, whensoever it be, amongst the blessed.

- *perchè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *alle beate genti* ‘to the blessed peoples’ → “amongst the blessed”: movement ‘to’ becomes movement “amongst”; the noun *genti* is left unexpressed, and “the blessed” functions as a collective noun phrase.

## Lines 121–136 — A Worthier Guide and Dante’s Request

> 121 Alle quai poi se tu vorrai salire,  
> 122 Anima fia a ciò di me più degna:  

Then to these, if thou desirest to ascend, there shall be a Spirit worthier than I to guide thee.

- *Alle quai* ‘to which’ → “to these”: the relative pronoun becomes a demonstrative.
- *vorrai* ‘wilt wish’ → “desirest”: the future becomes a present tense.
- *Anima fia* ‘a soul will be’ → “there shall be a Spirit”: “there” is supplied to form an existential construction, the subject follows the verb, and ‘soul’ becomes “Spirit”.
- *a ciò* ‘for this’ → “to guide thee”: the general reference becomes an explicit infinitive phrase, placed at the end of the sentence.
- *di me più degna* ‘than me more worthy’ → “worthier than I”: the comparative adjective precedes its complement, and the pronoun takes the subject form “I”, with an understood ‘am’.

> 123 Con lei ti lascierò nel mio partire;  

With her will I leave thee at my parting.

- *Con lei ti lascierò* ‘with her thee I shall leave’ → “With her will I leave thee”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, while the supplied subject follows the auxiliary.
- *nel mio partire* ‘in my departing’ → “at my parting”: “at” replaces ‘in’, and the Italian infinitive used as a noun becomes “parting”.

> 124 Chè quello Imperador, che lassù regna,  
> 125 Perch’ io fui ribellante alla sua legge,  
> 126 Non vuol che in sua città per me si vegna.  

For that Emperor who reigns above, because I was rebellious to his law, wills not that I come into his city.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *lassù regna* ‘up there reigns’ → “reigns above”: the adverb follows the verb, and “above” replaces the more explicitly locative ‘up there’.
- *Non vuol* ‘not wills’ → “wills not”: the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *che in sua città per me si vegna* ‘that into his city through me one should come’ → “that I come into his city”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal clause with “I” as subject, and the destination follows the verb. Carlyle takes the restriction as excluding the speaker’s own entry, rather than entry by someone through his agency; *per me* is therefore not separately represented.

> 127 In tutte parti impera, e quivi regge:  

In all parts he rules; and there he dwells.

- *regge* ‘rules’ → “dwells”: the translation adopts a locative interpretation in place of the verb’s ordinary sense of ruling or governing.

> 128 Quivi è la sua cittade, e l’ alto seggio:  

There is his city, and his high seat.

- Literal.

> 129 O felice colui, cui ivi elegge!  

O happy whom he chooses for it!”

- *colui, cui ivi elegge* ‘the one whom he chooses there’ → “whom he chooses for it”: the demonstrative antecedent ‘the one’ is omitted, and the locative adverb ‘there’ becomes “for it”, expressing the destination or object of the choosing.

> 130 Ed io a lui: Poeta, io ti richieggio  
> 131 Per quello Iddio, che tu non conoscesti,  
> 132 A ciò ch’ io fugga questo male e peggio,  

And I to him: “Poet, I beseech thee by that God whom thou knewest not: in order that I may escape this ill and worse,

- *io ti richieggio* ‘I thee beseech’ → “I beseech thee”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *non conoscesti* ‘not knewest’ → “knewest not”: the negative follows the verb.
- *A ciò ch’* ‘to this, that’ → “in order that”: the Italian expression of purpose becomes the corresponding English conjunction phrase.
- *io fugga* ‘I flee’ → “I may escape”: the subjunctive is expressed with “may”, and ‘flee’ becomes “escape”.

> 133 Che tu mi meni là dov’ or dicesti,  
> 134 Sì ch’ io vegga la porta di San Pietro,  
> 135 E color, che tu fai cotanto mesti.  

lead me where thou now hast said, so that I may see the Gate of St. Peter, and those whom thou makest so sad.”

- *Che tu mi meni* ‘that thou me lead’ → “lead me”: the subordinate clause completing the request becomes an imperative; the conjunction and explicit subject disappear, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *là dov’* ‘there where’ → “where”: the antecedent adverb ‘there’ is omitted.
- *or dicesti* ‘now thou saidst’ → “thou now hast said”: the supplied subject precedes “now”, and the Italian simple past becomes an English present perfect.
- *io vegga* ‘I see’ → “I may see”: the subjunctive is expressed with the auxiliary “may”.

> 136 Allor si mosse; ed io li tenni dietro.  

Then he moved; and I kept on behind him.

- *si mosse* ‘he moved himself’ → “he moved”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes an English intransitive verb, without a reflexive pronoun.
- *io li tenni dietro* ‘I to him kept behind’ → “I kept on behind him”: the dative pronoun is represented by “him” after “behind”, and “on” is supplied to express continuation.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Syntax

Carlyle generally keeps close to the Italian’s sequence of statements, images, and repeated expressions, but adjusts their internal arrangement to English usage. Adjectives commonly move before nouns, objects follow verbs, and participles stand beside their auxiliaries. More extensive rearrangements bring delayed subjects forward: *Molti son gli animali* becomes “The animals” … “are many”. Yet the translation also preserves marked inversions, as in “so bitter is it” and “A Poet I was”, retaining something of the Italian’s emphasis rather than consistently adopting ordinary English order.

Changes of construction often replace Italian idiom with an English equivalent. Reflexive verbs become intransitive verbs, as *Si volge* becomes “turns”; personal datives become possessives, as *mi fa tremar le vene e i polsi* becomes “makes my veins and pulses tremble”. Nouns and phrases sometimes change grammatical form: *con tanta pieta* becomes “so piteously”, *dell’altezza* becomes “of ascending”, and *ha più fame* becomes “she is hungrier”. Impersonal constructions may acquire personal subjects, while finite subordinate clauses become infinitives or participles. Tenses likewise do not correspond mechanically: Italian compound perfects sometimes become English simple pasts, and futures after conjunctions such as “until” become presents.

The vocabulary ranges from close correspondence to interpretive substitution. *Forte* becomes “stubborn”, *studio* becomes “zeal”, and *virtute* becomes “manfulness”, each selecting a particular shade of meaning. Other choices materially affect the relation expressed: *per cui io mi volsi*, ‘because of which I turned’, becomes “from which I turned back”. More substantial departures occur when *Che non lasciò giammai persona viva* is rendered as “that no one ever left alive”, reversing the grammatical roles, or when *per me si vegna* becomes “I come”, replacing entry through the speaker’s agency with his own entry.

Omissions and additions are usually small, though occasionally explanatory. The locative *v’* disappears from “that I discerned”, and *che uscia di sua vista* is compressed into “of her aspect”; conversely, *a ciò*, ‘for this’, is expanded into “to guide thee”. Throughout, English also supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and normally omits the Italian definite article before possessives: *lo mio maestro* becomes “my master”. These routine adjustments, largely left unrecorded in the detailed comparison, accompany a translation that remains closely tied to the original wording while repeatedly reshaping its grammar and, at selected points, making an interpretation explicit.
