# Inferno 20

## Lines 1–13 — A New Punishment Revealed

> 1 Di nuova pena mi convien far versi,  
> 2 E dar materia al ventesimo canto  
> 3 Della prima canzon, ch’ è de’ sommersi.  

Of new punishment behooves me to make verses, and give matter for the twentieth canto of the first canzone, which concerns the sunken.

- *mi convien* ‘to me behooves’ → “behooves me”: the pronoun follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *ch’ è de’* ‘which is of the’ → “which concerns the”: the copula and preposition become the transitive verb “concerns”.

> 4 Io era già disposto tutto quanto  
> 5 A risguardar nello scoverto fondo,  
> 6 Che si bagnava d’ angoscioso pianto:  

I now was all prepared to look into the depth discovered to me, which was bathed with tears of anguish;

- *Io era già* ‘I was already’ → “I now was”: *già* is rendered as “now” rather than ‘already’, and the adverb precedes the verb.
- *disposto tutto quanto* ‘prepared all entire’ → “all prepared”: the emphatic *tutto quanto* is condensed to “all” and placed before “prepared”.
- *nello scoverto fondo* ‘into the uncovered bottom’ → “into the depth discovered to me”: *fondo* becomes “depth” rather than ‘bottom’; the modifier follows its noun, and “to me” is supplied. “discovered” retains the sense of ‘laid open’.
- *si bagnava* ‘bathed itself’ → “was bathed”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes an English passive.
- *d’ angoscioso pianto* ‘of anguished weeping’ → “with tears of anguish”: “with” expresses the relation introduced by *di*; singular *pianto* becomes plural “tears”, and the preceding adjective becomes the following noun phrase “of anguish”.

> 7 E vidi gente per lo vallon tondo  
> 8 Venir, tacendo e lagrimando, al passo  
> 9 Che fanno le letanie in questo mondo.  

and through the circular valley I saw a people coming silent and weeping, at the pace which the Litanies make in this world.

- *vidi gente* ‘I saw people’ → “I saw a people”: the collective *gente* receives an indefinite article, presenting it as a single body of people.
- *per lo vallon tondo* ‘through the valley round’ → “through the circular valley”: the phrase moves before “I saw”; *tondo* becomes “circular” and precedes its noun.
- *Venir* ‘to come’ → “coming”: the infinitive dependent on the verb of seeing becomes a present participle.
- *tacendo* ‘keeping silent’ → “silent”: the gerund becomes an adjective.
- *Che fanno le letanie* ‘which make the litanies’ → “which the Litanies make”: the subject *le letanie*, which follows the verb in Italian, precedes it in English.

> 10 Come il viso mi scese in lor più basso,  

When my sight descended lower on them,

- *Come* ‘as’ → “When”: the temporal conjunction is rendered with “When”.
- *il viso mi scese* ‘the sight descended for me’ → “my sight descended”: the dative pronoun *mi* becomes a possessive modifying “sight”, replacing the definite article.
- *in lor più basso* ‘in them lower’ → “lower on them”: *in* becomes “on”, and “lower” moves before the prepositional phrase.

> 11 Mirabilmente apparve esser travolto  
> 12 Ciascun dal mento al principio del casso,  

each seemed wondrously distorted, from the chin to the commencement of the chest,

- *Mirabilmente apparve esser travolto Ciascun* ‘wondrously appeared to be twisted each’ → “each seemed wondrously distorted”: the subject moves from after the predicate to the beginning; *apparve esser* is compressed into “seemed”, without an explicit infinitive ‘to be’; “wondrously” moves from the beginning to immediately before “distorted”, which renders *travolto*.

> 13 Che dalle reni era tornato il volto;  

so that the face was turned towards the loins;

- *Che* ‘that’ → “so that”: “so” makes the consequential force of the conjunction explicit.
- *dalle reni* ‘at the loins’ → “towards the loins”: the spatial phrase is expressed directionally and moved from before the verb to the end of the clause.
- *era tornato il volto* ‘was turned the face’ → “the face was turned”: the subject moves from after the predicate to before it.

## Lines 14–24 — Faces Turned Backward

> 14 E indietro venir gli convenia,  
> 15 Perchè il veder dinanzi era lor tolto.  

and they had to come backward, for to look before them was denied.

- *indietro venir* ‘backward to come’ → “come backward”: the adverb moves after the verb.
- *gli convenia* ‘to them it was necessary’ → “they had to”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal construction with “they” as subject.
- *Perchè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *il veder dinanzi era lor tolto* ‘the seeing ahead was taken from them’ → “to look before them was denied”: the substantival infinitive becomes an English infinitive; ‘taken from’ becomes “denied.” The Italian *lor* identifies those deprived of sight ahead; “them” instead completes “before.”

> 16 Forse per forza già di parlasìa  
> 17 Si travolse così alcun del tutto;  
> 18 Ma io nol vidi, nè credo che sia.  

Perhaps by force of palsy some have been thus quite distorted; but I have not seen, nor do believe it to be so.

- *Forse per forza già di parlasìa Si travolse così alcun del tutto* ‘Perhaps by force at some time of palsy someone became twisted thus entirely’ → “Perhaps by force of palsy some have been thus quite distorted”: *già* receives no separate rendering. Singular *alcun* becomes the plural construction “some have”; the subject moves before the verb. The pronominal simple past becomes a passive present perfect, and the expressions of manner and degree both precede “distorted.”
- *io nol vidi* ‘I did not see it’ → “I have not seen”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, and the object pronoun contained in *nol* is omitted.
- *nè credo che sia* ‘nor I believe that it is’ → “nor do believe it to be so”: “do” is added, and the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction. “so” explicitly supplies the predicate left understood in Italian.

> 19 Se Dio ti lasci, Lettor, prender frutto  
> 20 Di tua lezione, or penza per te stesso,  
> 21 Com’ io potea tener lo viso asciutto,  

Reader, so God grant thee to take profit of thy reading, now think for thyself how I could keep my visage dry,

- *Se Dio ti lasci, Lettor* ‘if God let thee, Reader’ → “Reader, so God grant thee”: the Italian conditional form, used as a wish, becomes an English wish introduced by “so”; ‘let’ becomes “grant.” The vocative moves from within the wish to the beginning of the sentence.
- *prender frutto Di tua lezione* ‘take fruit of thy reading’ → “to take profit of thy reading”: the metaphorical ‘fruit’ becomes the explicit “profit.”
- *lo viso* ‘the face’ → “my visage”: the definite article becomes a possessive identifying the face as the speaker’s.

> 22 Quando la nostra imagine da presso  
> 23 Vidi sì torta, che il pianto degli occhi  
> 24 Le natiche bagnava per lo fesso.  

when near at hand I saw our image so contorted, that the weeping of the eyes bathed the hinder parts at their division?

- *Quando la nostra imagine da presso Vidi* ‘when our image near at hand I saw’ → “when near at hand I saw our image”: the object moves from before the adverbial phrase and verb to after “I saw.”
- *Le natiche bagnava* ‘the buttocks it bathed’ → “bathed the hinder parts”: the verb moves before its object, and the direct anatomical term becomes the euphemistic “hinder parts.” The Italian imperfect is rendered by an English simple past, without explicitly marking its continuing aspect.
- *per lo fesso* ‘through the cleft’ → “at their division”: “at” replaces ‘through,’ the concrete ‘cleft’ becomes “division,” and the definite article becomes the possessive “their.”

## Lines 25–36 — Pity Rebuked and Amphiaraüs Revealed

> 25 Certo io piangea, poggiato ad un de’ rocchi  
> 26 Del duro scoglio, sì che la mia Scorta  
> 27 Mi disse: Ancor se’ tu degli altri sciocchi?  

Certainly I wept, leaning on one of the rocks of the hard cliff, so that my Escort said to me: “Art thou, too, like the other fools?

- *io piangea* ‘I was weeping’ → “I wept”: the Italian imperfect presents an ongoing action; the English uses the simple past.
- *poggiato ad* ‘rested against’ → “leaning on”: the past participle becomes a present participle, and “on” replaces ‘against’.
- *de’ rocchi* ‘of the blocks’ → “of the rocks”: the word for individual blocks or masses of stone is rendered more generally.
- *Mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *Ancor se’ tu* ‘also art thou’ → “Art thou, too”: the additive adverb moves after the subject.
- *degli altri sciocchi* ‘of the other fools’ → “like the other fools”: membership in a group becomes comparison with that group.

> 28 Qui vive la pietà quand’ è ben morta.  

Here pity lives when it is altogether dead.

- *Qui vive la pietà* ‘here lives pity’ → “Here pity lives”: the subject moves before the verb, and the article before the abstract noun is omitted.
- *ben morta* ‘well dead’ → “altogether dead”: the intensifying *ben* is expressed as completeness.

> 29 Chi è più scellerato di colui,  
> 30 Ch’ al giudicio divin passïon porta?  

Who more impious than he that sorrows at God’s judgment?

- *Chi è* ‘who is’ → “Who”: the copula is omitted.
- *più scellerato* ‘more wicked’ → “more impious”: the English gives the adjective a specifically religious force.
- *colui* ‘that one’ → “he”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *Ch’ al giudicio divin passïon porta* ‘who to the divine judgment brings compassion’ → “that sorrows at God’s judgment”: the noun-and-verb expression becomes the single verb “sorrows”, interpreting *passïon* as sympathetic grief. The judgment phrase moves after the verb and becomes the occasion of sorrow through “at”, rather than its destination through ‘to’. The adjective *divin* becomes the possessive “God’s”, placed before the noun.

> 31 Drizza la testa, drizza, e vedi a cui  
> 32 S’ aperse agli occhi de’ Teban la terra,  
> 33 Quando gridavan tutti: Dove rui,  
> 34 Anfiarao? perchè lasci la guerra?  

Raise up thy head, raise up, and see him for whom the earth opened herself before the eyes of the Thebans, when they all cried, ‘Whither rushest thou, Amphiaraüs? Why leavest thou the war?’

- *Drizza la testa, drizza* ‘raise the head, raise’ → “Raise up thy head, raise up”: the verb is expressed by the phrasal verb “Raise up”, and the definite article becomes the possessive “thy”.
- *vedi a cui* ‘see to whom’ → “see him for whom”: “him” is supplied as the direct object of “see”, and *a* is interpreted as ‘for’.
- *S’ aperse agli occhi de’ Teban la terra* ‘opened itself to the eyes of the Thebans the earth’ → “the earth opened herself before the eyes of the Thebans”: the postponed subject moves before the verb. The reflexive becomes the explicitly feminine “herself”, and ‘to the eyes’ becomes “before the eyes”.
- *gridavan tutti* ‘were shouting all’ → “they all cried”: the postponed subject *tutti* becomes the preverbal “they all”; the imperfect is rendered by the simple past.
- *Dove rui* ‘where fallest thou’ → “Whither rushest thou”: “Whither” makes the direction of motion explicit, while “rushest” renders falling headlong as rushing.

> 35 E non restò di ruinare a valle  
> 36 Fino a Minòs, che ciascheduno afferra.  

And he ceased not rushing headlong down to Minos, who lays hold on every sinner.

- *non restò di ruinare a valle* ‘did not cease from falling downward’ → “he ceased not rushing headlong down”: the negative follows the finite verb rather than preceding it, and the construction with *di* and an infinitive becomes a directly attached gerund. Falling downward is expressed as “rushing headlong down”.
- *Fino a Minòs* ‘as far as Minos’ → “to Minos”: the explicit emphasis on the endpoint supplied by *Fino* is not separately represented.
- *che ciascheduno afferra* ‘who each one seizes’ → “who lays hold on every sinner”: the object moves after the verb; “lays hold on” replaces the single verb ‘seizes’. The unrestricted pronoun *ciascheduno* becomes “every sinner”, supplying the noun “sinner”.

## Lines 37–45 — Backward Sight and Tiresias’s Transformation

> 37 Mira, ch’ ha fatto petto delle spalle:  

Mark how he has made a breast of his shoulders:

- *Mira* ‘Look’ → “Mark”: the command to look becomes a command to take notice.
- *ch’* ‘that’ → “how”: the conjunction introduces the manner or result of the transformation rather than simply its occurrence.
- *ha fatto petto* ‘has made breast’ → “has made a breast”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *delle spalle* ‘of the shoulders’ → “of his shoulders”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 38 Perchè volle veder troppo davante,  
> 39 Dirietro guarda, e fa ritroso calle.  

because he wished to see too far before him, he now looks behind and goes backward.

- *troppo* ‘too much’ → “too far”: the excess is expressed specifically as distance.
- *davante* ‘ahead’ → “before him”: the directional adverb becomes a prepositional phrase with an explicit personal reference.
- *Dirietro guarda* ‘behind he looks’ → “he now looks behind”: the directional adverb moves after the verb, and “now” is supplied.
- *fa ritroso calle* ‘makes a backward path’ → “goes backward”: the verb-and-object construction becomes a verb of motion modified by an adverb.

> 40 Vedi Tiresia, che mutò sembiante,  
> 41 Quando di maschio femmina divenne,  
> 42 Cangiandosi le membra tutte quante;  

“Behold Tiresias who changed his aspect, when of male he was made woman, all his limbs transforming:

- *sembiante* ‘appearance’ → “his aspect”: a possessive absent from the Italian is supplied.
- *di maschio femmina divenne* ‘from male female he became’ → “of male he was made woman”: “of” renders the starting condition expressed by *di*; ‘became’ becomes the passive “was made,” and the predicate ‘female’ moves after the verb.
- *Cangiandosi le membra tutte quante* ‘the limbs changing, all of them’ → “all his limbs transforming”: the subject follows the gerund in Italian but precedes the participle in English. The reflexive construction becomes intransitive, the personal reference is made explicit by “his,” and the emphatic *tutte quante* is compressed into “all”.

> 43 E prima poi ribatter le convenne  
> 44 Li duo serpenti avvolti con la verga,  
> 45 Che riavesse le maschili penne.  

and afterwards he had again to strike the two involved serpents with his rod, before he could resume his manly plumes.

- *prima … Che* ‘first … before’ → “before”: the anticipatory *prima* is not separately represented; the temporal relationship is expressed solely by the conjunction introducing the final clause.
- *ribatter le convenne* ‘to strike again was necessary for her’ → “he had again to strike”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal construction expressing necessity. The feminine indirect-object pronoun *le* becomes the masculine subject “he,” and the repetition expressed by the prefix of *ribatter* becomes the separate adverb “again”.
- *Li duo serpenti avvolti* ‘the two serpents entwined’ → “the two involved serpents”: the participle moves from after the noun to before it.
- *con la verga* ‘with the rod’ → “with his rod”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *riavesse* ‘he might have again’ → “he could resume”: ‘have again’ becomes “resume,” and the Italian subjunctive is rendered with “could,” explicitly expressing ability.
- *le maschili penne* ‘the male feathers’ → “his manly plumes”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the adjective denoting male attributes is rendered by “manly”.

## Lines 46–60 — Aruns and Manto’s Wanderings

> 46 Aronta è quei ch’ al ventre gli s’ atterga,  

“That is Aruns coming back before him,

- *Aronta è quei* ‘Aruns is that one’ → “That is Aruns”: the demonstrative becomes the subject, and the name becomes the complement.
- *ch’ al ventre gli s’ atterga* ‘who sets his back against his belly’ → “coming back before him”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase. The translation introduces movement with “coming,” replaces the explicit reference to the belly with “before him,” and does not retain the reflexive construction.

> 47 Che ne’ monti di Luni, dove ronca  
> 48 Lo Carrarese che di sotto alberga,  

who in the mountains of Luni, where hoes the Carrarese that dwells beneath,

- *ronca* ‘clears the ground’ → “hoes”: the translation names a particular method of working the ground rather than clearing it.
- *di sotto alberga* ‘beneath lodges’ → “dwells beneath”: the adverbial phrase moves after the verb, and lodging is expressed more generally as dwelling.

> 49 Ebbe tra bianchi marmi la spelonca  
> 50 Per sua dimora; onde a guardar le stelle  
> 51 E il mar non gli era la veduta tronca.  

amongst white marbles had the cave for his abode; from which he could observe the stars and the sea with unobstructed view.

- *Ebbe tra bianchi marmi la spelonca* ‘had amongst white marbles the cave’ → “amongst white marbles had the cave”: the phrase expressing location moves before the verb.
- *onde* ‘whence’ → “from which”: the relative adverb becomes a preposition and relative pronoun.
- *a guardar le stelle E il mar non gli era la veduta tronca* ‘for looking at the stars and the sea, the view was not cut off for him’ → “he could observe the stars and the sea with unobstructed view”: the infinitive becomes the main verb with the supplied modal “could,” and the person expressed by *gli* becomes its subject. The clause about the view becomes a phrase introduced by “with”; the separate negative becomes the negative prefix in “unobstructed.”

> 52 E quella che ricopre le mammelle,  
> 53 Che tu non vedi, con le trecce sciolte,  
> 54 E ha di là ogni pilosa pelle,  

“And she that covers her bosom, which thou seest not, with her flowing tresses, and has all her hair on the other side,

- *quella* ‘that one’ → “she”: a demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *ricopre le mammelle* ‘covers the breasts’ → “covers her bosom”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the plural noun becomes a singular collective expression.
- *Che tu non vedi* ‘which thou not seest’ → “which thou seest not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *con le trecce sciolte* ‘with the tresses loosened’ → “with her flowing tresses”: the definite article becomes a possessive; “flowing” describes the tresses’ appearance rather than their being unbound, and precedes the noun.
- *ha di là ogni pilosa pelle* ‘has on the other side every hairy skin’ → “has all her hair on the other side”: the phrase expressing location moves to the end. Hair-bearing skin is rendered simply as “hair,” *ogni* becomes “all” with a mass noun, and “her” is supplied.

> 55 Manto fu, che cercò per terre molte,  
> 56 Poscia si pose là dove nacqu’ io:  

was Manto, who searched through many lands, then settled there where I was born:

- *Manto fu* ‘Manto was’ → “was Manto”: the predicate name moves after the verb.
- *cercò* ‘travelled about’ → “searched”: the translation takes the verb as searching rather than travelling through or ranging over places.
- *terre molte* ‘lands many’ → “many lands”: the modifier moves before the noun.
- *si pose* ‘placed herself’ → “settled”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *nacqu’ io* ‘was born I’ → “I was born”: the explicitly stated subject moves before the verb.

> 57 Onde un poco mi piace che m’ ascolte.  

whence it pleases me a little to have thee listen to me.

- *un poco mi piace* ‘a little to me is pleasing’ → “it pleases me a little”: the degree phrase moves after the verb and its object; the impersonal construction is expressed with anticipatory “it.”
- *che m’ ascolte* ‘that thou listen to me’ → “to have thee listen to me”: the finite subordinate clause, with its subjunctive verb, becomes an infinitival construction with the added “have.” Its subject becomes “thee,” and the object pronoun before the Italian verb becomes “to me” after the English verb.

> 58 Poscia che il padre suo di vita uscio,  
> 59 E venne serva la città di Baco,  
> 60 Questa gran tempo per lo mondo gìo.  

After her father went out of life, and the city of Bacchus came to be enslaved, she for a long time roamed the world.

- *il padre suo* ‘the father her’ → “her father”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *di vita uscio* ‘out of life went’ → “went out of life”: the phrase expressing departure moves after the verb.
- *E venne serva la città di Baco* ‘and became a slave the city of Bacchus’ → “and the city of Bacchus came to be enslaved”: the subject moves before the predicate. Becoming a slave is expressed through “came to be” followed by the passive “enslaved.”
- *Questa* ‘this one’ → “she”: a demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *gran tempo per lo mondo gìo* ‘a long time through the world went’ → “for a long time roamed the world”: “for” explicitly marks duration. The verb moves before the expression of place; “went” becomes the more specific “roamed,” and the prepositional phrase becomes its direct object.

## Lines 61–75 — Lake Benacus and Its Outflow

> 61 Suso in Italia bella giace un laco  
> 62 Appiè dell’ Alpe, che serra Lamagna  
> 63 Sovra Tiralli, ed ha nome Benaco.  

Up in beautiful Italy there lies a lake, at the foot of the Alps which shut in Germany above the Tyrol, and it is called Benacus.

- *in Italia bella* ‘in Italy beautiful’ → “in beautiful Italy”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *giace un laco* ‘lies a lake’ → “there lies a lake”: existential “there” is supplied.
- *dell’Alpe, che serra Lamagna* ‘of the Alp, which shuts in Germany’ → “of the Alps which shut in Germany”: the collective singular becomes plural, with corresponding plural agreement in the verb.
- *ed ha nome Benaco* ‘and has the name Benacus’ → “and it is called Benacus”: the construction with ‘has the name’ becomes a passive construction with “is called”.

> 64 Per mille fonti, credo, e più si bagna,  
> 65 Tra Garda e Val Camonica, Pennino  
> 66 Dell’ acqua che nel detto lago stagna.  

Through a thousand fountains, I believe, and more, the Pennine, between Garda and Val Camonica, is irrigated by the water which stagnates in that lake.

- *si bagna* ‘bathes itself’ → “is irrigated”: the reflexive or middle construction is rendered as a passive, and bathing becomes irrigation. The verb moves after the subject and the intervening phrase of location.
- *Pennino* ‘Pennine’ → “the Pennine”: the definite article is supplied, and the subject moves before the phrase locating it between Garda and Val Camonica.
- *Dell’acqua* ‘of the water’ → “by the water”: the Italian preposition introducing the water with which the bathing occurs becomes “by” in the passive construction.
- *che nel detto lago stagna* ‘which in the said lake stagnates’ → “which stagnates in that lake”: the verb moves before the phrase of location, and ‘the said lake’ becomes “that lake”.

> 67 Luogo è nel mezzo là, dove il Trentino  
> 68 Pastore, e quel di Brescia, e il Veronese  
> 69 Segnar porìa, se fesse quel cammino.  

At the middle there is a place where the Trentine pastor, and he of Brescia, and the Veronese might bless, if they went that way.

- *Luogo è nel mezzo là* ‘a place is in the middle there’ → “At the middle there is a place”: the phrase of location moves to the beginning, ‘in’ becomes “At”, and the locative ‘there’ is recast as existential “there” before the verb.
- *e quel di Brescia* ‘and that one of Brescia’ → “and he of Brescia”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *Segnar porìa* ‘make the sign he could’ → “might bless”: *segnar*, making the sign of the cross, is rendered by the action of blessing; the modal moves before the infinitive, and ‘could’ becomes “might”.
- *se fesse quel cammino* ‘if he made that journey’ → “if they went that way”: making a journey becomes going a particular way. The singular imperfect subjunctive is rendered with plural “they” and the English past form “went” in the hypothetical clause.

> 70 Siede Peschiera, bello e forte arnese  
> 71 Da fronteggiar Bresciani e Bergamaschi,  
> 72 Ove la riva intorno più discese.  

Peschiera, a fortress beautiful and strong to front the Brescians and the Bergamese, sits where the shore around is lowest.

- *Siede Peschiera* ‘sits Peschiera’ → “Peschiera” … “sits”: the subject moves before the verb, which is postponed until after the entire appositional description.
- *bello e forte arnese* ‘beautiful and strong defensive work’ → “a fortress beautiful and strong”: *arnese* is rendered as “fortress”; the noun moves before its adjectives and receives an indefinite article.
- *Da fronteggiar* ‘for confronting’ → “to front”: the construction expressing purpose or suitability becomes an English to-infinitive.
- *Bresciani e Bergamaschi* ‘Brescians and Bergamese’ → “the Brescians and the Bergamese”: definite articles are supplied before both names.
- *più discese* ‘descended more’ → “is lowest”: a past-tense verb describing descent becomes a present-tense statement of position, and the comparative ‘more’ is expressed through the superlative “lowest”.

> 73 Ivi convien che tutto quanto caschi  
> 74 Ciò che in grembo a Benaco star non può,  
> 75 E fassi fiume giù pe’ verdi paschi.  

There all that in the bosom of Benacus cannot stay, has to descend and make itself a river, down through the green pastures.

- *convien che … caschi* ‘it is necessary that … fall’ → “has to descend”: the impersonal construction with a subjunctive becomes a personal construction with “has to” and an infinitive. Falling is rendered as descending, and the necessity construction moves after the subject and its relative clause.
- *tutto quanto … Ciò che* ‘all of it … that which’ → “all that”: the emphatic expression of totality and the following demonstrative-relative construction are compressed.
- *in grembo a Benaco* ‘in the lap to Benacus’ → “in the bosom of Benacus”: ‘lap’ becomes “bosom”, and the relationship expressed by *a* is rendered with possessive “of”.
- *star non può* ‘stay it cannot’ → “cannot stay”: the infinitive moves after the negated modal.
- *E fassi fiume* ‘and makes itself river’ → “and make itself a river”: the independent present-tense verb becomes a second infinitive governed by “has to”, and an indefinite article is supplied before “river”.

## Lines 76–87 — The Mincio and the Virgin’s Retreat

> 76 Tosto che l’ acqua a correr mette co’,  
> 77 Non più Benaco, ma Mincio si chiama  
> 78 Fino a Governo, dove cade in Po.  

Soon as the water sets head to run, it is no longer named Benacus, but Mincio,—to Governo where it falls into the Po.

- *a correr mette co’* ‘to run puts head’ → “sets head to run”: the infinitive moves after the verb and its object, and ‘puts’ becomes “sets”.
- *Non più Benaco, ma Mincio si chiama* ‘no longer Benacus, but Mincio it is called’ → “it is no longer named Benacus, but Mincio”: the naming verb precedes the names rather than following them, and ‘called’ becomes “named”.
- *Fino a Governo* ‘as far as Governo’ → “to Governo”: the explicit expression of extent is reduced to “to”.
- *in Po* ‘into Po’ → “into the Po”: English supplies the article before the river’s name.

> 79 Non molto ha corso, che trova una lama,  
> 80 Nella qual si distende e la impaluda,  
> 81 E suol di state talora esser grama.  

Not far has it flowed, when it finds a level, on which it spreads and makes a marsh thereof, and is wont in summer to be at times unwholesome.

- *Non molto ha corso* ‘not much has it run’ → “Not far has it flowed”: the indefinite quantity becomes an explicit measure of distance, and ‘run’ becomes “flowed”.
- *che* ‘that’ → “when”: the Italian connective after the preceding negative expression is rendered as an English temporal conjunction.
- *una lama* ‘a low-lying hollow’ → “a level”: the translation takes the word as a stretch of level ground rather than explicitly describing a depression.
- *Nella qual* ‘in which’ → “on which”: the preposition changes from containment to surface.
- *si distende* ‘spreads itself’ → “it spreads”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an English intransitive verb.
- *e la impaluda* ‘and makes it marshy’ → “and makes a marsh thereof”: the Italian verb becomes a phrase with the noun “marsh”, and its direct-object pronoun becomes “thereof”.
- *talora esser grama* ‘sometimes to be wretched’ → “to be at times unwholesome”: the adverb moves after the infinitive, and the broad adjective ‘wretched’ receives the more specific interpretation “unwholesome”.

> 82 Quindi passando la vergine cruda  
> 83 Vide terra nel mezzo del pantano,  
> 84 Senza cultura, e d’ abitanti nuda.  

The cruel virgin, passing that way, saw land amidst the fen, uncultivated and naked of inhabitants.

- *Quindi passando* ‘passing through there’ → “passing that way”: the locative adverb becomes an expression of route, placed after the participle.
- *la vergine cruda* ‘the virgin cruel’ → “The cruel virgin”: the adjective precedes its noun, and the subject moves before the participial phrase.
- *nel mezzo del pantano* ‘in the middle of the fen’ → “amidst the fen”: the explicit noun ‘middle’ is absorbed into the preposition “amidst”.
- *Senza cultura* ‘without cultivation’ → “uncultivated”: a prepositional phrase becomes an adjective.
- *e d’ abitanti nuda* ‘and of inhabitants naked’ → “and naked of inhabitants”: the adjective moves before its complement.

> 85 Lì, per fuggire ogni consorzio umano,  
> 86 Ristette co’ suoi servi a far sue arti,  
> 87 E visse, e vi lasciò suo corpo vano.  

There, to shun all human intercourse, she halted with her ministers to do her arts; and there she lived and left her body vacant.

- *fuggire* ‘to flee’ → “to shun”: the verb is rendered as deliberate avoidance rather than flight.
- *ogni consorzio umano* ‘every association human’ → “all human intercourse”: “all” replaces the distributive ‘every’, ‘association’ becomes “intercourse”, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *servi* ‘servants’ → “ministers”: the translation uses a term suggesting agents or assistants rather than simply servants.
- *E visse, e vi lasciò* ‘and she lived, and there she left’ → “and there she lived and left”: the locative attached to the second Italian verb moves before the first English verb and applies to both coordinated actions.

## Lines 88–99 — Mantua’s Founding and True Origin

> 88 Gli uomini poi, che intorno erano sparti,  
> 89 S’ accolsero a quel luogo, ch’ era forte  
> 90 Per lo pantan ch’ avea da tutte parti.  

Afterwards the men, that were scattered round, gathered together on that spot; for it was strong by reason of the marsh it had on every side.

- *Gli uomini poi* ‘the men afterwards’ → “Afterwards the men”: the temporal adverb moves before the subject.
- *che intorno erano sparti* ‘that round were scattered’ → “that were scattered round”: the adverb moves after the verb and participle.
- *S’ accolsero* ‘gathered themselves’ → “gathered together”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb with “together”.
- *a quel luogo* ‘at that place’ → “on that spot”: the preposition changes, and “spot” replaces ‘place’.
- *ch’ era forte* ‘which was strong’ → “for it was strong”: the relative clause becomes a coordinating explanation, making its causal force explicit.
- *Per lo pantan* ‘because of the marsh’ → “by reason of the marsh”: the causal preposition is expanded into a prepositional phrase.
- *ch’ avea* ‘which it had’ → “it had”: the object relative pronoun is omitted, as English permits.
- *da tutte parti* ‘on all sides’ → “on every side”: the plural expression becomes distributive and singular.

> 91 Fer la città sovra quell’ ossa morte;  

They built the city over those dead bones;

- *Fer* ‘made’ → “They built”: the general verb is rendered by the more specific verb of construction.
- *quell’ ossa morte* ‘those bones dead’ → “those dead bones”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 92 E per colei, che il luogo prima elesse,  
> 93 Mantova l’ appellar senz’ altra sorte.  

and for her who first chose the place, they called it Mantua without other augury.

- *che il luogo prima elesse* ‘who the place first chose’ → “who first chose the place”: the object moves after the verb.
- *Mantova l’ appellar* ‘Mantua it they called’ → “they called it Mantua”: the fronted name moves to the end, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *senz’ altra sorte* ‘without other lot’ → “without other augury”: “augury” interprets *sorte* as divination by lots, rendering the broader practice rather than the particular means.

> 94 Già fur le genti sue dentro più spesse,  
> 95 Prima che la mattía di Casalodi,  
> 96 Da Pinamonte inganno ricevesse.  

Once the inhabitants were denser in it, ere the folly of Casalodi was cheated by Pinamonte.

- *Già* ‘already’ → “Once”: the adverb is understood in its retrospective sense, referring to a former time.
- *fur le genti sue* ‘were its people’ → “the inhabitants were”: the subject moves before the verb, ‘people’ becomes the more specific “inhabitants”, and the possessive is omitted.
- *dentro più spesse* ‘within more dense’ → “denser in it”: the comparative becomes a single inflected adjective; the adverb becomes a prepositional phrase with an explicit pronoun and moves after the adjective.
- *Da Pinamonte inganno ricevesse* ‘from Pinamonte deceit might receive’ → “was cheated by Pinamonte”: actively receiving deceit becomes passively being cheated; the source becomes the passive agent and moves after the verb. The imperfect subjunctive required by the Italian temporal construction becomes an English past indicative.

> 97 Però t’ assenno, che se tu mai odi  
> 98 Originar la mia terra altrimenti,  
> 99 La verità nulla menzogna frodi.  

Therefore I charge thee, if thou ever hearest other origin given to my city, let no falsehood defraud the truth.”

- *t’ assenno, che* ‘thee I instruct, that’ → “I charge thee,”: instruction becomes a more forceful charge, the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the conjunction introducing the instruction is omitted.
- *Originar la mia terra altrimenti* ‘give origin to my land otherwise’ → “other origin given to my city”: the infinitive becomes a noun accompanied by a passive participle; the Italian verb’s direct object becomes the recipient introduced by “to”. “City” interprets *terra* as a town rather than land, while the adverb ‘otherwise’ becomes the adjective “other” modifying “origin”.
- *La verità nulla menzogna frodi* ‘the truth let no falsehood defraud’ → “let no falsehood defraud the truth”: the fronted object moves after the verb, and the independent subjunctive injunction is expressed with “let”.

## Lines 100–111 — Dante’s Trust and an Augur

> 100 Ed io: Maestro, i tuoi ragionamenti  
> 101 Mi son sì certi, e prendon sì mia fede,  
> 102 Che gli altri mi sarien carboni spenti.  

And I: “Master, thy words are to me so certain, and so take hold of my belief, that all others would be to me extinguished coals.

- *ragionamenti* ‘reasonings’ → “words”: the reference to reasoned discourse becomes the more general “words”.
- *Mi son sì certi* ‘to me are so certain’ → “are to me so certain”: the verb precedes rather than follows “to me”.
- *e prendon sì mia fede* ‘and seize so my faith’ → “and so take hold of my belief”: the simple verb becomes a verbal phrase, “so” moves before it, and *fede* is rendered as “belief” rather than ‘faith’.
- *gli altri* ‘the others’ → “all others”: “all” is supplied in place of the definite article.
- *mi sarien* ‘to me would be’ → “would be to me”: “to me” moves after the verb.
- *carboni spenti* ‘coals extinguished’ → “extinguished coals”: the participial adjective moves before the noun.

> 103 Ma dimmi della gente che procede,  
> 104 Se tu ne vedi alcun degno di nota;  
> 105 Chè solo a ciò la mia mente rifiede.  

But tell me of the people that are passing, if thou seest any of them worthy of note; for to that alone my mind recurs.”

- *gente che procede* ‘people that proceeds’ → “people that are passing”: the singular collective noun takes plural agreement in English, and the simple present becomes a progressive construction; ‘proceeds’ is rendered as “are passing”.
- *ne vedi alcun* ‘of them seest any’ → “seest any of them”: the equivalent of *ne* moves from before the verb to after “any”.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *solo a ciò* ‘only to that’ → “to that alone”: the restrictive expression follows rather than precedes “to that”.
- *rifiede* ‘strikes again’ → “recurs”: the figurative verb is rendered as a verb of returning.

> 106 Allor mi disse: Quel, che dalla gota  
> 107 Porge la barba in su le spalle brune,  

Then he said to me: “That one, who from the cheek stretches forth his beard upon his dusky shoulders,

- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “he said to me”: the indirect-object pronoun moves after the verb, and the subject implicit in the Italian verb is expressed.
- *Quel* ‘That’ → “That one”: “one” is supplied to support the demonstrative used as a pronoun.
- *Porge la barba* ‘extends the beard’ → “stretches forth his beard”: the verb becomes a verbal phrase, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *le spalle brune* ‘the shoulders brown’ → “his dusky shoulders”: the definite article becomes a possessive, the adjective moves before the noun, and ‘brown’ is rendered as “dusky”.

> 108 Fu, quando Grecia fu di maschi vota  
> 109 Sì ch’ appena rimaser per le cune,  
> 110 Augure, e diede il punto con Calcanta  
> 111 In Aulide a tagliar la prima fune.  

was an augur, when Greece was so empty of males, that hardly they remained even in the cradles; and in Aulis he, with Calchas, gave the time for cutting the first cable.

- *Fu … Augure* ‘was … augur’ → “was an augur”: the predicate noun moves ahead of the intervening temporal clause, and English supplies an indefinite article.
- *di maschi vota Sì ch’* ‘of males empty, so that’ → “so empty of males, that”: the complement follows rather than precedes “empty”, and *Sì* moves before the adjective to form “so empty … that”.
- *per le cune* ‘throughout the cradles’ → “even in the cradles”: the distributive preposition becomes “in”, and “even” is supplied.
- *diede il punto con Calcanta In Aulide* ‘gave the moment with Calchas in Aulis’ → “in Aulis he, with Calchas, gave the time”: both prepositional phrases move before the verb, with the place preceding the companion; *punto* is rendered as the broader “time”.
- *a tagliar* ‘to cut’ → “for cutting”: the infinitive becomes a gerund governed by “for”.
- *fune* ‘rope’ → “cable”: the translation chooses a specifically nautical term.

## Lines 112–117 — Eurypylus and Michael Scot

> 112 Euripilo ebbe nome, e così il canta  
> 113 L’ alta mia Tragedía in alcun loco;  

Eurypylus his name; and my high Tragedy thus sings him in some place:

- *Euripilo ebbe nome* ‘Eurypylus he had name’ → “Eurypylus his name”: the past-tense naming construction becomes a verbless phrase, with “his” supplied.
- *così il canta L’ alta mia Tragedía* ‘thus him sings the high my Tragedy’ → “my high Tragedy thus sings him”: the subject moves before the verb, the object pronoun moves after it, and the possessive precedes the adjective.

> 114 Ben lo sai tu che la sai tutta quanta.  

well knowest it thou, who knowest the whole.

- *lo sai tu* ‘it knowest thou’ → “knowest it thou”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *la sai tutta quanta* ‘it knowest all entire’ → “knowest the whole”: the object pronoun and the emphatic expression describing its entirety become the single noun phrase “the whole”.

> 115 Quell’ altro che ne’ fianchi è così poco,  
> 116 Michele Scotto fu, che veramente  
> 117 Delle magiche frode seppe il giuoco.  

“That other who is so small about the flanks was Michael Scot; and of a truth he knew the play of magic frauds.

- *ne’ fianchi è così poco* ‘in the flanks is so little’ → “is so small about the flanks”: the phrase specifying where he is small moves after the description; “about” replaces ‘in’, and “small” renders ‘little’.
- *Michele Scotto fu* ‘Michael Scot was’ → “was Michael Scot”: the name moves after the verb.
- *che* ‘who’ → “and” and “he”: the relative clause becomes a coordinated main clause.
- *veramente* ‘truly’ → “of a truth”: the adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *Delle magiche frode seppe il giuoco* ‘of the magic frauds knew the play’ → “knew the play of magic frauds”: the dependent phrase moves after “play”, and the definite article before ‘magic frauds’ is omitted.

## Lines 118–130 — Diviners and the Setting Moon

> 118 Vedi Guido Bonatti, vedi Asdente,  
> 119 Ch’ avere inteso al cuoio ed allo spago  
> 120 Ora vorrebbe, ma tardi si pente.  

“See Guido Bonatti; see Asdente, who now would wish he had attended to his leather and his cord, but too late repents.

- *Ch’ avere inteso al cuoio ed allo spago Ora vorrebbe* ‘who to have attended to the leather and to the cord now would wish’ → “who now would wish he had attended to his leather and his cord”: the main verb and its adverb move before the complement that precedes them in Italian; the perfect infinitive becomes a finite past-perfect clause; the definite articles become possessives, and the second ‘to’ is omitted.
- *tardi* ‘late’ → “too late”: “too” makes explicit that the repentance comes beyond the useful time.

> 121 Vedi le triste che lasciaron l’ ago,  
> 122 La spola e il fuso, e fecersi indovine;  

See the wretched women who left the needle, the shuttle, and the spindle, and made themselves divineresses.

- *le triste* ‘the unhappy ones’ → “the wretched women”: the substantival feminine adjective becomes an adjective modifying the supplied noun “women”; ‘unhappy’ is rendered as “wretched”.

> 123 Fecer malìe con erbe e con imago.  

They wrought witchcraft with herbs and images.

- *Fecer malìe* ‘they made spells’ → “They wrought witchcraft”: ‘made’ becomes “wrought”, and the plural count noun becomes the singular mass noun “witchcraft”.
- *con erbe e con imago* ‘with herbs and with an image’ → “with herbs and images”: the repeated preposition is omitted, and singular *imago* becomes plural “images”.

> 124 Ma vienne omai; chè già tiene il confine  
> 125 D’ amenduo gli emisperi, e tocca l’ onda  
> 126 Sotto Sibilia, Caino e le spine.  

“But now come! for Cain and the thorns already holds the confine of both hemispheres, and under Seville touches the wave;

- *vienne omai* ‘come away now’ → “now come”: the directional particle attached to the imperative is not separately rendered, and “now” moves before the verb.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes coordinating “for”.
- *già tiene il confine D’ amenduo gli emisperi, e tocca l’ onda Sotto Sibilia, Caino e le spine* ‘already holds the confine of both the hemispheres, and touches the wave under Seville, Cain and the thorns’ → “Cain and the thorns already holds the confine of both hemispheres, and under Seville touches the wave”: the subject moves from after both verb clauses to before them; the definite article before ‘hemispheres’ is omitted; the place phrase moves before the verb and its object.

> 127 E già iernotte fu la Luna tonda:  
> 128 Ben ten dee ricordar, chè non ti nocque  
> 129 Alcuna volta per la selva fonda.  

and already yesternight the Moon was round: well must thou remember; for she did not hurt thee any time in the deep wood.”

- *fu la Luna tonda* ‘was the Moon round’ → “the Moon was round”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *Ben ten dee ricordar* ‘well must there be remembrance to thee of it’ → “well must thou remember”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal one, with “thou” as subject rather than the person to whom recollection comes; *ne*, ‘of it’, is omitted.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes coordinating “for”.
- *non ti nocque* ‘not thee harmed’ → “she did not hurt thee”: English supplies the auxiliary for negation and places the object after the verb; the understood subject is expressed as “she”.
- *selva fonda* ‘wood deep’ → “deep wood”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 130 Sì mi parlava, ed andavamo introcque.  

Thus he spake to me, and we went on meanwhile.

- *Sì mi parlava* ‘thus to me he was speaking’ → “Thus he spake to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb, and the imperfect is rendered by the English simple past.
- *ed andavamo introcque* ‘and we were going meanwhile’ → “and we went on meanwhile”: the imperfect becomes the simple past, and “on” is supplied to express continued movement.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Syntax

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s wording and sequence of thought, but repeatedly rearranges its syntax. Subjects placed after verbs move before them, objects follow rather than precede their verbs, and descriptive words usually move before their nouns: *carboni spenti* becomes “extinguished coals”. Longer phrases also change position, as *per lo vallon tondo* moves ahead of “I saw”. Yet the English sometimes preserves marked inversions, as in “where hoes the Carrarese” and “well knowest it thou”, retaining something of the Italian’s unfamiliar order.

Changes of construction often make the English more direct. Impersonal expressions of necessity become personal ones: *gli convenia* becomes “they had to”, and *convien che … caschi* becomes “has to descend”. Pronominal constructions receive several treatments, from the passive “was bathed” for *si bagnava* to the intransitive “settled” for *si pose*. Elsewhere, *Venir* becomes “coming”, *tacendo* becomes “silent”, and *passïon porta* becomes “sorrows”. Italian imperfects frequently appear as English simple pasts, leaving their continuing aspect less explicit; some simple pasts instead become present perfects.

The vocabulary ranges from close correspondence to interpretive substitution. *Prender frutto* becomes “to take profit”, making the metaphor’s meaning explicit; *scellerato* becomes the more specifically religious “impious”; and *le natiche* becomes the euphemistic “the hinder parts”. Some choices specify an action or setting, as “hoes” for *ronca* and “cable” for *fune*. Others remain strikingly close, preserving such expressions as “manly plumes”, “extinguished coals”, and “the play of magic frauds”, along with much of the repetition and direct address.

Small additions and omissions further adjust the balance. Carlyle supplies “to me” after “discovered”, adds “now” to *Dirietro guarda*, and expands *ciascheduno* to “every sinner”. Conversely, *già* sometimes receives no separate equivalent, *tutto quanto* can be compressed into “all”, and *Fino a* is reduced to “to”. Definite articles with body parts often become possessives, as *la testa* becomes “thy head”. More generally, beyond the individual changes recorded in the comparison, English regularly supplies subject pronouns already conveyed by Italian verb endings and omits the Italian articles before possessives. These routine adjustments, together with the larger syntactic recastings, produce readable English without consistently smoothing away the original’s distinctive phrasing.
