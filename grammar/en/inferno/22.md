# Inferno 22

## Lines 1–9 — Military Movements and Signals

> 1 Io vidi già cavalier muover campo,  
> 2 E cominciare stormo, e far lor mostra,  
> 3 E talvolta partir per loro scampo:  

I have ere now seen horsemen moving camp, and commencing the assault, and holding their muster, and at times retiring to escape:

- *Io vidi già* ‘I saw already’ → “I have ere now seen”: the simple past becomes the present perfect; *già* becomes “ere now” and moves before the participle.
- *muover campo* ‘move camp’ → “moving camp”: the Italian infinitive after a verb of seeing becomes an English participle.
- *cominciare stormo* ‘begin assault’ → “commencing the assault”: the infinitive becomes a participle, and the definite article is supplied.
- *far lor mostra* ‘make their display’ → “holding their muster”: the infinitive becomes a participle, and the phrase is rendered by an English military expression, with “holding” for *far* and “muster” for *mostra*.
- *partir per loro scampo* ‘depart for their escape’ → “retiring to escape”: *partir* becomes the participle “retiring,” with a specifically military sense; the prepositional phrase expressing purpose becomes an infinitive, and *loro* is not separately rendered.

> 4 Corridor vidi per la terra vostra,  
> 5 O Aretini; e vidi gir gualdane,  
> 6 Ferir torneamenti, e correr giostra,  

coursers have I seen upon your land, O Aretines! and seen the march of foragers, the shock of tournaments and race of jousts,

- *Corridor* ‘mounted raiders’ → “coursers”: Carlyle uses “coursers” for the riders engaged in raiding, rather than explicitly naming their military function.
- *vidi* ‘I saw’ → “have I seen”: the simple past becomes the present perfect, with the auxiliary placed before the subject after the fronted object.
- *per la terra vostra* ‘through the land of yours’ → “upon your land”: the preposition of movement through an area becomes “upon,” and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *e vidi* ‘and I saw’ → “and seen”: the repeated finite verb becomes a participle sharing the earlier subject and auxiliary.
- *gir gualdane* ‘raiding bands go’ → “the march of foragers”: the infinitive and its following subject become a noun phrase; the movement is expressed by “march,” and the raiding bands become “foragers” dependent on that noun through “of.”
- *Ferir torneamenti* ‘strike tournaments’ → “the shock of tournaments”: the infinitive becomes the noun “shock,” with an article supplied, and its object becomes an “of” phrase.
- *e correr giostra* ‘and run joust’ → “and race of jousts”: the infinitive becomes the noun “race,” its object becomes an “of” phrase, and singular *giostra* becomes plural “jousts.”

> 7 Quando con trombe, e quando con campane,  
> 8 Con tamburi e con cenni di castella,  
> 9 E con cose nostrali, e con istrane;  

now with trumpets, and now with bells, with drums and castle-signals, and with native things and foreign;

- *Quando con trombe, e quando con campane* ‘when with trumpets, and when with bells’ → “now with trumpets, and now with bells”: the repeated *quando* is rendered by the English correlative “now … now,” expressing alternation.
- *cenni di castella* ‘signals of castles’ → “castle-signals”: the ‘of’ construction becomes a compound noun, with plural *castella* represented by the singular attributive “castle.”
- *cose nostrali* ‘things native to us’ → “native things”: the adjective moves before the noun, and its explicit reference to the speaker’s own country is left implicit.
- *e con istrane* ‘and with foreign’ → “and foreign”: the repeated preposition *con* is omitted.

## Lines 10–21 — An Uncouth Signal and the Pitch

> 10 Nè già con sì diversa cennamella  
> 11 Cavalier vidi mover, nè pedoni,  
> 12 Nè nave a segno di terra o di stella.  

but never yet to so uncouth a cornet saw I cavaliers nor footmen move, nor ship by mark of land or star.

- *Nè già* ‘nor indeed’ → “but never yet”: the negative conjunction becomes adversative, and the emphatic *già* receives a temporal rendering.
- *con sì diversa cennamella* ‘with so strange a shawm’ → “to so uncouth a cornet”: “to” replaces ‘with’ for movement accompanied by a musical signal; “uncouth” emphasizes strangeness as roughness, and “cornet” names a different wind instrument.
- *Cavalier vidi mover, nè pedoni* ‘cavaliers I saw move, nor footmen’ → “saw I cavaliers nor footmen move”: the object moves after the verb and subject, while “move” follows both coordinated objects rather than the first alone.
- *a segno* ‘at a mark’ → “by mark”: the preposition explicitly presents the mark as a means of guidance.
- *di terra o di stella* ‘of land or of star’ → “of land or star”: the second *di* is not repeated.

> 13 Noi andavam con li dieci Dimoni:  

We went with the ten Demons:

- *Noi andavam* ‘we were going’ → “We went”: the Italian imperfect becomes a simple past, without explicitly marking the action as ongoing.

> 14 Ahi fiera compagnia! ma nella chiesa  
> 15 Co’ santi, ed in taverna co’ ghiottoni.  

Ah, hideous company! but, ‘In church with saints, and with guzzlers in the tavern.’

- *fiera* ‘fierce’ → “hideous”: an adjective suggesting ferocity becomes one expressing horror or repulsiveness.
- *nella chiesa Co’ santi* ‘in the church with the saints’ → “In church with saints”: both definite articles are omitted.
- *ed in taverna co’ ghiottoni* ‘and in tavern with the gluttons’ → “and with guzzlers in the tavern”: the companions precede the location; an article is supplied before “tavern” and omitted before “guzzlers”; “guzzlers” gives the broader *ghiottoni* a specifically drinking-related sense.

> 16 Pure alla pegola era la mia intesa,  
> 17 Per veder della bolgia ogni contegno,  
> 18 E della gente ch’ entro v’ era incesa.  

Yet my intent was on the pitch, to see each habit of the chasm and of the people that were burning in it.

- *alla pegola era la mia intesa* ‘toward the pitch was my attention’ → “my intent was on the pitch”: the subject moves before the verb and the prepositional phrase after it; *intesa*, here attention or concentration, becomes “intent,” and its direction is expressed with “on.”
- *della bolgia ogni contegno, E della gente* ‘of the pit every aspect, and of the people’ → “each habit of the chasm and of the people”: the first dependent phrase moves after the noun it qualifies; “each” renders *ogni* distributively, “chasm” replaces ‘pit,’ and “habit” renders *contegno* in the sense of appearance or condition.
- *ch’ entro v’ era incesa* ‘that within there was set afire’ → “that were burning in it”: the past-participial description becomes an active progressive; the singular verb agreeing with collective *gente* becomes plural with “people”; and the overlapping locative expressions *entro* and *v’* become the single phrase “in it.”

> 19 Come i delfini, quando fanno segno  
> 20 Ai marinar con l’ arco della schiena,  
> 21 Che s’ argomentin di campar lor legno;  

As dolphins, when with the arch of the back they make sign to mariners that they may prepare to save their ship;

- *i delfini* ‘the dolphins’ → “dolphins”: the definite article is omitted for the generic plural.
- *quando fanno segno Ai marinar con l’ arco della schiena* ‘when they make sign to the mariners with the arch of the back’ → “when with the arch of the back they make sign to mariners”: the instrumental phrase moves before the subject and verb, and the article contained in *Ai* is omitted.
- *Che s’ argomentin* ‘so that they bestir themselves’ → “that they may prepare”: the reflexive verb becomes non-reflexive “prepare,” while “may” expresses the subjunctive in the purpose clause.
- *legno* ‘wood’ → “ship”: the translation names the vessel directly instead of retaining the Italian designation by its material.

## Lines 22–36 — A Sinner Hooked From the Pitch

> 22 Talor così ad alleggiar la pena  
> 23 Mostrava alcun de’ peccatori il dosso,  
> 24 E nascondeva in men che non balena.  

so now and then, to ease the punishment, some sinner showed his back and hid in less time than it lightens.

- *Talor così* ‘now and then so’ → “so now and then”: the order of the two adverbial expressions is reversed.
- *Mostrava alcun de’ peccatori il dosso* ‘showed one of the sinners the back’ → “some sinner showed his back”: the subject moves before the verb; the partitive expression becomes an indefinite singular noun phrase; and the definite article before the body part becomes a possessive.
- *E nascondeva* ‘and concealed [it]’ → “and hid”: the Italian leaves the previously mentioned back as the understood object; the English uses an intransitive construction, making the sinner himself the one who hides.
- *in men che non balena* ‘in less than it lightens’ → “in less time than it lightens”: “time” is supplied after the comparative; the Italian’s expletive *non* after a comparative has no English negative counterpart.

> 25 E come all’ orlo dell’ acqua d’ un fosso  
> 26 Stan li ranocchi pur col muso fuori,  
> 27 Sì che celano i piedi e l’ altro grosso;  

And as at the edge of the water of a ditch, the frogs stand only with their muzzles out, so that they hide their feet and other bulk;

- *Stan li ranocchi* ‘stand the frogs’ → “the frogs stand”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *col muso fuori* ‘with the muzzle out’ → “with their muzzles out”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the distributive singular becomes plural.
- *celano i piedi* ‘hide the feet’ → “hide their feet”: the definite article before the body part becomes a possessive.
- *l’altro grosso* ‘the other bulk’ → “other bulk”: the definite article is omitted.

> 28 Sì stavan d’ ogni parte i peccatori:  

thus stood on every hand the sinners.

- *d’ogni parte* ‘on every side’ → “on every hand”: the spatial expression is rendered by a different English idiom.

> 29 Ma come s’ appressava Barbariccia,  
> 30 Così si ritraean sotto i bollori.  

But as Barbariccia approached, they instantly retired beneath the seething.

- *s’appressava Barbariccia* ‘drew near Barbariccia’ → “Barbariccia approached”: the subject moves before the verb, and the Italian pronominal verb becomes a non-reflexive English verb.
- *Così* ‘so’ → “instantly”: the second member of the *come … così* correlation is rendered as an explicit indication of immediate response.
- *si ritraean* ‘drew themselves back’ → “retired”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *sotto i bollori* ‘beneath the boilings’ → “beneath the seething”: the plural noun becomes a singular verbal noun.

> 31 Io vidi, ed anche il cuor mi s’ accapriccia,  
> 32 Uno aspettar così, com’ egli incontra  
> 33 Che una rana rimane, e l’ altra spiccia.  

I saw, and my heart still shudders thereat, one linger so, as it will happen that one frog remains while the other spouts away.

- *ed anche il cuor mi s’accapriccia* ‘and still the heart to me shudders’ → “and my heart still shudders thereat”: the possessive dative becomes “my”; “still” moves after the subject; the pronominal verb becomes non-reflexive; and “thereat” is supplied to identify the occasion of the shudder.
- *aspettar* ‘wait’ → “linger”: waiting is rendered as lingering, emphasizing delayed departure.
- *com’ egli incontra* ‘as it happens’ → “as it will happen”: “will” is supplied to express characteristic or occasional occurrence, rather than simple futurity.
- *e* ‘and’ → “while”: coordination becomes an explicit temporal and contrasting relation.
- *spiccia* ‘darts away’ → “spouts away”: the English chooses an expression associated with the sudden movement of liquid rather than a straightforward verb of darting or springing away.

> 34 E Graffiacan, che gli era più di contra,  
> 35 Gli arroncigliò le impegolate chiome,  
> 36 E trassel su, che mi parve una lontra.  

And Graffiacane, who was nearest to him, hooked his pitchy locks and haled him up, so that to me he seemed an otter.

- *che gli era più di contra* ‘who was more directly opposite him’ → “who was nearest to him”: being opposite or facing someone is interpreted as proximity; the comparative expression is rendered by a superlative, and the pronoun’s equivalent follows that superlative.
- *Gli arroncigliò le impegolate chiome* ‘to him he hooked the pitch-coated locks’ → “hooked his pitchy locks”: the possessive dative and definite article become “his”; the participial description of the locks as coated with pitch becomes the adjective “pitchy”.
- *E trassel su* ‘and drew him up’ → “and haled him up”: the English chooses a more forceful verb, conveying hauling or dragging.
- *che* ‘that’ → “so that”: “so” is supplied to make the result construction explicit.

## Lines 37–52 — The Captive Names His Homeland

> 37 Io sapea già di tutti quanti il nome,  
> 38 Sì li notai quando furono eletti,  
> 39 E poi che si chiamaro, attesi come.  

I already knew the name of every one, so well I noted them as they were chosen, and when they called each other, listened how.

- *Io sapea già* ‘I knew already’ → “I already knew”: the adverb moves before the verb.
- *di tutti quanti il nome* ‘of all of them the name’ → “the name of every one”: the possessive phrase follows the noun; the emphatic plural *tutti quanti* becomes the distributive singular “every one”.
- *Sì li notai* ‘so them I noted’ → “so well I noted them”: “well” is supplied, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *quando* ‘when’ → “as”: the temporal conjunction is changed.
- *poi che* ‘after’ → “when”: the translation expresses coincidence rather than explicitly marking succession.
- *si chiamaro* ‘each other they called’ → “they called each other”: *si* is read reciprocally, and its English equivalent follows the verb.
- *attesi* ‘I paid attention’ → “listened”: the translation specifies auditory attention.

> 40 O Rubicante, fa che tu gli metti  
> 41 Gli unghioni addosso sì che tu lo scuoi,  
> 42 Gridavan tutti insieme i maladetti.  

“O Rubicante, see thou plant thy clutches on him, and flay him!” shouted together all the accursed crew.

- *fa che tu* ‘make that thou’ → “see thou”: the causative exhortation becomes an English exhortation with “see”; the conjunction introducing the subordinate clause is omitted.
- *gli metti Gli unghioni addosso* ‘on him put the great claws’ → “plant thy clutches on him”: “plant” replaces ‘put’, and “clutches” replaces the augmentative ‘great claws’; the definite article becomes “thy”, and the reference to the victim moves after the object.
- *sì che tu lo scuoi* ‘so that thou flay him’ → “and flay him”: a subordinate clause expressing the intended result becomes a coordinated command; the repeated subject is omitted, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *Gridavan tutti insieme i maladetti* ‘were shouting all together the accursed ones’ → “shouted together all the accursed crew”: the imperfect becomes a simple past; “all” moves after “together”, and the substantivized plural adjective becomes an adjective modifying the supplied collective singular “crew”.

> 43 Ed io: Maestro mio, fa, se tu puoi,  
> 44 Che tu sappi chi è lo sciagurato  
> 45 Venuto a man degli avversarj suoi.  

And I: “Master, learn if thou canst, who is that piteous wight, fallen into the hand of his adversaries.”

- *Maestro mio* ‘my master’ → “Master”: the possessive is omitted.
- *fa, se tu puoi, Che tu sappi* ‘bring it about, if thou canst, that thou know’ → “learn if thou canst”: the causative construction becomes a single imperative expressing the acquisition of knowledge; the conditional phrase follows that imperative instead of interrupting the construction.
- *lo sciagurato* ‘the wretched one’ → “that piteous wight”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative, and the substantivized adjective becomes an adjective and noun; “piteous” emphasizes the pity the person inspires.
- *Venuto* ‘come’ → “fallen”: the verb of arrival is replaced by one suggesting an adverse turn of circumstances.
- *a man* ‘to hand’ → “into the hand”: the preposition is expanded to express entry, and an article is supplied.
- *degli avversarj suoi* ‘of the adversaries his’ → “of his adversaries”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 46 Lo Duca mio gli s’ accostò a lato,  
> 47 Domandollo ond’ ei fosse; e quei rispose:  
> 48 Io fui del regno di Navarra nato.  

My Guide drew close to him, and asked him whence he came; and he replied: “I was born in the kingdom of Navarre.

- *Lo Duca mio* ‘the leader mine’ → “My Guide”: “Guide” replaces ‘leader’, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *gli s’ accostò a lato* ‘to him brought himself near at the side’ → “drew close to him”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive English expression; the reference to the other person follows the verb, and the explicit mention of his side is omitted.
- *Domandollo* ‘asked him’ → “and asked him”: a coordinating conjunction is supplied.
- *ond’ ei fosse* ‘whence he were’ → “whence he came”: the imperfect subjunctive of ‘be’ becomes the indicative past of ‘come’, interpreting the question as one about origin.
- *quei* ‘that one’ → “he”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *fui del regno di Navarra nato* ‘was of the kingdom of Navarre born’ → “was born in the kingdom of Navarre”: the participle moves next to the auxiliary, before the phrase naming the kingdom; origin expressed with ‘of’ becomes the location of birth.

> 49 Mia madre a servo d’ un signor mi pose,  
> 50 Chè m’ avea generato d’ un ribaldo  
> 51 Distruggitor di sè e di sue cose.  

My mother placed me as servant of a lord; for she had borne me to a ribald waster of himself and of his substance.

- *a servo d’ un signor mi pose* ‘as servant of a lord me placed’ → “placed me as servant of a lord”: the verb moves before its object, and the phrase describing the position follows rather than precedes them.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *m’ avea generato* ‘me had begotten’ → “she had borne me”: ‘begotten’ is rendered by the specifically maternal “borne”, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *d’ un ribaldo* ‘by a scoundrel’ → “to a ribald”: “to” replaces the preposition identifying the father; *ribaldo*, a noun, becomes an adjective modifying “waster”.
- *Distruggitor* ‘destroyer’ → “waster”: the translation substitutes a term emphasizing squandering.
- *sue cose* ‘his things’ → “his substance”: the plural noun becomes a singular mass noun referring to possessions or wealth.

> 52 Poi fui famiglia del buon re Tebaldo;  

Then I was domestic with the good king Thibault:

- *famiglia* ‘household’ → “domestic”: the Italian collective noun is interpreted as an individual member of the household.
- *del* ‘of the’ → “with the”: household membership expressed through ‘of’ becomes an association expressed through “with”.

## Lines 53–63 — A Tusking and Further Questions

> 53 Quivi mi misi a far baratteria,  
> 54 Di che rendo ragione in questo caldo.  

here I set myself to doing barratry, of which I render reckoning in this heat.”

- *Quivi* ‘there’ → “here”: the spatial reference changes from there to here.
- *mi misi a far* ‘myself I put to do’ → “I set myself to doing”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb, and the infinitive becomes a gerund after “to”.

> 55 E Ciriatto, a cui di bocca uscia  
> 56 D’ ogni parte una sanna, come a porco,  
> 57 Gli fe’ sentir come l’ una sdrucìa.  

And Ciriatto, from whose mouth on either side came forth a tusk as from a hog, made him feel how one of them did rip.

- *a cui di bocca* ‘to whom from the mouth’ → “from whose mouth”: the Italian dative construction becomes an English possessive relative construction.
- *D’ ogni parte* ‘on every side’ → “on either side”: “either” specifies the two sides; the phrase also moves before “came forth”, whereas it follows *uscia*.
- *come a porco* ‘as to a hog’ → “as from a hog”: “from” replaces the Italian dative, expressing the implied emergence of the tusk.
- *Gli fe’ sentir* ‘to him made feel’ → “made him feel”: the pronoun preceding the causative verb becomes its following object.
- *l’ una* ‘the one’ → “one of them”: the definite article is replaced by an explicit partitive phrase.
- *sdrucìa* ‘ripped’ → “did rip”: the Italian imperfect becomes a periphrastic English past with “did”.

> 58 Tra male gatte era venuto il sorco;  

Amongst evil cats the mouse had come;

- *male gatte* ‘evil she-cats’ → “evil cats”: the feminine specification is not retained.
- *era venuto il sorco* ‘had come the mouse’ → “the mouse had come”: the subject moves before the verb.

> 59 Ma Barbariccia il chiuse con le braccia,  
> 60 E disse: State in là, mentr’ io lo inforco.  

but Barbariccia locked him in his arms, and said: “Stand off whilst I enfork him!”

- *il chiuse* ‘him enclosed’ → “locked him”: “locked” gives a stronger expression of enclosure, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *con le braccia* ‘with the arms’ → “in his arms”: the instrumental ‘with’ becomes “in”, expressing containment, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *State in là* ‘stay over there’ → “Stand off”: the indication of position becomes an idiomatic command to keep away.
- *io lo inforco* ‘I him enfork’ → “I enfork him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 61 E al Maestro mio volse la faccia:  
> 62 Dimanda, disse, ancor, se più disii  
> 63 Saper da lui, prima ch’ altri il disfaccia.  

And turning his face to my Master: “Ask on,” he said, “if thou wouldst learn more from him, before some other undo him.”

- *al Maestro mio* ‘to the Master my’ → “to my Master”: the possessive moves before its noun, and the whole phrase moves after the verb and its object.
- *volse la faccia* ‘he turned the face’ → “turning his face”: the finite past verb becomes a participle dependent on “said”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Dimanda … ancor* ‘ask … still’ → “Ask on”: the adverb becomes the idiomatic particle “on”, expressing continuation, and moves before the intervening “he said”.
- *più disii Saper* ‘more thou desirest to know’ → “thou wouldst learn more”: the present expression of desire becomes the modal “wouldst”; knowing becomes learning, and “more” moves after the verb.
- *il disfaccia* ‘him undo’ → “undo him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

## Lines 64–80 — Questioning Interrupted by Violence

> 64 Lo Duca dunque: Or di’, degli altri rii  
> 65 Conosci tu alcun che sia Latino,  
> 66 Sotto la pece? E quegli: Io mi partii  
> 67 Poco è, da un che fu di là vicino:  

The Guide therefore: “Now say, of the other sinners knowest thou any that is a Latian, beneath the pitch?” And he: “I parted just now from one who was a neighbour of theirs.

- *rii* ‘wicked ones’ → “sinners”: a substantivized adjective is rendered by a noun.
- *che sia Latino* ‘who be Latin’ → “that is a Latian”: the subjunctive becomes indicative, and the adjective becomes a noun naming a person.
- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *Poco è* ‘it is little [time]’ → “just now”: an impersonal expression of elapsed time becomes an adverbial phrase.
- *di là vicino* ‘a neighbour from there’ → “a neighbour of theirs”: the geographical expression *di là* is interpreted through a relationship to the people previously mentioned, expressed by “of theirs”.

> 68 Così foss’ io ancor con lui coverto,  
> 69 Ch’ io non temerei unghia, nè uncino.  

Would I still were covered with him, for I should not fear claw nor hook!”

- *Così foss’ io ancor con lui coverto* ‘So were I still with him covered’ → “Would I still were covered with him”: the optative construction becomes an English wish introduced by “Would”; “covered” moves before “with him”.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *unghia* ‘nail’ → “claw”: the anatomical term is rendered more specifically as a claw.

> 70 E Libicocco: Troppo avem sofferto,  
> 71 Disse; e presegli il braccio col ronciglio,  
> 72 Sì che, stracciando, ne portò un lacerto.  

And Libicocco cried: “Too much have we endured!” and with the hook seized his arm, and mangling carried off a part of brawn.

- *Disse* ‘said’ → “cried”: the reporting verb becomes more forceful and moves from after the quoted words to before them.
- *presegli il braccio col ronciglio* ‘seized for him the arm with the hook’ → “with the hook seized his arm”: the possessive dative *gli* becomes “his”; the instrumental phrase moves before the verb.
- *Sì che* ‘so that’ → “and”: the explicit construction of consequence becomes simple coordination.
- *ne portò un lacerto* ‘from it carried away a piece of flesh’ → “carried off a part of brawn”: *ne*, referring to the arm as the source, is not separately expressed; *lacerto* is expanded into “part of brawn”, specifying muscular flesh.

> 73 Draghignazzo anch’ ei volle dar di piglio  
> 74 Giù dalle gambe; onde il decurio loro  
> 75 Si volse intorno intorno con mal piglio.  

Draghignazzo, he too, wished to have a catch at the legs below; whereat their Decurion wheeled around around with evil aspect.

- *anch’ ei* ‘also he’ → “he too”: the pronoun and the additive adverb exchange places.
- *dar di piglio* ‘give a grip’ → “have a catch”: the Italian idiom for seizing is recast with a different verb and noun.
- *Giù dalle gambe* ‘down at the legs’ → “at the legs below”: the adverb of position moves after the noun phrase.
- *onde* ‘whence’ → “whereat”: the connection is expressed as a reaction to the preceding action rather than as something proceeding from it.
- *il decurio loro* ‘the decurion of them’ → “their Decurion”: the postposed possessive becomes a possessive determiner before the noun.
- *Si volse* ‘turned himself’ → “wheeled”: the pronominal construction becomes an intransitive verb, with a more vigorous expression of turning.

> 76 Quand’ elli un poco rappaciati foro,  
> 77 A lui ch’ ancor mirava sua ferita,  
> 78 Dimandò il Duca mio senza dimoro:  

When they were somewhat pacified, my Guide without delay asked him that still kept gazing on his wound:

- *un poco rappaciati foro* ‘a little pacified were’ → “were somewhat pacified”: the auxiliary moves before the participle and its modifier; “somewhat” replaces the phrase *un poco*.
- *A lui … Dimandò il Duca mio senza dimoro* ‘To him … asked the Guide my without delay’ → “my Guide without delay asked him”: the fronted indirect object becomes a direct object after the verb; the subject moves before the verb, the possessive before its noun, and “without delay” before “asked”.
- *ch’ ancor mirava sua ferita* ‘who still looked at his wound’ → “that still kept gazing on his wound”: the imperfect is expanded into “kept gazing”, emphasizing continued attention; “gazing on” replaces ‘looking at’.

> 79 Chi fu colui, da cui mala partita  
> 80 Di’ che facesti per venire a proda?  

“Who was he, from whom thou sayest that thou madest an ill departure to come ashore?”

- *Chi fu colui* ‘Who was that one’ → “Who was he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *da cui mala partita Di’ che facesti* ‘from whom an ill departure thou sayest that thou madest’ → “from whom thou sayest that thou madest an ill departure”: the anticipatory object *mala partita* moves after the verb that governs it.
- *per venire a proda* ‘to come to the bank’ → “to come ashore”: the prepositional phrase naming the destination becomes the directional adverb “ashore”.

## Lines 81–93 — Two Barrators and a Threat

> 81 Ed ei rispose: Fu frate Gomita,  
> 82 Quel di Gallura, vasel d’ ogni froda,  

And he answered: “It was Friar Gomita, he of Gallura, vessel of every fraud,

- *Quel di Gallura* ‘that one of Gallura’ → “he of Gallura”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.

> 83 Ch’ ebbe i nimici di suo donno in mano,  
> 84 E fe’ lor sì, che ciascun se ne loda:  

who had his master’s enemies in hand, and did so to them that they all praise him for it.

- *i nimici di suo donno* ‘the enemies of his lord’ → “his master’s enemies”: the possessive phrase moves before the noun and takes the English possessive construction; *donno* is rendered as “master’s” rather than ‘lord’s’.
- *fe’ lor sì* ‘did to them so’ → “did so to them”: the adverb moves before the indirect object.
- *ciascun se ne loda* ‘each one praises himself for it’ → “they all praise him for it”: the singular distributive subject becomes plural, and the reflexive construction is interpreted as praise directed at another person, expressed by “him”.

> 85 Denar si tolse, e lasciolli di piano,  
> 86 Sì com’ ei dice: e negli altri uficj anche  
> 87 Barattier fu non picciol, ma sovrano.  

Money took he for himself, and dismissed them smoothly, as he says; and in his other offices besides, he was no petty but a sovereign barrator.

- *Denar si tolse* ‘money for himself he took’ → “Money took he for himself”: the reflexive pronoun expressing personal advantage becomes a prepositional phrase after the verb; the supplied subject follows the verb.
- *lasciolli di piano* ‘let them go outright’ → “dismissed them smoothly”: letting them go becomes “dismissed them”, and *di piano* is interpreted as describing the ease of their release.
- *Sì com’ ei dice* ‘so as he says’ → “as he says”: *sì* is omitted.
- *negli altri uficj anche* ‘in the other offices also’ → “in his other offices besides”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the additive adverb is rendered as “besides”.
- *Barattier fu non picciol, ma sovrano* ‘barrator he was, not small, but sovereign’ → “he was no petty but a sovereign barrator”: the predicate noun moves from the beginning to the end, following both adjectives; “petty” renders *picciol*, and the adjective-negating *non* becomes the determiner “no”, balanced by the supplied article “a”.

> 88 Usa con esso donno Michel Zanche  
> 89 Di Logodoro; e a dir di Sardigna  
> 90 Le lingue lor non si sentono stanche.  

With him keeps company Don Michel Zanche of Logodoro; and in speaking of Sardinia the tongues of them do not feel weary.

- *Usa con esso* ‘associates with him’ → “With him keeps company”: the verb becomes the phrase “keeps company”, and the accompanying prepositional phrase moves before it.
- *donno* ‘lord’ → “Don”: the title is retained in an Italian form rather than translated.
- *a dir* ‘to speak’ → “in speaking”: the preposition and infinitive become a preposition and gerund, expressing the activity during which the following statement applies.
- *Le lingue lor* ‘the tongues their’ → “the tongues of them”: the postposed possessive becomes an *of*-phrase with a personal pronoun.
- *non si sentono stanche* ‘do not feel themselves weary’ → “do not feel weary”: the reflexive pronoun is omitted, leaving the adjective directly dependent on “feel”.

> 91 O me! vedete l’ altro che digrigna:  

Oh me! see that other grinning:

- *vedete l’ altro* ‘see the other’ → “see that other”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative.
- *che digrigna* ‘who bares his teeth’ → “grinning”: the finite relative clause becomes a participle, and the baring of teeth is expressed as grinning.

> 92 Io direi anche; ma io temo ch’ ello  
> 93 Non s’ apparecchi a grattarmi la tigna.  

I would say more; but fear he is preparing to claw my scurf.”

- *anche* ‘also’ → “more”: the additive adverb is interpreted as indicating further speech.
- *ma io temo* ‘but I fear’ → “but fear”: the explicitly stated subject pronoun is omitted.
- *ch’ ello Non s’ apparecchi* ‘that he may not prepare himself’ → “he is preparing”: the conjunction is omitted; *non*, which is expletive after the expression of fear, is not translated as a negative; the present subjunctive becomes an English present progressive, and the reflexive pronoun is omitted.
- *a grattarmi la tigna* ‘to scratch me the scurf’ → “to claw my scurf”: “claw” replaces ‘scratch’, while the pronoun marking the affected possessor becomes the possessive “my” before the noun.

## Lines 94–108 — An Offer and a Suspicion

> 94 E il gran proposto, volto a Farfarello  
> 95 Che stralunava gli occhi per ferire,  
> 96 Disse: Fatti in costà, malvagio uccello.  

And their great Marshal, turning to Farfarello, who rolled his eyes to strike, said: “Off with thee, villanous bird!”

- *il gran proposto* ‘the great chief’ → “their great Marshal”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the general designation ‘chief’ becomes the more specific title “Marshal”.
- *volto* ‘turned’ → “turning”: a past participle describing the resulting position becomes a present participle describing the action.
- *stralunava gli occhi* ‘was rolling the eyes’ → “rolled his eyes”: the imperfect becomes an English simple past, and the definite article before the body part becomes a possessive.
- *Fatti in costà* ‘put yourself over there’ → “Off with thee”: the reflexive imperative directing someone to take a position becomes an idiomatic command to move away.

> 97 Se voi volete vedere o udire,  
> 98 Ricominciò lo spaurato appresso,  
> 99 Toschi o Lombardi, io ne farò venire.  

“If you wish to see or hear Tuscans or Lombards,” the frightened sinner then resumed, “I will make them come.

- *Se voi volete vedere o udire, Ricominciò lo spaurato appresso, Toschi o Lombardi* ‘If you wish to see or hear, began again the frightened one afterward, Tuscans or Lombards’ → “If you wish to see or hear Tuscans or Lombards,” the frightened sinner then resumed”: the objects of ‘see or hear’ move before the intervening narrative clause, making the conditional clause continuous. The subject moves before the verb, and the temporal adverb also moves before it; ‘began again’ becomes “resumed”. The substantivized adjective *lo spaurato* is expanded with the supplied noun “sinner”.
- *ne* ‘of them’ → “them”: the partitive pronoun becomes an ordinary object pronoun, leaving its partitive force unexpressed.

> 100 Ma stien le Malebranche un poco in cesso,  
> 101 Sì che non teman delle lor vendette;  

But let the Malebranche stand a little back, that they may not fear their vengeance;

- *stien le Malebranche un poco in cesso* ‘may the Malebranche remain a little apart’ → “let the Malebranche stand a little back”: the jussive subjunctive becomes an English “let” construction; remaining apart is expressed as standing back.
- *Sì che* ‘so that’ → “that”: the connective is shortened, without a separate equivalent for *Sì*.
- *non teman delle lor vendette* ‘may they not fear of their revenges’ → “they may not fear their vengeance”: the subjunctive is expressed with “may”; the complement introduced by *di* becomes a direct object, and plural *vendette* becomes singular “vengeance”.

> 102 Ed io, seggendo in questo luogo stesso,  
> 103 Per un ch’ io son, ne farò venir sette,  
> 104 Quando sufolerò, com’ è nostr’ uso  
> 105 Di fare allor che fuori alcun si mette.  

and I, sitting in this same place, for one that I am, will make seven come, on whistling as is our wont to do when any of us gets out.”

- *questo luogo stesso* ‘this place itself’ → “this same place”: postposed *stesso* becomes the adjective “same” before the noun.
- *ne farò venir sette* ‘of them I will make come seven’ → “will make seven come”: the partitive *ne* is left unexpressed, and “seven” moves before “come”.
- *Quando sufolerò* ‘when I shall whistle’ → “on whistling”: a finite temporal clause in the future becomes a preposition followed by a gerund, with no explicit tense.
- *allor che* ‘at the time when’ → “when”: the temporal expression is compressed into a single conjunction.
- *fuori alcun si mette* ‘outside someone puts himself’ → “any of us gets out”: *alcun* becomes “any of us”, supplying the group to which the indefinite pronoun refers. The reflexive construction becomes “gets”, and *fuori* moves from the beginning of the clause to the end as “out”.

> 106 Cagnazzo a cotal motto levò il muso,  
> 107 Crollando il capo, e disse: Odi malizia  
> 108 Ch’ egli ha pensato per gittarsi giuso.  

Cagnazzo at these words raised his snout, shaking his head, and said: “Hear the malice he has contrived, to throw himself down!”

- *a cotal motto* ‘at such a remark’ → “at these words”: ‘such’ becomes the demonstrative “these”, and singular *motto* becomes plural “words”.
- *il muso* ‘the snout’ → “his snout”: the definite article before the body part becomes a possessive.
- *il capo* ‘the head’ → “his head”: the definite article before the body part becomes a possessive.
- *malizia* ‘malice’ → “the malice”: the definite article is supplied.
- *Ch’ egli ha pensato* ‘that he has thought out’ → “he has contrived”: the object relative pronoun is omitted, and ‘thought out’ is rendered by “contrived”.

## Lines 109–123 — The Navarrese Escapes

> 109 Ond’ ei, ch’ avea lacciuoli a gran divizia,  
> 110 Rispose: Malizioso son io troppo,  
> 111 Quando procuro a’ miei maggior tristizia!  

Whereat he, who had artifices in great store, replied: “Too malicious indeed! when I contrive for my companions greater sorrow.”

- *lacciuoli* ‘little snares’ → “artifices”: the concrete, diminutive metaphor is rendered as an abstract term for tricks.
- *a gran divizia* ‘in great abundance’ → “in great store”: abundance is expressed as a plentiful stock.
- *Malizioso son io troppo* ‘malicious am I too much’ → “Too malicious indeed”: “Too” moves before the adjective; the verb and its explicit subject are omitted, and “indeed” is supplied.
- *procuro* ‘bring about’ → “contrive”: the translation emphasizes deliberate scheming rather than the production of an outcome.
- *a’ miei* ‘for mine’ → “for my companions”: the substantival possessive is expanded with “companions”.

> 112 Alichin non si tenne, e di rintoppo  
> 113 Agli altri, disse a lui: Se tu ti cali,  
> 114 Io non ti verrò dietro di galoppo,  
> 115 Ma batterò sovra la pece l’ ali:  

Alichino held in no longer, and in opposition to the others said to him: “If thou stoop, I will not follow thee at gallop, but beat my wings above the pitch.

- *non si tenne* ‘did not restrain himself’ → “held in no longer”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive idiom, and simple negation becomes cessation through “no longer”.
- *ti cali* ‘lower thyself’ → “stoop”: a reflexive verb is replaced by an intransitive one.
- *Io non ti verrò dietro* ‘I shall not come behind thee’ → “I will not follow thee”: coming behind someone is expressed by the transitive verb “follow”, with “thee” as its object.
- *Ma batterò sovra la pece l’ ali* ‘but I shall beat above the pitch the wings’ → “but beat my wings above the pitch”: the future auxiliary is shared with the preceding clause; the object moves before the prepositional phrase, and the definite article becomes the possessive “my”.

> 116 Lascisi il colle, e sia la ripa scudo,  
> 117 A veder se tu sol più di noi vali.  

Let the height be left, and be the bank a screen; to see if thou alone prevailest over us.”

- *il colle* ‘the hill’ → “the height”: a specific landform is rendered by a more general term for elevated ground.
- *scudo* ‘shield’ → “a screen”: one protective object replaces another, and English supplies an indefinite article before the predicate noun.
- *più di noi vali* ‘more than we art worth’ → “prevailest over us”: comparative worth or ability becomes superiority expressed through “prevailest”; the verb moves before its complement, and the comparison becomes an “over” phrase.

> 118 O tu, che leggi, udirai nuovo ludo:  

O Reader, thou shalt hear new sport.

- *O tu, che leggi* ‘O thou who readest’ → “O Reader”: direct address to a pronoun qualified by a relative clause becomes direct address to a noun.

> 119 Ciascun dall’ altra costa gli occhi volse;  
> 120 Quel prima, ch’ a ciò fare era più crudo.  

All turned their eyes toward the other side, he first who had been most unripe for doing it.

- *Ciascun* ‘each’ → “All”: a singular distributive expression becomes a plural collective one.
- *dall’ altra costa gli occhi volse* ‘toward the other side the eyes turned’ → “turned their eyes toward the other side”: the verb moves before its object, the directional phrase moves to the end, and the definite article before “eyes” becomes “their”.
- *Quel* ‘that one’ → “he”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *a ciò fare* ‘for doing this’ → “for doing it”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, the infinitive becomes an English gerund, and the phrase moves after the adjective it complements.
- *era* ‘was’ → “had been”: the imperfect becomes a past perfect.

> 121 Lo Navarrese ben suo tempo colse;  

The Navarrese chose well his time;

- *ben suo tempo colse* ‘well his time seized’ → “chose well his time”: seizing the opportunity is rendered as choosing it, and the verb moves before the adverb and object.

> 122 Fermò le piante a terra, e in un punto  
> 123 Saltò, e dal proposto lor si sciolse:  

planted his soles upon the ground, and in an instant leapt and from their purpose freed himself.

- *Fermò le piante a terra* ‘set firm the soles on ground’ → “planted his soles upon the ground”: setting firmly is expressed by “planted”; the article before “soles” becomes “his”, and English supplies an article before “ground”.
- *in un punto* ‘in a point’ → “in an instant”: the temporal sense of ‘point’ is made explicit.
- *dal proposto lor* ‘from the purpose their’ → “from their purpose”: the postposed possessive moves before the noun.
- *si sciolse* ‘himself loosed’ → “freed himself”: loosening is rendered as freeing, and the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.

## Lines 124–138 — Escape and Combat

> 124 Di che ciascun di colpo fu compunto,  
> 125 Ma quei più, che cagion fu del difetto.  

Thereat each suddenly was stung with rage; but he most who had been cause of the mistake.

- *Di che* ‘at which’ → “Thereat”: the relative expression becomes a demonstrative adverb.
- *di colpo* ‘at a stroke’ → “suddenly”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *fu compunto* ‘was pricked’ → “was stung with rage”: “with rage” supplies a particular emotion not expressly named in the Italian.
- *quei più* ‘that one more’ → “he most”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun, and the comparative becomes a superlative.
- *che cagion fu del difetto* ‘who cause was of the fault’ → “who had been cause of the mistake”: the verb moves before its complement; the simple past becomes a past perfect; and ‘fault’ is rendered as “mistake”.

> 126 Però si mosse, e gridò: Tu se’ giunto.  

He therefore started forth, and shouted: “Thou’rt caught!”

- *Però si mosse* ‘therefore he moved’ → “He therefore started forth”: “therefore” follows the supplied subject, and “started forth” makes the movement more explicitly sudden and forward.
- *Tu se’ giunto* ‘thou art reached’ → “Thou’rt caught”: “caught” interprets *giunto* as overtaken or seized.

> 127 Ma poco valse; chè l’ ale al sospetto  
> 128 Non potero avanzar: quegli andò sotto,  
> 129 E quei drizzò, volando, suso il petto.  

But little it availed; for wings could not outspeed the terror. The sinner went under; and he, flying, raised up his breast.

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *l’ ale* ‘the wings’ → “wings”: the definite article is omitted.
- *al sospetto Non potero avanzar* ‘ahead of the apprehension could not advance’ → “could not outspeed the terror”: the preceding prepositional complement becomes a following direct object, and “terror” strengthens ‘apprehension’.
- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “The sinner”: an explicit identifying noun replaces the demonstrative pronoun.
- *quei* ‘that one’ → “he”: a personal pronoun replaces the demonstrative.
- *drizzò, volando, suso il petto* ‘straightened, flying, upward the breast’ → “flying, raised up his breast”: “flying” moves before the finite verb; straightening upward becomes “raised up”; and a possessive replaces the definite article before ‘breast’.

> 130 Non altrimenti l’ anitra di botto,  
> 131 Quando il falcon s’ appressa, giù s’ attuffa,  
> 132 Ed ei ritorna su crucciato e rotto.  

Not otherwise the duck suddenly dives down, when the falcon approaches; and he returns up angry and defeated.

- *di botto* ‘at a stroke’ → “suddenly”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *Quando il falcon s’ appressa, giù s’ attuffa* ‘when the falcon brings itself near, down it plunges itself’ → “dives down, when the falcon approaches”: the temporal clause moves after the main verb; both reflexive constructions become intransitive English verbs; and “down” follows rather than precedes its verb.
- *rotto* ‘broken’ → “defeated”: the translation selects a figurative sense of the participle.

> 133 Irato Calcabrina della buffa,  
> 134 Volando dietro gli tenne, invaghito  
> 135 Che quei campasse, per aver la zuffa.  

Calcabrina, furious at the trick, kept flying after him, desirous that the sinner might escape, to have a quarrel;

- *Irato Calcabrina della buffa* ‘Angry Calcabrina at the mockery’ → “Calcabrina, furious at the trick”: the adjective moves after the name; “furious” intensifies ‘angry’; and “trick” interprets the mockery as a deception.
- *Volando dietro gli tenne* ‘flying, behind him he kept’ → “kept flying after him”: the Italian construction expresses following, with flying as its manner; English makes “flying” the complement of “kept” and places “after him” after it.
- *invaghito* ‘enamoured’ → “desirous”: the figurative expression of attraction becomes a direct expression of desire.
- *quei* ‘that one’ → “the sinner”: an identifying noun replaces the demonstrative pronoun.
- *campasse* ‘should escape’ → “might escape”: the Italian imperfect subjunctive is expressed through the English modal “might” and an infinitive.
- *per aver la zuffa* ‘to have the brawl’ → “to have a quarrel”: the definite article becomes indefinite, and “quarrel” is less specifically physical than ‘brawl’.

> 136 E come il barattier fu disparito,  
> 137 Così volse gli artigli al suo compagno,  
> 138 E fu con lui sovra il fosso ghermito.  

and, when the barrator had disappeared, he turned his talons on his fellow, and was clutched with him above the ditch.

- *E come … Così* ‘and as … so’ → “and, when”: the paired temporal construction becomes a single subordinate clause introduced by “when”; *Così* has no separate equivalent.
- *fu disparito* ‘was disappeared’ → “had disappeared”: the Italian past-anterior construction uses ‘be’ as its auxiliary, whereas the English past perfect uses “had”.
- *gli artigli* ‘the talons’ → “his talons”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *al suo compagno* ‘to his companion’ → “on his fellow”: “on” replaces the directional ‘to’, making the hostile application of the talons explicit.
- *fu con lui sovra il fosso ghermito* ‘was with him above the ditch clutched’ → “was clutched with him above the ditch”: the participle moves beside its auxiliary, before the accompanying and locative phrases.

- *dall’ altra costa* ‘to the other side’ → “over to the other coast”: “over” is supplied, and *costa* is rendered as “coast” rather than the more general ‘side’; *da* here has directional force.

- *cotti* ‘cooked’ → “scalded”: the broader term for cooking is replaced by one specifying injury from intense wet heat.

## Summary — Close Texture, Flexible Construction

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s sequence of details, repetitions, and concrete vocabulary, but frequently reshapes their grammatical arrangement. Subjects move before verbs, objects follow them, and dependent phrases move beside the words they qualify. Thus *della bolgia ogni contegno* becomes “each habit of the chasm,” while *batterò sovra la pece l’ ali* becomes “beat my wings above the pitch.” Yet he also preserves conspicuous inversions and repetitions, as in “coursers have I seen” and “wheeled around around,” retaining something of the Italian’s unusual verbal texture.

Changes of construction often replace literal correspondence with English idiom. Infinitives after verbs of seeing become participles, as *muover campo* becomes “moving camp,” or nouns, as *Ferir torneamenti* becomes “the shock of tournaments.” Reflexive verbs frequently become intransitive ones; *si ritraean*, for example, becomes “retired.” Elsewhere a finite clause becomes a participial phrase, as *Quando sufolerò* becomes “on whistling,” or a noun and copula become an active verb, as *sghermitor … fue* becomes “unclutched them.” Tense and aspect are also adjusted: imperfects may become simple pasts, while “kept gazing” makes the continuing action of *mirava* explicit. Subordination sometimes gives way to coordination, notably when *sì che* becomes “and.”

Word choice ranges from close, sometimes strikingly literal equivalents to interpretive substitutions. “enfork,” “beglued,” and “the tongues of them” preserve distinctive features of the Italian expression. By contrast, *legno*, ‘wood,’ becomes “ship,” and *lacciuoli*, ‘little snares,’ becomes the abstract “artifices.” Other choices alter emphasis: *fiera* becomes “hideous,” *sospetto* becomes “terror,” and *cotti*, ‘cooked,’ becomes “scalded.” Such renderings may clarify a reference, strengthen an emotion, or narrow a broader Italian term.

Omissions and additions likewise vary between grammatical adjustment and interpretation. Repeated prepositions, partitive pronouns, and some connective words disappear; *grifagno* has no separate equivalent in “a sparrowhawk.” Conversely, “with rage” supplies an emotion after *fu compunto*, and “the sinner,” “companions,” and “pair” make references more explicit. Italian possessive datives and definite articles before body parts regularly become English possessives, as *Gli arroncigliò le impegolate chiome* becomes “hooked his pitchy locks.” Throughout, Carlyle also supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and omits the Italian articles that accompany possessives. These routine differences support a translation that remains closely tied to the original wording while allowing substantial local changes in syntax, idiom, and emphasis.
