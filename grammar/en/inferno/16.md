# Inferno 16

## Lines 1–13 — Three Shades Call to Dante

> 1 Già era in loco, ove s’ udia il rimbombo  
> 2 Dell’ acqua, che cadea nell’ altro giro,  
> 3 Simile a quel, che l’ arnie fanno, rombo;  

Already I was in a place where the resounding of the water, that fell into the other circle, was heard like the hum which bee-hives make;

- *in loco* ‘in place’ → “in a place”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *ove s’udia il rimbombo Dell’acqua, che cadea nell’altro giro* ‘where was heard the resounding of the water, that was falling into the other circle’ → “where the resounding of the water, that fell into the other circle, was heard”: the subject and its accompanying relative clause precede “was heard,” whereas the Italian puts the verb first. The imperfect *cadea* becomes the simple past “fell.”
- *Simile a quel, che l’arnie fanno, rombo* ‘similar to that hum which the hives make’ → “like the hum which bee-hives make”: “like” replaces the adjective-and-preposition construction *simile a*. The demonstrative *quel* becomes “the,” and the delayed noun *rombo* moves before the relative clause. The article before *arnie* is omitted, and “bee-hives” explicitly identifies the kind of hive.

> 4 Quando tre ombre insieme si partiro,  
> 5 Correndo, d’ una torma che passava  
> 6 Sotto la pioggia dell’ aspro martiro.  

when three shades together, running, quitted a troop that passed beneath the rain of the sharp torment.

- *si partiro, Correndo, d’una torma* ‘departed, running, from a troop’ → “running, quitted a troop”: “running” moves before the finite verb. The pronominal, intransitive construction *partirsi da* becomes transitive “quitted,” with the troop as its direct object rather than the complement of a preposition.
- *che passava* ‘that was passing’ → “that passed”: the imperfect, presenting an action in progress, is rendered by the English simple past.
- *aspro* ‘harsh’ → “sharp”: the adjective is rendered with a word suggesting the torment’s piercing intensity.

> 7 Venien vêr noi; e ciascuna gridava:  
> 8 Sostati tu, che all’ abito ne sembri  
> 9 Essere alcun di nostra terra prava.  

They came towards us, and each cried: “Stay thee, thou who by thy dress to us appearest to be some one from our perverse country.”

- *Venien* ‘they were coming’ → “They came”: the imperfect becomes the English simple past rather than a progressive construction.
- *ciascuna gridava* ‘each was crying out’ → “each cried”: the imperfect becomes the simple past.
- *all’abito* ‘by the dress’ → “by thy dress”: the definite article is replaced by a possessive, explicitly identifying the wearer.
- *di nostra terra prava* ‘of our land perverse’ → “from our perverse country”: “from” makes the relation one of origin; “country” renders *terra* in its territorial sense, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 10 Aimè, che piaghe vidi ne’ lor membri  
> 11 Recenti e vecchie dalle fiamme incese!  

Ah me! what wounds I saw upon their limbs, recent and old, kindled by the flames.

- *ne’ lor membri* ‘in their limbs’ → “upon their limbs”: the preposition is changed to locate the wounds on the limbs’ surface.
- *dalle fiamme incese* ‘by the flames kindled’ → “kindled by the flames”: the participle moves before the phrase naming its agent.

> 12 Ancor men duol, pur ch’ io me ne rimembri.  

It pains me yet, when I but think thereof.

- *Ancor men duol* ‘still to me there is pain from it’ → “It pains me yet”: the initial adverb moves to the end. The Italian construction with dative *me* and *ne*, referring to the cause of the pain, becomes a transitive construction with “It” as subject and “me” as direct object.
- *pur ch’io me ne rimembri* ‘if only I recall it to myself’ → “when I but think thereof”: the conditional construction is expressed temporally with “when,” while “but” retains the limiting force of *pur*. Remembering becomes thinking, and the reflexive *me* is not separately represented.

> 13 Alle lor grida il mio Dottor s’ attese,  

To their cries my Teacher listened;

- *s’attese* ‘gave heed’ → “listened”: the pronominal expression for directing one’s attention is rendered by a verb specifically denoting auditory attention.

## Lines 14–27 — Courtesy and the Wheeling Three

> 14 Volse il viso vêr me, e: Ora aspetta,  
> 15 Disse; a costor si vuole esser cortese:  

turned his face toward me, and said: “Now wait: to these courtesy is due.

- *il viso* ‘the face’ → “his face”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Ora aspetta, Disse* ‘Now wait, said’ → “said: “Now wait”: the reporting verb is moved before the words spoken.
- *a costor si vuole esser cortese* ‘to these one must be courteous’ → “to these courtesy is due”: the impersonal construction expressing obligation becomes a statement about what is owed; the adjective *cortese* becomes the noun “courtesy”.

> 16 E se non fosse il fuoco che saetta  
> 17 La natura del luogo, io dicerei,  
> 18 Che meglio stesse a te, ch’ a lor, la fretta.  

And were there not the fire, which the nature of the place darts, I should say the haste befitted thee more than them.”

- *se non fosse* ‘if there were not’ → “were there not”: the conditional conjunction is omitted, and inversion marks the condition instead.
- *che saetta La natura del luogo* ‘which darts the nature of the place’ → “which the nature of the place darts”: *la natura del luogo*, the subject of *saetta*, is moved before its verb; *che* remains the object.
- *Che meglio stesse a te, ch’ a lor, la fretta* ‘that haste should be more fitting for thee than for them’ → “the haste befitted thee more than them”: the introductory *Che* is omitted, and the postponed subject *la fretta* moves before the verb. The construction *stare a*, expressing suitability, becomes the transitive “befitted”, with the persons as direct objects rather than prepositional complements; *meglio* becomes “more”, and the imperfect subjunctive becomes an English past form.

> 19 Ricominciar, come noi ristemmo, ei  
> 20 L’ antico verso; e quando a noi fur giunti,  
> 21 Fenno una ruota di se tutti e trei.  

They recommenced, as we stood still, their ancient wail; and when they had reached us, all the three made of themselves a wheel.

- *Ricominciar … ei* ‘recommenced … they’ → “They recommenced”: the explicit subject moves from after the intervening clause to before the verb.
- *ristemmo* ‘stopped’ → “stood still”: the act of stopping is rendered as remaining stationary.
- *L’ antico verso* ‘the ancient verse’ → “their ancient wail”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and *verso* is interpreted as a lamenting cry rather than rendered as verse or song.
- *a noi fur giunti* ‘to us they were come’ → “they had reached us”: the initial prepositional complement becomes a direct object following “reached”; the Italian compound tense with *essere* is rendered with English “had”.
- *Fenno una ruota di se tutti e trei* ‘made a wheel of themselves all and three’ → “all the three made of themselves a wheel”: the postponed subject moves before the verb, while “of themselves” moves before “a wheel”. The idiomatic *tutti e trei* becomes “all the three”, without a counterpart to its conjunction *e*.

> 22 Qual soleano i campion far nudi ed unti,  
> 23 Avvisando lor presa e lor vantaggio,  
> 24 Prima che sien tra lor battuti e punti:  

As champions, naked and anointed, were wont to do, spying their grasp and vantage, ere they came to blows and thrusts at one another;

- *Qual soleano i campion far nudi ed unti* ‘as were wont the champions to do naked and anointed’ → “As champions, naked and anointed, were wont to do”: the subject moves before the verb, and its descriptive adjectives move with it, before “were wont to do”. The definite article before the generic plural *campion* is omitted.
- *Avvisando* ‘considering’ → “spying”: the verb is rendered in terms of watchful observation rather than consideration or assessment.
- *lor presa e lor vantaggio* ‘their grasp and their vantage’ → “their grasp and vantage”: the second possessive is omitted, leaving one “their” to govern both nouns.
- *Prima che sien tra lor battuti e punti* ‘before they be struck and pricked among themselves’ → “ere they came to blows and thrusts at one another”: the passive subjunctive construction becomes the active past-tense idiom “came to”. The participles become the nouns “blows” and “thrusts”, and the reciprocal *tra lor* becomes “at one another”, placed at the end.

> 25 Così, rotando, ciascuna il visaggio  
> 26 Drizzava a me, sì che in contrario il collo  
> 27 Faceva a’ piè continuo viaggio.  

thus, wheeling, each directed his visage toward me, so that the neck kept travelling in a direction contrary to the feet.

- *il visaggio Drizzava* ‘the visage directed’ → “directed his visage”: the object moves after the verb, and its definite article becomes a possessive.
- *in contrario … a’ piè* ‘in opposition … to the feet’ → “in a direction contrary to the feet”: “a direction” is supplied, and the separated parts of the expression are brought together after the expression of motion.
- *Faceva … continuo viaggio* ‘made … continuous journey’ → “kept travelling”: the verb–noun construction becomes a verbal expression, with “kept” conveying the continuity expressed by *continuo*.

## Lines 28–39 — Fame and Guidoguerra’s Deeds

> 28 E se miseria d’ esto loco sollo  
> 29 Rende in dispetto noi e nostri preghi,  
> 30 Cominciò l’ uno, e il tristo aspetto e brollo;  

And one of them began: “If the misery of this loose place, and our dreary and scorched aspect, bring us and our prayers into contempt,

- *E … Cominciò l’ uno* ‘And … began the one’ → “And one of them began”: the reporting clause moves from within the speech to its beginning, with subject before verb; the opening conjunction is attached to the narration rather than the speech. “one of them” makes the partitive sense of *l’ uno* explicit.
- *miseria* ‘misery’ → “the misery”: the definite article is supplied.
- *esto loco sollo* ‘this place loose’ → “this loose place”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Rende in dispetto noi e nostri preghi* ‘renders in contempt us and our prayers’ → “bring us and our prayers into contempt”: the construction becomes the English idiom “bring … into contempt,” with the object before the prepositional phrase. The singular verb becomes plural, agreeing with the coordinated misery and aspect.
- *e il tristo aspetto e brollo* ‘and the sad aspect and bare’ → “and our dreary and scorched aspect”: “our” replaces the definite article; “dreary” renders sadness as a quality of appearance, while “scorched” interprets *brollo* (‘bare, stripped’) through burning rather than bareness alone. Both adjectives precede the noun, and the whole phrase moves before the verb.

> 31 La fama nostra il tuo animo pieghi  
> 32 A dirne chi tu se’, che i vivi piedi  
> 33 Così sicuro per lo Inferno freghi.  

let our fame incline thy mind to tell us who thou art, that thus securely movest thy living feet through Hell.

- *La fama nostra il tuo animo pieghi* ‘our fame thy mind may bend’ → “let our fame incline thy mind”: the exhortative subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”; the possessive moves before “fame,” and the object follows “incline” rather than preceding the verb.
- *i vivi piedi* ‘the living feet’ → “thy living feet”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *Così sicuro* ‘so secure’ → “thus securely”: the adjective describing the person becomes an adverb modifying his movement.
- *freghi* ‘rubbest’ → “movest”: the more specific action of rubbing is generalized to movement. The verb moves before its object and the phrase expressing the path.

> 34 Questi, l’ orme di cui pestar mi vedi,  
> 35 Tutto che nudo e dipelato vada,  
> 36 Fu di grado maggior che tu non credi.  

He in whose footsteps thou seest me tread, all naked and peeled though he be, was higher in degree than thou believest.

- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “He”: a demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *l’ orme di cui pestar mi vedi* ‘the footprints of whom tread me thou seest’ → “in whose footsteps thou seest me tread”: “whose” replaces the construction with ‘of whom’; the footprints, directly governed by ‘tread’ in Italian, become a prepositional phrase with “in.” The infinitive moves after “seest me,” and the object pronoun follows rather than precedes the finite verb.
- *Tutto che nudo e dipelato vada* ‘although he goes naked and hairless’ → “all naked and peeled though he be”: the adjectives move before the concessive conjunction. The compound conjunction *Tutto che* means ‘although’; “all” adds emphasis to the adjectives in English. ‘Goes’ becomes “be,” retaining the subjunctive, while “peeled” interprets the stripping as loss of skin rather than specifically of hair.
- *di grado maggior* ‘of rank greater’ → “higher in degree”: the description with ‘of’ becomes a predicative adjective qualified by “in degree”; the comparative precedes rather than follows the noun.
- *che tu non credi* ‘than thou dost not believe’ → “than thou believest”: the Italian comparative’s non-negative, pleonastic *non* is omitted.

> 37 Nepote fu della buona Gualdrada:  
> 38 Guidoguerra ebbe nome, ed in sua vita  
> 39 Fece col senno assai e con la spada.  

Grandson of the good Gualdrada, his name was Guidoguerra; and in his lifetime he did much with counsel and with sword.

- *Nepote fu della buona Gualdrada* ‘grandson he was of the good Gualdrada’ → “Grandson of the good Gualdrada”: the finite clause becomes an introductory noun phrase, omitting ‘he was.’
- *Guidoguerra ebbe nome* ‘Guidoguerra he had as name’ → “his name was Guidoguerra”: the construction with ‘had’ becomes a copular clause; “his name” is the subject, and “Guidoguerra” follows the verb as its complement.
- *Fece col senno assai* ‘did with judgment much’ → “he did much with counsel”: “much” moves before the prepositional phrase, and “counsel” renders practical judgment or wisdom as advice.
- *con la spada* ‘with the sword’ → “with sword”: the definite article is omitted.

## Lines 40–51 — Names and an Unfulfilled Embrace

> 40 L’ altro, che appresso me l’ arena trita,  
> 41 E’ Tegghiaio Aldobrandi, la cui voce  
> 42 Nel mondo su dovrebbe esser gradita.  

The other, that treads the sand behind me, is Tegghiaio Aldobrandi, whose fame should be grateful up in the world.

- *appresso me l’ arena trita* ‘near me the sand tramples’ → “treads the sand behind me”: *appresso* is interpreted as following behind rather than simply being nearby; “treads” renders *trita*, literally tramples or wears down. The verb moves before its object, and the phrase locating the action moves to the end.
- *la cui voce* ‘whose voice’ → “whose fame”: “fame” interprets *voce* as reputation rather than literal voice.
- *Nel mondo su* ‘in the world above’ → “up in the world”: “up” moves before the prepositional phrase, and the whole phrase follows the predicate instead of preceding it.
- *dovrebbe esser gradita* ‘should be appreciated’ → “should be grateful”: the passive expression becomes an adjectival one, with “grateful” in its older sense of pleasing or welcome.

> 43 Ed io, che posto son con loro in croce,  
> 44 Iacopo Rusticucci fui; e certo  
> 45 La fiera moglie più ch’ altro mi nuoce.  

And I, who am placed with them in torment, was Jacopo Rusticucci; and certainly, more than aught else, my savage wife injures me.”

- *posto son* ‘placed am’ → “am placed”: the auxiliary precedes the participle instead of following it.
- *in croce* ‘on a cross’ → “in torment”: the figurative meaning replaces the literal expression.
- *Iacopo Rusticucci fui* ‘Jacopo Rusticucci I was’ → “was Jacopo Rusticucci”: the name follows the verb instead of preceding it.
- *La fiera moglie* ‘the savage wife’ → “my savage wife”: the definite article becomes a possessive, making the relationship explicit.
- *più ch’ altro* ‘more than anything else’ → “more than aught else”: the phrase moves before the subject rather than following it.
- *mi nuoce* ‘to me does harm’ → “injures me”: Italian *nuocere* takes an indirect object; English “injures” takes a direct object, placed after the verb.

> 46 S’ io fussi stato dal fuoco coverto,  
> 47 Gittato mi sarei tra lor disotto,  
> 48 E credo che il Dottor l’ avria sofferto.  

Had I been sheltered from the fire, I should have thrown myself amid them below, and I believe my Teacher would have permitted it;

- *S’ io fussi stato* ‘If I had been’ → “Had I been”: the conditional conjunction is omitted, and auxiliary–subject inversion marks the condition instead.
- *dal fuoco coverto* ‘from the fire covered’ → “sheltered from the fire”: “sheltered” expresses the protective sense of *coverto*, and the participle moves before its complement.
- *Gittato mi sarei* ‘thrown myself I should have’ → “I should have thrown myself”: the fronted participle moves after the auxiliary, and the reflexive pronoun follows the participle. The Italian reflexive perfect uses *essere*; English uses “have”.
- *che* ‘that’ → “I believe my Teacher would have permitted it”: the conjunction introducing the clause after “I believe” is omitted.
- *il Dottor* ‘the Teacher’ → “my Teacher”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *l’ avria sofferto* ‘it would have endured’ → “would have permitted it”: *sofferto* is interpreted in the permissive sense of allowing rather than enduring; the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 49 Ma perch’ io mi sarei bruciato e cotto,  
> 50 Vinse paura la mia buona voglia,  
> 51 Che di loro abbracciar mi facea ghiotto.  

but as I should have burnt and baked myself, fear overcame the good will which made me greedy to embrace them.

- *io mi sarei bruciato e cotto* ‘I myself should have burnt and cooked’ → “I should have burnt and baked myself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after both participles, and the broader *cotto* becomes the more specific “baked”. The Italian reflexive perfect uses *essere*, whereas English uses “have”.
- *Vinse paura* ‘overcame fear’ → “fear overcame”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *la mia buona voglia* ‘my good will’ → “the good will”: the possessive is omitted, leaving the definite article.
- *di loro abbracciar* ‘for them embracing’ → “to embrace them”: the prepositional infinitive construction becomes an English infinitive with “to”, and its object follows rather than precedes the verb. The whole complement moves from before *mi facea ghiotto* to after “greedy”.
- *mi facea ghiotto* ‘me made greedy’ → “made me greedy”: the object pronoun follows the verb instead of preceding it.

## Lines 52–63 — Sorrow, Affection, and Onward Journey

> 52 Poi cominciai: Non dispetto, ma doglia  
> 53 La vostra condizion dentro mi fisse  
> 54 Tanto, che tardi tutta si dispoglia,  

Then I began: “Not contempt, but sorrow, your condition fixed within me, so deeply that it will not leave me soon,

- *dentro mi fisse* ‘within me fixed’ → “fixed within me”: the verb moves before the phrase expressing where the sorrow was fixed.
- *Tanto* ‘so much’ → “so deeply”: degree is expressed as depth.
- *che tardi tutta si dispoglia* ‘that late it wholly sheds itself’ → “that it will not leave me soon”: the present reflexive construction becomes a future construction with “leave”; *tardi* is expressed negatively as “not … soon”; “me” is supplied, and *tutta* is not separately rendered.

> 55 Tosto che questo mio Signor mi disse  
> 56 Parole, per le quali io mi pensai,  
> 57 Che, qual voi siete, tal gente venisse.  

when this my Lord spake words to me, by which I felt that such men as you are might be coming.

- *Tosto che* ‘as soon as’ → “when”: the explicit immediacy is omitted.
- *mi disse Parole* ‘to me spoke words’ → “spake words to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb and its direct object.
- *io mi pensai* ‘I thought to myself’ → “I felt”: thinking is rendered as feeling, and the reflexive pronoun is not separately expressed.
- *qual voi siete, tal gente* ‘as you are, such people’ → “such men as you are”: the correlative order is reversed, and the singular collective *gente* becomes the plural, specifically masculine “men”.
- *venisse* ‘was coming’ → “might be coming”: the imperfect subjunctive becomes an explicitly modal English phrase with “might”.

> 58 Di vostra terra sono; e sempre mai  
> 59 L’ ovra di voi e gli onorati nomi  
> 60 Con affezion ritrassi ed ascoltai.  

Of your city am I, and always with affection have I rehearsed and heard your deeds and honoured names.

- *terra* ‘land’ → “city”: Carlyle takes the word in its local sense of a city rather than a country or territory.
- *sempre mai* ‘always ever’ → “always”: the reinforcing *mai* is absorbed into a single adverb.
- *L’ ovra di voi e gli onorati nomi* ‘the work of you and the honoured names’ → “your deeds and honoured names”: singular *ovra* becomes plural “deeds”; the postposed *di voi* becomes the possessive “your”, which governs both nouns; and the object phrase moves after the verbs.
- *ritrassi ed ascoltai* ‘I recounted and listened to’ → “have I rehearsed and heard”: the simple past verbs become present perfects, with the auxiliary preceding the subject; listening is rendered as hearing.

> 61 Lascio lo fele, e vo pei dolci pomi  
> 62 Promessi a me per lo verace Duca;  

I leave the gall, and go for the sweet apples promised me by my veracious Guide.

- *pomi* ‘fruits’ → “apples”: the general term is given a more specific rendering.
- *a me* ‘to me’ → “me”: the indirect object is expressed without its preposition.
- *lo* ‘the’ → “my”: the definite article is replaced by a possessive.

> 63 Ma fino al centro pria convien ch’ io tomi.  

But to the centre it behoves me first to fall.”

- *fino al centro* ‘as far as the centre’ → “to the centre”: the explicit expression of extent in *fino* is omitted.
- *pria convien ch’ io tomi* ‘first it is necessary that I fall’ → “it behoves me first to fall”: the impersonal construction with a finite subjunctive clause becomes “behoves” with an object and an infinitive; “first” moves from before the expression of necessity to immediately before the infinitive.

## Lines 64–78 — Florence’s Pride and Excess

> 64 Se lungamente l’ anima conduca  
> 65 Le membra tue, rispose quegli allora,  
> 66 E se la fama tua dopo te luca,  

“So may the soul long animate thy members,” he then replied, “and so thy fame shine after thee;

- *Se lungamente l’ anima conduca Le membra tue* ‘if long the soul should guide thy members’ → “So may the soul long animate thy members”: the conditional form introducing a wish becomes an English wish with “So may”; *conduca* is rendered as “animate” rather than ‘guide’. “Long” follows the subject instead of preceding it, and “thy” precedes “members” rather than following it.
- *rispose quegli allora* ‘replied that one then’ → “he then replied”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes the personal pronoun “he”; both subject and adverb move before the verb.
- *E se la fama tua dopo te luca* ‘and if thy fame after thee should shine’ → “and so thy fame shine after thee”: the conditional form again becomes an English wish. The possessive precedes its noun, and “after thee” follows the verb instead of preceding it.

> 67 Cortesia e valor, di’, se dimora  
> 68 Nella nostra città sì come suole,  
> 69 O se del tutto se n’ è gito fuora?  

tell, if courtesy and valour abide within our city as they were wont, or have gone quite out of it?

- *Cortesia e valor, di’, se dimora* ‘courtesy and valour, say, if it abides’ → “tell, if courtesy and valour abide”: the subjects, placed before the command in Italian, move into the indirect question. *Di’* becomes “tell” rather than ‘say’, and singular *dimora* becomes plural “abide”, agreeing with the two subjects.
- *Nella nostra città* ‘in our city’ → “within our city”: “within” replaces the simpler preposition ‘in’.
- *sì come suole* ‘so as it is wont’ → “as they were wont”: *sì come* is compressed to “as”; the singular present becomes a plural past.
- *O se del tutto se n’ è gito fuora* ‘or if altogether it has gone away outside’ → “or have gone quite out of it”: the repeated *se* is omitted. The singular verb becomes plural, continuing the agreement with “courtesy and valour”; Italian’s auxiliary *essere* becomes English “have”. *Del tutto* becomes “quite”, and the departure expressed by *se n’ è gito fuora* is rendered as “gone … out of it”, explicitly referring back to the city.

> 70 Chè Guglielmo Borsiere, il qual si duole  
> 71 Con noi per poco, e va là coi compagni,  
> 72 Assai ne cruccia con le sue parole.  

For Guglielmo Borsiere—who has been short time in pain with us, and yonder goes with our companions—greatly torments us with his words.”

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *il qual si duole Con noi per poco* ‘who suffers with us for a little while’ → “who has been short time in pain with us”: the present verb becomes a present-perfect construction with “in pain”. *Per poco* is interpreted as the short time elapsed so far; “time” is supplied, the preposition is omitted, and the duration moves before “with us”.
- *e va là coi compagni* ‘and goes there with the companions’ → “and yonder goes with our companions”: the adverb moves before the verb and becomes “yonder”; the definite article before *compagni* becomes the possessive “our”.
- *Assai ne cruccia* ‘greatly us torments’ → “greatly torments us”: the object pronoun follows the verb rather than preceding it.

> 73 La gente nuova, e i subiti guadagni,  
> 74 Orgoglio e dismisura han generata,  
> 75 Fiorenza, in te, sì che tu già ten piagni.  

“The upstart people and the sudden gains, O Florence, have engendered in thee pride and excess, so that thou already weepest thereat.”

- *gente nuova* ‘people new’ → “upstart people”: “upstart” gives *nuova* a specifically disparaging sense rather than simply rendering it as ‘new’; the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Orgoglio e dismisura han generata, Fiorenza, in te* ‘pride and immoderation have generated, Florence, in thee’ → “O Florence, have engendered in thee pride and excess”: the objects move after the verb, while the vocative moves before it and gains “O”. “In thee” moves before the objects. *Dismisura*, literally a lack of measure or moderation, becomes “excess”.
- *ten piagni* ‘thyself thereof weepest’ → “weepest thereat”: *ten* combines the reflexive *te* with *ne*. The reflexive element has no separate English equivalent; *ne* becomes “thereat” and follows the verb rather than preceding it.

> 76 Così gridai colla faccia levata:  

Thus I cried with face uplifted;

- *colla faccia levata* ‘with the face uplifted’ → “with face uplifted”: the definite article is omitted.

> 77 E i tre, che ciò inteser per risposta,  
> 78 Guatar l’ un l’ altro, come al ver si guata.  

and the three, who understood this as an answer, looked at one another as men look when truth is told.

- *che ciò inteser per risposta* ‘who this understood for an answer’ → “who understood this as an answer”: the object follows the verb rather than preceding it; *per* is rendered as “as”, expressing the capacity in which the words were understood.
- *Guatar l’ un l’ altro* ‘looked the one at the other’ → “looked at one another”: the articulated reciprocal expression becomes the English reciprocal pronoun “one another”, with “at” placed before it.
- *come al ver si guata* ‘as at the truth one looks’ → “as men look when truth is told”: impersonal *si* becomes the plural subject “men”. The prepositional phrase *al ver* becomes a temporal clause, “when truth is told”, supplying both the act of telling and a passive construction; the article before *ver* is not retained.

## Lines 79–93 — A Request for Remembrance and Departure

> 79 Se l’ altre volte sì poco ti costa,  
> 80 Risposer tutti, il soddisfare altrui,  
> 81 Felice te, che sì parli a tua posta!  

“If otherwhile it costs thee so little to satisfy others,” they all replied, “happy thou, who thus speakest at thy will!

- *l’ altre volte* ‘the other times’ → “otherwhile”: the plural noun phrase becomes an adverb.
- *sì poco ti costa* ‘so little costs thee’ → “it costs thee so little”: the adverbial phrase moves after the verb and pronoun, and anticipatory “it” is supplied.
- *Risposer tutti* ‘replied all’ → “they all replied”: the subject moves before the verb, and *tutti* is expanded to “they all”.
- *il soddisfare altrui* ‘the satisfying others’ → “to satisfy others”: the article-marked infinitive becomes an English to-infinitive and moves before the reporting clause.
- *Felice te* ‘happy thee’ → “happy thou”: the emphatic pronoun is rendered in the nominative rather than the objective form.
- *a tua posta* ‘at thy pleasure’ → “at thy will”: the idiom is expressed in terms of the speaker’s will.

> 82 Però, se campi d’ esti luoghi bui,  
> 83 E torni a riveder le belle stelle,  
> 84 Quando ti gioverà dicere: Io fui,  
> 85 Fa che di noi alla gente favelle.  

Therefore, if thou escape out of these gloomy regions, and return to see again the beauteous stars; when thou shalt rejoice to say, ‘I was,’ see that thou speak of us to men.”

- *campi d’* ‘escape from’ → “escape out of”: the preposition is expanded to emphasize emergence.
- *esti luoghi bui* ‘these places dark’ → “these gloomy regions”: “regions” replaces ‘places’, “gloomy” replaces ‘dark’, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *ti gioverà dicere* ‘it will please thee to say’ → “thou shalt rejoice to say”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal one, with the person experiencing pleasure made the subject of “rejoice”.
- *Fa che* ‘make that’ → “see that”: the imperative construction becomes the English idiom for ensuring that something is done.
- *di noi alla gente favelle* ‘of us to the people thou speak’ → “thou speak of us to men”: the verb precedes its complements, and the singular collective noun with its article becomes the bare plural “men”.

> 86 Indi rupper la ruota, ed a fuggirsi  
> 87 Ale sembiaron le lor gambe snelle.  

Then they broke their wheel; and, as they fled, their nimble legs seemed wings.

- *rupper la ruota* ‘broke the wheel’ → “they broke their wheel”: the definite article is replaced by a possessive.
- *a fuggirsi* ‘in fleeing away’ → “as they fled”: the preposition and pronominal infinitive become a finite temporal clause.
- *Ale sembiaron le lor gambe snelle* ‘wings seemed their legs nimble’ → “their nimble legs seemed wings”: the subject moves before the verb, the predicate noun “wings” follows it, and the adjective moves before “legs”.

> 88 Un amen non saria potuto dirsi  
> 89 Tosto così, com’ ei furo spariti:  

An “Amen” could not have been said so quickly as they vanished.

- *non saria potuto dirsi* ‘would not have been able to be said’ → “could not have been said”: the conditional-perfect construction with *potere* is recast using “could” and a perfect passive infinitive.
- *Tosto così, com’* ‘quickly so, as’ → “so quickly as”: the degree word moves before the adverb.
- *ei furo spariti* ‘they had vanished’ → “they vanished”: the Italian past anterior becomes an English simple past.

> 90 Perchè al Maestro parve di partirsi.  

Wherefore it pleased my Master to depart.

- *Perchè* ‘because’ → “Wherefore”: the connective is read consequentially, as ‘for which reason’, rather than causally.
- *al Maestro parve* ‘to the Master it seemed’ → “it pleased my Master”: the impersonal expression of judgment becomes an expression of pleasure; the indirect object becomes a direct object, and “my” replaces the definite article.
- *di partirsi* ‘to take himself away’ → “to depart”: the pronominal verb is rendered by a simple intransitive verb.

> 91 Io lo seguiva, e poco eravam iti,  
> 92 Che il suon dell’ acqua n’ era sì vicino,  
> 93 Che per parlar saremmo appena uditi.  

I followed him; and we had gone but little, when the sound of the water was so near us, that in speaking we should scarce have heard each other.

- *Io lo seguiva* ‘I him was following’ → “I followed him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the imperfect is rendered by the simple past.
- *poco eravam iti* ‘little we had gone’ → “we had gone but little”: the measure of distance moves after the verb, and restrictive “but” is supplied.
- *Che* ‘that’ → “when”: the conjunction introducing the next event is rendered explicitly as temporal.
- *n’ era sì vicino* ‘to us was so near’ → “was so near us”: the pronoun expressing proximity to the speakers moves after “near”.
- *per parlar* ‘for speaking’ → “in speaking”: the preposition is changed, and the infinitive becomes an English gerund.
- *saremmo appena uditi* ‘we should scarcely be heard’ → “we should scarce have heard each other”: the passive conditional becomes an active conditional perfect, with reciprocal “each other” supplied as its object.

## Lines 94–105 — The Roar of Falling Water

> 94 Come quel fiume, ch’ ha proprio cammino  
> 95 Prima da monte Veso in vêr levante,  
> 96 Dalla sinistra costa d’ Apennino,  

As that river—which has a path of its own, first from Monte Viso toward the east, on the left skirt of the Apennine;

- *ha proprio cammino* ‘has its own path’ → “has a path of its own”: the possessive adjective becomes a phrase following the noun, and the indefinite article is supplied.
- *Dalla sinistra costa d’ Apennino* ‘from the left flank of Apennine’ → “on the left skirt of the Apennine”: “on” replaces ‘from’, ‘flank’ becomes “skirt”, and English supplies the article before “Apennine”.

> 97 Che si chiama Acquacheta suso, avante  
> 98 Che si divalli giù nel basso letto,  
> 99 E a Forlì di quel nome è vacante,  

which is called Acquacheta above, ere it descends to its low bed, and is vacant of that name at Forli—

- *si divalli giù* ‘goes down-valley down’ → “it descends”: the specific sense of descending into a valley becomes the more general “descends”; *giù* is absorbed into that verb rather than separately translated.
- *nel basso letto* ‘into the low bed’ → “to its low bed”: “to” replaces ‘into’, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *E a Forlì di quel nome è vacante* ‘and at Forli of that name is vacant’ → “and is vacant of that name at Forli”: the verb and adjective move before their complement, while the place phrase moves to the end.

> 100 Rimbomba là sovra San Benedetto  
> 101 Dall’ alpe, per cadere ad una scesa,  
> 102 Dove dovria per mille esser ricetto;  

resounds from the mountain, there above San Benedetto, in falling at a descent, where for a thousand there should be refuge:

- *là sovra San Benedetto Dall’ alpe* ‘there above San Benedetto from the mountain’ → “from the mountain, there above San Benedetto”: the phrase expressing origin moves before the phrase expressing location.
- *per cadere* ‘to fall’ → “in falling”: the infinitive introduced by *per* becomes an English gerund phrase expressing the circumstance of the resounding, rather than purpose.
- *Dove dovria per mille esser ricetto* ‘where ought for a thousand to be refuge’ → “where for a thousand there should be refuge”: “for a thousand” moves before the modal verb, “should” renders ‘ought to’, and existential “there” is supplied.

> 103 Così, giù d’ una ripa discoscesa,  
> 104 Trovammo risonar quell’ acqua tinta,  
> 105 Sì che in poc’ ora avria l’ orecchia offesa.  

thus down from a steep bank we found that tainted water re-echoing, so that in little time it would have stunned the ear.

- *una ripa discoscesa* ‘a bank precipitous’ → “a steep bank”: the adjective moves before the noun, and ‘precipitous’ is rendered as “steep”.
- *Trovammo risonar quell’ acqua tinta* ‘we found resound that water stained’ → “we found that tainted water re-echoing”: the infinitive becomes an *-ing* participle and moves after the object; the adjective moves before “water”, with ‘stained’ rendered as “tainted”.
- *avria l’ orecchia offesa* ‘would have the ear injured’ → “it would have stunned the ear”: the past participle moves before the object, and ‘injured’ becomes “stunned”, specifying the effect on hearing.

## Lines 106–111 — The Cord Unfastened and Offered

> 106 Io aveva una corda intorno cinta,  

I had a cord girt round me;

- *intorno cinta* ‘around girded’ → “girt round me”: the participle precedes the adverb, and “me” is supplied to specify whom the cord encircles.

> 107 E con essa pensai alcuna volta  
> 108 Prender la lonza alla pelle dipinta.  

and with it I thought some time to catch the Leopard of the painted skin.

- *alla pelle dipinta* ‘with the skin painted’ → “of the painted skin”: the Italian construction identifying a characteristic is rendered with “of”, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 109 Poscia che l’ ebbi tutta da me sciolta,  
> 110 Sì come il Duca m’ avea comandato,  

After I had quite unloosed it from me, as my Guide commanded me,

- *l’ ebbi tutta da me sciolta* ‘it I had all from me unloosed’ → “I had quite unloosed it from me”: the object pronoun and “from me” move after the participle. *Tutta*, grammatically feminine to agree with the cord, is rendered by the adverb “quite”, expressing completeness.
- *Sì come* ‘just as’ → “as”: the emphatic element *Sì* is omitted.
- *il Duca* ‘the Leader’ → “my Guide”: “Guide” replaces ‘Leader’, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *m’ avea comandato* ‘to me had commanded’ → “commanded me”: the past perfect becomes a simple past, and the preceding indirect-object pronoun becomes an object following the verb.

> 111 Porsila a lui aggroppata e ravvolta.  

I held it out to him coiled and wound up.

- *Porsila* ‘I offered it’ → “I held it out”: the single verb is rendered by a phrase describing the physical act of offering; the attached object pronoun becomes a separate word after the verb.
- *aggroppata* ‘knotted’ → “coiled”: the translation interprets the cord as gathered into coils rather than tied into a knot.

## Lines 112–126 — A Signal and an Unbelievable Truth

> 112 Ond’ ei si volse inver lo destro lato,  
> 113 E alquanto di lungi dalla sponda  
> 114 La gittò giuso in quell’ alto burrato.  

Then he bent himself toward the right side, and threw it, some distance from the edge, down into that deep abyss.

- *Ond’* ‘whereupon’ → “Then”: the connective is rendered as a temporal transition.
- *ei si volse* ‘he turned himself’ → “he bent himself”: turning is rendered as bending.
- *alquanto di lungi dalla sponda* ‘somewhat far from the bank’ → “some distance from the edge”: the adverbial expression becomes a noun phrase; *sponda* becomes “edge”; and the phrase moves after the verb and its object.
- *La gittò* ‘it he threw’ → “threw it”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *burrato* ‘precipice’ → “abyss”: the translation names the depth rather than the precipitous drop.

> 115 E pur convien che novità risponda,  
> 116 Dicea fra me medesmo, al nuovo cenno  
> 117 Che il Maestro con l’ occhio sì seconda.  

“Surely,” said I within myself, “something new must answer this new signal, which my Master thus follows with his eye.”

- *E pur* ‘And indeed’ → “Surely”: the conjunction is omitted, while the emphatic adverb becomes an assertion of certainty.
- *convien che novità risponda* ‘it is necessary that novelty answer’ → “something new must answer”: the impersonal construction with a subjunctive becomes a personal construction with “must”; the abstract noun becomes “something new”.
- *Dicea* ‘I was saying’ → “said I”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the reporting clause is placed before the proposition it interrupts in Italian.
- *al nuovo cenno* ‘to the new signal’ → “this new signal”: English “answer” takes a direct object instead of the Italian prepositional complement; the definite article becomes a demonstrative.
- *Che il Maestro con l’ occhio sì seconda* ‘which the Master with the eye thus follows’ → “which my Master thus follows with his eye”: both definite articles become possessives, and the phrase describing the means of following moves after the verb.

> 118 Ahi quanto cauti gli uomini esser denno  
> 119 Presso a color, che non veggon pur l’ opra,  
> 120 Ma per entro i pensier miran col senno!  

Ah! how cautious ought men to be with those who see not only the deed, but with their sense look through into the thoughts!

- *quanto cauti gli uomini esser denno* ‘how cautious the men to be ought’ → “how cautious ought men to be”: the modal moves before the subject and the infinitive follows it; the generic definite article is omitted.
- *Presso a color* ‘near those’ → “with those”: an expression of proximity becomes one of association.
- *non veggon pur l’ opra* ‘do not see only the deed’ → “see not only the deed”: the negation moves after the verb, directly before “only”.
- *per entro i pensier miran* ‘through into the thoughts look’ → “look through into the thoughts”: the verb moves before its directional complement.
- *col senno* ‘with the understanding’ → “with their sense”: the intellectual faculty is called “sense”, the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves before the verb.

> 121 Ei disse a me: Tosto verrà di sopra  
> 122 Ciò ch’ io attendo; e che il tuo pensier sogna,  
> 123 Tosto convien ch’ al tuo viso si scopra.  

He said to me: “What I expect will soon come up; and what thy thought dreams of, soon must be discovered to thy view.”

- *Tosto verrà di sopra Ciò ch’ io attendo* ‘soon will come above that which I await’ → “What I expect will soon come up”: the subject moves before the verb, “soon” moves into the verb phrase, and ‘above’ becomes directional “up”; the demonstrative and relative become “What”, while awaiting becomes expecting.
- *che il tuo pensier sogna* ‘what your thought dreams’ → “what thy thought dreams of”: the directly transitive Italian verb is rendered with the preposition “of”.
- *Tosto convien ch’ al tuo viso si scopra* ‘soon it is necessary that to your sight it uncover itself’ → “soon must be discovered to thy view”: the impersonal necessity construction becomes “must”; the reflexive construction becomes passive; and the complement referring to sight moves after the verb, with *viso* rendered as “view”.

> 124 Sempre a quel ver, ch’ ha faccia di menzogna,  
> 125 De’ l’ uom chiuder le labbra quant’ ei puote,  
> 126 Però che senza colpa fa vergogna;  

Always to that truth which has an air of falsehood, a man should close his lips, if possible; for, though blameless, he incurs reproach.

- *ha faccia di menzogna* ‘has a face of falsehood’ → “has an air of falsehood”: the metaphor of a face becomes the more idiomatic expression “an air”.
- *De’ l’ uom chiuder le labbra* ‘ought the man to close the lips’ → “a man should close his lips”: the subject moves before the modal; the generic definite article becomes indefinite, and the article before the body part becomes a possessive.
- *quant’ ei puote* ‘as much as he can’ → “if possible”: a qualification of degree becomes a condition of possibility, with the personal verb replaced by an elliptical impersonal construction.
- *Però che* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating explanatory conjunction.
- *senza colpa* ‘without fault’ → “though blameless”: the prepositional phrase becomes an explicitly concessive phrase with an adjective.
- *fa vergogna* ‘causes shame’ → “he incurs reproach”: the Italian construction, in which the truth causes shame, is recast with the man as subject and recipient; “reproach” also shifts the emphasis from shame to adverse judgment.

## Lines 127–136 — An Oath and an Ascending Figure

> 127 Ma qui tacer nol posso: e per le note  
> 128 Di questa Commedìa, lettor, ti giuro,  

But here keep silent I cannot; and, Reader, I swear to thee, by the notes of this my Comedy—

- *tacer nol posso* ‘be silent about it I cannot’ → “keep silent I cannot”: *lo*, contracted into *nol*, has no separate equivalent; “keep silent” renders *tacer*.
- *per le note Di questa Commedìa* ‘by the notes of this Comedy’ → “by the notes of this my Comedy”: the phrase moves from before the address and oath to after them; “my” is supplied.
- *lettor, ti giuro* ‘reader, to thee I swear’ → “Reader, I swear to thee”: the indirect object moves after the verb.

> 129 S’ elle non sien di lunga grazia vote,  

so may they not be void of lasting favour—

- *S’ elle non sien di lunga grazia vote* ‘if they not be of long favour empty’ → “so may they not be void of lasting favour”: the conditional subjunctive becomes an explicit wish with “may”; the complement follows “void” rather than preceding it, and “lasting” renders *lunga* as duration rather than merely length.

> 130 Ch’ io vidi per quell’ aer grosso e scuro  
> 131 Venir notando una figura in suso,  
> 132 Meravigliosa ad ogni cor sicuro,  

that I saw, through that air gross and dark, come swimming upwards, a figure marvellous to every stedfast heart;

- *Venir notando una figura in suso* ‘come swimming a figure upwards’ → “come swimming upwards, a figure”: “upwards” moves before the subject of the infinitive.
- *ad ogni cor sicuro* ‘to every heart secure’ → “to every stedfast heart”: *sicuro* is interpreted as firmness or fearlessness and rendered “stedfast”; the adjective moves before its noun.

> 133 Sì come torna colui che va giuso  
> 134 Talvolta a solver l’ áncora, ch’ aggrappa  
> 135 O scoglio, od altro, che nel mare è chiuso,  

like as he returns, who on a time goes down to loose the anchor, which grapples a rock or other thing that in the sea is hid,

- *Sì come torna colui* ‘so as returns that one’ → “like as he returns”: “like as” replaces *sì come*; the demonstrative pronoun becomes “he” and moves before the verb.
- *che va giuso Talvolta* ‘who goes down sometimes’ → “who on a time goes down”: *talvolta* is rendered as an indefinite occasion rather than recurring occasions, and its equivalent moves before the verb.
- *O scoglio, od altro* ‘either rock or something else’ → “a rock or other thing”: the first *o* is omitted, an indefinite article is supplied before “rock,” and the substantival *altro* becomes the explicit noun phrase “other thing.”
- *è chiuso* ‘is enclosed’ → “is hid”: enclosure is interpreted as concealment.

> 136 Che in su si stende, e da piè si rattrappa.  

who spreads the arms and gathers up the feet.

- *in su si stende* ‘upwards stretches himself’ → “spreads the arms”: the reflexive construction becomes a transitive verb with “the arms” supplied as its object; *in su* has no separate equivalent, and the extension is interpreted specifically as a spreading of the arms.
- *da piè si rattrappa* ‘at the foot draws himself in’ → “gathers up the feet”: the reflexive construction becomes transitive, with the bodily part made the direct object; the singular *piè*, used adverbially, becomes plural “feet.”

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Syntax

Carlyle keeps much of the Italian’s vocabulary, imagery, and rhetorical shape, but regularly rearranges its syntax for English prose. Subjects and objects placed after or before their usual positions in Italian move into more customary English order: *Ricominciar … ei* becomes “They recommenced,” and *Vinse paura* becomes “fear overcame.” Adjectives generally move before their nouns, and separated phrases are brought together. Reporting clauses also shift position. Yet the translation retains some conspicuous inversions and unusual expressions, including “Of your city am I,” “their ancient wail,” and “vacant of that name.”

Changes of construction often accompany this reordering. Pronominal *partirsi da* becomes transitive “quitted”; *si vuole esser cortese*, ‘one must be courteous,’ becomes “courtesy is due”; and *Faceva … continuo viaggio*, ‘made a continuous journey,’ becomes “kept travelling.” Impersonal expressions of necessity become constructions with “must” or “behoves,” while wishes introduced by *se* become “So may” or “so.” Italian imperfects frequently appear as English simple pasts, and some compound past tenses are simplified. A more substantial recasting occurs in *saremmo appena uditi*, ‘we should scarcely be heard,’ rendered as “we should scarce have heard each other.”

Word choice ranges from close correspondence to interpretation and specification. “gall,” “sweet apples,” and “greedy to embrace them” preserve striking features of the Italian wording, though “apples” narrows the broader *pomi*, ‘fruits.’ Elsewhere *voce*, ‘voice,’ becomes “fame,” *in croce*, ‘on a cross,’ becomes “in torment,” and *gente nuova*, ‘new people,’ becomes “upstart people.” Conversely, *freghi*, ‘rubbest,’ is generalized to “movest.” Such choices sometimes make an implied meaning explicit and sometimes replace a concrete expression with its understood sense.

Small omissions and additions recur throughout. Reinforcing words may disappear, as when *sempre mai* becomes “always” and *Tosto che*, ‘as soon as,’ becomes “when”; reflexive elements often receive no separate equivalent. English supplies explanatory wording such as “a direction,” and the final “spreads the arms” names a bodily part left implicit in *in su si stende*. Definite articles frequently become possessives, as in *il viso*, “his face,” and *il Dottor*, “my Teacher.” Beyond the individual changes recorded in the comparison, English regularly supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits Italian articles before possessives. These routine adjustments help produce readable English while leaving much of the original’s verbal texture intact.
