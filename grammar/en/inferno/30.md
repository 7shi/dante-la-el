# Inferno 30

## Lines 1–11 — Athamas’s Madness

> 1 Nel tempo che Giunone era crucciata  
> 2 Per Semele contra il sangue Tebano,  
> 3 Come mostrò già una ed altra fiata,  

At the time that Juno was incensed for Semele against the Theban blood, as she already more than once had shewn,

- *Nel tempo che* ‘In the time that’ → “At the time that”: “At” replaces ‘In’.
- *sangue Tebano* ‘blood Theban’ → “Theban blood”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *mostrò già una ed altra fiata* ‘she showed already one and another time’ → “she already more than once had shewn”: the simple past becomes past perfect; ‘one and another time’ becomes “more than once”; and both adverbial expressions precede the verb.

> 4 Atamante divenne tanto insano,  
> 5 Che veggendo la moglie co’ duo figli  
> 6 Venir carcata di ciascuna mano,  

Athamas grew so insane, that he, seeing his wife, with the two sons, come laden on either hand,

- *divenne* ‘became’ → “grew”: the change of state is expressed with “grew” rather than ‘became’.
- *la moglie* ‘the wife’ → “his wife”: the definite article is replaced by a possessive.
- *di ciascuna mano* ‘in each hand’ → “on either hand”: the Italian locative construction becomes “on”; “either” renders ‘each’ with reference to the two hands.

> 7 Gridò: Tendiam le reti, sì ch’ io pigli  
> 8 La lionessa e i lioncini al varco;  

cried: “Spread we the nets, that I may take the lioness and her young lions at the pass;”

- *Tendiam* ‘Let us stretch’ → “Spread we”: ‘stretch’ becomes “Spread”, and the first-person plural exhortation is expressed by an archaic English construction with the subject following the verb.
- *sì ch’ io pigli* ‘so that I may take’ → “that I may take”: ‘so’ is not separately represented; the Italian subjunctive is expressed with “may”.
- *i lioncini* ‘the little lions’ → “her young lions”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the diminutive suffix is rendered by the separate adjective “young”.

> 9 E poi distese i dispietati artigli,  

and then stretched out his pitiless talons,

- *i dispietati artigli* ‘the pitiless talons’ → “his pitiless talons”: the definite article is replaced by a possessive.

> 10 Prendendo l’ un che avea nome Learco,  

grasping the one who had the name Learchus;

- *Prendendo* ‘Taking’ → “grasping”: the translation chooses a verb that more specifically expresses a firm hold.
- *avea nome Learco* ‘had name Learchus’ → “had the name Learchus”: English supplies the definite article before “name”.

> 11 E rotollo, e percosselo ad un sasso;  

and whirled him, and dashed him on a rock:

- *percosselo* ‘struck him’ → “dashed him”: “dashed” expresses the blow as a forceful collision.
- *ad un sasso* ‘against a rock’ → “on a rock”: the preposition marking the object struck is rendered by “on” rather than ‘against’.

## Lines 12–21 — Drowning and Hecuba’s Madness

> 12 E quella s’ annegò con l’ altro incarco.  

and she with her other burden drowned herself.

- *quella* ‘that woman’ → “she”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *s’ annegò* ‘herself drowned’ → “drowned herself”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.
- *con l’ altro incarco* ‘with the other burden’ → “with her other burden”: the phrase moves before the verb, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 13 E quando la fortuna volse in basso  
> 14 L’ altezza de’ Troian, che tutto ardiva,  
> 15 Sì che insieme col regno il Re fu casso;  

And when Fortune brought low the all-daring pride of the Trojans, so that the King together with his kingdom was blotted out;

- *volse in basso* ‘turned downward’ → “brought low”: the verb and directional phrase become an English idiom of abasement.
- *L’ altezza de’ Troian, che tutto ardiva* ‘the height of the Trojans, which dared everything’ → “the all-daring pride of the Trojans”: “pride” interprets the figurative *altezza*; the relative clause becomes a compound adjective placed before the noun.
- *insieme col regno il Re* ‘together with the kingdom the King’ → “the King together with his kingdom”: the accompanying phrase moves after the subject, and “his” replaces the definite article.
- *fu casso* ‘was annulled’ → “was blotted out”: the adjective becomes an English phrasal passive.

> 16 Ecuba trista, misera e cattiva,  
> 17 Poscia che vide Polisena morta,  
> 18 E del suo Polidoro, in su la riva  
> 19 Del mar, si fu la dolorosa accorta,  

Hecuba, sad, miserable, and captive, after she had seen Polyxena her daughter slain, and on the sea-strand, forlorn, discerned the mangled body of her Polydorus:

- *vide* ‘saw’ → “had seen”: the simple past becomes a past perfect.
- *Polisena morta* ‘Polyxena dead’ → “Polyxena her daughter slain”: “her daughter” is supplied, and “slain” specifies a violent death rather than simply the state of being dead.
- *del suo Polidoro … si fu … accorta* ‘of her Polydorus … had become aware’ → “discerned the mangled body of her Polydorus”: the reflexive construction with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object; the complement moves after the verb. “the mangled body” is supplied, making Polydorus the possessor of the object perceived rather than naming him directly as that object. The English shares “had” with “had seen”.
- *in su la riva / Del mar* ‘upon the shore of the sea’ → “on the sea-strand”: the noun and its following prepositional modifier become a compound noun.
- *la dolorosa* ‘the sorrowful woman’ → “forlorn”: the substantivized adjective, which restates the subject, becomes a descriptive adjective and moves before the verb; “forlorn” also gives sorrow a shade of desolation.

> 20 Forsennata latrò sì come cane;  

she, out of her senses, barked like a dog;

- *Forsennata* ‘distraught’ → “she, out of her senses”: the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase, and “she” resumes the already named subject after the intervening clauses.
- *sì come cane* ‘so as dog’ → “like a dog”: the comparative construction becomes “like”, with no separate equivalent for *sì*; English supplies the indefinite article.

> 21 Tanto dolor le fe’ la mente torta.  

to such a degree had sorrow wrung her soul.

- *Tanto dolor le fe’ la mente torta* ‘so much sorrow made her mind twisted’ → “to such a degree had sorrow wrung her soul”: the quantity expressed by *tanto*, modifying *dolor*, becomes a fronted phrase expressing the degree of the action. The simple-past construction ‘made … twisted’ becomes the past-perfect verb “had … wrung”, with auxiliary–subject inversion. *Mente* becomes “soul” rather than ‘mind’, and the affected-person pronoun *le* is expressed through the possessive “her”.

## Lines 22–33 — Two Shadows Bite and Mangle

> 22 Ma nè di Tebe furie, nè Troiane  
> 23 Si vider mai in alcun tanto crude,  
> 24 Non punger bestie, non che membra umane,  

But neither Theban Furies nor Trojan were ever seen in aught so cruel—not in stinging brutes, and much less human limbs;

- *di Tebe furie* ‘of Thebes furies’ → “Theban Furies”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adjective, and the modifier–noun order is reversed.
- *Si vider mai* ‘were seen ever’ → “were ever seen”: the Italian passive construction with *si* becomes an English passive with “were”; the adverb moves before the participle.
- *in alcun* ‘in anyone’ → “in aught”: the indefinite pronoun is interpreted as referring to anything rather than anyone.
- *Non punger bestie* ‘not to sting beasts’ → “not in stinging brutes”: the infinitive becomes a gerund governed by the supplied preposition “in”; “brutes” replaces ‘beasts’.
- *non che* ‘not to say’ → “and much less”: the Italian expression becomes an English phrase marking the stronger exclusion, with “and” supplied.

> 25 Quant’ io vidi due ombre smorte e nude,  
> 26 Che mordendo correvan di quel modo  
> 27 Che il porco, quando del porcil si schiude.  

as I saw two shadows, pale and naked, which ran biting in the manner that a hungry swine does when he is thrust out from his sty.

- *smorte* ‘deathly pale’ → “pale”: the translation reduces the force of the adjective.
- *mordendo correvan* ‘biting ran’ → “ran biting”: the gerund moves after the finite verb.
- *di quel modo Che* ‘in that manner that’ → “in the manner that”: the demonstrative becomes the definite article.
- *il porco* ‘the swine’ → “a hungry swine does”: the generic definite article becomes indefinite; “hungry” is added, and “does” supplies a verb standing for the running already mentioned.
- *del porcil* ‘from the sty’ → “from his sty”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves after the verb.
- *si schiude* ‘gets out’ → “he is thrust out”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes a passive construction suggesting that an external force drives the animal out.

> 28 L’ una giunse a Capocchio, ed in sul nodo  
> 29 Del collo l’ assannò, sì che tirando  
> 30 Grattar gli fece il ventre al fondo sodo.  

The one came to Capocchio, and fixed its tusks on his neck-joint, so that, dragging him, it made the solid bottom claw his belly.

- *giunse a Capocchio* ‘reached Capocchio’ → “came to Capocchio”: arrival at the destination is expressed as movement towards it.
- *in sul nodo Del collo l’assannò* ‘on the knot of the neck it seized him with its fangs’ → “fixed its tusks on his neck-joint”: the verb is expanded into a phrase with “tusks” as its object; the Italian object pronoun becomes the possessive “his”. The location moves after the verb, and ‘knot of the neck’ becomes the compound “neck-joint”.
- *tirando* ‘dragging’ → “dragging him”: the object is supplied.
- *Grattar gli fece il ventre al fondo sodo* ‘it made him scrape his belly on the hard bottom’ → “it made the solid bottom claw his belly”: the translation takes the bottom as the agent doing the scraping rather than the surface against which the belly scrapes. “Claw” replaces ‘scrape’, and “solid” renders ‘hard’.

> 31 E l’ Aretin, che rimase tremando,  
> 32 Mi disse: Quel folletto è Gianni Schicchi,  
> 33 E va rabbioso altrui così conciando.  

And the Aretine, who remained trembling, said to me: “That goblin is Gianni Schicchi; and, rabid, he goes thus mangling others.”

- *Mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *va rabbioso* ‘goes rabid’ → “rabid, he goes”: the adjective moves before the verb, and the subject implicit in the Italian verb ending is expressed.
- *altrui così conciando* ‘others thus treating’ → “thus mangling others”: the object moves after the gerund; “mangling” makes explicit the damaging treatment conveyed contextually by *conciando*.

## Lines 34–48 — The Two Furious Spirits

> 34 Oh, diss’ io lui, se l’ altro non ti ficchi  
> 35 Li denti addosso, non ti sia fatica  
> 36 A dir chi è, pria che di qui si spicchi.  

“Oh!” said I to him, “so may the other not plant its teeth on thee, be pleased to tell us who it is, ere it snatch itself away.”

- *se l’ altro non ti ficchi Li denti addosso* ‘if the other should not stick the teeth onto thee’ → “so may the other not plant its teeth on thee”: the conditional wording becomes an explicit wish with “so may”; “plant” renders *ficchi*, the definite article becomes the possessive “its”, and the reference to the person bitten follows the teeth rather than preceding the verb.
- *non ti sia fatica* ‘may it not be toil to thee’ → “be pleased”: a negative, impersonal request becomes a positive imperative addressed directly to the hearer.
- *A dir chi è* ‘to say who it is’ → “to tell us who it is”: “tell” replaces ‘say’, and “us” supplies a recipient not expressed in the Italian.
- *di qui si spicchi* ‘from here it detach itself’ → “it snatch itself away”: “snatch” gives the separation a more abrupt force; “away” follows the verb and replaces ‘from here’, leaving the explicit reference to this place unexpressed.

> 37 Ed egli a me: Quell’ è l’ anima antica  
> 38 Di Mirra scellerata, che divenne  
> 39 Al padre fuor del dritto amore amica.  

And he to me: “That is the ancient spirit of flagitious Myrrha, who loved her father with more than rightful love.

- *l’ anima antica* ‘the soul ancient’ → “the ancient spirit”: “spirit” replaces ‘soul’, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *Mirra scellerata* ‘Myrrha wicked’ → “flagitious Myrrha”: the adjective moves before the name.
- *che divenne Al padre fuor del dritto amore amica* ‘who became to her father, outside rightful love, a friend’ → “who loved her father with more than rightful love”: *amica* is understood in its sexual sense, and becoming a lover is recast as the verb “loved”, losing the explicit change of state. The father becomes the verb’s direct object, with “her” supplied; ‘outside rightful love’ becomes “with more than rightful love”, expressing excess rather than departure from a proper limit.

> 40 Questa a peccar con esso così venne,  
> 41 Falsificando sè in altrui forma,  

She came to sin with him disguised in alien form;

- *Questa* ‘this one’ → “She”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *a peccar con esso così venne* ‘to sin with him thus came’ → “came to sin with him”: the finite verb moves before the infinitive phrase, and *così*, which anticipates the following comparison, has no separate rendering.
- *Falsificando sè* ‘falsifying herself’ → “disguised”: an active gerund with a reflexive object becomes a past participle describing her condition; the falsification is interpreted as disguise.
- *in altrui forma* ‘in another’s form’ → “in alien form”: the possessive expression becomes an adjective describing the form as other than her own.

> 42 Come l’ altro, che in là sen va, sostenne,  
> 43 Per guadagnar la donna della torma,  
> 44 Falsificare in sè Buoso Donati,  
> 45 Testando, e dando al testamento norma.  

even as the other who there is going away, undertook, that he might gain the Lady of the troop, to disguise himself as Buoso Donati, making a testament and giving to it legal form.”

- *Come* ‘as’ → “even as”: “even” adds emphasis to the comparison.
- *che in là sen va* ‘who goes away towards there’ → “who there is going away”: the directional expression becomes “there”, and the Italian present becomes an English present progressive.
- *sostenne* ‘bore’ → “undertook”: the verb of bearing or enduring is interpreted as taking an action upon oneself.
- *Per guadagnar la donna della torma* ‘to gain the lady of the troop’ → “that he might gain the Lady of the troop”: an infinitival purpose phrase becomes a finite clause with “might”.
- *Falsificare in sè Buoso Donati* ‘to counterfeit in himself Buoso Donati’ → “to disguise himself as Buoso Donati”: Buoso Donati, the Italian verb’s direct object, becomes the complement of “as”; *in sè* becomes the reflexive direct object “himself”.
- *al testamento* ‘to the testament’ → “to it”: a pronoun replaces the repeated noun.
- *norma* ‘rule’ → “legal form”: the noun is interpreted as prescribed form, with “legal” making the nature of that form explicit.

> 46 E poi che i duo rabbiosi fur passati,  
> 47 Sovra i quali io avea l’ occhio tenuto,  
> 48 Rivolsilo a guardar gli altri mal nati.  

And when the furious two, on whom I had kept my eye, were passed, I turned it to observe the other ill-born spirits.

- *poi che* ‘after’ → “when”: the explicit indication of succession becomes a more general temporal conjunction.
- *i duo rabbiosi fur passati* ‘the two raging ones were passed’ → “the furious two” and “were passed”: the substantival adjective becomes “furious” modifying “two”, reversing their order. The predicate is postponed until after the relative clause, whereas it precedes that clause in Italian.
- *Sovra i quali io avea l’ occhio tenuto* ‘on whom I had the eye kept’ → “on whom I had kept my eye”: the participle moves before its object, and “my” replaces the definite article.
- *Rivolsilo* ‘I turned it back’ → “I turned it”: the explicit sense of turning back is not separately represented.
- *gli altri mal nati* ‘the other ill-born’ → “the other ill-born spirits”: “spirits” supplies a noun for the Italian substantival expression.

## Lines 49–63 — Master Adam’s Misery and Thirst

> 49 Io vidi un fatto a guisa di liuto,  
> 50 Pur ch’ egli avesse avuta l’ anguinaia  
> 51 Tronca dal lato che l’ uomo ha forcuto.  

I saw one shapen like a lute, if he had only had his groin cut short at the part where man is forked.

- *fatto* ‘made’ → “shapen”: making is rendered specifically as shaping.
- *a guisa di* ‘in the manner of’ → “like”: the phrase becomes a simple comparison.
- *Pur ch’ egli avesse avuta* ‘provided that he had had’ → “if he had only had”: the proviso is expressed through “if” and “only”, with “only” placed within the verb phrase.
- *l’ anguinaia* ‘the groin’ → “his groin”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Tronca* ‘cut off’ → “cut short”: complete severance is expressed as shortening.
- *dal lato che l’ uomo ha forcuto* ‘on the side that man has forked’ → “at the part where man is forked”: ‘side’ becomes “part”; the relative clause describing the part that man has becomes a clause describing where man himself is forked. The generic definite article before *uomo* is omitted.

> 52 La grave idropisia, che sì dispaia  
> 53 Le membra con l’ umor che mal converte,  
> 54 Che il viso non risponde alla ventraia,  

The heavy dropsy, which with its ill-digested humour so disproportions the limbs, that the visage corresponds not to the paunch,

- *con l’ umor che mal converte* ‘with the humour that it badly converts’ → “with its ill-digested humour”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the active relative clause becomes a passive participial adjective before “humour”. “Ill-digested” interprets the conversion as bodily assimilation. The whole phrase moves before “so disproportions the limbs”.
- *non risponde* ‘does not answer’ → “corresponds not”: ‘answer’ is rendered in its sense of correspondence or proportion, and the negative follows rather than precedes the verb.

> 55 Faceva lui tener le labbra aperte,  
> 56 Come l’ etico fa, che per la sete  
> 57 L’ un verso il mento, e l’ altro in su riverte.  

made him hold his lips apart, as does the hectic patient, who for thirst curls the one lip towards the chin, and the other upwards.

- *le labbra aperte* ‘the lips open’ → “his lips apart”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the lips’ being open is expressed as their being apart.
- *Come l’ etico fa* ‘as the consumptive does’ → “as does the hectic patient”: the substantivized adjective becomes an adjective modifying the supplied noun “patient”; the verb moves before its subject.
- *per la sete* ‘for the thirst’ → “for thirst”: the definite article is omitted.
- *L’ un* ‘the one’ → “the one lip”: the noun understood from the preceding reference to the lips is supplied.
- *riverte* ‘turns back’ → “curls”: the turning is rendered more specifically as curling, and the verb moves from the end of the relative clause to precede its objects.

> 58 O voi, che senza alcuna pena siete,  
> 59 E non so io perchè, nel mondo gramo,  
> 60 Diss’ egli a noi, guardate e attendete  
> 61 Alla miseria del maestro Adamo:  

“O ye! who are exempt from every punishment, and why I know not, in this grim world,” said he to us, “look and attend to the misery of Master Adam.

- *senza alcuna pena siete* ‘without any punishment are’ → “are exempt from every punishment”: the verb moves before its complement; ‘without’ becomes “exempt from”, and ‘any’ becomes “every”.
- *E non so io perchè* ‘and I do not know why’ → “and why I know not”: “why” moves before the subject and verb; the explicit Italian subject, which follows the verb, precedes it in English, while the negative moves after it.
- *nel mondo gramo* ‘in the world wretched’ → “in this grim world”: the definite article becomes the demonstrative “this”; ‘wretched’ becomes “grim”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *del maestro Adamo* ‘of the master Adam’ → “of Master Adam”: the definite article before the title is omitted.

> 62 Io ebbi vivo assai di quel ch’ io volli,  
> 63 E ora, lasso! un gocciol d’ acqua bramo.  

When alive, I had enough of what I wished; and now, alas! I crave one little drop of water.

- *vivo* ‘alive’ → “When alive”: “When” makes the temporal relationship explicit, and the phrase moves before “I had”.
- *assai* ‘much’ → “enough”: the translation chooses sufficiency rather than simply abundance.
- *di quel ch’* ‘of that which’ → “of what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun are combined into “what”.
- *lasso* ‘wretched’ → “alas”: an adjective used exclamatorily of the speaker becomes an interjection.
- *un gocciol d’ acqua bramo* ‘a little drop of water I crave’ → “I crave one little drop of water”: the verb moves before its object, and *un* is rendered as “one” rather than ‘a’.

## Lines 64–75 — Tormenting Memories of Romena

> 64 Li ruscelletti, che de’ verdi colli  
> 65 Del Casentin discendon giuso in Arno,  
> 66 Facendo i lor canali e freddi e molli,  

The rivulets that from the verdant hills of Casentino descend into the Arno, making their channels cool and moist,

- *verdi* ‘green’ → “verdant”: a more literary adjective replaces the ordinary colour word.
- *discendon giuso in Arno* ‘descend down into Arno’ → “descend into the Arno”: the reinforcing *giuso* (‘down’) is omitted, and English supplies an article before the river’s name.
- *e freddi e molli* ‘both cold and moist’ → “cool and moist”: the first element of the paired *e … e* construction is omitted, and “cool” moderates ‘cold’.

> 67 Sempre mi stanno innanzi, e non indarno;  

stand constantly before me, and not in vain;

- *Sempre mi stanno innanzi* ‘always they stand before me’ → “stand constantly before me”: *sempre* becomes “constantly” and moves after the verb; the preceding pronoun *mi* becomes “me” after “before”.

> 68 Chè l’ imagine lor via più m’ asciuga,  
> 69 Che il male, ond’ io nel volto mi discarno.  

for the image of them dries me up far more than the disease which from my visage wears the flesh.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *l’ imagine lor* ‘their image’ → “the image of them”: the possessive *lor* is expressed through an “of” phrase.
- *via più m’ asciuga* ‘much more dries me’ → “dries me up far more”: the intensifying comparative moves after the verb and its object, the object pronoun moves after the verb, and “up” is supplied.
- *il male* ‘the ill’ → “the disease”: the broader noun is rendered specifically as a bodily disease.
- *ond’ io nel volto mi discarno* ‘through which I lose flesh in my face’ → “which from my visage wears the flesh”: the instrumental relative becomes the subject of “wears”, making the disease, rather than *io*, the grammatical agent. The pronominal *mi discarno* is recast as a transitive verb with “the flesh” as its object; location ‘in my face’ becomes source “from my visage”, placed before the verb.

> 70 La rigida giustizia, che mi fruga,  
> 71 Tragge cagion del luogo, ov’ io peccai,  
> 72 A metter più gli miei sospiri in fuga.  

The rigid Justice, which searches me, takes occasion from the place at which I sinned, to give my sighs a quicker flight.

- *che mi fruga* ‘which prods me’ → “which searches me”: Carlyle takes *fruga* in its searching or probing sense rather than its sense of prodding or tormenting.
- *Tragge cagion* ‘draws occasion’ → “takes occasion”: English substitutes its idiomatic verb for the Italian metaphor of drawing.
- *ov’ io peccai* ‘where I sinned’ → “at which I sinned”: the relative adverb becomes a preposition followed by a relative pronoun.
- *A metter più gli miei sospiri in fuga* ‘to put my sighs more to flight’ → “to give my sighs a quicker flight”: ‘put to flight’ becomes “give … a … flight”; the adverb *più* is interpreted as increased speed and recast as the adjective “quicker”, modifying “flight”.

> 73 Ivi è Romena, là dov’ io falsai  
> 74 La lega suggellata del Batista,  

There is Romena where I falsified the alloy, sealed with the Baptist’s image;

- *là dov’ io falsai* ‘there where I falsified’ → “where I falsified”: the reinforcing *là* (‘there’) is omitted.
- *suggellata del Batista* ‘sealed with the Baptist’ → “sealed with the Baptist’s image”: “image” is supplied to make the implied representation explicit, and the Baptist becomes its grammatical possessor.

> 75 Per ch’ io il corpo suso arso lasciai.  

for which on earth I left my body burnt.

- *io il corpo suso arso lasciai* ‘I the body up above burnt left’ → “on earth I left my body burnt”: *suso* (‘up above’) is interpreted as “on earth” and moved to the beginning of the clause. “My” replaces the definite article before *corpo*, and the verb moves before its object and the predicative “burnt”.

## Lines 76–90 — A Sight Worth More Than Water

> 76 Ma s’ io vedessi qui l’ anima trista  
> 77 Di Guido, o d’ Alessandro, o di lor frate,  
> 78 Per fonte Branda non darei la vista.  

But if I could see the miserable soul of Guido here, or of Alessandro, or their brother, for Branda’s fount I would not give the sight.

- *s’ io vedessi* ‘if I saw’ → “if I could see”: the Italian subjunctive becomes a modal construction expressing possibility.
- *qui l’ anima trista Di Guido* ‘here the sad soul of Guido’ → “the miserable soul of Guido here”: “here” moves after the noun phrase, the adjective moves before “soul”, and *trista* receives the stronger rendering “miserable”.
- *o di lor frate* ‘or of their brother’ → “or their brother”: the repeated preposition *di* is omitted.
- *Per fonte Branda* ‘for fount Branda’ → “for Branda’s fount”: the name becomes a possessive preceding “fount”.
- *non darei la vista* ‘not would I give the sight’ → “I would not give the sight”: the negative moves between the English auxiliary and main verb.

> 79 Dentro c’è l’ una già, se l’ arrabbiate  
> 80 Ombre, che vanno intorno, dicon vero:  

One is in already, if the mad shadows that are going round speak true.

- *Dentro c’è l’ una già* ‘inside there is the one already’ → “One is in already”: the existential construction becomes a statement with “One” as its subject; the definite article disappears, and the initial location follows the verb.
- *l’ arrabbiate Ombre* ‘the enraged shadows’ → “the mad shadows”: “mad” renders the adjective in terms of frenzy rather than explicitly of anger.
- *che vanno intorno* ‘that go round’ → “that are going round”: the simple present becomes the English progressive.
- *dicon vero* ‘say true’ → “speak true”: “speak” replaces ‘say’.

> 81 Ma che mi val, ch’ ho le membra legate?  

But what avails it me whose limbs are tied?

- *Ma che mi val* ‘But what to me avails’ → “But what avails it me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb and its English subject.
- *ch’ ho le membra legate* ‘who have the limbs bound’ → “whose limbs are tied”: a relative clause describing what the speaker has becomes a possessive relative clause with “limbs” as its subject and a passive predicate.

> 82 S’ io fossi pur di tanto ancor leggiero,  
> 83 Ch’ io potessi in cent’ anni andare un’ oncia,  
> 84 Io sarei messo già per lo sentiero,  

Were I only still so light, that I could move one inch in a hundred years, I had already put myself upon the road,

- *S’ io fossi* ‘If I were’ → “Were I”: conditional inversion replaces the explicit conjunction.
- *pur di tanto ancor leggiero* ‘only to such a degree still light’ → “only still so light”: the degree phrase is compressed into “so” and placed after “still”.
- *in cent’ anni andare un’ oncia* ‘in a hundred years go an inch’ → “move one inch in a hundred years”: the time phrase moves after the distance, and “move” replaces ‘go’.
- *Io sarei messo già* ‘I would be put already’ → “I had already put myself”: the Italian conditional construction becomes an English pluperfect used conditionally; “put” is made explicitly reflexive, and “already” moves before the participle.
- *per lo sentiero* ‘along the path’ → “upon the road”: “upon” replaces the directional ‘along’, and “road” replaces ‘path’.

> 85 Cercando lui tra questa gente sconcia,  
> 86 Con tutto ch’ ella volge undici miglia,  
> 87 E men d’ un mezzo di traverso non ci ha.  

to seek him among this disfigured people, though it winds round eleven miles, and is not less than half a mile across.

- *Cercando lui* ‘seeking him’ → “to seek him”: the gerund becomes an infinitive expressing purpose.
- *questa gente sconcia* ‘this people disfigured’ → “this disfigured people”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Con tutto ch’* ‘with all that’ → “though”: the Italian concessive phrase becomes a single conjunction.
- *ella volge undici miglia* ‘it turns eleven miles’ → “it winds round eleven miles”: “winds round” expands the verb’s expression of circular extension.
- *E men d’ un mezzo di traverso non ci ha* ‘and less than a half across there is not’ → “and is not less than half a mile across”: the existential construction becomes a predicate continuing the preceding “it”; the negative precedes the comparison, “across” moves to the end, and “mile” is supplied.

> 88 Io son per lor tra sì fatta famiglia:  

Through them am I in such a crew:

- *Io son per lor* ‘I am through them’ → “Through them am I”: the causal phrase moves to the front, followed by inversion of subject and verb.
- *tra sì fatta famiglia* ‘among a family of such a kind’ → “in such a crew”: “in” replaces ‘among’, the description of kind is compressed into “such”, and “crew” replaces ‘family’.

> 89 Ei m’ indussero a battere i fiorini,  
> 90 Ch’ avevan tre carati di mondiglia.  

they induced me to stamp the florens that had three carats of alloy.”

- *Ei m’ indussero* ‘they me induced’ → “they induced me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *battere* ‘beat’ → “stamp”: the translation uses the verb’s specific minting sense.
- *di mondiglia* ‘of refuse’ → “of alloy”: the translation identifies the material specifically as an admixture of metal rather than using the Italian’s derogatory term.

## Lines 91–99 — The Two Fevered Falsifiers

> 91 Ed io a lui: Chi son li duo tapini,  
> 92 Che fuman come man bagnata il verno,  
> 93 Giacendo stretti a’ tuoi destri confini?  

And I to him: “Who are the abject two, lying close to thy right confines, and smoking like a hand bathed in winter-time?”

- *li duo tapini* ‘the two wretches’ → “the abject two”: the noun meaning wretches becomes the adjective “abject”, while “two” takes the place of the noun.
- *Che fuman … Giacendo* ‘who smoke … lying’ → “lying” … “and smoking”: the relative clause becomes a participial phrase coordinated with “lying”, and the two descriptions change order.
- *stretti* ‘pressed together’ → “close”: a plural adjective describing the two becomes an adverb modifying “lying”.
- *man bagnata* ‘hand wetted’ → “a hand bathed”: an indefinite article is supplied, and “bathed” renders the more general ‘wetted’.
- *il verno* ‘the winter’ → “in winter-time”: the Italian temporal expression becomes an English prepositional phrase, with ‘winter’ expanded to “winter-time”.

> 94 Qui li trovai, e poi volta non dierno,  
> 95 Rispose, quando piovvi in questo greppo,  
> 96 E non credo che dieno in sempiterno.  

“Here I found them, when I rained into this pinfold,” he answered; “and since then they have not given a turn, and may not give, I think, to all eternity.

- *li trovai* ‘them I found’ → “I found them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *e poi* ‘and then’ → “and since then”: “since” makes explicit the continuing interval from the earlier event.
- *volta non dierno* ‘a turn they did not give’ → “they have not given a turn”: the object moves after the verb, an indefinite article is supplied, and the simple past becomes a present perfect.
- *Rispose, quando* ‘he answered, when’ → “when” … “he answered”: the temporal clause moves before the reporting clause and before the statement that they have not turned.
- *greppo* ‘craggy slope’ → “pinfold”: a word for rough, rocky terrain is rendered as an enclosure.
- *E non credo che dieno* ‘and I do not believe that they give’ → “and may not give, I think”: the governing clause becomes a parenthetical “I think”; its negative moves to “give”, and the subordinate subjunctive is represented by “may”.
- *in sempiterno* ‘in eternity’ → “to all eternity”: the preposition changes, and “all” is supplied to express unending duration.

> 97 L’ una è la falsa, che accusò Giuseppo;  

One is the false wife who accused Joseph;

- *L’una* ‘the one’ → “One”: the definite article is omitted.
- *la falsa* ‘the false woman’ → “the false wife”: the substantivized feminine adjective receives an explicit noun, “wife”, specifying a relationship not stated in the Italian phrase.

> 98 L’ altro è il falso Sinon Greco da Troia:  

the other is false Sinon, the Greek from Troy.

- *il falso Sinon* ‘the false Sinon’ → “false Sinon”: the definite article before the qualified name is omitted.
- *Greco* ‘Greek’ → “the Greek”: an article is supplied, making the national designation an explicit noun phrase in apposition.

> 99 Per febbre acuta gittan tanto leppo.  

Burning fever makes them reek so strongly.”

- *Per febbre acuta gittan tanto leppo* ‘because of acute fever they emit so much stench’ → “Burning fever makes them reek so strongly.”: the causal prepositional phrase becomes the subject of a causative construction with “makes”, and the implied subject of *gittan* becomes “them”. ‘Acute’ is interpreted as “Burning” and placed before “fever”; ‘emit … stench’ is compressed into “reek”, while the quantity ‘so much’ becomes the adverbial degree “so strongly”.

## Lines 100–114 — Blows and Bitter Retorts

> 100 E l’ un di lor, che si recò a noia  
> 101 Forse d’ esser nomato sì oscuro,  
> 102 Col pugno gli percosse l’ epa croia.  

And one of them, who took offence perhaps at being named thus darkly, smote the rigid belly of him with his fist:

- *l’un di lor* ‘the one of them’ → “one of them”: the definite article is omitted.
- *si recò a noia* ‘took it as an annoyance to himself’ → “took offence”: the reflexive expression becomes an English idiom.
- *d’esser nomato* ‘of being named’ → “at being named”: the preposition changes to suit “took offence”, and the passive infinitive becomes a gerund.
- *sì oscuro* ‘so obscurely’ → “thus darkly”: *sì* becomes “thus” rather than ‘so’, and the adverbially used *oscuro* is rendered figuratively as “darkly”.
- *Col pugno* ‘with the fist’ → “with his fist”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves from before the verb to after its object.
- *gli percosse l’epa croia* ‘to him smote the hard belly’ → “smote the rigid belly of him”: the possessive dative *gli* becomes “of him”, following the noun; *croia* becomes “rigid” rather than ‘hard’ and precedes “belly”.

> 103 Quella sonò, come fosse un tamburo:  

it sounded like a drum;

- *Quella* ‘that’ → “it”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *come fosse un tamburo* ‘as if it were a drum’ → “like a drum”: the clause with a subjunctive verb becomes a prepositional phrase expressing comparison.

> 104 E mastro Adamo gli percosse il volto  
> 105 Col braccio suo, che non parve men duro,  

and Master Adam smote him in the face with his arm, that did not seem less hard,

- *gli percosse il volto* ‘to him smote the face’ → “smote him in the face”: the dative becomes the direct object “him”, while the Italian direct object becomes the prepositional phrase “in the face”.
- *Col braccio suo* ‘with the arm his’ → “with his arm”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *non parve* ‘seemed not’ → “did not seem”: English expresses the negative with the auxiliary “did”.

> 106 Dicendo a lui: Ancor che mi sia tolto  
> 107 Lo muover, per le membra che son gravi,  
> 108 Ho io il braccio a tal mestier disciolto.  

saying to him: “Though I am kept from moving by my weighty limbs, I have an arm free for such necessity.”

- *Ancor che* ‘even though’ → “Though”: the explicit concessive emphasis of ‘even’ is not separately represented.
- *mi sia tolto Lo muover* ‘from me be taken away the moving’ → “I am kept from moving”: the person affected becomes the grammatical subject instead of the substantivized infinitive *Lo muover*. Being deprived of movement becomes being prevented from moving, and the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *per le membra che son gravi* ‘because of the limbs that are heavy’ → “by my weighty limbs”: the causal phrase becomes a phrase introduced by “by”; the definite article becomes a possessive; and the relative clause becomes the adjective “weighty”, placed before the noun.
- *Ho io* ‘have I’ → “I have”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *il braccio* ‘the arm’ → “an arm”: the definite article becomes indefinite.
- *a tal mestier disciolto* ‘for such a task unbound’ → “free for such necessity”: *disciolto* becomes “free” and moves before the phrase specifying its purpose; *mestier* is rendered as “necessity” rather than ‘task’.

> 109 Ond’ ei rispose: Quando tu andavi  
> 110 Al fuoco, non l’ avei tu così presto;  
> 111 Ma sì e più l’ avei quando coniavi.  

Thereat he answered: “When thou wast going to the fire, thou hadst it not so ready; but as ready, and more, when thou wast coining.”

- *Ond’* ‘whence’ → “Thereat”: the connective becomes an adverb marking the occasion of the answer.
- *non l’avei tu così presto* ‘not it hadst thou so ready’ → “thou hadst it not so ready”: the subject moves before the verb, while the object pronoun and the negative move after it.
- *Ma sì e più l’avei* ‘but so and more thou hadst it’ → “but as ready, and more,”: *sì* is expanded to “as ready”, repeating the preceding adjective; *l’avei* is left implicit rather than repeated.

> 112 E l’ idropico: Tu di’ ver di questo;  
> 113 Ma tu non fosti sì ver testimonio,  
> 114 Là ’ve del ver fosti a Troia richiesto.  

And he of the dropsy: “In this thou sayest true; but thou wast not so true a witness there, when questioned of the truth at Troy.”

- *l’idropico* ‘the dropsical man’ → “he of the dropsy”: the substantivized adjective becomes a pronoun qualified by a prepositional phrase.
- *Tu di’ ver di questo* ‘thou sayest truth of this’ → “In this thou sayest true”: the final prepositional phrase moves to the beginning, with “In” replacing ‘of’; the noun *ver* becomes the adverbially used “true”.
- *non fosti* ‘not wast’ → “wast not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *sì ver testimonio* ‘so true witness’ → “so true a witness”: English supplies the indefinite article before the predicate noun.
- *Là ’ve* ‘there where’ → “there, when”: the locative relative becomes a temporal connective.
- *del ver fosti a Troia richiesto* ‘of the truth thou wast at Troy questioned’ → “questioned of the truth at Troy”: the finite passive becomes a participial clause, leaving its subject and auxiliary implicit; both prepositional phrases move after “questioned”.

## Lines 115–129 — Sinon and the Coiner Trade Taunts

> 115 S’ io dissi falso, e tu falsasti il conio,  
> 116 Disse Sinone; e son qui per un fallo,  
> 117 E tu per più che alcun altro Dimonio.  

“If I spoke false, thou too didst falsify the coin,” said Sinon; “and I am here for one crime, and thou for more than any other Demon.”

- *dissi* ‘said’ → “spoke”: the verb of saying becomes a verb of speaking.
- *e tu* ‘and thou’ → “thou too”: the conjunction is replaced by an additive adverb following the pronoun.
- *falsasti il conio* ‘falsifiedst the coinage’ → “didst falsify the coin”: the simple past becomes an auxiliary construction; *conio* is rendered collectively as “coin”.
- *un fallo* ‘one fault’ → “one crime”: “crime” gives *fallo* a more specifically culpable sense.

> 118 Ricorditi, spergiuro, del cavallo,  
> 119 Rispose quei ch’ aveva enfiata l’ epa:  
> 120 E sieti reo che tutto il mondo sallo.  

“Bethink thee, perjurer, of the horse,” answered he who had the inflated paunch; “and be it a torture to thee that all the world knows thereof.”

- *aveva enfiata l’epa* ‘had the belly swollen’ → “had the inflated paunch”: the participle describing the belly’s condition becomes an attributive adjective; “inflated” replaces ‘swollen’, and “paunch” replaces ‘belly’.
- *sieti reo* ‘may it be grievous to thee’ → “be it a torture to thee”: the adjective *reo* becomes the noun phrase “a torture”; the attached pronoun is expressed by a prepositional phrase after that noun phrase.
- *sallo* ‘knows it’ → “knows thereof”: the direct-object pronoun becomes an adverb meaning ‘of it’.

> 121 A te sia rea la sete onde ti crepa,  
> 122 Disse il Greco, la lingua, e l’ acqua marcia  
> 123 Che il ventre innanzi agli occhi sì t’ assiepa.  

“To thee be torture the thirst that cracks thy tongue,” replied the Greek, “and the foul water which makes that belly such a hedge before thy eyes.”

- *A te sia rea* ‘to thee may it be grievous’ → “To thee be torture”: the adjective *rea* becomes the noun “torture”.
- *onde ti crepa … la lingua* ‘from which the tongue cracks for thee’ → “that cracks thy tongue”: the relative expression of cause becomes the subject of a transitive verb. The tongue, originally the subject of intransitive *crepa*, becomes its object, and the dative *ti* becomes the possessive “thy”.
- *Disse il Greco* ‘said the Greek’ → “replied the Greek”: “replied” makes the answering function explicit.
- *l’acqua marcia* ‘the water rotten’ → “the foul water”: “foul” replaces ‘rotten’, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *Che il ventre innanzi agli occhi sì t’assiepa* ‘which so hedges up the belly for thee before the eyes’ → “which makes that belly such a hedge before thy eyes”: the verb *assiepa* becomes the construction “makes … a hedge”; *sì* becomes “such”, modifying the resulting noun phrase. The article before *ventre* becomes the demonstrative “that”, the personal reference in *t’* is expressed through “thy eyes”, and the phrase locating the obstruction moves to the end.

> 124 Allora il monetier: Così si squarcia  
> 125 La bocca tua per dir mal come suole;  
> 126 Chè s’ i’ ho sete, ed umor mi rinfarcia,  
> 127 Tu hai l’ arsura, e il capo che ti duole:  

Then the coiner: “Thus thy jaw gapes wide, as usual, to speak ill; for if I have thirst, and moisture stuffs me, thou hast the burning, and the head that pains thee:

- *si squarcia* ‘tears itself open’ → “gapes wide”: the pronominal verb becomes an intransitive verb with an adverb; the image of tearing is softened into wide opening.
- *La bocca tua* ‘the mouth thine’ → “thy jaw”: “jaw” replaces ‘mouth’, the possessive precedes the noun, and the subject moves before its verb.
- *per dir mal* ‘to say evil’ → “to speak ill”: the expression becomes an English verbal idiom, with “ill” modifying “speak”.
- *come suole* ‘as it is wont’ → “as usual”: the finite clause becomes an abbreviated adverbial phrase and moves before “to speak ill”.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *mi rinfarcia* ‘me stuffs’ → “stuffs me”: the object pronoun follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *che ti duole* ‘which aches for thee’ → “that pains thee”: an intransitive verb with a dative becomes a transitive verb with a direct object.

> 128 E per leccar lo specchio di Narcisso,  
> 129 Non vorresti a invitar molte parole.  

and to make thee lap the mirror of Narcissus thou wouldst not require many words of invitation.”

- *per leccar* ‘to lick’ → “to make thee lap”: the causative “make thee” is supplied, and “lap” replaces ‘lick’. The infinitive consequently expresses what would induce the addressee to act, rather than simply the act itself.
- *Non vorresti a invitar molte parole* ‘thou wouldst not want, for inviting, many words’ → “thou wouldst not require many words of invitation”: *vorresti* is interpreted as needing rather than wishing; the infinitive *a invitar* becomes the noun phrase “of invitation” and moves after “many words”.

## Lines 130–141 — Shame Before the Master

> 130 Ad ascoltarli er’ io del tutto fisso,  
> 131 Quando il Maestro mi disse: Or pur mira,  
> 132 Che per poco è che teco non mi risso!  

I was standing all intent to hear them, when the Master said to me: “Now keep looking, a little longer and I quarrel with thee!”

- *Ad ascoltarli* ‘to listen to them’ → “to hear them”: listening is rendered as hearing, and the infinitive phrase moves from the beginning to follow the description of the speaker’s attentiveness.
- *er’ io del tutto fisso* ‘was I wholly fixed’ → “I was standing all intent”: “standing” is supplied; fixed becomes “intent,” and the subject moves before the verb.
- *mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *Or pur mira* ‘now just look’ → “Now keep looking”: the imperative and its reinforcing adverb become an explicitly continuing action.
- *Che per poco è che teco non mi risso* ‘for it is by little that I do not quarrel with thee’ → “a little longer and I quarrel with thee!”: the introductory conjunction is omitted, and the impersonal construction expressing nearness to quarrelling becomes an implied condition followed by its consequence. “longer” supplies a temporal sense; the Italian negative disappears, and “with thee” moves after the verb.

> 133 Quand’ io il senti’ a me parlar con ira,  
> 134 Volsimi verso lui con tal vergogna,  
> 135 Ch’ ancor per la memoria mi si gira.  

When I heard him speak to me in anger, I turned towards him with such shame, that it comes over me again as I but think of it.

- *io il senti’* ‘I him heard’ → “I heard him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *a me parlar* ‘to me speak’ → “speak to me”: the infinitive precedes its indirect object.
- *con ira* ‘with anger’ → “in anger”: the preposition changes.
- *Volsimi* ‘I turned myself’ → “I turned”: the Italian reflexive becomes an English intransitive verb, without an expressed reflexive object.
- *ancor* ‘still’ → “again”: continuing shame is expressed as recurring shame, and the adverb moves after the verb phrase.
- *per la memoria* ‘through memory’ → “as I but think of it”: the prepositional phrase becomes a finite temporal clause. The noun becomes the verb “think,” with “of it” expressing its object and “but” supplying the sense of merely remembering.
- *mi si gira* ‘turns itself about in me’ → “it comes over me”: the reflexive expression of turning or revolving becomes an idiom for a feeling returning.

> 136 E’ quale è quei, che suo dannaggio sogna,  
> 137 Che sognando desidera sognare,  
> 138 Sì che quel ch’ è, come non fosse, agogna;  

And as one who dreams of something hurtful to him, and dreaming wishes it a dream, so that he longs for that which is, as if it were not;

- *quale è quei* ‘as is that one’ → “as one”: the copula is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes indefinite.
- *suo dannaggio sogna* ‘his harm dreams’ → “dreams of something hurtful to him”: the object moves after the verb, which takes “of” rather than a direct object. The noun becomes “something hurtful,” and the possessive becomes “to him.”
- *Che* ‘who’ → “and”: a repeated relative clause becomes a coordinated continuation.
- *desidera sognare* ‘desires to dream’ → “wishes it a dream”: the infinitive becomes an object-and-complement construction. The translation interprets the wish as wanting the experience to be only a dream, rather than simply wanting to dream.
- *Sì che quel ch’ è, come non fosse, agogna* ‘so that that which is, as it were not, he longs for’ → “so that he longs for that which is, as if it were not”: the verb moves before its object, leaving the comparison at the end. “if” makes explicit the hypothetical force of *come* with the subjunctive.

> 139 Tal mi fec’ io, non potendo parlare,  
> 140 Che disiava scusarmi, e scusava  
> 141 Me tuttavia, e nol mi credea fare.  

such grew I, who, without power to speak, wished to excuse myself and all the while excused, and did not think that I was doing it.

- *Tal mi fec’ io* ‘such did I make myself’ → “such grew I”: reflexive *farsi*, here expressing becoming, is rendered by intransitive “grew.”
- *non potendo parlare* ‘not being able to speak’ → “without power to speak”: the negative participial construction becomes a prepositional phrase, with ability expressed by the noun “power.”
- *Che* ‘that’ → “who”: the conjunction is taken as a relative referring to the speaker, and is moved before “without power to speak.”
- *e scusava Me tuttavia* ‘and excused myself all the while’ → “and all the while excused”: the adverbial phrase moves before the verb, and the reflexive object is omitted, understood from the preceding “myself.”
- *e nol mi credea fare* ‘and did not believe myself to do it’ → “and did not think that I was doing it”: the infinitival construction becomes a finite clause, with an explicit subject and a progressive verb. The object pronoun contained in *nol* moves to the end of that clause.

## Lines 142–148 — Shame and Vulgar Curiosity

> 142 Maggior difetto men vergogna lava,  
> 143 Disse il Maestro, che il tuo non è stato;  

“Less shame washes off a greater fault than thine has been,” said the Master:

- *Maggior difetto men vergogna lava* ‘greater fault less shame washes’ → “Less shame washes off a greater fault”: the Italian object–subject–verb order becomes subject–verb–object; “a” is supplied before the singular count noun, and “off” makes the removal expressed by *lava* explicit.
- *che il tuo non è stato* ‘than thine has not been’ → “than thine has been”: the negative is omitted because *non* is expletive in this comparison, not a denial. The comparative clause is moved before “said the Master,” which interrupts it in Italian.

> 144 Però d’ ogni tristizia ti disgrava:  

“therefore unload thee of all sorrow.

- *d’ ogni tristizia ti disgrava* ‘of every sorrow unburden thyself’ → “unload thee of all sorrow”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb and its object; “all sorrow” replaces ‘every sorrow’, and “unload thee” renders the reflexive command with “thee” rather than ‘thyself’.

> 145 E fa ragion ch’ io ti sia sempre allato,  

And count that I am always at thy side,

- *fa ragion* ‘make reckoning’ → “count”: the verb-and-noun expression becomes a single verb.
- *ch’ io ti sia sempre allato* ‘that I be always beside thee’ → “that I am always at thy side”: the subjunctive *sia* becomes indicative “am”; *allato* is expanded into “at thy side,” with *ti* represented by the possessive “thy”.

> 146 Se più avvien che fortuna t’ accoglia,  
> 147 Dove sien genti in simigliante piato;  

should it again fall out that Fortune brings thee where people are in similar contests;

- *Se più avvien* ‘if again it happens’ → “should it again fall out”: the present-tense conditional clause becomes an inverted clause with “should,” dispensing with ‘if’; *avvien* is rendered by the idiom “fall out”.
- *che fortuna t’ accoglia* ‘that fortune receive thee’ → “that Fortune brings thee”: receiving someone at a place becomes bringing someone there, and the subjunctive *accoglia* becomes indicative “brings”.
- *Dove sien genti* ‘where there be people’ → “where people are”: the subjunctive becomes indicative, and the postverbal subject moves before the verb.
- *in simigliante piato* ‘in a similar quarrel’ → “in similar contests”: singular *piato* becomes plural “contests,” and ‘quarrel’ is rendered by a broader term for contention.

> 148 Chè voler ciò udire è basso voglia.  

for the wish to hear it is a vulgar wish.”

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *voler ciò udire* ‘to wish this to hear’ → “the wish to hear it”: the infinitive *voler* becomes a noun with a definite article; demonstrative *ciò* becomes “it,” and its position shifts from before to after ‘hear’.
- *basso voglia* ‘low desire’ → “a vulgar wish”: “vulgar” interprets ‘low’ as a judgment of taste or conduct, “wish” renders ‘desire’, and an indefinite article is supplied.

## Summary — Close Texture, Recast Syntax

Carlyle remains close to the Italian’s sequence of thought, often preserving its repetitions, connective words, and striking expressions. “Pitiless talons”, “the mirror of Narcissus”, and “a vulgar wish” retain the substance of their Italian counterparts with little expansion. His departures from word-for-word rendering chiefly accommodate English syntax, clarify compressed expressions, or give a phrase a more specific force. The English nevertheless retains an archaic character through constructions such as “Spread we”, conditional inversion, and forms such as “thou” and “thee”.

Word order changes throughout: objects and object pronouns generally move after their verbs, adjectives precede nouns, and verbs placed late in Italian clauses move forward. Larger rearrangements bring related elements together or alter their emphasis. The relative clause *che tutto ardiva* becomes the preceding compound adjective “all-daring”; *con l’umor che mal converte* becomes “with its ill-digested humour”, replacing a relative clause with a participial adjective. Elsewhere a gerund becomes a purpose infinitive, as *Cercando lui* becomes “to seek him”, or a phrase becomes a full clause, as *per la memoria* becomes “as I but think of it”. Changes of tense and mood include past perfects for Italian simple pasts, English progressives for simple forms, and modal constructions for subjunctives.

Lexical choices range from close equivalents to interpretative substitutions. *Altezza*, ‘height’, becomes “pride”; *mente*, ‘mind’, becomes “soul”; and *mondiglia*, ‘refuse’, becomes the more specifically monetary “alloy”. Some choices sharpen physical action: *Prendendo* becomes “grasping”, and *percosselo* becomes “dashed him”. Others redirect an image or its grammatical agency. In “it made the solid bottom claw his belly”, the ground becomes the agent of the scraping; *Per febbre acuta gittan tanto leppo* is recast as “Burning fever makes them reek so strongly”, turning the cause into the subject and the emitted substance into a verb.

Supplied words frequently make an implication explicit: “her daughter”, “the mangled body”, “the Baptist’s image”, and “legal form” add relationships, objects, or qualifications not separately stated in the corresponding Italian phrases. “Hungry” adds a further descriptive detail. Conversely, reinforcing words such as *giuso* and *là* sometimes disappear, repeated expressions may be replaced by pronouns, and repeated verbs may remain understood. Definite articles often become possessives, as *la moglie* becomes “his wife”. Two routine adjustments underlie the comparison without being individually recorded throughout: English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings already identify the subject, and omits the Italian definite article before possessives. These recurring grammatical accommodations coexist with a strong attachment to the original’s vocabulary and imagery.
