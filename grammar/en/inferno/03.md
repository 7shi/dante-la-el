# Inferno 3

## Lines 1–12 — The Gate’s Warning

> 1 Per me si va nella città dolente:  

Through me is the way into the doleful city;

- *si va* ‘one goes’ → “is the way”: the impersonal verb of movement becomes a construction with “is” and the noun “way”.
- *città dolente* ‘city doleful’ → “doleful city”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 2 Per me si va nell’ eterno dolore:  

through me the way into the eternal pain;

- *si va* ‘one goes’ → “the way”: the impersonal verb becomes a noun phrase, with ‘is’ understood from the preceding clause.

> 3 Per me si va tra la perduta gente.  

through me the way among the people lost.

- *si va* ‘one goes’ → “the way”: the impersonal verb again becomes a noun phrase, with the verb understood.
- *perduta gente* ‘lost people’ → “people lost”: the adjective moves after the noun; the Italian singular collective noun becomes the grammatically plural “people”.

> 4 Giustizia mosse il mio alto Fattore:  

Justice moved my High Maker:

- Literal.

> 5 Fecemi la divina Potestate,  
> 6 La somma Sapienza e il primo Amore.  

Divine Power made me, Wisdom Supreme, and Primal Love.

- *Fecemi la divina Potestate* ‘made me the divine Power’ → “Divine Power made me”: the first coordinated subject moves before the verb, and its definite article is omitted.
- *La somma Sapienza* ‘the supreme Wisdom’ → “Wisdom Supreme”: the adjective moves after the noun, and the definite article is omitted.
- *il primo Amore* ‘the first Love’ → “Primal Love”: the ordinal adjective becomes an adjective expressing primordial character, and the definite article is omitted.

> 7 Dinanzi a me non fur cose create,  
> 8 Se non eterne, ed io eterno duro:  

Before me were no things created, but eternal; and eternal I endure.

- *non fur cose create* ‘not were things created’ → “were no things created”: negation of the verb becomes the negative determiner “no” before the noun.
- *Se non* ‘if not’ → “but”: the two-word exceptive construction becomes a single conjunction.
- *io eterno duro* ‘I eternal endure’ → “eternal I endure”: the adjective moves before the subject pronoun.

> 9 Lasciate ogni speranza, voi ch’ entrate.  

Leave all hope, ye that enter.

- *ogni speranza* ‘every hope’ → “all hope”: distributive ‘every’ becomes totalizing “all”, treating hope as an undivided whole.

> 10 Queste parole di colore oscuro  
> 11 Vid’ io scritte al sommo d’ una porta;  

These words, of colour obscure, saw I written above a gate.

- *al sommo d’ una porta* ‘at the top of a gate’ → “above a gate”: the phrase locating the writing at the gate’s top becomes a prepositional phrase locating it above the gate.

> 12 Per ch’ io: Maestro, il senso lor m’ è duro.  

Whereat I: “Master, their meaning to me is hard.”

- *Per ch’* ‘for which’ → “Whereat”: the prepositional relative expression becomes a compound relative adverb expressing a response.
- *il senso lor* ‘the meaning their’ → “their meaning”: the possessive moves before the noun, and the definite article is omitted.

## Lines 13–30 — Entering the Place of Wailing

> 13 Ed egli a me, come persona accorta:  

And he to me, as one experienced:

- *come persona accorta* ‘as a discerning person’ → “as one experienced”: the noun becomes the pronoun “one”; discernment or prudence is rendered as experience.

> 14 Qui si convien lasciare ogni sospetto;  

“Here must all distrust be left;

- *si convien lasciare* ‘it is necessary to leave’ → “must” and “be left”: the impersonal construction with an active infinitive becomes a modal passive; the Italian object becomes the English subject.
- *ogni sospetto* ‘every suspicion’ → “all distrust”: distributive ‘every’ becomes collective “all,” and suspicion or apprehension is rendered as distrust.

> 15 Ogni viltà convien che qui sia morta.  

all cowardice must here be dead.

- *Ogni viltà* ‘every cowardice’ → “all cowardice”: distributive ‘every’ becomes collective “all” with the abstract noun.
- *convien che qui sia morta* ‘it is necessary that here it be dead’ → “must here be dead”: the impersonal main verb and subordinate subjunctive become a single construction with the modal “must.”

> 16 Noi sem venuti al luogo ov’ io t’ ho detto  
> 17 Che tu vedrai le genti dolorose,  
> 18 Ch’ hanno perduto il ben dello intelletto.  

We are come to the place where I told thee thou shouldst see the wretched people, who have lost the good of the intellect.”

- *io t’ ho detto* ‘I thee have told’ → “I told thee”: the compound perfect becomes a simple past, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Che tu vedrai* ‘that thou wilt see’ → “thou shouldst see”: the conjunction is omitted, and the future becomes a future-in-the-past construction after “told.”
- *le genti dolorose* ‘the peoples sorrowful’ → “the wretched people”: the plural noun becomes collective “people”; the adjective moves before it, and sorrowfulness is rendered as wretchedness.

> 19 E poichè la sua mano alla mia pose,  
> 20 Con lieto volto, ond’ io mi confortai,  
> 21 Mi mise dentro alle segrete cose.  

And placing his hand on mine, with a cheerful countenance that comforted me, he led me into the secret things.

- *poichè la sua mano alla mia pose* ‘after he placed his hand to mine’ → “placing his hand on mine”: the finite temporal clause becomes a participial phrase, without an explicit equivalent of ‘after’; the verb moves before its object, and ‘to’ becomes “on.”
- *Con lieto volto* ‘with cheerful face’ → “with a cheerful countenance”: an indefinite article is supplied, and ‘face’ is rendered by “countenance.”
- *ond’ io mi confortai* ‘whence I comforted myself’ → “that comforted me”: the relative adverb indicating the source of comfort becomes a relative pronoun serving as subject. The reflexive construction becomes transitive, with the narrator as object rather than subject.
- *Mi mise* ‘me he put’ → “he led me”: ‘put’ becomes the more specific “led,” and the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 22 Quivi sospiri, pianti, e alti guai  
> 23 Risonavan per l’ aer senza stelle,  

Here sighs, plaints, and deep wailings resounded through the starless air:

- *Quivi* ‘there’ → “Here”: the spatial reference shifts from ‘there’ to “Here.”
- *pianti* ‘weepings’ → “plaints”: weeping is rendered as lamentation or complaint.
- *alti guai* ‘high wails’ → “deep wailings”: “deep” replaces the adjective indicating height or loudness.
- *l’ aer senza stelle* ‘the air without stars’ → “the starless air”: the postposed prepositional phrase becomes an adjective before the noun.

> 24 Per ch’ io al cominciar ne lagrimai  

it made me weep at first.

- *Per ch’ io al cominciar ne lagrimai* ‘wherefore I at the beginning wept at it’ → “it made me weep at first.”: the causal connection and the reference expressed by *ne* are recast in “it made me weep.” The first-person finite verb becomes an infinitive in a causative construction, with the narrator as object; the phrase containing the substantivized infinitive *cominciar* becomes “at first” and moves to the end.

> 25 Diverse lingue, orribili favelle,  
> 26 Parole di dolore, accenti d’ ira,  
> 27 Voci alte e fioche, e suon di man con elle,  

Strange tongues, horrible outcries, words of pain, tones of anger, voices deep and hoarse, and sounds of hands amongst them,

- *Diverse lingue* ‘various tongues’ → “Strange tongues”: variety or difference is rendered as strangeness.
- *orribili favelle* ‘horrible speeches’ → “horrible outcries”: speech or utterance is rendered more specifically as crying out.
- *accenti* ‘accents’ → “tones”: the noun is rendered by a near synonym referring to vocal sound.
- *Voci alte e fioche* ‘voices high and faint’ → “voices deep and hoarse”: height or loudness becomes depth, while faintness or weakness becomes hoarseness.
- *suon* ‘sound’ → “sounds”: the singular becomes plural.
- *con elle* ‘with them’ → “amongst them”: accompaniment is expressed instead as intermixture.

> 28 Facevano un tumulto, il qual s’ aggira  
> 29 Sempre in quell’ aria senza tempo tinta,  
> 30 Come la rena quando il turbo spira.  

made a tumult, which turns itself unceasing in that air for ever dyed, as sand when the whirlwind breathes.

- *Facevano* ‘were making’ → “made”: the imperfect is rendered by a simple past rather than an explicitly progressive construction.
- *il qual s’ aggira* ‘which turns about’ → “which turns itself”: the pronominal verb expressing movement around becomes an explicitly reflexive construction with “itself.”
- *Sempre* ‘always’ → “unceasing”: the adverb becomes an adjective describing the action as continuous.
- *senza tempo* ‘without time’ → “for ever”: timelessness is interpreted as endless duration, with the phrase qualifying “dyed.”
- *Come la rena* ‘as the sand’ → “as sand”: the definite article is omitted before the generic mass noun.
- *spira* ‘blows’ → “breathes”: the verb is rendered in its breathing sense rather than its ordinary wind-related sense of blowing.

## Lines 31–42 — Souls Without Blame or Praise

> 31 Ed io, ch’ avea d’ error la testa cinta,  
> 32 Dissi: Maestro, che è quel ch’ i’ odo?  

And I, my head begirt with error, said: “Master, what is this that I hear?

- *ch’ avea d’ error la testa cinta* ‘who had with error the head girded’ → “my head begirt with error”: the finite relative clause becomes an absolute construction without a finite verb; the head’s possessor is expressed by “my”, and the phrase describing what girds it moves to the end.
- *che è quel* ‘what is that’ → “what is this”: the demonstrative changes from ‘that’ to “this”.

> 33 E che gente è, che par nel duol sì vinta?  

and who are these that seem so overcome with pain?”

- *che gente è* ‘what people is it’ → “who are these”: the noun-based question becomes a question with “who”; the singular collective construction becomes plural, and “these” supplies an explicit demonstrative subject.
- *che par* ‘that seems’ → “that seem”: the singular verb becomes plural to agree with “these”.
- *nel duol sì vinta* ‘in the pain so overcome’ → “so overcome with pain”: the prepositional phrase moves after the participle, ‘in’ becomes “with”, and the definite article is omitted.

> 34 Ed egli a me: Questo misero modo  
> 35 Tengon l’ anime triste di coloro,  
> 36 Che visser senza infamia e senza lodo.  

And he to me: “This miserable mode the dreary souls of those sustain, who lived without blame, and without praise.

- *Tengon* ‘keep’ → “sustain”: the verb expresses enduring rather than keeping or maintaining a mode, and moves from before its subject to after the complete subject phrase.
- *l’ anime triste* ‘the sad souls’ → “the dreary souls”: ‘sad’ is rendered as “dreary”, and the adjective, which follows the noun in Italian, precedes it in English.
- *infamia* ‘infamy’ → “blame”: the term for disgrace or ill repute becomes the less forceful term “blame”.

> 37 Mischiate sono a quel cattivo coro  
> 38 Degli angeli che non furon ribelli,  
> 39 Nè fur fedeli a Dio, ma per sè foro.  

They are mixed with that caitiff choir of the angels, who were not rebellious, nor were faithful to God; but were for themselves.

- *Mischiate sono a* ‘mixed are to’ → “They are mixed with”: the participle moves after the finite verb, an explicit subject pronoun is supplied, and “with” replaces ‘to’ as the English complement of “mixed”.
- *per sè foro* ‘for themselves were’ → “were for themselves”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase.

> 40 Cacciârli i ciel per non esser men belli,  

Heaven chased them forth to keep its beauty from impair;

- *Cacciârli* ‘chased them’ → “chased them forth”: “forth” makes the outward direction explicit.
- *i ciel* ‘the heavens’ → “Heaven”: the plural becomes singular, the definite article disappears, and the subject moves from after the verb to before it.
- *per non esser men belli* ‘in order not to be less beautiful’ → “to keep its beauty from impair”: the negative construction describing a state becomes an affirmative construction describing preservation. The adjective becomes the noun “beauty”, “its” is supplied, and becoming ‘less beautiful’ is recast as suffering impairment.

> 41 Nè lo profondo inferno gli riceve,  
> 42 Chè alcuna gloria i rei avrebber d’ elli.  

and the deep Hell receives them not, for the wicked would have some glory over them.”

- *Nè* ‘nor’ → “and” … “not”: the negative conjunction is divided into a coordinating conjunction and a separate verbal negation.
- *gli riceve* ‘them receives’ → “receives them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating causal conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *alcuna gloria i rei avrebber* ‘some glory the guilty would have’ → “the wicked would have some glory”: the fronted object moves after the subject and verb; ‘the guilty’ is rendered as “the wicked”.
- *d’ elli* ‘from them’ → “over them”: the preposition is interpreted as expressing comparative superiority rather than a source of glory.

## Lines 43–57 — The Unremembered And Their Restless Procession

> 43 Ed io: Maestro, che è tanto greve  
> 44 A lor, che lamentar gli fa sì forte?  

And I: “Master, what is so grievous to them, that makes them lament thus bitterly?”

- *che lamentar gli fa* ‘that to lament them makes’ → “that makes them lament”: the causative verb comes before its object and the infinitive, rather than after them.
- *sì forte* ‘so loudly’ → “thus bitterly”: “thus” replaces the degree-word ‘so’, while “bitterly” emphasizes distress rather than loudness.

> 45 Rispose: Dicerolti molto breve.  

He answered: “I will tell it to thee very briefly.

- *molto breve* ‘very short’ → “very briefly”: the adjective used adverbially in Italian becomes an explicit English adverb.

> 46 Questi non hanno speranza di morte,  

These have no hope of death;

- *non hanno speranza* ‘do not have hope’ → “have no hope”: verbal negation becomes a negative determiner attached to the noun.

> 47 E la lor cieca vita è tanto bassa,  
> 48 Che invidiosi son d’ ogni altra sorte.  

and their blind life is so mean, that they are envious of every other lot.

- *bassa* ‘low’ → “mean”: the adjective is rendered in its qualitative sense of low worth.
- *invidiosi son* ‘envious are’ → “they are envious”: the predicate adjective moves after the verb.

> 49 Fama di loro il mondo esser non lassa,  

Report of them the world permits not to exist.

- *Fama* ‘fame’ → “Report”: the translation takes the noun as what is reported or remembered about someone, rather than simply renown.
- *esser non lassa* ‘to be does not let’ → “permits not to exist”: the dependent infinitive moves after the governing verb; ‘let’ becomes “permits”, and ‘be’ is rendered explicitly as “exist”.

> 50 Misericordia e Giustizia gli sdegna:  

Mercy and Judgment disdains them.

- *Giustizia* ‘Justice’ → “Judgment”: the translation substitutes the exercise or pronouncement of justice for justice itself.
- *gli sdegna* ‘them disdains’ → “disdains them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 51 Non ragioniam di lor, ma guarda e passa.  

Let us not speak of them; but look, and pass.”

- *Non ragioniam* ‘not discourse’ → “Let us not speak”: the first-person plural exhortation is expressed with “Let us”; the negative follows that expression, and ‘discourse’ becomes the more general “speak”.

> 52 Ed io, che riguardai, vidi un’ insegna,  
> 53 Che girando correva tanto ratta,  
> 54 Che d’ ogni posa mi pareva indegna:  

And I, who looked, saw an ensign, which whirling ran so quickly that it seemed to scorn all pause.

- *ratta* ‘swift’ → “quickly”: the feminine adjective agreeing with the ensign becomes an adverb modifying its running.
- *Che d’ ogni posa mi pareva indegna* ‘that of every rest to me it seemed unworthy’ → “that it seemed to scorn all pause”: ‘unworthy of’ is interpreted as an active rejection and recast as the infinitive “to scorn”. The phrase preceding the Italian predicate becomes the object following “scorn”; ‘every rest’ becomes “all pause”, and *mi*, ‘to me’, is omitted.

> 55 E dietro le venia sì lunga tratta  
> 56 Di gente, ch’ io non avrei mai creduto,  
> 57 Che morte tanta n’ avesse disfatta.  

And behind it came so long a train of people, that I should never have believed death had undone so many.

- *le* ‘her’ → “it”: the pronoun’s grammatical feminine gender, referring to the ensign, becomes English neuter.
- *sì lunga tratta* ‘so long train’ → “so long a train”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *ch’ io non avrei mai creduto* ‘that I would not ever have believed’ → “that I should never have believed”: ‘not ever’ is combined into “never”, placed between the auxiliaries; “should” expresses the first-person conditional for which modern English generally uses ‘would’.
- *Che morte tanta n’ avesse disfatta* ‘that death so much of it had undone’ → “death had undone so many”: the introductory conjunction is omitted, and the object moves after the verb. The singular quantity *tanta*, referring to the collective *gente*, becomes the plural count-expression “so many”; the partitive *n’*, ‘of it’, is left implicit. The Italian pluperfect subjunctive is rendered by an English past perfect without a distinct subjunctive form.

## Lines 58–69 — The Cowardly and Their Torment

> 58 Poscia ch’ io v’ ebbi alcun riconosciuto,  

After I had recognised some amongst them,

- *io v’ ebbi alcun riconosciuto* ‘I there had someone recognised’ → “I had recognised some amongst them”: the locative *v’* (‘there’) is interpreted as “amongst them” and moved to the end; singular *alcun* becomes plural “some” and follows the completed verb phrase. The Italian past anterior becomes the English past perfect.

> 59 Guardai, e vidi l’ ombra di colui  
> 60 Che fece per viltate il gran rifiuto.  

I looked and saw the shadow of him who from cowardice made the great refusal.

- *fece per viltate* ‘made from cowardice’ → “from cowardice made”: the phrase expressing cause is moved before the verb.

> 61 Incontanente intesi, e certo fui,  
> 62 Che quest’ era la setta dei cattivi,  
> 63 A Dio spiacenti ed a’ nemici sui.  

Forthwith I understood and felt assured, that this was the crew of caitiffs, hateful to God and to his enemies.

- *certo fui* ‘certain I was’ → “felt assured”: being certain becomes feeling assured, and the verb precedes its complement.
- *setta* ‘sect’ → “crew”: a term for a distinct group or faction becomes a disparaging collective noun.
- *dei cattivi* ‘of the base ones’ → “of caitiffs”: the substantivized adjective becomes an English noun, and the definite article is omitted.
- *A Dio spiacenti* ‘to God displeasing’ → “hateful to God”: “hateful” strengthens ‘displeasing’, and the adjective is placed before its complement.
- *nemici sui* ‘enemies his’ → “his enemies”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 64 Questi sciaurati, che mai non fur vivi,  
> 65 Erano ignudi e stimolati molto  
> 66 Da mosconi e da vespe ch’ eran ivi.  

These unfortunate, who never were alive, were naked, and sorely goaded by hornets and by wasps that were there:

- *sciaurati* ‘wretched ones’ → “unfortunate”: the English word suggests misfortune more directly and conveys less of the Italian word’s condemnation.
- *stimolati molto* ‘goaded much’ → “sorely goaded”: the adverb moves before the participle, and “sorely” expresses painful severity rather than merely degree.
- *mosconi* ‘large flies’ → “hornets”: the translation names a different insect.

> 67 Elle rigavan lor di sangue il volto,  
> 68 Che mischiato di lagrime, a’ lor piedi  
> 69 Da fastidiosi vermi era ricolto.  

these made their faces stream with blood, which mixed with tears was gathered at their feet by loathsome worms.

- *Elle* ‘they’ → “these”: a personal pronoun becomes a demonstrative pronoun.
- *rigavan lor di sangue il volto* ‘streaked for them with blood the face’ → “made their faces stream with blood”: the simple verb becomes a causative construction. The possessive dative *lor* becomes “their”; the distributive singular *il volto* becomes plural “faces” and serves as the subject of “stream”; the blood phrase moves after the reference to the faces.
- *di lagrime* ‘of tears’ → “with tears”: English uses “with” to introduce what is mixed with the blood.
- *a’ lor piedi Da fastidiosi vermi era ricolto* ‘at their feet by loathsome worms was gathered’ → “was gathered at their feet by loathsome worms”: the passive verb moves before both the phrase of place and the phrase naming the agent.

## Lines 70–85 — The Shore of Acheron

> 70 E poi che a riguardare oltre mi diedi,  
> 71 Vidi gente alla riva d’ un gran fiume:  

And then, as I looked onwards, I saw people on the Shore of a great River.

- *poi che a riguardare oltre mi diedi* ‘after I gave myself to looking farther’ → “then, as I looked onwards”: the temporal conjunction becomes “then, as”; the reflexive expression for applying oneself to looking becomes the simple “looked”.
- *alla riva* ‘at the shore’ → “on the Shore”: the preposition changes from ‘at’ to “on”.

> 72 Perch’ io dissi: Maestro, or mi concedi,  
> 73 Ch’ io sappia quali sono, e qual costume  
> 74 Le fa parer di trapassar sì pronte,  
> 75 Com’ io discerno per lo fioco lume.  

Whereat I said: “Master, now grant that I may know who these are; and what usage makes them seem so ready to pass over, as I discern by the faint light.”

- *Perch’ io dissi* ‘Wherefore I said’ → “Whereat I said”: the connective expressing a consequence becomes one marking the occasion for speaking.
- *mi concedi, Ch’ io sappia* ‘grant me that I know’ → “grant that I may know”: the indirect object *mi* is omitted, and the Italian subjunctive is represented by “may know”.
- *quali sono* ‘who they are’ → “who these are”: “these” makes the reference demonstrative rather than leaving it implicit in the verb.
- *Le fa parer di trapassar sì pronte* ‘them makes appear to cross so ready’ → “makes them seem so ready to pass over”: the object pronoun follows “makes”; “so ready” moves before the infinitive, which English attaches directly to “ready”.

> 76 Ed egli a me: Le cose ti fien conte,  
> 77 Quando noi fermerem li nostri passi  
> 78 Sulla trista riviera d’ Acheronte.  

And he: “The things shall be told thee, when we stay our steps upon the joyless strand of Acheron.”

- *Ed egli a me* ‘And he to me’ → “And he”: ‘to me’ is omitted.
- *Le cose ti fien conte* ‘The things to thee shall be known’ → “The things shall be told thee”: being known becomes being told, replacing an adjective with a passive verb; the recipient pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Quando noi fermerem* ‘when we shall halt’ → “when we stay”: the future becomes a present tense, as usual in an English temporal clause referring to the future.
- *trista riviera* ‘sad riverbank’ → “joyless strand”: “joyless” recasts ‘sad’ as an absence of joy, while “strand” is less specifically a riverbank.

> 79 Allor con gli occhi vergognosi e bassi,  
> 80 Temendo no ’l mio dir gli fusse grave,  
> 81 Infino al fiume dal parlar mi trassi.  

Then, with eyes ashamed and downcast, fearing my words might have offended him, I kept myself from speaking till we reached the stream.

- *con gli occhi* ‘with the eyes’ → “with eyes”: the definite article is omitted.
- *bassi* ‘low’ → “downcast”: the adjective is rendered by a word specifying the downward direction of the eyes.
- *Temendo no* ‘fearing lest’ → “fearing”: the apprehensive *no*, a form of *non* used after a verb of fearing, has no separate English equivalent here; it does not negate the feared event.
- *’l mio dir gli fusse grave* ‘my speaking might be burdensome to him’ → “my words might have offended him”: the substantivized infinitive becomes the plural noun “words”; being burdensome to someone becomes offending him. The imperfect subjunctive becomes “might have offended”, explicitly presenting the possible offence as already committed.
- *Infino al fiume* ‘as far as the river’ → “till we reached the stream”: the spatial endpoint becomes a temporal clause, supplying “we reached”; ‘river’ becomes “stream”. The phrase moves from the beginning to the end of the clause.
- *dal parlar mi trassi* ‘from speaking myself I withdrew’ → “I kept myself from speaking”: withdrawal becomes continued restraint, and the reflexive pronoun and the phrase “from speaking” follow the verb.

> 82 Ed ecco verso noi venir per nave  
> 83 Un vecchio bianco per antico pelo,  
> 84 Gridando: Guai a voi, anime prave!  

And lo! an old man, white with ancient hair, comes towards us in a bark, shouting: “Woe to you, depraved spirits!

- *verso noi venir per nave* ‘towards us to come by boat’ → “comes towards us in a bark”: the infinitive dependent on *ecco* becomes a finite present-tense verb; the directional phrase follows it. Travel ‘by boat’ becomes location “in a bark”, using a more particular term for the vessel.
- *Un vecchio* ‘an old one’ → “an old man”: English supplies “man” for the substantivized adjective, and places the subject before rather than after the verb.
- *bianco per antico pelo* ‘white through ancient hair’ → “white with ancient hair”: causal *per* becomes “with”, expressing the accompanying characteristic rather than explicitly its cause.
- *anime prave* ‘souls depraved’ → “depraved spirits”: ‘souls’ becomes “spirits”, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 85 Non isperate mai veder lo Cielo:  

Hope not ever to see Heaven.

- *Non isperate mai* ‘not hope ever’ → “Hope not ever”: the negative moves after the imperative verb.
- *lo Cielo* ‘the Heaven’ → “Heaven”: the definite article is omitted.

## Lines 86–102 — Charon’s Warning and Rebuke

> 86 I’ vegno per menarvi all’ altra riva,  
> 87 Nelle tenebre eterne, in caldo e in gelo.  

I come to lead you to the other shore; into the eternal darkness; into fire and into ice.

- *Nelle tenebre eterne* ‘in the darknesses eternal’ → “into the eternal darkness”: the preposition expresses movement into rather than position within; the plural noun becomes an English mass noun, and the adjective precedes it.
- *in caldo e in gelo* ‘in heat and in frost’ → “into fire and into ice”: “into” again expresses movement; heat becomes “fire,” and frost or freezing cold becomes “ice.”

> 88 E tu che sei costì, anima viva,  
> 89 Partiti da cotesti, che son morti.  

And thou, who art there alive, depart thee from these who are dead.”

- *anima viva* ‘living soul’ → “alive”: the noun of direct address is omitted, and its adjective becomes a complement of “art” within the relative clause.
- *da cotesti* ‘from those near thee’ → “from these”: the demonstrative referring to those near the person addressed becomes the nearer English demonstrative.

> 90 Ma poi ch’ ei vide, ch’ io non mi partiva,  
> 91 Disse: Per altre vie, per altri porti  
> 92 Verrai a piaggia, non qui, per passare:  

But when he saw that I departed not, he said: “By other ways, by other ferries; not here shalt thou pass over.

- *poi ch’* ‘after’ → “when”: the explicit relation of temporal succession becomes a more general temporal connection.
- *io non mi partiva* ‘I was not departing’ → “I departed not”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, the negative follows the verb, and the Italian pronominal verb is rendered without a reflexive pronoun.
- *per altri porti* ‘by other harbours’ → “by other ferries”: the places of passage are rendered as ferries rather than harbours.
- *Verrai a piaggia, non qui, per passare* ‘thou wilt come to shore, not here, to cross’ → “not here shalt thou pass over”: coming to shore is omitted; the infinitive expressing purpose becomes the main verb, taking over the future construction, and the negative phrase is placed first.

> 93 Più lieve legno convien che ti porti.  

A lighter boat must carry thee.”

- *Più lieve legno* ‘lighter wood’ → “A lighter boat”: an indefinite article is supplied, and the material standing for the vessel is replaced by the vessel’s name.
- *convien che ti porti* ‘it is fitting that it carry thee’ → “must carry thee”: the impersonal expression and its subordinate subjunctive clause become a modal verb with an infinitive; the object pronoun follows rather than precedes the verb.

> 94 E il Duca a lui: Caron, non ti crucciare:  
> 95 Vuolsi così colà, dove si puote  
> 96 Ciò che si vuole; e più non dimandare.  

And my guide to him: “Charon, vex not thyself. Thus it is willed there, where what is willed can be done: and ask no more.”

- *il Duca* ‘the leader’ → “my guide”: the noun is rendered by a more specific description of function, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *non ti crucciare* ‘not thyself vex’ → “vex not thyself”: the negative and reflexive pronoun follow the verb; the Italian infinitive used for a negative command becomes an English imperative.
- *Vuolsi così* ‘it is willed thus’ → “Thus it is willed”: the adverb moves before the passive construction.
- *dove si puote Ciò che si vuole* ‘where one can what one wills’ → “where what is willed can be done”: the impersonal constructions become passives, and “done” supplies the action left implicit with *puote*. The clause identifying what is possible precedes the modal verb rather than following it.
- *e più non dimandare* ‘and more not ask’ → “and ask no more”: the verb moves before the negative expression, and the separate negative becomes “no” before “more.”

> 97 Quinci fur quete le lanose gote  
> 98 Al nocchier della livida palude,  
> 99 Che intorno agli occhi avea di fiamme rote.  

Then the woolly cheeks were quiet of the steersman on the livid marsh, who round his eyes had wheels of flame.

- *Quinci* ‘hence’ → “Then”: the connective is rendered temporally rather than as an explicit indication of consequence.
- *fur quete le lanose gote* ‘were quiet the woolly cheeks’ → “the woolly cheeks were quiet”: the subject moves before the verb and its complement.
- *Al nocchier* ‘to the steersman’ → “of the steersman”: the Italian dative indicating the possessor becomes an English possessive phrase.
- *della livida palude* ‘of the livid marsh’ → “on the livid marsh”: the relationship expressed by *di* becomes an explicit location.
- *intorno agli occhi* ‘around the eyes’ → “round his eyes”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *di fiamme rote* ‘of flames wheels’ → “wheels of flame”: the noun precedes its dependent phrase, and plural flames become singular mass “flame.”

> 100 Ma quell’ anime, ch’ eran lasse e nude,  
> 101 Cangiâr colore e dibattero i denti,  
> 102 Ratto che inteser le parole crude.  

But those spirits, who were foreworn and naked, changed colour and chattered with their teeth, soon as they heard the bitter words.

- *anime* ‘souls’ → “spirits”: a different designation is used for the same referents.
- *lasse* ‘weary’ → “foreworn”: weariness is expressed as being worn out.
- *dibattero i denti* ‘beat the teeth’ → “chattered with their teeth”: the transitive construction becomes an intransitive verb with a phrase introduced by “with,” and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Ratto che* ‘immediately when’ → “soon as”: the adverb and conjunction become an idiomatic English temporal expression.
- *inteser* ‘understood’ → “heard”: the translation selects the verb’s auditory sense rather than its sense of comprehension.
- *le parole crude* ‘the words harsh’ → “the bitter words”: harshness is expressed as bitterness, and the adjective precedes the noun.

## Lines 103–120 — The Damned Cross the River

> 103 Bestemmiavano Iddio e i lor parenti,  
> 104 L’ umana specie, il luogo, il tempo, e il seme  
> 105 Di lor semenza e di lor nascimenti.  

They blasphemed God and their parents; the human kind; the place, the time, and origin of their seed, and of their birth.

- *Bestemmiavano* ‘they were blaspheming’ → “They blasphemed”: the Italian imperfect becomes an English simple past, without explicitly marking the action as continuing.
- *L’ umana specie* ‘the human species’ → “the human kind”: *specie* is rendered by “kind” rather than ‘species’.
- *e il seme Di lor semenza e di lor nascimenti* ‘and the seed of their seed and of their births’ → “and origin of their seed, and of their birth”: the first *seme* is interpreted as “origin”, avoiding the repetition of ‘seed’; its definite article is omitted, and plural *nascimenti* becomes singular “birth”.

> 106 Poi si ritrasser tutte quante insieme,  
> 107 Forte piangendo, alla riva malvagia,  
> 108 Che attende ciascun uom, che Dio non teme.  

Then all of them together, sorely weeping, drew to the accursed shore, which awaits every man that fears not God.

- *si ritrasser* ‘drew themselves back’ → “drew”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, and the sense of withdrawal expressed by ‘back’ is not separately rendered.
- *tutte quante insieme* ‘all together’ → “all of them together”: “of them” makes the reference explicit, and the phrase moves from after the verb to before it.
- *Forte piangendo* ‘loudly weeping’ → “sorely weeping”: the translation expresses the intensity of distress rather than explicitly the loudness of the weeping.
- *alla riva malvagia* ‘to the wicked shore’ → “to the accursed shore”: “accursed” replaces ‘wicked’, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Dio non teme* ‘God [he] does not fear’ → “fears not God”: the object moves after the verb, and the negative likewise follows rather than precedes the verb.

> 109 Caron dimonio, con occhi di bragia  
> 110 Loro accennando, tutte le raccoglie;  
> 111 Batte col remo qualunque s’ adagia.  

Charon the demon, with eyes of glowing coal, beckoning them, collects them all; smites with his oar whoever lingers.

- *Caron dimonio* ‘Charon demon’ → “Charon the demon”: the definite article is supplied before the appositional noun.
- *Loro accennando* ‘to them signalling’ → “beckoning them”: signalling is interpreted specifically as beckoning; the Italian indirect object becomes an English direct object and follows the participle.
- *tutte le raccoglie* ‘all [of them] he gathers them’ → “collects them all”: the anticipatory *tutte* and its resumptive object pronoun *le* become the single postverbal construction “them all”.
- *col remo* ‘with the oar’ → “with his oar”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *qualunque s’ adagia* ‘whoever makes himself comfortable’ → “whoever lingers”: the reflexive expression is interpreted as delaying and becomes a nonreflexive verb.

> 112 Come d’ autunno si levan le foglie  
> 113 L’ una appresso dell’ altra, infin che il ramo  
> 114 Vede alla terra tutte le sue spoglie;  

As the leaves of autumn fall off one after the other, till the branch sees all its spoils upon the ground;

- *Come d’ autunno si levan le foglie* ‘As in autumn the leaves detach themselves’ → “As the leaves of autumn fall off”: the temporal phrase ‘in autumn’ becomes “of autumn”, modifying “leaves”; the reflexive verb becomes “fall off”, and the subject moves from after the verb to before it.
- *L’ una appresso dell’ altra* ‘the one after the other’ → “one after the other”: the first definite article is omitted.
- *Vede alla terra tutte le sue spoglie* ‘sees upon the ground all its spoils’ → “sees all its spoils upon the ground”: the phrase specifying location moves after the object.

> 115 Similemente il mal seme d’ Adamo:  
> 116 Gittansi di quel lito ad una ad una,  
> 117 Per cenni, come augel per suo richiamo.  

so one by one the evil seed of Adam cast themselves from that shore at signals, as the bird at its call.

- *Similemente* ‘similarly’ → “so”: the explicit adverb of similarity becomes the correlative “so”, answering the preceding “As”.
- *ad una ad una* ‘one at a time’ → “one by one”: the distributive expression is recast and moved from after the phrase describing departure to before the subject.
- *Per cenni* ‘by signals’ → “at signals”: the preposition presents the signals as prompting the action rather than as its means.
- *come augel* ‘as bird’ → “as the bird”: the definite article is supplied.

> 118 Così sen vanno su per l’ onda bruna,  
> 119 Ed avanti che sian di là discese,  
> 120 Anche di qua nova schiera s’ aduna.  

Thus they depart on the brown water; and ere they have landed on the other shore, again a fresh crowd collects on this.

- *sen vanno* ‘go themselves away’ → “they depart”: the Italian pronominal expression is rendered by a single nonreflexive verb.
- *su per l’ onda bruna* ‘over the brown wave’ → “on the brown water”: the compound preposition becomes “on”, without separately expressing movement over or along the surface; singular ‘wave’ becomes the mass noun “water”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *sian di là discese* ‘they have on that side descended’ → “they have landed on the other shore”: the perfect subjunctive becomes an English present perfect indicative; ‘descended’ is interpreted as “landed”, and the deictic phrase ‘on that side’ becomes the explicit “on the other shore” and moves after the participle.
- *di qua* ‘on this side’ → “on this”: ‘this side’ is represented by “this”, with “shore” understood from the preceding clause; the phrase moves from before the subject to after the verb.
- *nova schiera* ‘new band’ → “a fresh crowd”: an indefinite article is supplied, ‘new’ becomes “fresh”, and ‘band’ is rendered by the less organized collective “crowd”.
- *s’ aduna* ‘gathers itself’ → “collects”: the reflexive construction becomes an English intransitive verb.

## Lines 121–136 — Divine Justice and Dante’s Collapse

> 121 Figliuol mio, disse il Maestro cortese,  
> 122 Quelli, che muoion nell’ ira di Dio,  
> 123 Tutti convegnon qui d’ ogni paese:  

“My son,” said the courteous Master, “those who die under God’s wrath, all assemble here from every country.

- *Figliuol mio* ‘little son my’ → “My son”: the possessive moves before the noun, and the affectionate diminutive is not separately expressed.
- *il Maestro cortese* ‘the Master courteous’ → “the courteous Master”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *nell’ ira di Dio* ‘in the wrath of God’ → “under God’s wrath”: “under” replaces ‘in’, and the phrase naming the possessor becomes a possessive before the noun.

> 124 E pronti sono a trapassar lo rio,  
> 125 Chè la divina Giustizia li sprona  
> 126 Sì, che la tema si volge in disio.  

And they are prompt to pass the river, for Divine Justice spurs them so, that fear is changed into desire.

- *pronti sono* ‘ready are’ → “they are prompt”: the predicate adjective follows the verb rather than preceding it; ‘ready’ is expressed as “prompt”.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *la divina Giustizia* ‘the divine Justice’ → “Divine Justice”: the definite article is omitted before the abstract noun.
- *li sprona* ‘them spurs’ → “spurs them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *la tema si volge in disio* ‘the fear turns itself into desire’ → “fear is changed into desire”: the definite article is omitted, and the Italian pronominal construction becomes an English passive.

> 127 Quinci non passa mai anima buona:  

By this way no good spirit ever passes;

- *Quinci* ‘from here’ → “By this way”: the adverb indicating a point of departure becomes a phrase indicating a route.
- *non passa mai anima buona* ‘never passes a soul good’ → “no good spirit ever passes”: the subject moves before the verb and its adjective before the noun; ‘soul’ becomes “spirit”. Negation is transferred to the subject through “no”, with “ever” replacing ‘never’ in the resulting construction.

> 128 E però se Caron di te si lagna,  
> 129 Ben puoi saper omai, che il suo dir suona.  

and hence, if Charon complains of thee, thou easily now mayest know the import of his words.”

- *di te si lagna* ‘of thee complains’ → “complains of thee”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb, and the Italian pronominal verb is represented by a non-reflexive English verb.
- *Ben puoi saper omai* ‘well canst thou know by now’ → “thou easily now mayest know”: *ben* is interpreted as ease of understanding rather than simply ‘well’; ‘canst’ becomes “mayest”. *Omai* is shortened to “now” and moves from after the infinitive to before the modal verb.
- *che il suo dir suona* ‘what his saying means’ → “the import of his words”: the clause becomes a noun phrase. The substantivized infinitive *dir*, ‘saying’, becomes the plural “words”, while the meaning expressed by *suona* is recast in the noun “import”.

> 130 Finito questo, la buia campagna  
> 131 Tremò sì forte, che dello spavento  
> 132 La mente di sudore ancor mi bagna.  

When he had ended, the dusky champaign trembled so violently, that the remembrance of my terror bathes me still with sweat.

- *Finito questo* ‘this finished’ → “When he had ended”: the absolute participial construction becomes a temporal clause with a personal subject and a pluperfect verb; ‘this’ is no longer explicitly represented.
- *sì forte* ‘so strongly’ → “so violently”: the description of the trembling’s strength is expressed as violence.
- *dello spavento / La mente* ‘of the fright the mind’ → “the remembrance of my terror”: *mente* is interpreted as memory or recollection rather than the mental faculty. The preceding phrase is placed after its noun, and “my” is supplied before “terror”.
- *di sudore ancor mi bagna* ‘with sweat still me bathes’ → “bathes me still with sweat”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, “still” follows the object, and the phrase specifying sweat moves to the end.

> 133 La terra lagrimosa diede vento,  
> 134 E balenò d’ una luce vermiglia,  
> 135 La qual mi vinse ciascun sentimento;  

The tearful ground gave out wind, and flashed with a crimson light, which conquered all my senses:

- *terra lagrimosa* ‘ground tearful’ → “tearful ground”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *diede vento* ‘gave wind’ → “gave out wind”: “out” is supplied, making the emission explicit.
- *una luce vermiglia* ‘a light vermilion’ → “a crimson light”: the adjective moves before the noun, and the colour is rendered as crimson rather than vermilion.
- *La qual* ‘the which’ → “which”: the article accompanying the Italian relative pronoun is omitted.
- *mi vinse ciascun sentimento* ‘for me conquered each sense’ → “conquered all my senses”: the affected-person pronoun *mi* becomes the possessive “my”; the distributive singular ‘each sense’ becomes the collective plural “all my senses”.

> 136 E caddi, come l’ uom, cui sonno piglia.  

and I fell, like one who is seized with sleep.

- *come l’ uom* ‘like the man’ → “like one”: the noun with its generic definite article becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *cui sonno piglia* ‘whom sleep seizes’ → “who is seized with sleep”: the active clause becomes passive, the relative pronoun becomes its subject, and sleep is introduced by “with” rather than serving as the grammatical subject.

## Summary — Close Wording, Flexible Construction

Carlyle generally keeps close to the Italian’s sequence of statements, repetitions, and imagery, often preserving its unusual phrasing rather than smoothing it into ordinary English. Expressions such as “the good of the intellect”, “the secret things”, and “my head begirt with error” retain the Italian’s vocabulary and compactness. His word order, however, alternates between literal inversion and English rearrangement: “saw I” preserves *Vid’ io*, while “Divine Power made me” places the subject before the verb. Adjectives commonly move before their nouns, though “people lost” and “Wisdom Supreme” introduce postposed adjectives where the Italian has them before the noun.

Changes of construction frequently make the English less strictly literal. *Si va*, ‘one goes’, becomes “is the way”; *si convien lasciare*, ‘it is necessary to leave’, becomes “must” with “be left”. Finite clauses may become participial phrases, as in “placing his hand on mine”, while the compressed *Finito questo*, ‘this finished’, expands into “When he had ended”. Reflexive or pronominal expressions receive several treatments: *s’ aggira* becomes explicitly reflexive in “turns itself”, *s’ aduna* becomes the intransitive “collects”, and *si volge* becomes the passive “is changed”. Distributive singulars also give way to collective or plural expressions: *ogni speranza* becomes “all hope”, and *ciascun sentimento* becomes “all my senses”.

Vocabulary ranges from close equivalents to more interpretive choices. *Infamia*, ‘infamy’, becomes the less forceful “blame”, whereas *spiacenti*, ‘displeasing’, becomes “hateful”. Elsewhere the translation changes the quality described: *alte e fioche*, ‘high and faint’, becomes “deep and hoarse”, and *mosconi*, ‘large flies’, becomes “hornets”. Some choices make an implied reference explicit, as when *legno*, ‘wood’, becomes “boat”; others recast an expression more substantially, as when *mi pareva indegna*, ‘seemed to me unworthy’, becomes “seemed to scorn”.

Omissions and additions likewise vary in scale. Carlyle leaves out the personal qualification *mi*, ‘to me’, in the description of the ensign, and does not separately render *Verrai a piaggia*, ‘thou wilt come to shore’. Conversely, “till we reached the stream” supplies an action where *Infino al fiume* gives only an endpoint, and “can be done” supplies the action implicit in *si puote*. Alongside these particular changes are routine grammatical adjustments not individually tracked in the comparison: English subject pronouns are supplied where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and Italian articles before possessives are omitted. The translation thus remains closely tied to the original’s verbal texture while repeatedly reshaping its grammar and, at selected points, interpreting rather than simply reproducing its wording.
