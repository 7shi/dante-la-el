# Inferno 14

## Lines 1–15 — The Edge of the Sandy Plain

> 1 Poichè la carità del natio loco  
> 2 Mi strinse, raunai le fronde sparte,  
> 3 E rende’ le a colui, ch’ era già roco.  

The love of my native place constraining me, I gathered up the scattered leaves; and gave them back to him, who was already hoarse.

- *Poichè … Mi strinse* ‘Since … constrained me’ → “constraining me”: the causal clause with a finite past-tense verb becomes an absolute participial construction; the conjunction is not separately expressed, and the object follows the participle.
- *del natio loco* ‘of the native place’ → “of my native place”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *raunai* ‘I gathered’ → “I gathered up”: the translation adds the particle “up”.
- *le fronde sparte* ‘the leaves scattered’ → “the scattered leaves”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *E rende’ le* ‘and returned them’ → “and gave them back”: the simple verb becomes a phrasal expression.
- *a colui* ‘to that one’ → “to him”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.

> 4 Indi venimmo al fine, ove si parte  
> 5 Lo secondo giron dal terzo, e dove  
> 6 Si vede di giustizia orribil’ arte.  

Then we came to the limit, where the second round is separated from the third, and where is seen the fearful art of justice.

- *Indi* ‘from there’ → “Then”: the translation takes the adverb temporally rather than spatially.
- *al fine* ‘to the end’ → “to the limit”: the endpoint is expressed as a boundary.
- *ove si parte Lo secondo giron dal terzo* ‘where separates itself the second round from the third’ → “where the second round is separated from the third”: the pronominal construction becomes an explicit passive, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *di giustizia orribil’ arte* ‘of justice fearful art’ → “the fearful art of justice”: the modifying phrase moves after its noun, and the definite article is supplied.

> 7 A ben manifestar le cose nuove,  
> 8 Dico ch’ arrivammo ad una landa,  
> 9 Che dal suo letto ogni pianta rimuove.  

To make the new things clear, I say we reached a plain which from its bed repels all plants.

- *A ben manifestar le cose nuove* ‘To make well manifest the things new’ → “To make the new things clear”: the verb is recast as “make” with an adjective following the object; “new” moves before its noun, and *ben* is not separately rendered.
- *Dico ch’* ‘I say that’ → “I say”: the conjunction introducing the reported clause is omitted.
- *arrivammo ad* ‘we arrived at’ → “we reached”: an intransitive verb with a preposition becomes a transitive verb.
- *ogni pianta rimuove* ‘every plant removes’ → “repels all plants”: the verb moves before its object; removal is expressed as repulsion, and the distributive singular becomes a plural with “all”.

> 10 La dolorosa selva l’ è ghirlanda  
> 11 Intorno, come il fosso tristo ad essa:  

The dolorous wood is a garland to it round about, as to the wood the dismal fosse.

- *l’ è ghirlanda Intorno* ‘to it is garland around’ → “is a garland to it round about”: the preceding indirect-object pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase following the noun, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *come il fosso tristo ad essa* ‘as the fosse sad to it’ → “as to the wood the dismal fosse”: the pronoun is replaced by its explicit referent, “the wood”; that phrase moves before “the dismal fosse”, and the adjective moves before its noun, with ‘sad’ rendered as “dismal”.

> 12 Quivi fermammo i piedi a randa a randa.  

Here we stayed our feet close to its very edge.

- *Quivi* ‘there’ → “Here”: the translation changes the spatial demonstrative.
- *fermammo i piedi* ‘we stopped the feet’ → “we stayed our feet”: ‘stopped’ is expressed by “stayed”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *a randa a randa* ‘at the edge, at the edge’ → “close to its very edge”: the repeated expression becomes a phrase of close proximity, with emphasis supplied by “very”; “its” is also supplied.

> 13 Lo spazzo era una rena arida e spessa,  
> 14 Non d’ altra foggia fatta che colei,  
> 15 Che da’ piè di Caton già fu soppressa.  

The ground was a sand, dry and thick, not different in its fashion from that which once was trodden by the feet of Cato.

- *Lo spazzo* ‘the space’ → “The ground”: the word for the open area is rendered as its surface.
- *Non d’ altra foggia fatta che colei* ‘not made in another fashion than that one’ → “not different in its fashion from that”: the participle ‘made’ is omitted; ‘another’ becomes the predicative adjective “different”, the possessive “its” is supplied, and the comparison is reconstructed with “from”.
- *Che da’ piè di Caton già fu soppressa* ‘which by the feet of Cato already was pressed down’ → “which once was trodden by the feet of Cato”: the agent phrase moves after the verb; *già* is interpreted as “once” rather than ‘already’, and ‘pressed down’ is rendered more specifically as “trodden”.

## Lines 16–27 — The Torment of Naked Souls

> 16 O vendetta di Dio, quanto tu dei  
> 17 Esser temuta da ciascun, che legge  
> 18 Ciò che fu manifesto agli occhi miei!  

O vengeance of God! how shouldst thou be feared by every one who reads what was revealed to my eyes!

- *quanto* ‘how much’ → “how”: the explicit expression of degree is reduced to an exclamatory adverb.
- *tu dei Esser temuta* ‘thou oughtest to be feared’ → “shouldst thou be feared”: obligation is expressed with “shouldst”, and the subject follows the modal verb rather than preceding it.
- *da ciascun* ‘by each’ → “by every one”: the distributive pronoun is rendered by a two-word expression.
- *fu manifesto* ‘was manifest’ → “was revealed”: a construction with a predicative adjective becomes a passive verb.
- *agli occhi miei* ‘to the eyes my’ → “to my eyes”: the possessive precedes the noun rather than following it.

> 19 D’ anime nude vidi molte gregge,  
> 20 Che piangean tutte assai miseramente,  

I saw many herds of naked souls, who were all lamenting very miserably;

- *D’ anime nude vidi molte gregge* ‘Of souls naked I saw many herds’ → “I saw many herds of naked souls”: the dependent phrase placed first in Italian follows “herds” in English, and “naked” precedes its noun.
- *piangean tutte* ‘were weeping all’ → “were all lamenting”: weeping becomes the broader expression “lamenting”, and “all” moves between the auxiliary and the participle.

> 21 E parea posta lor diversa legge.  

and there seemed imposed upon them a diverse law:

- *parea* ‘seemed’ → “there seemed”: English supplies anticipatory “there” before the postponed subject.
- *posta lor* ‘set for them’ → “imposed upon them”: the setting of a law is expressed as its imposition, with the corresponding change of preposition.
- *diversa legge* ‘different law’ → “a diverse law”: an indefinite article is supplied.

> 22 Supin giaceva in terra alcuna gente;  

Some were lying supine upon the ground;

- *Supin giaceva in terra* ‘Supine lay on the earth’ → “were lying supine upon the ground”: the predicative adjective moves after the verb; the imperfect becomes an explicitly progressive construction; and the verb becomes plural to agree with “Some”.
- *alcuna gente* ‘some people’ → “Some”: the noun is omitted, the singular collective expression becomes a plural pronoun, and the subject moves from the end of the clause to the beginning.

> 23 Alcuna si sedea tutta raccolta,  

some sitting all crouched up;

- *Alcuna si sedea* ‘Some [people] sat’ → “some sitting”: the singular collective reference becomes plural in English; the finite reflexive verb becomes a participle, with the auxiliary understood from the preceding clause.
- *tutta raccolta* ‘all drawn together’ → “all crouched up”: the participle describing a contracted position becomes an explicit expression of crouching.

> 24 Ed altra andava continuamente.  

and others roaming incessantly.

- *altra andava* ‘other [people] went’ → “others roaming”: the singular collective reference becomes a plural pronoun; going is rendered more specifically as roaming; and the finite imperfect becomes a participle parallel to ‘sitting’.

> 25 Quella che giva intorno era più molta,  

Those that moved about were much more numerous;

- *Quella che giva intorno era più molta* ‘That [people] which went around was more numerous’ → “Those that moved about were much more numerous”: the singular collective reference and its verbs become plural; going around becomes moving about; and “much” supplies additional emphasis to the comparison.

> 26 E quella men, che giaceva al tormento,  
> 27 Ma più al duolo avea la lingua sciolta.  

and those that were lying in the torment were fewer, but uttered louder cries of pain.

- *quella men, che giaceva al tormento* ‘that [people] less, which lay at the torment’ → “those that were lying in the torment were fewer”: the singular collective reference becomes plural; the relative clause moves before the comparison; and “were”, understood from the preceding Italian clause, is supplied explicitly. “Fewer” specifies number, the imperfect becomes progressive, and the preposition is rendered as “in”.
- *più al duolo avea la lingua sciolta* ‘had the tongue looser for pain’ → “uttered louder cries of pain”: the construction about having a looser tongue becomes a direct statement about uttering cries. The comparison is transferred to loudness, the singular ‘tongue’ is replaced by plural “cries”, and the phrase expressing the tongue’s freedom in relation to pain becomes “of pain”, qualifying those cries.

## Lines 28–42 — The Rain of Fire

> 28 Sovra tutto il sabbion d’ un cader lento  
> 29 Piovean di fuoco dilatate falde,  
> 30 Come di neve in alpe senza vento.  

Over all the great sand, falling slowly, rained dilated flakes of fire, like those of snow in Alps without a wind.

- *Sovra tutto il sabbion* ‘over all the sand’ → “Over all the great sand”: “great” is supplied.
- *d’ un cader lento* ‘with a slow falling’ → “falling slowly”: the prepositional phrase becomes a participial phrase, and the adjective becomes an adverb.
- *di fuoco dilatate falde* ‘of fire dilated flakes’ → “dilated flakes of fire”: the material phrase moves after the noun.
- *Come di neve* ‘as of snow’ → “like those of snow”: “those” supplies an explicit pronoun for the flakes understood in the Italian.
- *in alpe* ‘on a mountain’ → “in Alps”: the generic singular mountain designation is interpreted as the plural geographical name.
- *senza vento* ‘without wind’ → “without a wind”: the indefinite article is supplied.

> 31 Quali Alessandro in quelle parti calde  
> 32 D’ India vide sovra lo suo stuolo  
> 33 Fiamme cadere infino a terra salde;  

As the flames which Alexander, in those hot regions of India, saw fall upon his host, entire to the ground;

- *Quali … Fiamme* ‘such as … flames’ → “As the flames which”: the comparison is recast with an explicit relative clause, and “flames” moves before “Alexander”.
- *in quelle parti calde D’ India* ‘in those regions hot of India’ → “in those hot regions of India”: the adjective moves before its noun.
- *infino a terra* ‘all the way to the ground’ → “to the ground”: the emphatic extent expressed by *infino* is not separately rendered.
- *salde* ‘intact’ → “entire”: the adjective moves from after the destination phrase to before it.

> 34 Per ch’ ei provvide a scalpitar lo suolo  
> 35 Con le sue schiere, perciocchè il vapore  
> 36 Me’ si stingueva mentre ch’ era solo:  

whereat he with his legions took care to tramp the soil, for the fire was more easily extinguished while alone:

- *Per ch’* ‘because of which’ → “whereat”: the explicitly causal connection becomes a relative adverb indicating the occasion for the action.
- *provvide a* ‘made provision to’ → “took care to”: provision is expressed through an idiom of care.
- *Con le sue schiere* ‘with his ranks’ → “with his legions”: the general military term becomes the more specific “legions”, and the phrase moves from after the trampling clause to before “took care”.
- *perciocchè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *il vapore* ‘the vapour’ → “the fire”: the translation takes the noun in its contextual sense of fiery heat rather than rendering its ordinary physical designation.
- *Me’* ‘better’ → “more easily”: the comparative is interpreted specifically as ease.
- *si stingueva* ‘was going out’ → “was … extinguished”: the pronominal verb becomes an English passive.
- *mentre ch’ era solo* ‘while it was alone’ → “while alone”: the finite clause is reduced by omitting its subject and copula.

> 37 Tale scendeva l’ eternale ardore;  
> 38 Onde l’ arena s’ accendea, com’ esca  
> 39 Sotto il focile, a doppiar lo dolore,  

so fell the eternal heat, by which the sand was kindled, like tinder under flint and steel, redoubling the pain.

- *Tale* ‘such’ → “so”: the comparative adjective is rendered adverbially.
- *scendeva* ‘descended’ → “fell”: descent is expressed specifically as falling.
- *Onde* ‘whence’ → “by which”: the connection of consequence becomes an explicitly instrumental relative phrase.
- *s’ accendea* ‘was catching fire’ → “was kindled”: the pronominal construction becomes passive.
- *il focile* ‘the fire-steel’ → “flint and steel”: “flint” is supplied, expanding the single implement into a pair, and the definite article is omitted.
- *a doppiar* ‘to double’ → “redoubling”: the infinitive construction becomes a participle expressing the resulting effect.

> 40 Senza riposo mai era la tresca  
> 41 Delle misere mani, or quindi or quinci  
> 42 Iscotendo da se l’ arsura fresca.  

Ever restless was the dance of miserable hands, now here, now there, shaking off the fresh burning.

- *Senza riposo mai* ‘without rest ever’ → “Ever restless”: the phrase expressing absence of rest becomes an adjective, with “Ever” placed first.
- *or quindi or quinci* ‘now from there, now from here’ → “now here, now there”: the deictic order is reversed, and the directional force of ‘from’ is omitted.
- *Iscotendo da se* ‘shaking from themselves’ → “shaking off”: the reflexive prepositional phrase is replaced by the particle “off”.
- *l’ arsura fresca* ‘the burning fresh’ → “the fresh burning”: the adjective moves before the noun.

## Lines 43–58 — Defiance Amid the Fire

> 43 Io cominciai: Maestro, tu che vinci  
> 44 Tutte le cose, fuor che i Dimon duri,  
> 45 Ch’ all’ entrar della porta incontro uscinci,  

I began: “Master, thou who conquerest all things, save the hard Demons, that came forth against us at the entrance of the gate:

- *Tutte le cose* ‘all the things’ → “all things”: the definite article is omitted.
- *Dimon duri* ‘Demons hard’ → “hard Demons”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *all’ entrar della porta* ‘at the entering of the gate’ → “at the entrance of the gate”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun; the phrase moves from before the verb to the end of the relative clause.
- *incontro uscinci* ‘against us came forth’ → “came forth against us”: the verb precedes the phrase expressing opposition; the enclitic *ci* is rendered by “us”.

> 46 Chi è quel grande, che non par che curi  
> 47 Lo incendio, e giace dispettoso e torto,  
> 48 Sì che la pioggia non par che il maturi?  

who is that great spirit, who seems to care not for the fire, and lies disdainful and contorted, so that the rain seems not to ripen him?”

- *quel grande* ‘that great one’ → “that great spirit”: “spirit” supplies a noun for the substantivized adjective.
- *che non par che curi / Lo incendio* ‘who does not seem that he cares for the fire’ → “who seems to care not for the fire”: the subordinate clause with a subjunctive verb becomes an infinitive construction, and the negative moves from the verb of seeming to the verb of caring.
- *la pioggia non par che il maturi* ‘the rain does not seem that it ripens him’ → “the rain seems not to ripen him”: the subordinate subjunctive clause becomes an infinitive construction; the negative moves to the infinitive, and the object pronoun follows it.

> 49 E quel medesmo, che si fue accorto  
> 50 Ch’ io dimandava il mio Duca di lui,  
> 51 Gridò: Qual fui vivo, tal son morto.  

And he himself, remarking that I asked my Guide concerning him, exclaimed: “What I was living, that am I dead.

- *quel medesmo* ‘that same one’ → “he himself”: the demonstrative expression becomes a personal pronoun with an emphatic reflexive.
- *che si fue accorto* ‘who had noticed’ → “remarking”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase, leaving its past time and anteriority unexpressed.
- *dimandava* ‘was asking’ → “asked”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past.
- *Duca* ‘Leader’ → “Guide”: the noun is rendered by a different designation of the role.
- *di lui* ‘of him’ → “concerning him”: the preposition is rendered explicitly as marking the subject of the inquiry.
- *Gridò* ‘cried out’ → “exclaimed”: a verb of shouting becomes a verb of emphatic utterance.

> 52 Se Giove stanchi il suo fabbro, da cui  
> 53 Crucciato prese la folgore acuta,  
> 54 Onde l’ ultimo dì percosso fui;  

Though Jove weary out his smith, from whom in anger he took the sharp bolt with which on my last day I was transfixed;

- *Se* ‘if’ → “Though”: the conditional conjunction is interpreted concessively.
- *stanchi* ‘weary’ → “weary out”: the particle “out” adds emphasis to exhaustion.
- *Crucciato* ‘angered’ → “in anger”: the participial adjective becomes an adverbial prepositional phrase.
- *la folgore acuta* ‘the thunderbolt sharp’ → “the sharp bolt”: “bolt” replaces the more explicit noun, and the adjective precedes it.
- *l’ ultimo dì* ‘the last day’ → “on my last day”: English supplies a preposition for the temporal expression, and “my” replaces the definite article.
- *percosso fui* ‘struck I was’ → “I was transfixed”: the passive auxiliary precedes the participle, and the general sense of being struck becomes the more specific sense of being pierced through.

> 55 E s’ egli stanchi gli altri a muta a muta  
> 56 In Mongibello alla fucina negra,  

and though he weary out the others, one by one, at the black forge in Mongibello,

- *s’* ‘if’ → “though”: the conditional conjunction is again interpreted concessively.
- *stanchi* ‘weary’ → “weary out”: the particle “out” emphasizes exhaustion.
- *a muta a muta* ‘by turns’ → “one by one”: successive turns or shifts are rendered as a succession of individuals.
- *In Mongibello alla fucina negra* ‘in Mongibello at the forge black’ → “at the black forge in Mongibello”: the two prepositional phrases exchange places, and the adjective precedes its noun.

> 57 Gridando: Buon Vulcano, aiuta, aiuta,  
> 58 Sì com’ ei fece alla pugna di Flegra;  

crying: ‘Help, help, good Vulcan!’ as he did at the strife of Phlegra;

- *Buon Vulcano, aiuta, aiuta* ‘Good Vulcan, help, help’ → “Help, help, good Vulcan!”: the vocative moves after the repeated imperative.
- *Sì com’* ‘so as’ → “as”: the introductory *Sì* is absorbed into the single comparative conjunction.
- *pugna* ‘battle’ → “strife”: the specific word for combat is rendered by a broader word for conflict.

## Lines 59–74 — Pride and Its Punishment

> 59 E me saetti di tutta sua forza,  
> 60 Non ne potrebbe aver vendetta allegra.  

and hurl at me with all his might, yet should he not thereby have joyful vengeance.”

- *me saetti* ‘let him strike me with thunderbolts’ → “hurl at me”: the verb’s specific reference to thunderbolts is left implicit; the direct object becomes a prepositional phrase following the verb.
- *Non ne potrebbe aver* ‘he could not thereby have’ → “yet should he not thereby have”: “yet” is supplied to mark the contrast; ‘could’ becomes “should,” and the subject follows the modal verb.
- *vendetta allegra* ‘vengeance joyful’ → “joyful vengeance”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 61 Allora il Duca mio parlò di forza  
> 62 Tanto, ch’ io non l’ avea sì forte udito:  

Then my Guide spake with a force such as I had not heard before:

- *il Duca mio* ‘my leader’ → “my Guide”: the title is rendered as “Guide,” and the possessive, which follows the Italian noun, precedes the English noun.
- *parlò di forza Tanto, ch’ io non l’ avea sì forte udito* ‘spoke with such force that I had not heard him so forcefully’ → “spake with a force such as I had not heard before”: the degree-and-result construction becomes a comparison qualifying “force”; the object ‘him’ disappears, the repeated description of forcefulness is compressed, and “before” is supplied.

> 63 O Capaneo, in ciò che non s’ ammorza  
> 64 La tua superbia, sei tu più punito:  

“O Capaneus! in that thy pride remains unquenched, thou art punished more.

- *non s’ ammorza La tua superbia* ‘is not extinguished thy pride’ → “thy pride remains unquenched”: the subject moves before the predicate, and the negative verb becomes “remains” with a negative participial adjective, making persistence explicit.
- *sei tu più punito* ‘art thou more punished’ → “thou art punished more”: the subject precedes the verb, and “more” follows the participle.

> 65 Nullo martirio, fuor che la tua rabbia,  
> 66 Sarebbe al tuo furor dolor compito.  

No torture, except thy own raving, would be pain proportioned to thy fury.”

- *la tua rabbia* ‘thy rage’ → “thy own raving”: “own” is supplied for emphasis, and the emotion is rendered as its frenzied expression.
- *Sarebbe al tuo furor dolor compito* ‘would be for thy fury complete pain’ → “would be pain proportioned to thy fury”: *compito* is interpreted as adequate or commensurate rather than simply complete; the phrase relating the pain to the fury moves after the noun and its qualification.

> 67 Poi si rivolse a me con miglior labbia,  

Then to me he turned with gentler lip,

- *si rivolse a me* ‘he turned himself to me’ → “to me he turned”: the reflexive pronoun is omitted, and the prepositional phrase precedes the subject and verb.
- *con miglior labbia* ‘with better countenance’ → “with gentler lip”: the general comparative ‘better’ becomes “gentler,” and *labbia*, meaning countenance or facial expression, is represented by “lip.”

> 68 Dicendo: Quel fu l’ un de’ sette regi  
> 69 Ch’ assiser Tebe; ed ebbe, e par ch’ egli abbia  
> 70 Dio in disdegno, e poco par che il pregi:  

saying: “That was the one of the seven kings who laid siege to Thebes; and he held, and seems to hold, God in defiance and prize him lightly.

- *Ch’ assiser Tebe* ‘who besieged Thebes’ → “who laid siege to Thebes”: the simple verb becomes a verbal phrase, with its direct object introduced by “to.”
- *ed ebbe, e par ch’ egli abbia Dio in disdegno* ‘and he had, and it seems that he has, God in disdain’ → “and he held, and seems to hold, God in defiance”: ‘have in disdain’ becomes ‘hold in defiance’; the impersonal construction with a finite subordinate clause becomes a personal construction with an infinitive.
- *e poco par che il pregi* ‘and little it seems that he values him’ → “and prize him lightly”: the repeated *par che* is omitted, with the preceding “seems to” governing “prize” as well as “hold.” The finite subjunctive becomes an infinitive; ‘little’ becomes “lightly,” and both the object pronoun and the adverb follow the verb.

> 71 Ma, come io dissi lui, li suoi dispetti  
> 72 Sono al suo petto assai debiti fregi.  

But, as I told him, his revilings are ornaments that well befit his breast.

- *dissi lui* ‘said to him’ → “told him”: the verb changes from ‘say’ to ‘tell,’ taking the person addressed without a preposition.
- *li suoi dispetti* ‘his scornful acts’ → “his revilings”: the broader expression of contempt is rendered specifically as verbal abuse.
- *Sono al suo petto assai debiti fregi* ‘are to his breast much-due ornaments’ → “are ornaments that well befit his breast”: the adjective *debiti* becomes the verb “befit” in an added relative clause, while *assai* becomes “well.” The phrase ‘to his breast’ moves after the noun and becomes the object of “befit.”

> 73 Or mi vien dietro, e guarda che non metti  
> 74 Ancor li piedi nell’ arena arsiccia;  

Now follow me, and see thou place not yet thy feet upon the burning sand;

- *mi vien dietro* ‘come behind me’ → “follow me”: the verb and directional expression become a single transitive verb, with the pronoun following it.
- *guarda che non metti* ‘see that thou dost not put’ → “see thou place not”: the conjunction is omitted, and the negative follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *li piedi* ‘the feet’ → “thy feet”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *nell’ arena arsiccia* ‘in the scorched sand’ → “upon the burning sand”: “upon” replaces ‘in,’ and the adjective describing the sand as scorched becomes “burning,” presenting the heat as ongoing.

## Lines 75–84 — A Red Stream Across the Sand

> 75 Ma sempre al bosco gli ritieni stretti.  

but always keep them back close to the wood.”

- *gli ritieni* ‘them hold back’ → “keep them back”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *al bosco ... stretti* ‘to the wood ... close’ → “close to the wood”: the Italian places the prepositional phrase before the word it complements; English reverses that order.

> 76 Tacendo divenimmo là ove spiccia  
> 77 Fuor della selva un picciol fiumicello,  
> 78 Lo cui rossore ancor mi raccapriccia.  

In silence we came to where there gushes forth from the wood a little rivulet, the redness of which still makes me shudder.

- *Tacendo* ‘keeping silent’ → “In silence”: the verbal gerund becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *divenimmo là ove* ‘we arrived there where’ → “we came to where”: arriving is expressed as coming; *là* is not separately rendered, and “to” introduces the destination.
- *spiccia* ‘gushes’ → “there gushes”: English supplies introductory “there” before the verb and its following subject.
- *Lo cui rossore* ‘whose redness’ → “the redness of which”: the possessive relative construction becomes a noun followed by an “of” phrase.
- *mi raccapriccia* ‘me makes-shudder’ → “makes me shudder”: the single Italian verb becomes an English causative phrase, with the object pronoun after “makes”.

> 79 Quale del Bulicame esce il ruscello,  
> 80 Che parton poi tra lor le peccatrici;  
> 81 Tal per l’ arena giù sen giva quello.  

As from the Bulicame issues the streamlet, which the sinful women share amongst themselves; so this ran down across the sand.

- *parton poi tra lor le peccatrici* ‘divide then among themselves the female sinners’ → “the sinful women share amongst themselves”: the subject moves before the verb; dividing is expressed as sharing; *poi* is omitted; and the feminine noun becomes the adjective-and-noun phrase “sinful women”.
- *per l’ arena giù sen giva quello* ‘across the sand down went that one on its way’ → “this ran down across the sand”: the subject moves from last to first, and the phrase describing the path moves to the end. “this” replaces the more distant demonstrative *quello*, while the pronominal expression for going on one’s way becomes “ran”.

> 82 Lo fondo suo ed ambo le pendici  
> 83 Fatt’ eran pietra, e i margini da lato:  

Its bottom and both its shelving banks were petrified, and also the margins near it;

- *Lo fondo suo* ‘the bottom its’ → “Its bottom”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *ambo le pendici* ‘both the slopes’ → “both its shelving banks”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “shelving banks” expresses the slopes as sloping riverbanks.
- *Fatt’ eran pietra* ‘made were stone’ → “were petrified”: the auxiliary moves before the participle, and the construction with the noun *pietra* is compressed into the verb “petrified”.
- *e i margini* ‘and the margins’ → “and also the margins”: “also” is supplied.
- *da lato* ‘at the side’ → “near it”: a position at the side becomes proximity, with “it” supplying an explicit reference point.

> 84 Per ch’ io m’ accorsi che il passo era lici.  

whereby I discerned that our passage lay there.

- *io m’ accorsi* ‘I became aware’ → “I discerned”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes a simple, non-pronominal English verb.
- *il passo* ‘the passage’ → “our passage”: the definite article is replaced by a supplied possessive.
- *era* ‘was’ → “lay”: the verb of being becomes a verb expressing location.

## Lines 85–98 — The Stream and Crete

> 85 Tra tutto l’ altro ch’ io t’ ho dimostrato,  
> 86 Poscia che noi entrammo per la porta,  
> 87 Lo cui sogliare a nessuno è negato,  

“Amidst all the rest that I have shown thee, since we entered by the gate whose threshold is denied to none,

- *tutto l’altro* ‘all the other [things]’ → “all the rest”: the substantivized adjective *altro* becomes the noun “rest”.
- *io t’ho dimostrato* ‘I to thee have shown’ → “I have shown thee”: the object pronoun moves from before the auxiliary to after the participle.
- *per la porta* ‘through the gate’ → “by the gate”: passage through the gate is expressed with “by” rather than ‘through’.
- *Lo cui sogliare a nessuno è negato* ‘the threshold of which to no one is denied’ → “whose threshold is denied to none”: the possessive relative becomes “whose”, and the recipient phrase moves after the verb.

> 88 Cosa non fu dagli tuoi occhi scorta  
> 89 Notabile, com’ è il presente rio,  
> 90 Che sopra sè tutte fiammelle ammorta.  

thy eyes have discerned nothing so notable as the present stream, which quenches all the flames above it.”

- *Cosa non fu dagli tuoi occhi scorta* ‘A thing was not by thy eyes discerned’ → “thy eyes have discerned nothing”: the passive becomes active, with “thy eyes” as subject; the simple past becomes a present perfect; and the indefinite noun with a negated verb becomes the negative pronoun “nothing”.
- *Notabile, com’è il presente rio* ‘notable, as is the present stream’ → “so notable as the present stream”: “so” is supplied to form the English comparison, and the explicit verb *è* is omitted.
- *Che sopra sè tutte fiammelle ammorta* ‘which above itself all little flames extinguishes’ → “which quenches all the flames above it”: the verb moves before its object, and the phrase expressing position moves to the end; the reflexive *sè* becomes “it”; the diminutive force of *fiammelle* is dropped; and “the” is supplied before “flames”.

> 91 Queste parole fur del Duca mio:  

These were words of my Guide.

- *Queste parole fur del Duca mio* ‘These words were of my Leader’ → “These were words of my Guide”: “These” stands alone as the subject, while “words” moves into the predicate; *Duca* is rendered as “Guide” rather than ‘Leader’, and the possessive moves before its noun.

> 92 Per ch’ io pregai, che mi largisse il pasto,  
> 93 Di cui largito m’ aveva il disio.  

Wherefore I prayed him to bestow on me the food, for which he had bestowed the appetite.

- *io pregai* ‘I prayed’ → “I prayed him”: “him” is supplied as the person addressed.
- *che mi largisse il pasto* ‘that he might bestow on me the food’ → “to bestow on me the food”: the finite clause with a subjunctive verb becomes an infinitive construction dependent on “prayed him”.
- *Di cui largito m’aveva il disio* ‘for which bestowed on me he had the desire’ → “for which he had bestowed the appetite”: the inverted participle–auxiliary order becomes the ordinary English auxiliary–participle order; *m’*, ‘on me’, is omitted; and “appetite” replaces ‘desire’, extending the wording associated with “food”.

> 94 In mezzo il mar siede un paese guasto,  
> 95 Diss’ egli allora, che s’ appella Creta,  
> 96 Sotto il cui Rege fu già il mondo casto.  

“In the middle of the sea lies a waste country,” he then said, “which is named Crete, under whose King the world once was chaste.

- *siede un paese guasto* ‘sits a country laid waste’ → “lies a waste country”: “lies” replaces ‘sits’, and the description *guasto* moves before the noun as the adjective “waste”.
- *Diss’egli allora* ‘Said he then’ → “he then said”: the subject and adverb move before the verb.
- *che s’appella Creta* ‘which calls itself Crete’ → “which is named Crete”: the Italian pronominal construction is rendered by an English passive.
- *fu già il mondo casto* ‘was once the world chaste’ → “the world once was chaste”: the subject moves before the verb, with “once” placed between them.

> 97 Una montagna v’ è, che già fu lieta  
> 98 D’ acque e di fronde, che si chiama Ida;  

A mountain is there, called Ida, which once was glad with waters and with foliage:

- *Una montagna v’è* ‘A mountain there is’ → “A mountain is there”: the locative “there” moves after the verb.
- *D’acque e di fronde* ‘of waters and of foliage’ → “with waters and with foliage”: both occurrences of *di* become “with”, expressing what furnished the mountain rather than retaining ‘of’.
- *che si chiama Ida* ‘which is called Ida’ → “called Ida”: the finite relative clause becomes a participial phrase and moves ahead of the clause beginning “which once was glad”.

## Lines 99–108 — The Old Man Within the Mountain

> 99 Ora è deserta come cosa vieta.  

now it is deserted like an antiquated thing.

- *cosa vieta* ‘thing antiquated’ → “an antiquated thing”: the indefinite article is supplied, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 100 Rea la scelse già per cuna fida  
> 101 Del suo figliuolo, e per celarlo meglio,  
> 102 Quando piangea, vi facea far le grida.  

Rhea of old chose it for the faithful cradle of her son; and the better to conceal him, when he wept, caused cries to be made on it.

- *la scelse già* ‘it chose formerly’ → “of old chose it”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, while the temporal adverb becomes “of old” and moves before it.
- *per cuna fida* ‘for cradle faithful’ → “for the faithful cradle”: the definite article is supplied, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *per celarlo meglio* ‘to conceal him better’ → “the better to conceal him”: the comparative adverb moves before the infinitive, and “the” is supplied.
- *vi* ‘there’ → “on it”: the locative pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase and moves to the end of the clause.
- *facea far le grida* ‘made [people] make the cries’ → “caused cries to be made”: the causative construction with an active infinitive becomes “caused” plus a passive infinitive; the definite article before the noun is omitted.

> 103 Dentro dal monte sta dritto un gran veglio,  
> 104 Che tien volte le spalle invêr Damiata,  
> 105 E Roma guarda sì come suo speglio.  

“Within the mountain stands erect a great Old Man, who keeps his shoulders turned towards Damietta, and looks at Rome as if it were his mirror.

- *Dentro dal monte* ‘inside of the mountain’ → “Within the mountain”: the composite Italian preposition becomes the single English preposition “Within”.
- *tien volte le spalle* ‘keeps turned the shoulders’ → “keeps his shoulders turned”: the participle moves after the object, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Roma guarda* ‘Rome he regards’ → “looks at Rome”: the object moves after the verb; the directly transitive Italian verb becomes “looks” with the preposition “at”.
- *sì come suo speglio* ‘just as his mirror’ → “as if it were his mirror”: the compact comparison becomes an explicit hypothetical clause, supplying “if it were”.

> 106 La sua testa è di fino oro formata,  

His head is shapen of fine gold,

- *è di fino oro formata* ‘is of fine gold formed’ → “is shapen of fine gold”: the participle moves before the phrase expressing the material.

> 107 E puro argento son le braccia e il petto;  

his arms and his breast are pure silver;

- *E puro argento son le braccia e il petto* ‘and pure silver are the arms and the breast’ → “his arms and his breast are pure silver”: the initial conjunction is omitted; the subject moves before the verb and the predicative phrase after it; both definite articles become possessives.

> 108 Poi è di rame infino alla forcata:  

then he is of brass to the cleft:

- *di rame* ‘of copper’ → “of brass”: a different metal is named.
- *infino alla forcata* ‘up to the fork’ → “to the cleft”: “to” renders the fuller expression of extent, while the anatomical ‘fork’ or ‘crotch’ is rendered as “cleft”.

## Lines 109–123 — The Tears Become Infernal Rivers

> 109 Da indi in giuso è tutto ferro eletto,  
> 110 Salvo che il destro piede è terra cotta,  
> 111 E sta in su quel, più che in su l’ altro, eretto.  

from thence downwards he is all of chosen iron, save that the right foot is of baked clay; and he rests more on this than on the other.

- *tutto ferro eletto* ‘all iron chosen’ → “all of chosen iron”: “of” is supplied to express material, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *è terra cotta* ‘is baked earth’ → “is of baked clay”: “of” is supplied, and *terra* is rendered more specifically as “clay”.
- *sta … eretto* ‘stands … upright’ → “rests”: standing upright becomes resting; *eretto* has no separate equivalent.
- *in su quel, più che in su l’altro* ‘on that, more than on the other’ → “more on this than on the other”: “more” moves before the first prepositional phrase, and *quel* becomes “this” rather than ‘that’.

> 112 Ciascuna parte, fuor che l’ oro, è rotta  
> 113 D’ una fessura che lagrime goccia,  
> 114 Le quali accolte foran quella grotta.  

Every part, except the gold, is broken with a fissure that drops tears, which collected perforate that grotto.

- *lagrime goccia* ‘tears drops’ → “drops tears”: the object moves after the verb.

> 115 Lor corso in questa valle si diroccia:  

Their course descends from rock to rock into this valley.

- *in questa valle si diroccia* ‘into this valley precipitates itself’ → “descends from rock to rock into this valley”: the destination moves from before the verb to after it. The reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb; “from rock to rock” expands the verb’s suggestion of a rocky descent.

> 116 Fanno Acheronte, Stige, e Flegetonta;  
> 117 Poi sen van giù per questa stretta doccia  
> 118 Infin là, ove più non si dismonta:  

They form Acheron, Styx, and Phlegethon; then, by this narrow conduit, go down to where there is no more descent.

- *Fanno* ‘make’ → “form”: making is expressed as constituting or forming.
- *sen van giù* ‘go away down’ → “go down”: the pronominal expression *se ne vanno* becomes a simple verb of motion, without separately expressing departure.
- *per questa stretta doccia* ‘through this narrow channel’ → “by this narrow conduit”: “by” replaces ‘through’, and *doccia* is interpreted as a conduit; the phrase moves before “go down”.
- *Infin là, ove* ‘as far as there, where’ → “to where”: the expression marking the endpoint is compressed.
- *più non si dismonta* ‘one descends no farther’ → “there is no more descent”: the impersonal verbal construction becomes an existential construction with the noun “descent”; spatial ‘farther’ becomes “more”.

> 119 Fanno Cocito; e qual sia quello stagno,  
> 120 Tu il vederai; però qui non si conta.  

They form Cocytus; and thou shalt see what kind of lake that is: here therefore I describe it not.”

- *Fanno* ‘make’ → “form”: making is again expressed as forming.
- *qual sia quello stagno, Tu il vederai* ‘what that pool may be, thou wilt see it’ → “thou shalt see what kind of lake that is”: the embedded question moves after the main verb, and the resumptive *il* is omitted. *Qual* becomes “what kind of”, *stagno* becomes “lake”, and *quello*, originally modifying the noun, becomes the pronoun “that”. The subjunctive *sia* is rendered by indicative “is”.
- *però qui* ‘therefore here’ → “here therefore”: the adverbs exchange positions.
- *non si conta* ‘it is not recounted’ → “I describe it not”: the impersonal construction becomes an explicit first-person statement, recounting becomes describing, and the negative moves after the verb and object.

> 121 Ed io a lui: Se il presente rigagno  
> 122 Si deriva così dal nostro mondo,  
> 123 Perchè ci appar pure a questo vivagno?  

And I to him: “If the present rill thus flows down from our world, why does it appear to us only on this bank?”

- *Si deriva così* ‘is derived thus’ → “thus flows down”: the passive or reflexive expression of derivation becomes an intransitive expression of downward movement, and the adverb moves before the verb.
- *Perchè ci appar* ‘why to us appears’ → “why does it appear to us”: English supplies the interrogative auxiliary “does” and places “to us” after the verb.
- *a questo vivagno* ‘at this edge’ → “on this bank”: *vivagno* is interpreted specifically as a bank, and “on” replaces ‘at’.

## Lines 124–135 — The Circle and the Rivers

> 124 Ed egli a me: Tu sai che il luogo è tondo,  

And he to me: “Thou knowest that the place is round:

- Literal.

> 125 E tutto che tu sii venuto molto  
> 126 Pur a sinistra giù calando al fondo,  
> 127 Non se’ ancor per tutto il cerchio vôlto;  

and though thou hast come far, always to the left, descending towards the bottom; thou hast not yet turned through the entire circle.

- *tutto che tu sii venuto molto* ‘although thou have come much’ → “though thou hast come far”: the Italian perfect subjunctive becomes an English present perfect indicative; “far” expresses *molto* as distance rather than quantity.
- *giù calando al fondo* ‘down descending to the bottom’ → “descending towards the bottom”: *giù* is not separately rendered, its direction being implicit in “descending”; “towards” replaces “to”.
- *Non se’ ancor per tutto il cerchio vôlto* ‘thou art not yet through all the circle turned’ → “thou hast not yet turned through the entire circle”: English uses “hast” rather than the Italian auxiliary ‘art’; “turned” moves before the prepositional phrase, and ‘all the circle’ becomes “the entire circle”.

> 128 Perchè, se cosa n’ apparisce nuova,  
> 129 Non dee addur maraviglia al tuo volto.  

Wherefore if aught new appears to us, it ought not to bring wonder on thy countenance.”

- *se cosa n’apparisce nuova* ‘if something to us appears new’ → “if aught new appears to us”: “aught” renders the indefinite *cosa*; “new” moves next to it, while the pronoun *n’*, expanded into “to us”, moves after the verb.
- *Non dee addur* ‘not ought to bring’ → “it ought not to bring”: the negative follows the modal in English rather than preceding it.
- *al tuo volto* ‘to thy face’ → “on thy countenance”: “on” replaces the Italian preposition of direction, and “countenance” is a more formal equivalent of ‘face’.

> 130 Ed io ancor: Maestro, ove si trova  
> 131 Flegetonte e Letéo, chè dell’ un taci,  
> 132 E l’ altro di’ che si fa d’ esta piova?  

And I again: “Master, where is Phlegethon and Lethe found; for thou speakest not of the one, and sayest the other is formed by this rain?”

- *ove si trova Flegetonte e Letéo* ‘where is found Phlegethon and Lethe’ → “where is Phlegethon and Lethe found”: the names move between the auxiliary and participle in the English question.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *dell’un taci* ‘of the one thou art silent’ → “thou speakest not of the one”: the affirmative expression of silence becomes a negative expression of speaking; the prepositional phrase moves after the verb.
- *E l’altro di’ che* ‘and the other thou sayest that’ → “and sayest the other”: the fronted *l’altro* moves after “sayest”, becoming the subject of the following English clause; *che* is not explicitly rendered.
- *si fa d’esta piova* ‘is made from this rain’ → “is formed by this rain”: “formed” replaces ‘made’, while “by” presents the rain as the cause or means rather than explicitly as the material source.

> 133 In tutte tue question certo mi piaci,  
> 134 Rispose; ma il bollor dell’ acqua rossa  
> 135 Dovea ben solver l’ una che tu faci.  

“In all thy questions truly thou pleasest me,” he answered; “but the boiling of the red water might well resolve one of those thou askest.

- *certo* ‘certainly’ → “truly”: an expression of certainty becomes an affirmation of truth.
- *mi piaci* ‘to me thou art pleasing’ → “thou pleasest me”: the Italian indirect-object pronoun before the verb becomes the direct object after the English verb.
- *acqua rossa* ‘water red’ → “red water”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Dovea ben solver* ‘ought well to have resolved’ → “might well resolve”: the retrospective expectation expressed by the imperfect *dovea* is softened into possibility with “might”; the past reference is less explicit.
- *l’una che tu faci* ‘the one which thou makest’ → “one of those thou askest”: the definite singular becomes a partitive expression with plural “those”; the relative pronoun is omitted, and ‘makest’ becomes “askest”, the English verb appropriate to questions.

## Lines 136–142 — Seeing Lethe and Leaving the Wood

> 136 Letè vedrai, ma fuor di questa fossa,  
> 137 Là ove vanno l’ anime a lavarsi,  
> 138 Quando la colpa pentuta è rimossa.  

Lethe thou shalt see, but out of this abyss, there where the spirits go to wash themselves, when their guilt is taken off by penitence.”

- *fossa* ‘pit’ → “abyss”: a stronger noun replaces the ordinary word for a pit or ditch.
- *vanno l’ anime* ‘go the souls’ → “the spirits go”: “spirits” replaces ‘souls’, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *la colpa* ‘the guilt’ → “their guilt”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *pentuta è rimossa* ‘repented of is removed’ → “is taken off by penitence”: the participle modifying ‘guilt’ becomes a phrase expressing the means of its removal; ‘is removed’ becomes “is taken off”.

> 139 Poi disse: Omai è tempo da scostarsi  
> 140 Dal bosco: fa che diretro a me vegne.  

Then he said: “Now it is time to quit the wood. See that thou follow me.

- *scostarsi Dal bosco* ‘move away from the wood’ → “quit the wood”: the pronominal verb with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object.
- *fa che diretro a me vegne* ‘make it so that behind me thou come’ → “See that thou follow me”: the imperative ‘make’ becomes “See”; ‘come behind me’ becomes “follow me”, replacing the expression of position with a direct object and moving the verb before it.

> 141 Li margini fan via, che non son arsi,  
> 142 E sopra loro ogni vapor si spegne.  

The margins, which are not burning, form a path; and over them all fire is quenched.”

- *fan* ‘make’ → “form”: the verb is rendered by a different term for constituting the path.
- *che non son arsi* ‘which are not burnt’ → “which are not burning”: the relative clause moves from after ‘make a path’ to immediately after its antecedent, “The margins”; the past participle becomes an active progressive construction.
- *ogni vapor* ‘every vapour’ → “all fire”: “fire” interprets rather than literally renders *vapor*, and the distributive singular becomes a mass expression.
- *si spegne* ‘extinguishes itself’ → “is quenched”: the pronominal construction becomes an English passive.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Syntax

Carlyle keeps much of the Italian’s vocabulary, imagery, and rhetorical shape, while frequently rearranging its syntax for English prose. Adjectives generally move before their nouns, objects follow their verbs, and modifying phrases follow the words they qualify: *di fuoco dilatate falde* becomes “dilated flakes of fire”. Yet he also preserves conspicuous inversions, as in “Ever restless was the dance of miserable hands”, and follows repeated or balanced expressions closely, notably “What I was living, that am I dead.”

Changes of construction often matter more than changes of vocabulary. Finite clauses become participial or infinitive phrases: *che si fue accorto* becomes “remarking”, and *par ch’ egli abbia* becomes “seems to hold”. Italian pronominal verbs frequently become English passives, as *si spegne* becomes “is quenched”. Elsewhere the direction is reversed: *Cosa non fu dagli tuoi occhi scorta* becomes “thy eyes have discerned nothing”, replacing a passive with an active construction and changing the tense. Compact descriptions may also be expanded into clauses, as *assai debiti fregi* becomes “ornaments that well befit his breast”.

Word choices range from close, sometimes markedly literal equivalents to interpretive substitutions. “dolorous”, “dilated”, and “ripen” retain distinctive features of the Italian wording. By contrast, *vapore* becomes “fire”, *miglior labbia* becomes “gentler lip”, and *dolor compito* becomes “pain proportioned to thy fury”. The rendering of *avea la lingua sciolta* as “uttered louder cries” replaces the bodily expression with an explicit description of its effect. Small additions also clarify or emphasize: *quel grande* becomes “that great spirit”, *il focile* becomes “flint and steel”, and *la tua rabbia* becomes “thy own raving”. Conversely, words such as *ben*, *poi*, and the emphatic element of *infino* sometimes receive no separate equivalent.

Alongside these individual departures are routine grammatical adjustments not recorded throughout the comparison. English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits the Italian definite article before possessives. Definite articles also sometimes become possessives, as *i piedi* becomes “our feet”. Taken together, these changes produce readable English prose without consistently smoothing away the source’s unusual phrasing: Carlyle remains close to its sequence and verbal texture, but freely redistributes grammatical information and occasionally makes an interpretation more explicit than the Italian wording itself.
