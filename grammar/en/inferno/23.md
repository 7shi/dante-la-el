# Inferno 23

## Lines 1–9 — Silent Travel and a Remembered Fable

> 1 Taciti, soli, e senza compagnia  
> 2 N’ andavam l’ un dinanzi e l’ altro dopo,  

Silent, apart, and without escort we went on, the one before and the other after;

- *soli* ‘alone’ → “apart”: being alone is expressed as being apart.
- *senza compagnia* ‘without company’ → “without escort”: “escort” gives a narrower sense to *compagnia*.
- *N’ andavam* ‘we were going away’ → “we went on”: the pronominal expression is rendered with “on”, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.

> 3 Come i frati Minor vanno per via.  

as the Minor Friars go their way.

- *i frati Minor* ‘the friars minor’ → “the Minor Friars”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *per via* ‘along the road’ → “their way”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverbial noun phrase, with a possessive supplied.

> 4 Volto era in su la favola d’ Isopo  
> 5 Lo mio pensier per la presente rissa,  
> 6 Dov’ ei parlò della rana e del topo:  

My thought was turned, by the present strife, to Æsop’s fable where he speaks of the frog and mouse;

- *Volto era … Lo mio pensier* ‘turned was … my thought’ → “My thought was turned”: the subject moves before the verb, and the participle follows the auxiliary.
- *in su la favola d’ Isopo* ‘upon the fable of Æsop’ → “to Æsop’s fable”: “to” replaces the preposition of position; the following *of*-phrase becomes a possessive before the noun.
- *per la presente rissa* ‘because of the present brawl’ → “by the present strife”: the causal phrase is expressed with “by”; “strife” replaces the more concrete ‘brawl’, and the phrase moves before the reference to the fable.
- *Dov’ ei parlò* ‘where he spoke’ → “where he speaks”: the past tense becomes a present tense.
- *della rana e del topo* ‘of the frog and of the mouse’ → “of the frog and mouse”: the second preposition and article are omitted.

> 7 Chè più non si pareggia mo ed issa,  
> 8 Che l’ un coll’ altro fa, se ben s’ accoppia  
> 9 Principio e fine con la mente fissa:  

for Ay and Yea pair not better, than does the one case with the other, if with attentive mind the beginning and end of each be well accoupled.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *più non si pareggia* ‘do not match each other more’ → “pair not better”: the reciprocal construction becomes the intransitive “pair”, ‘more’ becomes “better”, and the verb phrase moves after its subject.
- *mo ed issa* ‘now and now’ → “Ay and Yea”: two synonymous expressions meaning ‘now’ are replaced by two synonymous expressions of affirmation.
- *Che l’ un coll’ altro fa* ‘than the one with the other does’ → “than does the one case with the other”: “case” is supplied, and the verb moves before the subject.
- *ben s’ accoppia* ‘well is coupled’ → “be well accoupled”: the Italian present indicative becomes an English subjunctive; the verb moves after its subject, and “well” moves between the auxiliary and participle.
- *Principio e fine* ‘beginning and end’ → “the beginning and end of each”: the article and “of each” are supplied.
- *con la mente fissa* ‘with the mind fixed’ → “with attentive mind”: “attentive” interprets ‘fixed’ as concentrated attention; the article is omitted, the adjective precedes the noun, and the phrase moves before the subject of the conditional clause.

## Lines 10–24 — Fear of Pursuit

> 10 E come l’ un pensier dell’ altro scoppia,  
> 11 Così nacque di quello un altro poi,  
> 12 Che la prima paura mi fe’ doppia.  

And as one thought from the other springs, so arose from that another then, which made my first fear double.

- *l’ un pensier* ‘the one thought’ → “one thought”: the definite article is omitted.
- *scoppia* ‘bursts forth’ → “springs”: the verb of bursting becomes one of springing forth.
- *nacque* ‘was born’ → “arose”: the metaphor of birth is replaced by a verb of arising.
- *la prima paura mi fe’ doppia* ‘the first fear it made double for me’ → “made my first fear double”: the verb precedes its object, and the affected-person pronoun *mi* is expressed through the possessive “my”.

> 13 Io pensava così: Questi per noi  
> 14 Sono scherniti, e con danno e con beffa  
> 15 Sì fatta, ch’ assai credo che lor nôi.  

I thus bethought me: “These through us are put to scorn, and with damage and mockery of such sort, as I believe must greatly vex them.

- *Io pensava così* ‘I was thinking thus’ → “I thus bethought me”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, “thus” moves before the verb, and the non-reflexive Italian verb becomes the reflexive expression “bethought me”.
- *Sono scherniti* ‘are mocked’ → “are put to scorn”: a single participle is expanded into a verbal phrase.
- *e con beffa* ‘and with mockery’ → “and mockery”: the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *Sì fatta* ‘so fashioned’ → “of such sort”: the participial expression, agreeing with *beffa*, becomes a prepositional phrase describing its kind.
- *ch’* ‘that’ → “as”: the connective following the expression of degree or kind is changed.
- *assai credo che lor nôi* ‘much I believe that it vexes them’ → “I believe must greatly vex them”: “greatly” is placed directly before “vex”, making its attachment explicit; the second *che* is omitted, and the subjunctive *nôi* is rendered with the added modal “must”.

> 16 Se l’ ira sovra il mal voler s’ aggueffa,  
> 17 Ei ne verranno dietro più crudeli,  
> 18 Che cane a quella levre ch’ egli acceffa.  

If rage be added to their malice, they will pursue us, fiercer than a dog that leveret which he snaps!”

- *l’ ira* ‘the anger’ → “rage”: the definite article is omitted, and the stronger English noun is chosen.
- *sovra il mal voler* ‘upon the evil will’ → “to their malice”: the phrase naming an evil disposition is compressed into “malice”, “their” replaces the definite article, and ‘upon’ becomes “to”.
- *s’ aggueffa* ‘winds itself into a skein’ → “be added”: the figurative reflexive verb becomes a nonfigurative passive; the Italian present indicative is rendered by the English subjunctive “be”.
- *ne verranno dietro* ‘will come behind us’ → “will pursue us”: the expression of movement becomes a transitive verb of pursuit, with *ne* represented by its direct object.
- *più crudeli* ‘more cruel’ → “fiercer”: cruelty is expressed as fierceness, and the two-word comparative becomes an inflected comparative.
- *cane* ‘dog’ → “a dog”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *a quella levre* ‘after that hare’ → “that leveret”: the preposition is omitted, leaving an object for an understood verb of pursuit; “leveret” specifies a young hare, whereas *levre* does not specify age.
- *acceffa* ‘seizes with his muzzle’ → “snaps”: the muzzle-specific verb is rendered by a shorter English verb suggesting a snapping seizure.

> 19 Già mi sentia tutto arricciar li peli  
> 20 Della paura, e stava indietro intento,  

Already I felt my hair all rise with fear; and was looking back intently,

- *mi sentia tutto arricciar li peli* ‘I felt the hairs bristle all over me’ → “I felt my hair all rise”: the reflexive reference is expressed through “my”; plural *peli* becomes the collective singular “hair”; the verb follows “hair” rather than preceding it; and bristling is rendered more generally as rising. The imperfect *sentia* becomes a simple past.
- *Della paura* ‘from the fear’ → “with fear”: the causal expression is rendered with “with”, and the definite article is omitted.
- *e stava indietro intento* ‘and remained intent behind’ → “and was looking back intently”: the state of being attentive becomes an explicit act of looking; *stava* helps form a progressive construction, and the adjective *intento* becomes the adverb “intently”.

> 21 Quando io dissi: Maestro, se non celi  
> 22 Te e me tostamente, io pavento  
> 23 Di Malebranche: noi gli avem già dietro:  

as I said: “Master, if thou do not hide thyself and me speedily, I dread the Malebranche: they are already after us.

- *Quando* ‘when’ → “as”: the temporal conjunction is changed.
- *non celi* ‘hidest not’ → “do not hide”: English expresses the negative with the auxiliary “do”.
- *Te* ‘thee’ → “thyself”: the Italian stressed personal pronoun is rendered by an explicitly reflexive English pronoun.
- *pavento Di Malebranche* ‘am afraid of Malebranche’ → “dread the Malebranche”: the construction with *di* becomes a transitive verb with a direct object, and the definite article is supplied.
- *noi gli avem già dietro* ‘we have them already behind’ → “they are already after us”: the Italian object becomes the English subject, while the Italian subject becomes “us”; possession is recast as position or pursuit, and “already” precedes the positional phrase.

> 24 Io gli immagino sì, che già gli sento.  

I so imagine them that I hear them now.”

- *Io gli immagino sì* ‘I them imagine so’ → “I so imagine them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and “so” moves before it.
- *già gli sento* ‘already them I hear’ → “I hear them now”: the object pronoun follows the verb, while *già* becomes “now” rather than ‘already’ and moves to the end of the clause.

## Lines 25–42 — Shared Thoughts and Sudden Flight

> 25 E quei: S’ io fossi d’ impiombato vetro,  
> 26 L’ immagine di fuor tua non trarrei  
> 27 Più tosto a me, che quella dentro impetro.  

And he: “If I were of leaded glass, I should not draw thy outward image more quickly to me, than I impress that from within.

- *L’ immagine di fuor tua non trarrei* ‘the image from outside thine I should not draw’ → “I should not draw thy outward image”: the object moves after the verb, the possessive moves before the noun, and the adverbial phrase becomes the adjective “outward”.
- *Più tosto* ‘sooner’ → “more quickly”: an expression of relative speed replaces the temporal comparative.
- *che quella dentro impetro* ‘than that within I obtain’ → “than I impress that from within”: the object moves after the verb; “from” is supplied; and *impetro* is interpreted as impressing or taking an imprint, rather than obtaining.

> 28 Pur mo venieno i tuoi pensier tra i miei  
> 29 Con simile atto e con simile faccia,  
> 30 Sì che d’ entrambi un sol consiglio fei.  

Even now thy thoughts have entered among mine, with similar act and similar face; so that of both I have made one resolve.

- *Pur mo* ‘just now’ → “Even now”: “Even” replaces the restrictive emphasis of ‘just’.
- *venieno i tuoi pensier* ‘were coming thy thoughts’ → “thy thoughts have entered”: the subject moves before the verb, the imperfect becomes a present perfect, and coming becomes entering.
- *e con simile faccia* ‘and with similar face’ → “and similar face”: the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *d’ entrambi un sol consiglio fei* ‘of both one single counsel I made’ → “of both I have made one resolve”: the verb moves before its object and becomes present perfect; *un sol* is reduced to “one”; and *consiglio* is rendered as a decision rather than counsel.

> 31 S’ egli è, che sì la destra costa giaccia,  
> 32 Che noi possiam nell’ altra bolgia scendere,  
> 33 Noi fuggirem l’ immaginata caccia.  

In case the right coast so slopes, that we may descend into the other chasm, we shall escape the imagined chase.”

- *S’ egli è, che* ‘if it is that’ → “In case”: the impersonal construction is compressed into a conjunction.
- *sì la destra costa giaccia* ‘so the right flank lies’ → “the right coast so slopes”: “so” moves after the subject; *costa* becomes “coast”, in its topographical sense; and lying is interpreted specifically as sloping. The Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *nell’ altra bolgia scendere* ‘into the other pouch descend’ → “descend into the other chasm”: the verb moves before the destination phrase, and the figurative ‘pouch’ becomes “chasm”.
- *Noi fuggirem* ‘we shall flee’ → “we shall escape”: flight is expressed as successful evasion.

> 34 Già non compìo di tal consiglio rendere,  
> 35 Ch’ io gli vidi venir con l’ ale tese,  
> 36 Non molto lungi, per volerne prendere.  

He had not ended giving this resolve, when I saw them come with wings extended, not far off, in will to seize us.

- *Già non compìo* ‘already he did not finish’ → “He had not ended”: the simple past becomes past perfect, expressing the unfinished action relative to what follows; *già* has no separate English equivalent.
- *di tal consiglio rendere* ‘of such counsel to render’ → “giving this resolve”: the prepositional infinitive becomes an English gerund, moved before its object; “this” replaces ‘such’, “giving” replaces ‘rendering’, and counsel becomes a resolve.
- *Ch’* ‘that’ → “when”: the conjunction is rendered explicitly as temporal.
- *con l’ ale tese* ‘with the wings extended’ → “with wings extended”: the definite article is omitted.
- *Non molto lungi* ‘not very far’ → “not far off”: *molto* is omitted, while “off” expands the expression of distance.
- *per volerne prendere* ‘for wishing to take us’ → “in will to seize us”: the infinitive expressing desire becomes the noun “will” in a prepositional phrase; *prendere* becomes the stronger “seize”; and the attached pronoun *ne* becomes a separate object after that verb.

> 37 Lo Duca mio di subito mi prese,  

My Guide suddenly took me;

- *Lo Duca mio* ‘the leader my’ → “My Guide”: “Guide” replaces ‘leader’, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *di subito* ‘of a sudden’ → “suddenly”: the prepositional expression becomes a single adverb.
- *mi prese* ‘me took’ → “took me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 38 Come la madre ch’ al romore è desta,  
> 39 E vede presso a sè le fiamme accese,  

as a mother—that is awakened by the noise, and near her sees the kindled flames—

- *Come la madre* ‘as the mother’ → “as a mother”: the definite article becomes indefinite.
- *ch’ al romore è desta* ‘who at the noise is awakened’ → “that is awakened by the noise”: the phrase describing the stimulus moves after the verb, and “by” presents it explicitly as the cause of awakening.
- *E vede presso a sè* ‘and sees near to herself’ → “and near her sees”: the place phrase moves before the verb, and the reflexive pronoun becomes the ordinary personal pronoun “her”.
- *le fiamme accese* ‘the flames kindled’ → “the kindled flames”: the participial adjective moves before the noun.

> 40 Che prende il figlio, e fugge, e non s’ arresta,  
> 41 Avendo più di lui che di sè cura,  
> 42 Tanto che solo una camicia vesta:  

who takes her child and flies, and caring more for him than for herself, pauses not so long as even to cast a shift about her.

- *Che prende il figlio* ‘who takes the son’ → “who takes her child”: the article becomes a possessive, and the specifically male noun becomes “child”.
- *e non s’ arresta* ‘and does not stop herself’ → “and” … “pauses not”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb; the following participial clause is inserted between the conjunction and the verb.
- *Avendo più di lui che di sè cura* ‘having more of him than of herself care’ → “caring more for him than for herself”: the expression ‘having care’ becomes the verbal participle “caring”, the dependent phrases follow it, and *di* becomes “for”. The whole clause moves before the verb it qualifies.
- *Tanto che solo una camicia vesta* ‘so much that she may wear only a shirt’ → “so long as even to cast a shift about her”: *tanto che* is read as a duration construction rather than a result construction; the finite subjunctive becomes an infinitive. “Even” replaces ‘only’, shifting the emphasis from the sole garment worn to the minimum act of dressing; *vesta* is expanded into “cast” and “about her”, and *camicia* is rendered specifically as “shift”.

## Lines 43–57 — The Descent Into the Next Chasm

> 43 E giù dal collo della ripa dura  
> 44 Supin si diede alla pendente roccia,  
> 45 Che l’ un de’ lati all’ altra bolgia tura.  

And down from the ridge of the hard bank, supine he gave himself to the pendent rock, which dams up one side of the other chasm.

- *collo* ‘neck’ → “ridge”: the anatomical metaphor is replaced by a topographical term.
- *ripa dura* ‘bank hard’ → “hard bank”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *Supin si diede* ‘supine himself gave’ → “supine he gave himself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *tura* ‘blocks’ → “dams up”: a phrasal verb replaces the single verb, and moves from the end of the relative clause to before its object.
- *l’un de’ lati all’altra bolgia* ‘one of the sides to the other ditch’ → “one side of the other chasm”: the partitive construction is simplified; the Italian dative becomes an English possessive relationship with “of”; and *bolgia* is rendered as “chasm”.

> 46 Non corse mai sì tosto acqua per doccia  
> 47 A volger ruota di mulin terragno,  
> 48 Quand’ ella più verso le pale approccia,  

Never did water run so fast through spout to turn a land-mill’s wheel, when it approaches nearest to the ladles,

- *Non corse mai sì tosto acqua* ‘not ran ever so fast water’ → “Never did water run so fast”: the negative and *mai* combine as “Never”; English introduces auxiliary “did”, with the subject between the auxiliary and the main verb rather than after the verb and its adverbial modifiers.
- *per doccia* ‘through channel’ → “through spout”: “spout” gives a more specific rendering of the water channel.
- *ruota di mulin terragno* ‘wheel of ground-based mill’ → “a land-mill’s wheel”: the *di* construction becomes a possessive; the adjective becomes part of the compound “land-mill”; and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *ella* ‘she’ → “it”: Italian grammatical gender is replaced by the English neuter pronoun.
- *più verso le pale approccia* ‘more towards the blades approaches’ → “approaches nearest to the ladles”: the verb moves before its modifiers; comparative *più* is interpreted as indicating the point of greatest proximity; and the wheel’s blades are called “ladles”.

> 49 Come il Maestro mio per quel vivagno,  
> 50 Portandosene me sovra il suo petto,  
> 51 Come suo figlio, e non come compagno.  

as my Master down that bank, carrying me away upon his breast, as his son and not as his companion.

- *il Maestro mio* ‘the Master my’ → “my Master”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *per quel vivagno* ‘along that selvage’ → “down that bank”: the textile metaphor becomes a topographical description, while “down” makes the direction explicit.
- *Portandosene me* ‘carrying me away with him’ → “carrying me away”: the reflexive element expressing that he carries the speaker with him is not separately rendered.
- *compagno* ‘companion’ → “his companion”: the possessive is supplied.

> 52 Appena furo i piè suoi giunti al letto  
> 53 Del fondo giù ch’ ei giunsero in sul colle  
> 54 Sovresso noi: ma non gli era sospetto;  

Scarcely had his feet reached the bed of the depth below, when they reached the height above us: but no fear it gave him;

- *Appena furo i piè suoi giunti al letto* ‘scarcely were the feet his arrived at the bed’ → “Scarcely had his feet reached the bed”: the Italian compound tense with *essere* becomes an English perfect with “had”; the possessive precedes its noun; and intransitive arrival at a place becomes transitive “reached”, without a preposition.
- *Del fondo giù* ‘of the bottom below’ → “of the depth below”: “depth” replaces the concrete noun ‘bottom’.
- *ch’* ‘that’ → “when”: the conjunction is recast to fit the English temporal construction introduced by “Scarcely”.
- *giunsero in sul colle* ‘arrived upon the hill’ → “reached the height”: the verb becomes transitive, absorbing the preposition, and “height” replaces ‘hill’.
- *ma non gli era sospetto* ‘but it was not a cause of apprehension to him’ → “but no fear it gave him”: the construction with ‘was’ becomes one with “gave”; the verbal negative becomes “no” modifying the noun; and *sospetto* is interpreted as “fear”.

> 55 Chè l’ alta Providenza, che lor volle  
> 56 Porre ministri della fossa quinta,  
> 57 Potere indi partirsi a tutti tolle.  

for the high Providence, that willed to place them ministers of the fifth ditch, takes the power of leaving it from all.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *lor volle Porre* ‘them willed to place’ → “willed to place them”: the object pronoun moves from before the finite verb to after the infinitive.
- *della fossa quinta* ‘of the ditch fifth’ → “of the fifth ditch”: the ordinal precedes the noun.
- *Potere indi partirsi a tutti tolle* ‘power from there to depart from all takes away’ → “takes the power of leaving it from all”: the main verb moves from the end to the beginning; “the” is supplied before “power”; the infinitive becomes an *of*-phrase with a gerund; and departure ‘from there’ becomes transitive “leaving it”, turning the adverb of place into a pronoun object. The removal expressed by *tolle* is conveyed by “takes” together with “from all”.

## Lines 58–69 — The Gilded Cloaks of Lead

> 58 Laggiù trovammo una gente dipinta,  
> 59 Che giva intorno assai con lenti passi,  
> 60 Piangendo, e nel sembiante stanca e vinta.  

There beneath we found a painted people, who were going round with steps exceeding slow, weeping, and in their look tired and overcome.

- *Laggiù* ‘down there’ → “There beneath”: the downward indication is expressed by “beneath”.
- *una gente dipinta* ‘a people painted’ → “a painted people”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Che giva* ‘which was going’ → “who were going”: the singular verb agreeing with the collective *gente* becomes plural, and “who” refers to its individual members.
- *assai con lenti passi* ‘much, with slow steps’ → “with steps exceeding slow”: *assai* is taken as intensifying the slowness; “exceeding” precedes “slow”, and the adjective phrase follows rather than precedes “steps”.
- *nel sembiante* ‘in the look’ → “in their look”: a possessive replaces the definite article.

> 61 Egli avean cappe con cappucci bassi  
> 62 Dinanzi agli occhi, fatte della taglia  
> 63 Che per li monaci in Cologna fassi.  

They had cloaks on, with deep hoods before their eyes, made in the shape that they make for the monks in Cologne.

- *avean cappe* ‘had cloaks’ → “had cloaks on”: “on” is supplied, making the wearing explicit.
- *cappucci bassi* ‘hoods low’ → “deep hoods”: “deep” replaces ‘low’, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Dinanzi agli occhi* ‘before the eyes’ → “before their eyes”: a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *fatte della taglia* ‘made of the cut’ → “made in the shape”: ‘cut’ becomes “shape”, with “in” replacing ‘of’.
- *Che per li monaci in Cologna fassi* ‘that for the monks in Cologne is made’ → “that they make for the monks in Cologne”: the passive construction with *si* becomes active with an indefinite “they”; the verb moves before the phrase identifying those for whom the cloaks are made.

> 64 Di fuor dorate son, sì ch’ egli abbaglia;  

Outward they are gilded, so that it dazzles;

- *Di fuor dorate son* ‘on the outside gilded they are’ → “Outward they are gilded”: the opening phrase becomes “Outward”, and the predicate adjective moves after the verb.

> 65 Ma dentro tutte piombo, e gravi tanto,  
> 66 Che Federigo le mettea di paglia.  

but within all lead, and so heavy, that Frederick’s compared to them were straw.

- *gravi tanto* ‘heavy so’ → “so heavy”: the degree adverb moves before the adjective.
- *Che Federigo le mettea di paglia* ‘that Frederick put them on of straw’ → “that Frederick’s compared to them were straw”: Frederick changes from the subject of an active verb to a possessive standing for his cloaks; the object pronoun is absorbed into that possessive construction. The verb of putting on becomes “were”, and ‘of straw’ becomes the predicate “straw”. “Compared to them” supplies the comparison implicit in the Italian.

> 67 O in eterno faticoso manto!  

O weary mantle for eternity!

- *in eterno* ‘forever’ → “for eternity”: the adverbial expression becomes a prepositional phrase and moves from before the adjective to after the noun.
- *faticoso* ‘toilsome’ → “weary”: a different adjective conveys the burden expressed by the Italian.

> 68 Noi ci volgemmo ancor pure a man manca  
> 69 Con loro insieme, intenti al tristo pianto:  

We turned again to the left hand, along with them, intent upon their dreary weeping.

- *ci volgemmo* ‘turned ourselves’ → “turned”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *ancor pure* ‘again, still’ → “again”: *pure*, emphasizing continuation, is not separately represented.
- *a man manca* ‘to left hand’ → “to the left hand”: the definite article is supplied.
- *Con loro insieme* ‘with them together’ → “along with them”: the following adverb *insieme* is absorbed into the opening expression “along with”.
- *al* ‘to the’ → “upon their”: the preposition is adapted to English “intent”, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *tristo* ‘sad’ → “dreary”: a different adjective renders the quality of the weeping.

## Lines 70–84 — A Halt Among the Burdened

> 70 Ma per lo peso quella gente stanca  
> 71 Venia sì pian, che noi eravam nuovi  
> 72 Di compagnia ad ogni muover d’ anca.  

But that people, tired by their burden, came so slowly that our company was new at every movement of the hip.

- *per lo peso quella gente stanca* ‘because of the weight that weary people’ → “that people, tired by their burden”: the phrase explaining the weariness moves after the noun; ‘because of’ becomes “by”, ‘weight’ becomes “burden”, and the definite article becomes the possessive “their”.
- *noi eravam nuovi Di compagnia* ‘we were new in company’ → “our company was new”: the personal subject becomes a possessive, and ‘company’ becomes the grammatical subject; the plural predicate becomes singular.
- *ad ogni muover d’ anca* ‘at every moving of hip’ → “at every movement of the hip”: the substantivized infinitive becomes the noun “movement”, and an article is supplied before “hip”.

> 73 Per ch’ io al Duca mio: Fa che tu trovi  
> 74 Alcun, ch’ al fatto o al nome si conosca;  
> 75 E gli occhi, sì andando, intorno muovi.  

Wherefore I to my Guide: “See that thou find some one who may by deed or name be known; and move thy eyes around as we go on.”

- *al Duca mio* ‘to the leader my’ → “to my Guide”: ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *Fa che tu trovi* ‘make that thou find’ → “See that thou find”: the imperative construction becomes the English idiom meaning to ensure something.
- *ch’ al fatto o al nome si conosca* ‘who by the deed or by the name be known’ → “who may by deed or name be known”: the subjunctive is expressed with “may”; the articles and the repeated preposition are omitted.
- *gli occhi, sì andando, intorno muovi* ‘the eyes, thus going, around move’ → “move thy eyes around as we go on”: the verb moves before its object, and the article becomes “thy”. The gerund becomes a finite temporal clause placed last; ‘thus going’ is interpreted as continuing to walk, with “we” and “on” supplied.

> 76 Ed un, che intese la parola Tosca,  
> 77 Dirietro a noi gridò: Tenete i piedi,  
> 78 Voi, che correte sì per l’ aura fosca:  

And one, who understood the Tuscan speech, cried after us: “Stay your feet, ye who run so fast through the brown air.

- *la parola Tosca* ‘the word Tuscan’ → “the Tuscan speech”: ‘word’ is rendered collectively as “speech”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Dirietro a noi gridò* ‘behind us cried’ → “cried after us”: the verb moves before the adverbial phrase, and the spatial ‘behind us’ becomes the idiomatic “after us”.
- *Tenete i piedi* ‘hold the feet’ → “Stay your feet”: ‘hold’ becomes “Stay”, expressing an arrest of movement, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *sì* ‘so’ → “so fast”: “fast” explicitly supplies the degree of speed implied by the Italian.
- *l’ aura fosca* ‘the air dark’ → “the brown air”: “brown” gives a colour-specific rendering of ‘dark’ or ‘dusky’, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 79 Forse ch’ avrai da me quel che tu chiedi.  

Perhaps thou shalt obtain from me that which thou askest.”

- *Forse ch’* ‘perhaps that’ → “Perhaps”: the introductory *che* is omitted.
- *avrai* ‘thou wilt have’ → “thou shalt obtain”: ‘have’ is interpreted as acquiring or receiving, rather than possessing.

> 80 Onde il Duca si volse, e disse: Aspetta,  
> 81 E poi secondo il suo passo procedi.  

Whereat my Guide turned round and said: “Wait, and then at his pace proceed.”

- *Onde* ‘whence’ → “Whereat”: the connective is rendered as a response to the preceding words rather than literally as ‘from which’.
- *il Duca* ‘the leader’ → “my Guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘leader’ becomes “Guide”.
- *si volse* ‘turned himself’ → “turned round”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb with “round” supplied.
- *secondo il suo passo* ‘according to his step’ → “at his pace”: the prepositional phrase is made idiomatic, and *passo* is understood as rate of movement rather than an individual step.

> 82 Ristetti, e vidi duo mostrar gran fretta  
> 83 Dell’ animo, col viso, d’ esser meco;  
> 84 Ma tardavagli il carco e la via stretta.  

I stood still, and saw two, showing by their look great haste of mind to be with me; but the load and the narrow way retarded them.

- *Ristetti* ‘I stopped’ → “I stood still”: the act of stopping is expressed through the resulting stationary posture.
- *vidi duo mostrar* ‘I saw two show’ → “saw two, showing”: the infinitive dependent on the verb of perception becomes a participle.
- *gran fretta Dell’ animo, col viso* ‘great haste of the mind, with the face’ → “by their look great haste of mind”: the phrase describing the outward expression moves before the object; ‘with’ becomes “by”, ‘face’ becomes “look”, and its article becomes “their”. The article before ‘mind’ is omitted.
- *d’ esser meco* ‘of being with me’ → “to be with me”: the prepositional infinitive phrase becomes an English infinitive complement to “haste”.
- *Ma tardavagli il carco e la via stretta* ‘but retarded them the load and the way narrow’ → “but the load and the narrow way retarded them”: the compound subject moves before the verb, the object pronoun follows it, and “narrow” moves before its noun.

## Lines 85–99 — The Hypocrites Question Dante

> 85 Quando fur giunti, assai con l’ occhio bieco  
> 86 Mi rimiraron senza far parola;  

When they came up, long with eye askance they viewed me, without uttering a word.

- *fur giunti* ‘had arrived’ → “came up”: the completed-past construction becomes a simple past, and “up” makes the approach explicit.
- *assai* ‘much’ → “long”: intensity or extent is interpreted as duration.
- *con l’ occhio bieco* ‘with the eye oblique’ → “with eye askance”: the definite article is omitted, and the adjective becomes the adverb “askance”.
- *Mi rimiraron* ‘me they viewed’ → “they viewed me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *senza far parola* ‘without making word’ → “without uttering a word”: the Italian idiom is replaced by an English expression, with an indefinite article supplied.

> 87 Poi si volsero in sè, e dicean seco:  

Then they turned to one another, and said between them:

- *si volsero in sè* ‘they turned themselves to themselves’ → “they turned to one another”: the reflexive expressions are interpreted reciprocally.
- *dicean* ‘were saying’ → “said”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *seco* ‘with themselves’ → “between them”: the reflexive expression is rendered as an exchange between the speakers.

> 88 Costui par vivo all’ atto della gola.  

“This one seems alive by the action of his throat!

- *all’ atto della gola* ‘at the action of the throat’ → “by the action of his throat”: “by” presents the action as evidence, and the definite article before *gola* becomes a possessive.

> 89 E s’ ei son morti, per qual privilegio  
> 90 Vanno scoverti della grave stola?  

And if they are dead, by what privilege go they divested of the heavy stole?”

- *Vanno scoverti* ‘they go uncovered’ → “go they divested”: the subject follows the verb in the question, and “divested” replaces ‘uncovered’, suggesting removal rather than simply absence of covering.

> 91 Poi dissermi: O Tosco, ch’ al collegio  
> 92 Degl’ ipocriti tristi se’ venuto,  
> 93 Dir chi tu se’ non avere in dispregio.  

Then they said to me: “O Tuscan, that art come to the college of the sad hypocrites! to tell us who thou art disdain not.”

- *ch’ al collegio Degl’ ipocriti tristi se’ venuto* ‘that to the college of the hypocrites sad art come’ → “that art come to the college of the sad hypocrites”: the verb moves before the phrase expressing destination, and the adjective moves before its noun.
- *Dir* ‘to say’ → “to tell us”: “tell” replaces ‘say’, and the recipient “us” is supplied.
- *non avere in dispregio* ‘not to hold in contempt’ → “disdain not”: the phrase containing a noun becomes a single verb. The Italian negative command, formed with an infinitive, becomes an English imperative, with the negative following the verb.

> 94 Ed io a loro: I’ fui nato e cresciuto  
> 95 Sovra il bel fiume d’ Arno alla gran villa,  
> 96 E son col corpo ch’ i’ ho sempre avuto.  

And I to them: “On Arno’s beauteous river, in the great city I was born and grew; and I am with the body that I have always had.

- *I’ fui nato e cresciuto* ‘I was born and brought up’ → “I was born and grew”: the construction sharing an auxiliary between two participles becomes “was born” followed by the separate active verb “grew”.
- *Sovra il bel fiume d’ Arno alla gran villa* ‘On the beauteous river of Arno, at the great city’ → “On Arno’s beauteous river, in the great city”: both place phrases move before the statement of birth and upbringing. The phrase ‘of Arno’ becomes a possessive before the noun, replacing the definite article; ‘at’ becomes “in”.

> 97 Ma voi chi siete, a cui tanto distilla,  
> 98 Quant’ io veggio, dolor giù per le guance?  

But you, who are ye from whom distils such sorrow as I see, down your cheeks?

- *voi chi siete* ‘you who are’ → “you, who are ye”: the initial *voi* is retained as “you”, while a second pronoun, “ye”, is supplied within the question.
- *a cui* ‘to whom’ → “from whom”: the Italian dative relation is recast as a source.
- *tanto distilla, Quant’ io veggio, dolor* ‘so much distils, as I see, sorrow’ → “distils such sorrow as I see”: the separated quantity-word and noun are brought together after the verb, and the quantitative ‘so much … as’ becomes “such … as”.
- *giù per le guance* ‘down along the cheeks’ → “down your cheeks”: the two-word directional expression is condensed into “down”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 99 E che pena è in voi, che sì sfavilla?  

And what punishment is on ye that glitters so?”

- *in voi* ‘in you’ → “on ye”: the preposition changes from ‘in’ to “on”.
- *che sì sfavilla* ‘that so glitters’ → “that glitters so”: the adverb moves after the verb.

## Lines 100–108 — Lead Mantles and Chosen Friars

> 100 E l’ un rispose a me: Le cappe rance  
> 101 Son di piombo sì grosse, che li pesi  
> 102 Fan così cigolar le lor bilance.  

And one of them replied to me: “Our orange mantles are of lead so thick, that the weights thus cause their scales to creak.

- *l’un* ‘the one’ → “one of them”: the definite expression becomes an indefinite one, with “of them” making the reference explicit.
- *Le* ‘the’ → “Our”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *cappe rance* ‘mantles orange’ → “orange mantles”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Fan così cigolar le lor bilance* ‘make thus creak their scales’ → “thus cause their scales to creak”: “thus” moves before the causative verb; ‘make’ becomes “cause”, and the scales move before the infinitive, which consequently takes “to”.

> 103 Frati Godenti fummo, e Bolognesi,  
> 104 Io Catalano, e costui Loderingo  
> 105 Nomati; e da tua terra insieme presi,  

We were Jovial Friars, and Bolognese: I named Catalano, and Loderingo he; and by thy city chosen together,

- *Frati Godenti fummo* ‘Friars Rejoicing we were’ → “We were Jovial Friars”: the verb moves before its complement, the adjective moves before the noun, and *Godenti* is rendered by “Jovial” rather than ‘Rejoicing’.
- *Io Catalano, e costui Loderingo Nomati* ‘I Catalano, and this one Loderingo named’ → “I named Catalano, and Loderingo he”: the shared participle moves from the end to before the first name; the demonstrative ‘this one’ becomes “he”, and the second name precedes its subject.
- *terra* ‘land’ → “city”: Carlyle selects the word’s urban sense.
- *insieme presi* ‘together taken’ → “chosen together”: ‘taken’ is interpreted as “chosen”, and the adverb moves after the participle.

> 106 Come suole esser tolto un uom solingo  
> 107 Per conservar sua pace: e fummo tali,  
> 108 Ch’ ancor si pare intorno dal Gardingo.  

as usually one solitary man is chosen, to maintain its peace. And we were such, that it yet appears round the Gardingo.”

- *Come suole esser tolto un uom solingo* ‘as is wont to be taken a man solitary’ → “as usually one solitary man is chosen”: the habitual construction *suole esser* becomes “usually” with “is”; ‘taken’ becomes “chosen”; ‘a’ becomes “one”; the adjective moves before its noun, and the subject moves before the passive verb.
- *Per conservar* ‘for preserving’ → “to maintain”: the purpose construction becomes an English infinitive with “to”, and ‘preserving’ is rendered as “maintain”.
- *ancor si pare* ‘still is apparent’ → “it yet appears”: the pronominal construction *si pare* becomes the simple verb “appears” with an explicit impersonal “it”; the adverb follows that subject.
- *intorno dal Gardingo* ‘around by the Gardingo’ → “round the Gardingo”: the Italian combination of adverb and preposition becomes the single English preposition “round”.

## Lines 109–120 — The Crucified Man’s Punishment

> 109 Io cominciai: O Frati, i vostri mali . . . .  
> 110 Ma più non dissi, ch’ agli occhi mi corse  
> 111 Un crocifisso in terra con tre pali.  

I began: “O Friars, your evil”—But said no more, for to my eyes came one, cross-fixed in the ground with three stakes.

- *i vostri mali* ‘your evils’ → “your evil”: the plural noun becomes singular.
- *Ma più non dissi* ‘But more I did not say’ → “But said no more”: the negation is transferred from the verb to “more,” and “no more” follows the verb.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *agli occhi mi corse* ‘to the eyes to me ran’ → “to my eyes came”: the personal dative becomes the possessive “my”; “came” replaces ‘ran,’ weakening the suggestion of sudden movement.
- *Un crocifisso* ‘a crucified man’ → “one, cross-fixed”: the substantivized participle becomes a pronoun followed by a descriptive participle.
- *in terra* ‘in earth’ → “in the ground”: “ground” renders *terra*, and the definite article is supplied.

> 112 Quando mi vide, tutto si distorse,  
> 113 Soffiando nella barba co’ sospiri.  

When he saw me, he writhed all over, blowing into his beard with sighs.

- *Quando mi vide* ‘When me he saw’ → “When he saw me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *tutto si distorse* ‘all he twisted himself’ → “he writhed all over”: the reflexive verb becomes intransitive, while *tutto*, describing the whole person, becomes the adverbial phrase “all over,” placed after the verb.
- *nella barba* ‘in the beard’ → “into his beard”: “into” makes the direction explicit, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 114 E il frate Catalan, ch’ a ciò s’ accorse,  
> 115 Mi disse: Quel confitto, che tu miri,  
> 116 Consigliò i Farisei, che convenia  
> 117 Porre un uom per lo popolo a’ martiri.  

And Friar Catalano, who perceived this, said to me: “That confixed one, on whom thou gazest, counselled the Pharisees that it was expedient to put one man to tortures for the people.

- *il frate* ‘the friar’ → “Friar”: the definite article is omitted before the title.
- *ch’ a ciò s’ accorse* ‘who of this became aware’ → “who perceived this”: the pronominal construction with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object; “this” follows the verb.
- *Mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *Quel confitto* ‘that transfixed’ → “That confixed one”: “one” explicitly supplies the nominal function of the substantivized participle.
- *che tu miri* ‘whom thou regardest’ → “on whom thou gazest”: the transitive verb becomes a verb with a prepositional complement.
- *convenia* ‘it was fitting’ → “it was expedient”: the translation interprets fitness as practical expediency.
- *un uom* ‘a man’ → “one man”: *un* is given its numerical force rather than rendered as an indefinite article.
- *per lo popolo a’ martiri* ‘for the people to tortures’ → “to tortures for the people”: the two prepositional phrases exchange places.

> 118 Attraversato e nudo è per la via,  
> 119 Come tu vedi; ed è mestier che senta,  
> 120 Qualunque passa, com’ ei pesa pria.  

Traverse and naked he is upon the road, as thou seest; and has to feel the weight of every one that passes.

- *Attraversato* ‘laid crosswise’ → “Traverse”: the Italian participle becomes an adjective describing position.
- *per la via* ‘along the road’ → “upon the road”: the preposition is rendered as one of position upon a surface.
- *è mestier che senta* ‘it is necessary that he feel’ → “has to feel”: the impersonal expression followed by a subjunctive clause becomes a personal construction expressing necessity with “has to.”
- *Qualunque passa, com’ ei pesa pria* ‘whoever passes, how much he weighs first’ → “the weight of every one that passes”: the fronted reference to whoever passes becomes an “of” phrase following “weight”; the clause ‘how much he weighs’ becomes the noun “weight.” *Pria*, ‘first,’ is omitted.

## Lines 121–136 — Punishment and a Broken Crossing

> 121 E a tal modo il suocero si stenta  
> 122 In questa fossa, e gli altri del Concilio,  
> 123 Che fu per li Giudei mala sementa.  

And after the like fashion his father-in-law is racked in this ditch, and the others of that Council, which was a seed of evil for the Jews.”

- *a tal modo* ‘in such a manner’ → “after the like fashion”: the preposition and the expression of likeness are recast.
- *il suocero* ‘the father-in-law’ → “his father-in-law”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *si stenta* ‘suffers’ → “is racked”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes an English passive, with a more specific expression of torment.
- *del Concilio* ‘of the Council’ → “of that Council”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative.
- *Che fu per li Giudei mala sementa* ‘which was for the Jews evil seed’ → “which was a seed of evil for the Jews”: the phrase identifying those affected moves to the end; an indefinite article is supplied; and the adjective becomes a noun introduced by “of”.

> 124 Allor vid’ io maravigliar Virgilio  
> 125 Sopra colui, ch’ era disteso in croce  
> 126 Tanto vilmente nell’ eterno esilio.  

Then I saw Virgil wonder over him that was distended on the cross so ignominiously in the eternal exile.

- *vid’ io* ‘saw I’ → “I saw”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *maravigliar Virgilio* ‘wonder Virgil’ → “Virgil wonder”: the subject of the infinitive moves before it.
- *disteso in croce* ‘stretched out in a cross’ → “distended on the cross”: “distended” renders the stretching out, while the expression of a cross-shaped position is interpreted as being on a cross; the definite article is supplied.
- *Tanto vilmente* ‘so vilely’ → “so ignominiously”: the adverb is rendered specifically in terms of disgrace.

> 127 Poscia drizzò al Frate cotal voce:  
> 128 Non dispiaccia, se vi lece, dirci  
> 129 Se alla man destra giace alcuna foce,  

Afterwards he to the Friar addressed these words: “Let it not displease you, so it be lawful for you, to tell us if on the right hand lies any gap

- *drizzò al Frate cotal voce* ‘directed to the Friar such a voice’ → “he to the Friar addressed these words”: the recipient moves before the verb; directing a voice becomes addressing words; singular *voce* becomes plural “words”; and *cotal* becomes the demonstrative “these”.
- *Non dispiaccia* ‘let it not displease’ → “Let it not displease you”: the person potentially displeased is made explicit.
- *se vi lece* ‘if it is permitted to you’ → “so it be lawful for you”: the condition is expressed with “so” and the English subjunctive “be”; permission is phrased as lawfulness.
- *alla man destra* ‘at the hand right’ → “on the right hand”: the preposition changes, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *alcuna foce* ‘some outlet’ → “any gap”: “any” suits the indirect question, while “gap” renders the opening rather than explicitly its function as an outlet.

> 130 Onde noi ambedue possiamo uscirci  
> 131 Senza costringer degli angeli neri,  
> 132 Che vegnan d’ esto fondo a dipartirci.  

by which we both may go out hence, without constraining any of the Black Angels to come and extricate us from this bottom.”

- *Onde* ‘whence’ → “by which”: the relative expression of departure becomes one identifying the means or passage.
- *possiamo* ‘can’ → “may”: ability or possibility is expressed with a different modal verb.
- *degli angeli neri* ‘of the angels black’ → “any of the Black Angels”: the partitive construction is made explicit with “any”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Che vegnan d’ esto fondo a dipartirci* ‘that they come from this bottom to take us away’ → “to come and extricate us from this bottom”: the subjunctive relative clause becomes an infinitival complement of “constraining”; the purpose infinitive becomes a coordinated verb; “extricate” replaces taking away; and the phrase of departure moves after that verb.

> 133 Rispose adunque: Più che tu non speri  
> 134 S’ appressa un sasso, che dalla gran cerchia  
> 135 Si muove, e varca tutti i vallon feri,  

So he answered: “Nearer than thou dost hope, there is a stone that moves from the Great Barrier, and bridges all the cruel valleys,

- *Rispose adunque* ‘answered therefore’ → “So he answered”: the connective moves before the subject and verb, and “So” replaces ‘therefore’.
- *Più che tu non speri* ‘more than thou dost not hope’ → “Nearer than thou dost hope”: the comparison is made explicitly spatial through “Nearer”; the Italian comparative’s non-negating *non* is omitted.
- *S’ appressa un sasso* ‘a stone draws near’ → “there is a stone”: the verb of approach becomes an existential construction, with proximity conveyed by the preceding comparative.
- *che dalla gran cerchia Si muove* ‘which from the great circuit moves itself’ → “that moves from the Great Barrier”: the source phrase moves after the verb; the pronominal verb becomes an ordinary English intransitive; and *cerchia* is interpreted as a barrier rather than a circuit or enclosing ring.
- *e varca tutti i vallon feri* ‘and crosses all the valleys fierce’ → “and bridges all the cruel valleys”: crossing becomes specifically bridging; ‘fierce’ becomes “cruel”; and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 136 Salvo ch’ a questo è rotto, e nol coperchia.  

save that in this ’tis broken and covers it not.

- *Salvo ch’ a questo è rotto* ‘except that at this it is broken’ → “save that in this ’tis broken”: “save” replaces ‘except’, and “in” renders the locative *a*.
- *e nol coperchia* ‘and not it covers’ → “and covers it not”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the negative moves after the object.

## Lines 137–148 — The Guide Discovers the Deception

> 137 Montar potrete su per la ruina,  
> 138 Chè giace in costa, e nel fondo soperchia.  

Ye may mount up by its ruins, which slope down the side, and on the bottom make a heap.”

- *Montar potrete su per* ‘Mount ye will be able up along’ → “Ye may mount up by”: the future expression of ability becomes “may”; the modal precedes the infinitive, and “by” replaces ‘along’.
- *la ruina* ‘the ruin’ → “its ruins”: the singular becomes plural, and a possessive replaces the definite article.
- *Chè giace in costa* ‘for it lies on the slope’ → “which slope down the side”: the causal clause becomes a relative clause modifying “ruins”; the singular verb becomes plural, and lying on a slope is recast as sloping down a side.
- *e nel fondo soperchia* ‘and in the bottom rises above’ → “and on the bottom make a heap”: “on” replaces ‘in’, and the verb describing projection above the bottom is expanded into “make a heap”, with a plural subject carried over from “ruins”.

> 139 Lo Duca stette un poco a testa china,  
> 140 Poi disse: Mal contava la bisogna  
> 141 Colui, che i peccator di là uncina.  

The Guide stood still a while with head bent down, then said: “Falsely did he tell the way, who hooks the sinners yonder.”

- *Lo Duca* ‘the Leader’ → “The Guide”: the designation is rendered in terms of guidance rather than leadership.
- *stette un poco* ‘stood a little’ → “stood still a while”: “still” makes the cessation of movement explicit, and “a while” expresses the duration meant by *un poco*.
- *Mal* ‘ill’ → “Falsely”: the general adverb is given the more specific sense of untruthfulness.
- *contava la bisogna Colui* ‘was recounting the matter that one’ → “did he tell the way”: the imperfect becomes a simple past with auxiliary “did”; the demonstrative subject becomes “he” and moves before the main verb; ‘the matter’ is interpreted specifically as “the way”.
- *che i peccator di là uncina* ‘who the sinners yonder hooks’ → “who hooks the sinners yonder”: the verb moves before its object, leaving “yonder” at the end.

> 142 E il Frate: Io udi’ già dire a Bologna  
> 143 Del Diavol vizj assai, tra i quali udi’,  
> 144 Ch’ egli è bugiardo e padre di menzogna.  

And the Friar: “I heard once at Bologna many of the Devil’s vices told; amongst which, I heard that he is a liar and the father of lies.”

- *udi’ … dire* ‘heard tell’ → “heard” … “told”: the active infinitive becomes a passive participle, placed after the vices that are its subject.
- *già* ‘already’ → “once”: the adverb is interpreted as referring to an unspecified past occasion.
- *Del Diavol vizj assai* ‘of the Devil vices many’ → “many of the Devil’s vices”: the postposed quantity moves to the front, and the preceding phrase of possession becomes a possessive within a partitive construction.
- *padre di menzogna* ‘father of falsehood’ → “the father of lies”: a definite article is supplied before “father”, and the singular abstract noun becomes the plural “lies”.

> 145 Appresso il Duca a gran passi sen gì,  
> 146 Turbato un poco d’ ira nel sembiante:  

Then with large steps my Guide went on, somewhat disturbed with anger in his look:

- *il Duca* ‘the Leader’ → “my Guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the designation is rendered as “Guide”.
- *a gran passi* ‘at large steps’ → “with large steps”: English uses “with” for the manner of movement, and the phrase moves before the subject.
- *sen gì* ‘went himself away’ → “went on”: the pronominal expression loses its reflexive form, while movement away becomes continued movement.
- *Turbato un poco* ‘disturbed a little’ → “somewhat disturbed”: the qualification moves before the participle and becomes a single adverb.
- *nel sembiante* ‘in the look’ → “in his look”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 147 Ond’ io dagl’ incarcati mi parti’  
> 148 Dietro alle poste delle care piante.  

whereat I from the laden spirits parted, following the prints of his beloved feet.

- *Ond’* ‘whence’ → “whereat”: the connective is rendered as a response to the preceding action rather than literally as ‘from which’.
- *dagl’ incarcati* ‘from the laden’ → “from the laden spirits”: “spirits” supplies a noun for the substantivized participle.
- *mi parti’* ‘parted myself’ → “parted”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive verb without a reflexive pronoun.
- *Dietro alle poste* ‘behind the tracks’ → “following the prints”: the prepositional expression becomes a participial phrase, making the act of following explicit.
- *delle care piante* ‘of the dear soles’ → “of his beloved feet”: a possessive replaces the definite article, and “feet” renders the more specific ‘soles’.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Recasting

Carlyle keeps much of the Italian’s vocabulary, imagery, and sequence of clauses, while adjusting their arrangement to English prose. Subjects commonly move before verbs, objects follow them, and adjectives and possessives precede their nouns: *Volto era … Lo mio pensier* becomes “My thought was turned”, and *cappe rance* becomes “orange mantles”. Yet he also preserves marked arrangements, such as “supine he gave himself” and “but no fear it gave him”. More generally, English subject pronouns make explicit persons already indicated by Italian verb endings, while the Italian articles before possessives ordinarily disappear.

Changes of construction often make the English more direct or idiomatic. Reflexive and pronominal verbs become ordinary intransitives, as *ci volgemmo* becomes “We turned”; impersonal expressions become personal ones, as *è mestier che senta* becomes “has to feel”. Elsewhere the grammatical centre shifts: *noi eravam nuovi Di compagnia* becomes “our company was new”, and *com’ ei pesa* is condensed into “the weight”. Tenses are also adjusted: Italian imperfects sometimes become simple pasts, while *venieno* becomes “have entered”. These changes coexist with close echoes of Italian syntax and diction, including “with similar act and similar face” and the unusual “accoupled”.

Word choice ranges from close correspondence to interpretation or substitution. *Senza compagnia* becomes the narrower “without escort”, *levre*, ‘hare’, becomes “leveret”, and *fosca*, ‘dark’ or ‘dusky’, becomes “brown”. Some figurative expressions are made more explicit or less figurative: *s’ aggueffa*, ‘winds itself into a skein’, becomes “be added”, while *vivagno*, ‘selvage’, becomes “bank”. The substitution of “Ay and Yea” for *mo ed issa*, both meaning ‘now’, preserves synonymy rather than the words’ meaning. By contrast, such phrases as “a painted people” and “weary mantle for eternity” retain the Italian’s distinctive imagery.

Small additions and omissions further shape the prose. Carlyle supplies “case”, “of each”, and “spirits” to identify references left implicit, and “compared to them” to spell out a comparison. Definite articles frequently become possessives, as *il figlio* becomes “her child” and *nella barba* becomes “into his beard”. Repeated prepositions are often dropped, and words such as *pure* and *pria* receive no separate equivalent. Occasionally the departure is more substantial: *solo una camicia vesta* is recast as “even to cast a shift about her”, changing the emphasis of the dressing clause. Overall, the translation remains closely tied to the Italian’s verbal texture, but repeatedly clarifies, rearranges, and interprets rather than following it word for word.
