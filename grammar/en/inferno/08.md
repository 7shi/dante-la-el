# Inferno 8

## Lines 1–9 — Signals From the Tower

> 1 Io dico seguitando, ch’ assai prima  
> 2 Che noi fussimo al piè dell’ alta torre,  
> 3 Gli occhi nostri n’ andâr suso alla cima,  

I say continuing, that, long before we reached the foot of the high tower, our eyes went upwards to its summit,

- *assai prima Che* ‘much before’ → “long before”: the degree expressed by *assai* is rendered as a length of time.
- *noi fussimo al piè* ‘we were at the foot’ → “we reached the foot”: being at a place becomes arriving there; the Italian imperfect subjunctive becomes an English simple past indicative.
- *Gli occhi nostri* ‘the eyes our’ → “our eyes”: the possessive precedes rather than follows the noun.
- *n’ andâr* ‘went thence’ → “went”: the pronominal particle *ne*, attached to the verb of movement, has no separate English equivalent.
- *alla cima* ‘to the summit’ → “to its summit”: the definite article becomes a possessive referring to the tower.

> 4 Per due fiammette che i vedemmo porre,  
> 5 E un’ altra da lungi render cenno,  
> 6 Tanto, che a pena il potea l’ occhio torre.  

because of two flamelets, that we saw put there, and another from far give signal back; so far that the eye could scarcely catch it.

- *Per* ‘for’ → “because of”: the causal sense of the preposition is made explicit.
- *che i vedemmo porre* ‘that there we saw put’ → “that we saw put there”: the locative *i*, meaning ‘there’, moves to the end; the Italian active infinitive *porre*, with an unspecified agent, becomes passive “put” after “saw”.
- *render cenno* ‘return a signal’ → “give signal back”: the single verb becomes a verb with “back”, and the singular noun is used without an article.
- *Tanto* ‘so much’ → “so far”: the degree is expressly interpreted as distance.
- *a pena il potea l’ occhio torre* ‘scarcely it could the eye take’ → “the eye could scarcely catch it”: the subject moves before the modal verb, the adverb follows it, and the object follows the main verb; *torre*, ‘take’, becomes “catch” in the sense of visual perception.

> 7 Ed io, rivolto al mar di tutto il senno,  

And I, turning to the Sea of all intelligence,

- *rivolto* ‘turned’ → “turning”: the past participle becomes a present participle, presenting the action rather than its resulting state.
- *tutto il senno* ‘all the intelligence’ → “all intelligence”: the definite article before the abstract noun is omitted.

> 8 Dissi: Questo che dice? e che risponde  
> 9 Quell’ altro foco? e chi son quei che il fenno?  

said: “What says this? and what replies yon other fire? And who are they that made it?”

- *Questo che dice* ‘this what says’ → “What says this”: the interrogative object comes first, and the demonstrative subject moves after the verb.
- *chi son quei* ‘who are those’ → “who are they”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *il fenno* ‘it made’ → “made it”: the object pronoun moves from before the verb to after it.

## Lines 10–24 — Phlegyas Approaches in Anger

> 10 Ed egli a me: Su per le sucide onde  
> 11 Già puoi scorgere quello che s’ aspetta,  
> 12 Se il fummo del pantan nol ti nasconde.  

And he to me: “Over the squalid waves, already thou mayest discern what is expected, if the vapour of the fen conceal it not from thee.”

- *Su per* ‘up along’ → “Over”: the combined directional expression becomes a single preposition.
- *le sucide onde* ‘the filthy waves’ → “the squalid waves”: “squalid” gives a broader sense of foulness than the more directly physical ‘filthy’.
- *quello che s’ aspetta* ‘that which one awaits’ → “what is expected”: the demonstrative and relative become “what”, and the impersonal construction becomes passive.
- *il fummo* ‘the smoke’ → “the vapour”: the translation interprets the substance as marsh vapour rather than smoke.
- *nol ti nasconde* ‘does not hide it from thee’ → “conceal it not from thee”: the object pronouns move after the verb, and the Italian indicative becomes the uninflected conditional form “conceal”.

> 13 Corda non pinse mai da se saetta,  
> 14 Che sì corresse via per l’ aer snella,  

Never did cord impel from itself an arrow, that ran through the air so quickly,

- *Corda non pinse mai* ‘cord never impelled’ → “Never did cord impel”: the negative adverb moves to the beginning, requiring inversion and the auxiliary “did”.
- *saetta* ‘arrow’ → “an arrow”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *Che sì corresse via per l’ aer snella* ‘that so ran away through the air, swift’ → “that ran through the air so quickly”: the imperfect subjunctive becomes a simple past indicative; the adjective *snella*, describing the arrow, becomes the adverb “quickly”; “so” is placed immediately before it; and *via*, ‘away’, is omitted.

> 15 Com’ io vidi una nave piccioletta  
> 16 Venir per l’ acqua verso noi in quella,  

as a little bark which I saw come towards us then,

- *Com’ io vidi una nave piccioletta Venir per l’ acqua* ‘as I saw a boat very little come through the water’ → “as a little bark which I saw come”: the comparison is recast around the boat, with “which I saw come” made a relative clause; the diminutive adjective becomes the preceding “little”; *nave* is rendered by the more specific “bark”; and ‘through the water’ is omitted.
- *in quella* ‘at that moment’ → “then”: the idiomatic phrase becomes a single adverb.

> 17 Sotto il governo d’ un sol galeoto,  
> 18 Che gridava: Or se’ giunta, anima fella?  

under the guidance of a single steersman, who cried: “Now art thou arrived, fell spirit?”

- *Sotto il governo* ‘under the steering’ → “under the guidance”: the nautical sense of directing the vessel is expressed by the more general “guidance”.
- *d’ un sol galeoto* ‘of a single boatman’ → “of a single steersman”: the translation specifies steering rather than the broader work of a boatman or oarsman.
- *gridava* ‘was crying’ → “cried”: the imperfect’s ongoing action is rendered by a simple past.
- *Or se’ giunta* ‘now thou art arrived’ → “Now art thou arrived”: English inverts subject and verb to mark the question.
- *anima fella* ‘soul wicked’ → “fell spirit”: “spirit” replaces ‘soul’, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 19 Flegiàs, Flegiàs, tu gridi a vôto,  
> 20 Disse lo mio Signore, a questa volta:  
> 21 Più non ci avrai, se non passando il loto.  

“Phlegyas, Phlegyas,” said my Lord, “this time thou criest in vain. Thou shalt not have us longer than while we pass the wash.”

- *tu gridi a vôto, Disse lo mio Signore, a questa volta* ‘thou criest in vain, said my Lord, on this occasion’ → “said my Lord, “this time thou criest in vain”: the reporting clause moves forward, interrupting the speech immediately after the repeated name rather than after ‘thou criest in vain’; the prepositional phrase *a questa volta* becomes the unprepositioned “this time” and moves before “thou criest in vain”.
- *Più non ci avrai* ‘no longer us thou wilt have’ → “Thou shalt not have us longer”: the object moves after the verb, and the initial *Più* is relocated as “longer” at the end.
- *se non passando il loto* ‘except passing the mud’ → “than while we pass the wash”: the exception becomes a comparison dependent on “longer”; the gerund becomes a finite temporal clause with “we” as its explicit subject; and ‘mud’ becomes “wash”, emphasizing the watery expanse.

> 22 Quale colui, che grande inganno ascolta,  
> 23 Che gli sia fatto, e poi se ne rammarca,  
> 24 Tal si fe’ Flegiàs nell’ ira accolta.  

As one who listens to some great deceit which has been done to him, and then sore resents it; such grew Phlegyas in his gathered rage.

- *Quale colui* ‘as he’ → “As one”: the demonstrative becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *che grande inganno ascolta* ‘who great deceit hears’ → “who listens to some great deceit”: the object moves after the verb, “some” is supplied, and hearing is rendered as the more deliberate “listens to”.
- *Che gli sia fatto* ‘which to him be done’ → “which has been done to him”: the subjunctive construction becomes present-perfect indicative, explicitly presenting the deceit as completed; the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *e poi se ne rammarca* ‘and then grieves over it’ → “and then sore resents it”: grieving or lamenting becomes resentment, “sore” supplies an intensifier, and the reflexive construction with *ne*, ‘over it’, becomes a transitive verb with “it” as its object.
- *Tal si fe’ Flegiàs* ‘such made himself Phlegyas’ → “such grew Phlegyas”: the reflexive ‘made himself’ becomes the intransitive “grew”, expressing a change of state.
- *nell’ ira accolta* ‘in the anger gathered’ → “in his gathered rage”: “his” replaces the definite article, and the participial adjective moves before the noun.

## Lines 25–36 — Entering the Boat and Meeting a Muddy Spirit

> 25 Lo Duca mio discese nella barca,  
> 26 E poi mi fece entrare appresso lui,  

My Guide descended into the skiff, and then made me enter after him;

- *Lo Duca mio* ‘my leader’ → “My Guide”: *Duca* is rendered as “Guide,” and the possessive moves from after the noun to before it.
- *barca* ‘boat’ → “skiff”: the translation uses a more specific term for a small boat.
- *mi fece entrare* ‘me made enter’ → “made me enter”: the pronoun moves after the causative verb, as English requires.

> 27 E sol, quand’ io fui dentro, parve carca.  

and not till I was in, did it seem laden.

- *sol, quand’ io fui dentro* ‘only when I was in’ → “not till I was in”: an affirmative restriction becomes a negative construction with “till.”
- *parve carca* ‘seemed laden’ → “did it seem laden”: the preceding negative expression prompts subject–auxiliary inversion and the addition of “did.”

> 28 Tosto che il Duca ed io nel legno fui,  
> 29 Segando se ne va l’ antica prora  
> 30 Dell’ acqua più che non suol con altrui.  

Soon as my Guide and I were in the boat, its ancient prow went on, cutting more of the water than it is wont with others.

- *il Duca* ‘the leader’ → “my Guide”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and *Duca* is rendered as “Guide.”
- *nel legno fui* ‘in the wood I was’ → “were in the boat”: *legno*, literally ‘wood,’ is rendered by the vessel it denotes; the locative phrase moves after the verb, and the Italian first-person singular becomes plural to agree with “my Guide and I.”
- *Segando se ne va l’ antica prora* ‘cutting goes away the ancient prow’ → “its ancient prow went on, cutting”: the gerund moves from before the main verb and its subject to after them; the subject moves before the verb; the present becomes past; the pronominal expression of departure becomes “went on”; and the definite article becomes “its.”
- *Dell’ acqua più* ‘of the water more’ → “more of the water”: the quantity word moves before the partitive phrase.
- *che non suol* ‘than it is not wont’ → “than it is wont”: the comparative *non*, which does not negate the comparison, is omitted.

> 31 Mentre noi correvam la morta gora,  
> 32 Dinanzi mi si fece un pien di fango,  
> 33 E disse: Chi se’ tu, che vieni anzi ora?  

Whilst we were running through the dead channel, there rose before me one full of mud, and said: “Who art thou, that comest before thy time?”

- *noi correvam la morta gora* ‘we ran the dead channel’ → “we were running through the dead channel”: the imperfect is expressed by the past progressive, and “through” is supplied where Italian makes the channel the direct object of *correvam*.
- *Dinanzi mi si fece* ‘before me he made himself’ → “there rose before me”: the reflexive expression meaning ‘presented himself’ becomes “rose”; “there” is supplied, and the phrase locating the figure before the speaker moves after the verb.
- *anzi ora* ‘before the hour’ → “before thy time”: *ora* is rendered as “time,” and the possessive “thy” is supplied.

> 34 Ed io a lui: S’ io vegno, io non rimango;  
> 35 Ma tu chi sei, che sì sei fatto brutto?  

And I to him: “If I come, I remain not. But thou, who art thou, that hast become so foul?”

- *io non rimango* ‘I not remain’ → “I remain not”: the negative moves from before the verb to after it.
- *tu chi sei* ‘thou who art’ → “thou, who art thou”: the initial pronoun is retained as a detached topic and repeated within the question.
- *che sì sei fatto brutto* ‘that so art made ugly’ → “that hast become so foul”: *sei fatto* is interpreted as the compound tense of reflexive *farsi*, ‘to become,’ rather than as a passive; “so” moves immediately before the adjective, and *brutto* is rendered as “foul.”

> 36 Rispose: Vedi che son un che piango.  

He answered: “Thou seest that I am one who weep.”

- Literal.

## Lines 37–48 — Rebuke, Praise, and Condemnation

> 37 Ed io a lui: Con piangere e con lutto,  
> 38 Spirito maledetto, ti rimani;  
> 39 Ch’ io ti conosco, ancor sie lordo tutto.  

And I to him: “With weeping, and with sorrow, accursed spirit, remain thou! For I know thee, all filthy as thou art.”

- *piangere* ‘to weep’ → “weeping”: the Italian infinitive used as a noun becomes an English *-ing* form.
- *lutto* ‘mourning’ → “sorrow”: the expression of grief becomes the more general feeling.
- *Spirito maledetto* ‘spirit accursed’ → “accursed spirit”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *ti rimani* ‘remain thyself’ → “remain thou”: the pronominal construction becomes a simple intransitive imperative; *ti* has no separate equivalent.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *ancor sie lordo tutto* ‘although thou art filthy all over’ → “all filthy as thou art”: the concessive construction is recast with “as”, and the adjective and its modifier move before the subject and verb.

> 40 Allora stese al legno ambe le mani:  
> 41 Per che il Maestro accorto lo sospinse,  
> 42 Dicendo: Via costà con gli altri cani.  

Then he stretched both hands to the boat, whereat the wary Master thrust him off, saying: “Away there with the other dogs!”

- *al legno* ‘to the wood’ → “to the boat”: the material standing for the vessel is replaced by the vessel’s ordinary name; the phrase moves after the direct object.
- *ambe le mani* ‘both the hands’ → “both hands”: the definite article is omitted.
- *Per che* ‘for which reason’ → “whereat”: the connective expressing consequence becomes a relative adverb referring to the preceding action.
- *il Maestro accorto* ‘the Master wary’ → “the wary Master”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *lo sospinse* ‘him pushed away’ → “thrust him off”: the object pronoun moves after the verb, and the action is expressed by a verb with the particle “off”.
- *costà* ‘there by thee’ → “there”: the Italian indication of a place near the person addressed is not made explicit.

> 43 Lo collo poi con le braccia mi cinse,  
> 44 Baciommi il volto, e disse: Alma sdegnosa,  
> 45 Benedetta colei che in te s’ incinse.  

And he put his arms about my neck, kissed my face, and said: “Indignant soul! blessed be she that bore thee.

- *Lo collo poi con le braccia mi cinse* ‘the neck then with the arms he encircled for me’ → “And he put his arms about my neck”: “And” replaces the temporal connection supplied by *poi*. The neck, originally the direct object, becomes the object of “about”; the arms, originally instrumental, become the direct object. The possessive “his” is supplied, and the personal pronoun *mi* becomes “my” modifying “neck”.
- *Baciommi il volto* ‘kissed me the face’ → “kissed my face”: the personal pronoun attached to the verb becomes a possessive modifying the body part.
- *Alma sdegnosa* ‘soul indignant’ → “Indignant soul”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Benedetta* ‘blessed’ → “blessed be”: English supplies a verb to express the blessing.
- *in te s’ incinse* ‘with thee became pregnant’ → “bore thee”: becoming pregnant is rendered as bearing a child, and the Italian pronominal construction with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with “thee” as its object.

> 46 Quei fu al mondo persona orgogliosa:  

In your world, that was an arrogant personage.

- *al mondo* ‘in the world’ → “In your world”: the definite article is replaced by “your”, and the phrase moves to the beginning of the sentence.
- *persona orgogliosa* ‘person arrogant’ → “an arrogant personage”: “personage” replaces the ordinary noun ‘person’, the adjective moves before it, and English supplies an indefinite article.

> 47 Bontà non è che sua memoria fregi:  

Good there is none to ornament the memory of him:

- *Bontà non è* ‘goodness there is not’ → “Good there is none”: the abstract noun becomes substantival “Good”, and the negative construction uses “none” rather than simply negating the verb.
- *che … fregi* ‘that … may adorn’ → “to ornament”: the finite relative clause, whose verb is subjunctive, becomes an infinitive construction; the verb now precedes its object.
- *sua memoria* ‘his memory’ → “the memory of him”: the possessive adjective becomes a following phrase with “of”.

> 48 Così è l’ ombra sua qui furiosa.  

so is his shadow here in fury.

- *l’ombra sua* ‘the shadow his’ → “his shadow”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *furiosa* ‘furious’ → “in fury”: the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase containing a noun.

## Lines 49–61 — The Rending of Filippo Argenti

> 49 Quanti si tengon or lassù gran regi,  
> 50 Che qui staranno come porci in brago,  
> 51 Di se lasciando orribili dispregi!  

How many up there now think themselves great kings, that shall lie here like swine in mire, leaving behind them horrible reproaches!”

- *si tengon or lassù gran regi* ‘hold themselves now up there great kings’ → “up there now think themselves great kings”: ‘hold themselves’ becomes “think themselves”; the expressions of place and time move before the verb and exchange order.
- *Che qui staranno* ‘who here will stay’ → “that shall lie here”: the general verb of remaining becomes the more specific “lie,” and “here” moves after the verb.
- *Di se lasciando* ‘of themselves leaving’ → “leaving behind them”: the phrase ‘of themselves,’ qualifying the reproaches, becomes “behind them,” attached to “leaving.”

> 52 Ed io: Maestro, molto sarei vago  
> 53 Di vederlo attuffare in questa broda,  
> 54 Prima che noi uscissimo del lago.  

And I: “Master, I should be glad to see him dipped in this swill, ere we quit the lake.”

- *molto sarei vago* ‘very much I would be desirous’ → “I should be glad”: *molto* is omitted, and being desirous becomes being glad.
- *Di vederlo attuffare* ‘of seeing someone plunge him’ → “to see him dipped”: the prepositional complement becomes an English infinitive; the active infinitive *attuffare*, with an unspecified agent, becomes the passive participle “dipped.”
- *broda* ‘broth’ → “swill”: the translation chooses a more explicitly contemptuous English noun.
- *Prima che noi uscissimo del lago* ‘before we should go out of the lake’ → “ere we quit the lake”: ‘go out of’ becomes the transitive “quit”; the Italian imperfect subjunctive becomes an English present form in a temporal clause.

> 55 Ed egli a me: Avanti che la proda,  
> 56 Ti si lasci veder, tu sarai sazio:  
> 57 Di tal disio converrà che tu goda.  

And he to me: “Before the shore comes to thy view, thou shalt be satisfied. It is fitting that thou shouldst be gratified in such a wish.”

- *Avanti che la proda, Ti si lasci veder* ‘before the shore lets itself be seen by you’ → “Before the shore comes to thy view”: the reflexive construction becomes an idiom of coming into view; the infinitive ‘see’ becomes the noun “view,” and the personal pronoun becomes the possessive “thy.”
- *sazio* ‘sated’ → “satisfied”: the metaphor of satiety is rendered by a more general term.
- *Di tal disio converrà che tu goda* ‘of such a desire it will be necessary that you take pleasure’ → “It is fitting that thou shouldst be gratified in such a wish”: future necessity becomes present fittingness; active enjoyment becomes passive gratification, with “shouldst” expressing the subordinate subjunctive. The phrase identifying the desire moves from the beginning to the end.

> 58 Dopo ciò poco, vidi quello strazio  
> 59 Far di costui alle fangose genti,  
> 60 Che Dio ancor ne lodo e ne ringrazio.  

A little after this, I saw the muddy people make such rending of him, that even now I praise and thank God for it.

- *Dopo ciò poco* ‘after this a little’ → “A little after this”: the expression of the short interval moves before the expression of sequence.
- *vidi quello strazio Far di costui alle fangose genti* ‘I saw that torment done to this man by the muddy people’ → “I saw the muddy people make such rending of him”: the Italian construction places the torment first and marks the agents with *alle*; English places “the muddy people” immediately after “saw,” as the subject of “make.” “Such” replaces the demonstrative ‘that’ in anticipation of the following result clause; “rending” makes *strazio* more specifically physical, and “him” replaces ‘this man.’
- *Dio ancor ne lodo e ne ringrazio* ‘God still for it I praise and for it thank’ → “even now I praise and thank God for it”: “God” moves from before the verbs to after both; *ancor* becomes the emphatic “even now,” placed first. The two occurrences of *ne* are combined into one final “for it.”

> 61 Tutti gridavano: A Filippo Argenti.  

All cried: “At Filippo Argenti!”

- *gridavano* ‘were crying’ → “cried”: the Italian imperfect, presenting the cries as ongoing, becomes an English simple past.

## Lines 62–75 — The Approach to Dis

> 62 Lo Fiorentino spirito bizzarro  
> 63 In se medesmo si volgea co’ denti.  

The passionate Florentine spirit turned with his teeth upon himself.

- *Lo Fiorentino spirito bizzarro* ‘the Florentine spirit irascible’ → “The passionate Florentine spirit”: “passionate” renders the more specifically ill-tempered *bizzarro*, and the adjective moves before the noun and its other modifier.
- *In se medesmo* ‘in himself’ → “upon himself”: the preposition expresses an attack directed against himself; the phrase moves from before the verb to after the phrase about his teeth.
- *si volgea* ‘was turning himself’ → “turned”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, and the Italian imperfect becomes an English simple past.
- *co’ denti* ‘with the teeth’ → “with his teeth”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 64 Quivi il lasciammo, che più non ne narro:  

Here we left him, so that of him I tell no more.

- *Quivi* ‘there’ → “Here”: the spatial demonstrative changes.
- *il lasciammo* ‘him we left’ → “we left him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *che più non ne narro* ‘for no more of him I tell’ → “so that of him I tell no more”: *che* is read as introducing a consequence rather than an explanation; the reference to him moves before the verb, while the negative expression moves after it.

> 65 Ma negli orecchi mi percosse un duolo,  
> 66 Per ch’ io avanti intento l’ occhio sbarro.  

But in my ears a wailing smote me, whereat I bent my eyes intently forwards.

- *negli orecchi* ‘in the ears’ → “in my ears”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *mi percosse un duolo* ‘me struck a grief’ → “a wailing smote me”: *duolo* is rendered as an audible expression of grief; the subject moves before the verb and the object after it.
- *Per ch’* ‘because of which’ → “whereat”: the connective phrase becomes a single relative adverb.
- *io avanti intento l’ occhio sbarro* ‘I forwards intent the eye open wide’ → “I bent my eyes intently forwards”: opening the eye wide becomes directing the eyes; the present becomes past, the singular becomes plural, and the definite article becomes a possessive. *Intento*, describing the speaker, becomes the adverb “intently”; the verb moves before its object, and “forwards” moves to the end.

> 67 E il buon Maestro disse: Omai, figliuolo,  
> 68 S’ appressa la città che ha nome Dite,  
> 69 Co’ gravi cittadin, col grande stuolo.  

And the kind Master said: “Now, Son, the city that is named of Dis draws nigh, with its grave citizens, with its great company.”

- *buon* ‘good’ → “kind”: the translation selects kindness as the particular quality intended.
- *S’ appressa la città che ha nome Dite* ‘draws near the city that has the name Dis’ → “the city that is named of Dis draws nigh”: the subject and its relative clause move before the main verb; having a name becomes the passive construction “is named of”.
- *Co’ gravi cittadin* ‘with the grave citizens’ → “with its grave citizens”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *col grande stuolo* ‘with the great host’ → “with its great company”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “company” replaces ‘host’.

> 70 Ed io: Maestro, già le sue meschite  
> 71 Là entro certo nella valle cerno  

And I: “Master, already I discern its mosques, distinctly there within the valley,

- *già le sue meschite Là entro certo nella valle cerno* ‘already its mosques there inside certainly in the valley I discern’ → “already I discern its mosques, distinctly there within the valley”: the verb moves from the end to before its object. *Certo* becomes “distinctly”, emphasizing clarity of perception rather than certainty, and moves before the spatial phrase. *Là entro … nella valle* is compressed into “there within the valley”, without separately rendering both ‘inside’ and ‘in’.

> 72 Vermiglie, come se di fuoco uscite  
> 73 Fossero. Ed ei mi disse: Il foco eterno,  
> 74 Ch’ entro le affoca, le dimostra rosse,  
> 75 Come tu vedi in questo basso inferno.  

red as if they had come out of fire.” And to me he said: “The eternal fire, which causes them to glow within, shows them red, as thou seest, in this low Hell.”

- *Vermiglie* ‘vermilion’ → “red”: the translation uses a less specific colour term.
- *di fuoco uscite Fossero* ‘out of fire come forth they had’ → “they had come out of fire”: the auxiliary precedes the participle, and the phrase expressing origin follows the verb.
- *Ed ei mi disse* ‘and he to me said’ → “And to me he said”: the indirect object moves before the subject.
- *Il foco eterno* ‘the fire eternal’ → “The eternal fire”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Ch’ entro le affoca* ‘which within them sets afire’ → “which causes them to glow within”: the single verb becomes a causative phrase, with “glow” emphasizing the visible effect of the burning; the object follows “causes”, and “within” moves to the end.
- *le dimostra rosse* ‘them shows red’ → “shows them red”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

## Lines 76–87 — Arrival at the Iron Walls

> 76 Noi pur giugnemmo dentro all’ alte fosse,  
> 77 Che vallan quella terra sconsolata:  

We now arrived in the deep fosses, which moat that joyless city.

- *pur* ‘at last’ → “now”: the sense of arrival finally achieved is rendered as a temporal transition.
- *dentro all’ alte fosse* ‘within the deep fosses’ → “in the deep fosses”: “in” replaces the more explicit *dentro a*, ‘within’; the adjective *alte* is understood as describing depth.
- *quella terra sconsolata* ‘that city joyless’ → “that joyless city”: *terra* is rendered in its sense of ‘city’, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 78 Le mura mi parea, che ferro fosse.  

The walls seemed to me as if they were of iron.

- *Le mura mi parea, che ferro fosse* ‘It seemed to me that the walls were iron’ → “The walls seemed to me as if they were of iron”: the impersonal construction *mi parea* becomes a construction with “The walls” as subject; *che* becomes “as if”; the singular *fosse* becomes the plural “were”; and “of” is supplied before the material.

> 79 Non senza prima far grande aggirata,  
> 80 Venimmo in parte, dove il nocchier, forte,  
> 81 Uscite, ci gridò; qui è l’ entrata.  

Not before making a long circuit, did we come to a place where the boatman loudly cried to us: “Go out: here is the entrance.”

- *Non senza prima far grande aggirata* ‘Not without first making a great circuit’ → “Not before making a long circuit”: ‘not without first’ is recast as “Not before”; *grande* becomes “long”, specifying the circuit’s extent.
- *Venimmo* ‘we came’ → “did we come”: the preceding negative phrase prompts inversion and the auxiliary “did”.
- *in parte* ‘in a place’ → “to a place”: the preposition expresses destination in English, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *forte … ci gridò* ‘loudly … to us he cried’ → “loudly cried to us”: the reporting verb is moved before the command, and the object pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase following the verb.

> 82 Io vidi più di mille in sulle porte  
> 83 Dal ciel piovuti, che stizzosamente  
> 84 Dicean: Chi è costui, che senza morte  
> 85 Va per lo regno della morta gente?  

Above the gates I saw more than a thousand spirits, rained from Heaven, who angrily exclaimed: “Who is that, who, without death, goes through the kingdom of the dead?”

- *in sulle porte* ‘upon the gates’ → “Above the gates”: position upon the gates becomes position above them, and the phrase moves to the beginning of the sentence.
- *più di mille* ‘more than a thousand’ → “more than a thousand spirits”: “spirits” supplies a noun where the Italian leaves the beings counted unnamed.
- *Dal ciel piovuti* ‘from Heaven rained’ → “rained from Heaven”: the participle moves before the phrase expressing origin.
- *Dicean* ‘were saying’ → “exclaimed”: the neutral verb of speaking becomes a more emphatic one, and the imperfect is rendered by a simple past.
- *Chi è costui* ‘Who is this one’ → “Who is that”: the personal demonstrative becomes “that”, rather than ‘this one’.
- *morta gente* ‘dead people’ → “the dead”: the collective noun and its adjective become a substantival adjective.

> 86 E il savio mio Maestro fece segno  
> 87 Di voler lor parlar segretamente.  

And my sage Master made a sign of wishing to speak with them in secret.

- *savio mio Maestro* ‘sage my Master’ → “my sage Master”: the possessive moves before the descriptive adjective.
- *fece segno* ‘made sign’ → “made a sign”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *Di voler* ‘of to wish’ → “of wishing”: the Italian infinitive following a preposition becomes an English gerund.
- *lor parlar* ‘to them to speak’ → “to speak with them”: the pronoun moves after the infinitive, and speaking ‘to’ them becomes speaking “with” them.
- *segretamente* ‘secretly’ → “in secret”: an adverb becomes a prepositional phrase.

## Lines 88–102 — Threats and a Plea for Safety

> 88 Allor chiusero un poco il gran disdegno,  

Then they somewhat shut up their great disdain,

- *chiusero* ‘closed’ → “shut up”: a simple verb becomes a phrasal verb.
- *un poco* ‘a little’ → “somewhat”: the phrase becomes a single adverb and is placed before the verb rather than after it.
- *il gran disdegno* ‘the great disdain’ → “their great disdain”: the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 89 E disser: Vien tu solo, e quei sen vada,  
> 90 Che sì ardito entrò per questo regno:  

and said: “Come thou alone; and let that one go, who has entered so daringly into this kingdom.

- *e quei sen vada* ‘and that one go away’ → “and let that one go”: the third-person subjunctive command is expressed with “let”; the explicit sense of departure in *sen vada* is not separately rendered.
- *sì ardito* ‘so daring’ → “so daringly”: an adjective describing the subject becomes an adverb describing the action and moves after the verb.
- *entrò* ‘entered’ → “has entered”: the simple past becomes a present perfect.
- *per questo regno* ‘through this kingdom’ → “into this kingdom”: passage through becomes entry into.

> 91 Sol si ritorni per la folle strada:  
> 92 Provi se sa; chè tu qui rimarrai,  
> 93 Che scorto l’ hai per sì buia contrada.  

Let him return alone his foolish way: try, if he can; for thou shalt stay here, that hast escorted him through so dark a country.”

- *Sol si ritorni* ‘alone let him return’ → “Let him return alone”: “alone” moves after the verb; the pronominal *si* has no separate English equivalent.
- *per la folle strada* ‘by the foolish road’ → “his foolish way”: the preposition is omitted, leaving an expression of the route without a preposition; the definite article becomes a possessive, and *strada* becomes “way”.
- *Provi se sa* ‘let him try if he knows how’ → “try, if he can”: the third-person subjunctive is rendered by the bare “try”, with “let him” understood from the preceding command; knowing how becomes being able.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *tu qui rimarrai* ‘thou here shalt remain’ → “thou shalt stay here”: the adverb moves after the verb, and *rimanere* is rendered as “stay”.
- *Che scorto l’ hai* ‘who escorted him hast’ → “that hast escorted him”: the auxiliary precedes the participle, and the object pronoun follows it; the relative pronoun is rendered as “that”.
- *sì buia contrada* ‘so dark country’ → “so dark a country”: English supplies an indefinite article within the construction “so dark a”.

> 94 Pensa, Lettor, s’ io mi disconfortai  
> 95 Nel suon delle parole maledette:  
> 96 Ch’ io non credetti ritornarci mai.  

Judge, Reader, if I was discouraged at the sound of the accursed words; for I believed not that I ever should return by it.

- *Pensa* ‘think’ → “Judge”: thinking is rendered as forming a judgment.
- *s’ io mi disconfortai* ‘if I lost heart’ → “if I was discouraged”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes a construction with “was” and a participle.
- *Nel suon* ‘in the sound’ → “at the sound”: the preposition changes to express the occasion or cause of the reaction.
- *parole maledette* ‘words accursed’ → “accursed words”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *io non credetti* ‘I not believed’ → “I believed not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *ritornarci mai* ‘ever to return here’ → “that I ever should return by it”: the infinitive becomes a finite clause with “that”, an explicit subject, and “should”. The locative *ci* is interpreted as referring to a route, “by it”, rather than rendered as ‘here’.

> 97 O caro Duca mio, che più di sette  
> 98 Volte m’ hai sicurtà renduta, e tratto  
> 99 D’ alto periglio che incontra mi stette,  
> 100 Non mi lasciar, diss’ io, così disfatto:  

“O my loved Guide, who more than seven times hast restored me to safety, and rescued from deep peril that stood before me, leave me not so undone,” I said:

- *caro Duca mio* ‘dear leader my’ → “my loved Guide”: the possessive moves before the noun; *caro* becomes “loved”, and *Duca* is rendered according to its sense of a guide rather than simply a leader.
- *m’ hai sicurtà renduta* ‘to me hast safety restored’ → “hast restored me to safety”: Italian makes safety the thing restored and the speaker its recipient; English makes the speaker the direct object and safety the resulting condition. The auxiliary and participle are also brought together.
- *tratto* ‘drawn’ → “rescued”: the verb’s sense of deliverance replaces its literal sense of drawing or pulling.
- *D’ alto periglio* ‘from great peril’ → “from deep peril”: *alto*, expressing the peril’s magnitude, is rendered with the spatial adjective “deep”.
- *che incontra mi stette* ‘that against me stood’ → “that stood before me”: the verb moves before the expression relating the peril to the speaker, and “before” replaces the adversative sense of *incontra*.
- *Non mi lasciar* ‘not me leave’ → “leave me not”: the object and negative move after the verb; the Italian negative command, formed with an infinitive, becomes an English imperative.
- *diss’ io* ‘said I’ → “I said”: subject–verb order is reversed, and the reporting clause moves from before *così disfatto* to after “so undone”.

> 101 E se l’ andar più oltre c’ è negato,  
> 102 Ritroviam l’ orme nostre insieme ratto.  

“and if to go farther be denied us, let us retrace our steps together rapidly.”

- *l’ andar* ‘the going’ → “to go”: the substantivized infinitive with its definite article becomes an English to-infinitive.
- *più oltre* ‘more beyond’ → “farther”: the two-word expression becomes a single comparative adverb.
- *c’ è negato* ‘to us is denied’ → “be denied us”: the recipient moves after the participle, without a preposition; the Italian indicative ‘is’ becomes the English subjunctive “be” in the conditional clause.
- *Ritroviam l’ orme nostre* ‘let us find again the footprints our’ → “let us retrace our steps”: finding the footprints again becomes the idiomatic “retrace our steps”; the possessive moves before the noun.

## Lines 103–117 — Reassurance and Closed Gates

> 103 E quel Signor, che lì m’ avea menato,  
> 104 Mi disse: Non temer, chè il nostro passo  
> 105 Non ci può torre alcun: da tal n’ è dato.  

And that Lord, who had led me thither, said to me: “Fear not; for our passage none can take from us: by Such has it been given to us.

- *lì m’ avea menato* ‘there me had led’ → “had led me thither”: the object and adverb follow the verb; “thither” expresses direction, whereas *lì* denotes location.
- *Mi disse* ‘to me said’ → “said to me”: the indirect object moves after the verb.
- *Non temer* ‘not fear’ → “Fear not”: the negative follows the imperative.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *il nostro passo / Non ci può torre alcun* ‘our passage not from us can take anyone’ → “our passage none can take from us”: the negative and indefinite subject become “none,” placed before the verbs; “from us” moves after “take.”
- *da tal n’ è dato* ‘by such a one to us it is given’ → “by Such has it been given to us”: *tal* is rendered substantivally without ‘a one’; the present passive becomes a present perfect passive, the auxiliary precedes the subject, and the indirect object moves to the end.

> 106 Ma qui m’ attendi; e lo spirito lasso  
> 107 Conforta e ciba di speranza buona,  
> 108 Ch’ io non ti lascerò nel mondo basso.  

But thou, wait here for me; and comfort and feed thy wearied spirit with good hope; for I will not forsake thee in the low world.”

- *qui m’ attendi* ‘here me await’ → “thou, wait here for me”: the adverb and object move after the verb; transitive ‘await’ becomes “wait” with “for,” and the expressed “thou” gives the subject additional emphasis.
- *lo spirito lasso / Conforta e ciba* ‘the spirit weary comfort and feed’ → “comfort and feed thy wearied spirit”: the object follows the verbs, the article becomes a possessive, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *di speranza buona* ‘of hope good’ → “with good hope”: “with” replaces *di*, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *io non ti lascerò* ‘I not thee shall leave’ → “I will not forsake thee”: “forsake” gives ‘leave’ the more specific sense of abandonment; the negative follows the auxiliary, and the object follows the main verb.
- *nel mondo basso* ‘in the world low’ → “in the low world”: the adjective precedes the noun.

> 109 Così sen va, e quivi m’ abbandona  
> 110 Lo dolce padre, ed io rimango in forse;  
> 111 Chè il sì, e il no nel capo mi tenzona.  

Thus the gentle Father goes, and leaves me here. And I remain in doubt; for yes and no contend within my head.

- *sen va … Lo dolce padre* ‘goes away … the sweet father’ → “the gentle Father goes”: the delayed subject moves before the verb, “gentle” replaces ‘sweet,’ and the departure expressed by *sen va* is reduced to “goes.”
- *quivi m’ abbandona* ‘there me abandons’ → “leaves me here”: “here” replaces ‘there,’ “leaves” replaces ‘abandons,’ and both object and adverb move after the verb.
- *in forse* ‘in perhaps’ → “in doubt”: the Italian idiom, using *forse* substantivally, becomes an English noun phrase.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *il sì, e il no* ‘the yes and the no’ → “yes and no”: the articles marking the words as nouns are omitted.
- *nel capo mi tenzona* ‘in the head to me contends’ → “contend within my head”: the singular verb becomes plural; the phrase locating the contention follows the verb, and the personal dative *mi* becomes the possessive “my.”

> 112 Udir non potei quello che a lor porse:  

I could not hear that which he offered to them.

- *Udir non potei* ‘hear I could not’ → “I could not hear”: the fronted infinitive moves after the modal verb and negative.
- *a lor porse* ‘to them he offered’ → “he offered to them”: the indirect object moves after the verb.

> 113 Ma ei non stette là con essi guari,  
> 114 Che ciascun dentro a pruova si ricorse.  

But he had not long stood with them, when they all, vying with one another, rushed in again.

- *non stette … guari* ‘did not stay … long’ → “had not long stood”: the simple past becomes a past perfect, *stette* is interpreted as ‘stood’ rather than ‘stayed,’ and the duration adverb precedes the participle.
- *là con essi* ‘there with them’ → “with them”: the adverb *là* is omitted.
- *Che* ‘that’ → “when”: the conjunction is recast to suit the English construction expressing how little time elapsed.
- *ciascun* ‘each’ → “they all”: a distributive singular becomes a collective plural.
- *a pruova* ‘in rivalry’ → “vying with one another”: the adverbial phrase becomes a participial phrase with an explicit reciprocal expression, placed before the whole movement phrase.
- *dentro … si ricorse* ‘inside … ran back’ → “rushed in again”: the direction follows the verb, ‘ran’ becomes the more forceful “rushed,” the Italian reflexive is not separately expressed, and the return conveyed by *ri-* becomes “again.”

> 115 Chiuser le porte quei nostri avversari  
> 116 Nel petto al mio Signor, che fuor rimase,  
> 117 E rivolsesi a me con passi rari.  

These our adversaries closed the gates on the breast of my Master, who remained without; and turned to me with slow steps.

- *Chiuser le porte quei nostri avversari* ‘closed the gates those our adversaries’ → “These our adversaries closed the gates”: the subject moves from after the object to before the verb, and “These” replaces ‘those.’
- *Nel petto al mio Signor* ‘in the breast to my Lord’ → “on the breast of my Master”: “on” replaces ‘in,’ the Italian construction with *a* becomes an English possessive construction with “of,” and “Master” replaces ‘Lord.’
- *che fuor rimase* ‘who outside remained’ → “who remained without”: the adverb moves after the verb.
- *rivolsesi a me* ‘turned himself back to me’ → “turned to me”: the reflexive verb becomes intransitive, with neither the reflexive pronoun nor the sense of turning back separately expressed.
- *con passi rari* ‘with steps infrequent’ → “with slow steps”: the spacing or infrequency expressed by *rari* is interpreted as slowness, and the adjective precedes the noun.

## Lines 118–130 — Virgil Promises the City Will Open

> 118 Gli occhi alla terra, e le ciglia avea rase  
> 119 D’ ogni baldanza, e dicea ne’ sospiri:  
> 120 Chi m’ ha negate le dolenti case?  

He had his eyes upon the ground, and his eyebrows shorn of all boldness, and said with sighs: “Who hath denied me the doleful houses?”

- *Gli occhi alla terra, e le ciglia avea* ‘the eyes toward the earth, and the eyebrows he had’ → “He had his eyes upon the ground, and his eyebrows”: the verb moves before both objects; the definite articles become possessives; the direction of the eyes is expressed as their resting “upon the ground”.
- *rase* ‘shaved’ → “shorn”: a different verb expresses the removal.
- *D’ ogni* ‘of every’ → “of all”: distributive ‘every’ becomes collective “all”.
- *dicea ne’ sospiri* ‘was saying amid the sighs’ → “said with sighs”: the imperfect becomes a simple past; the prepositional phrase expresses accompaniment rather than setting, and its definite article is omitted.
- *m’ ha negate* ‘to me hath denied’ → “hath denied me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 121 Ed a me disse: Tu, perch’ io m’ adiri,  
> 122 Non sbigottir, ch’ io vincerò la pruova,  
> 123 Qual ch’ alla difension dentro s’ aggiri.  

And to me he said: “Thou, be not dismayed, though I get angry; for I will master the trial, whatever be contrived within for hindrance.

- *Tu, perch’ io m’ adiri, / Non sbigottir* ‘Thou, although I become angry, do not become frightened’ → “Thou, be not dismayed, though I get angry”: the concessive clause moves after the command; becoming frightened is expressed as being dismayed, and the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative.
- *ch’ io vincerò la pruova* ‘because I will conquer the trial’ → “for I will master the trial”: the subordinating conjunction becomes coordinating “for”, and conquering becomes mastering.
- *Qual ch’ alla difension dentro s’ aggiri* ‘whoever may be moving about inside for the defence’ → “whatever be contrived within for hindrance”: a clause referring to whoever is active within becomes one referring to whatever is devised there. Reflexive movement becomes passive contrivance, ‘defence’ becomes “hindrance”, and the purpose phrase moves to the end.

> 124 Questa lor tracotanza non è nuova,  
> 125 Chè già l’ usaro a men segreta porta,  
> 126 La qual senza serrame ancor si trova.  

This insolence of theirs is nothing new; for they shewed it once at a less secret gate, which still is found unbarred.

- *Questa lor tracotanza* ‘This their insolence’ → “This insolence of theirs”: the possessive moves after the noun and takes the construction “of theirs”.
- *non è nuova* ‘is not new’ → “is nothing new”: the negative construction introduces the pronoun “nothing”.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating one.
- *già l’ usaro* ‘already they used it’ → “they shewed it once”: ‘already’ is interpreted as referring to a previous occasion; ‘used’ becomes “shewed”, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *a men segreta porta* ‘at less secret gate’ → “at a less secret gate”: English supplies an indefinite article.
- *La qual senza serrame ancor si trova* ‘which without a bolt still is found’ → “which still is found unbarred”: the phrase describing the absence of a fastening becomes the adjective “unbarred” and moves after the verb.

> 127 Sovr’ essa vedestù la scritta morta:  

Over it thou sawest the dead inscription.

- *Sovr’ essa vedestù* ‘Over it sawest thou’ → “Over it thou sawest”: the subject pronoun, attached to the Italian verb, moves before the English verb.
- *la scritta morta* ‘the inscription dead’ → “the dead inscription”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 128 E già di qua da lei discende l’ erta,  
> 129 Passando per li cerchi senza scorta  
> 130 Tal, che per lui ne fia la terra aperta.  

And already, on this side of it, comes down the steep, passing the circles without escort, One by whom the city shall be opened to us.”

- *discende l’ erta* ‘descends the steep’ → “comes down the steep”: the single verb becomes a verb with a directional adverb.
- *Passando per li cerchi* ‘passing through the circles’ → “passing the circles”: the preposition is omitted, and the circles become the direct object of “passing”.
- *Tal, che per lui* ‘Such a one that through him’ → “One by whom”: the correlative construction becomes a relative clause; ‘such’ is omitted, and the separate pronoun referring back to the person becomes relative “whom”.
- *ne fia la terra aperta* ‘for us will be the city opened’ → “the city shall be opened to us”: the subject moves before the passive verb, whose parts are brought together; the preceding beneficiary pronoun becomes the final phrase “to us”.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Syntax

Carlyle generally keeps close to the Italian’s vocabulary, images, and sequence of expression, while rearranging its syntax for English. Objects and delayed subjects commonly move into subject–verb–object order, adjectives precede their nouns, and auxiliaries come before participles. Yet he also preserves marked arrangements and compressed expressions, as in “And he to me” and “Good there is none”. His English therefore retains much of the Italian’s rhetorical shape without reproducing every grammatical feature.

Changes of construction recur throughout. Reflexive and pronominal verbs often become simple English verbs: *si fe’* becomes “grew”, and *si volgea* becomes “turned”. Infinitives and gerunds may become finite clauses, as *passando il loto* becomes “while we pass the wash”; elsewhere a finite clause becomes an infinitive, as *che … fregi* becomes “to ornament”. Grammatical roles sometimes change more substantially: *m’ hai sicurtà renduta*, ‘hast restored safety to me’, becomes “hast restored me to safety”. Italian imperfects frequently become English simple pasts, and subjunctives are often rendered by indicatives or English modal constructions.

The choice of words ranges from close equivalents to interpretive substitutions. *Fummo* becomes “vapour”, *duolo* becomes “a wailing”, and *sbarro*, ‘open wide’, becomes “bent”, shifting from opening the eyes to directing them. Some choices narrow or intensify the Italian: *barca* becomes “skiff”, *broda* becomes “swill”, and *strazio* becomes “rending”. Others generalize it, as *vermiglie* becomes “red”. Adjectives may become adverbs or noun phrases: *snella* becomes “quickly”, *ardito* becomes “daringly”, and *furiosa* becomes “in fury”.

Small omissions and additions also help reshape the prose. Movement particles and reflexive pronouns often receive no separate equivalent; *molto*, *via*, and the phrase *per l’ acqua* are omitted in particular passages. Conversely, “spirits” supplies a noun after *più di mille*, and possessives clarify relationships where Italian uses an article, as in “its ancient prow” and “his teeth”. Two routine adjustments extend beyond the individually noted examples: English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and omits Italian definite articles before possessives. Overall, Carlyle’s departures chiefly make grammatical relationships explicit and recast Italian constructions into English, while preserving much of the original wording, repetition, and figurative force.
