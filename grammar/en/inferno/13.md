# Inferno 13

## Lines 1–12 — The Wood of the Harpies

> 1 Non era ancor di là Nesso arrivato,  
> 2 Quando noi ci mettemmo per un bosco,  
> 3 Che da nessun sentiero era segnato.  

Nessus had not yet reached the other side, when we moved into a wood, which by no path was marked.

- *Non era ancor di là Nesso arrivato* ‘Not yet on the far side had Nessus arrived’ → “Nessus had not yet reached the other side”: the subject moves to the beginning; ‘had arrived’ with an adverbial phrase of place becomes the transitive “had not yet reached” with “the other side” as its object.
- *ci mettemmo per* ‘put ourselves through’ → “moved into”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and movement through the wood becomes entry into it.

> 4 Non frondi verdi, ma di color fosco;  

Not green the foliage, but of colour dusky;

- *Non frondi verdi* ‘Not leaves green’ → “Not green the foliage”: the plural noun becomes a singular collective noun with a supplied definite article, and the adjective moves before it.

> 5 Non rami schietti, ma nodosi e involti;  

not smooth the branches, but gnarled and warped;

- *Non rami schietti* ‘Not branches straight’ → “not smooth the branches”: the adjective moves before the noun, a definite article is supplied, and *schietti* is interpreted as smooth rather than straight.
- *involti* ‘entwined’ → “warped”: intertwining is rendered as distortion of shape.

> 6 Non pomi v’ eran, ma stecchi con tosco.  

apples none were there, but withered sticks with poison.

- *Non pomi v’ eran* ‘No fruits there were’ → “apples none were there”: the general ‘fruits’ becomes the more specific “apples”; the noun is placed before the negative, and the locative follows the verb.
- *stecchi* ‘sticks’ → “withered sticks”: “withered” makes explicit a quality suggested by the Italian noun.

> 7 Non han sì aspri sterpi nè sì folti  
> 8 Quelle fiere selvagge, che in odio hanno  
> 9 Tra Cecina e Corneto i luoghi colti.  

No holts so rough or dense have those wild beasts, that hate the cultivated tracts, between Cecina and Corneto.

- *Non han* ‘do not have’ → “No” and “have”: the negative is transferred from the verb to its object, and the verb moves after that object.
- *sì aspri sterpi nè sì folti* ‘so rough thickets nor so dense’ → “holts so rough or dense”: “holts” denotes woods or wooded groves rather than specifically scrub or thorny thickets; the first adjective moves after the noun, ‘nor’ becomes “or” under the preceding negative, and the second ‘so’ is omitted.
- *che in odio hanno* ‘that in hatred have’ → “that hate”: the construction with ‘have’ and a noun becomes a simple verb.
- *Tra Cecina e Corneto i luoghi colti* ‘between Cecina and Corneto the places cultivated’ → “the cultivated tracts, between Cecina and Corneto”: the geographical phrase moves after the object, ‘places’ becomes “tracts”, and the adjective moves before its noun.

> 10 Quivi le brutte Arpie lor nido fanno,  

Here the unseemly Harpies make their nest,

- *brutte* ‘ugly’ → “unseemly”: physical ugliness is expressed by a word that can also suggest unbecomingness or impropriety.
- *lor nido fanno* ‘their nest make’ → “make their nest”: the verb moves before its object.

> 11 Che cacciâr delle Strofade i Troiani  
> 12 Con tristo annunzio di futuro danno.  

who chased the Trojans from the Strophades with dismal note of future woe.

- *cacciâr delle Strofade i Troiani* ‘drove from the Strophades the Trojans’ → “chased the Trojans from the Strophades”: the direct object moves before the phrase of separation, and expulsion is expressed as chasing.
- *annunzio* ‘announcement’ → “note”: an explicit announcement becomes the less specific “note”.
- *danno* ‘harm’ → “woe”: harm or damage is rendered as suffering or distress.

## Lines 13–24 — Strange Creatures and Unseen Cries

> 13 Ale hanno late, e colli e visi umani,  
> 14 Piè con artigli, e pennuto il gran ventre:  

Wide wings they have, and necks and faces human, feet with claws, and their large belly feathered.

- *Ale hanno late* ‘wings they have wide’ → “Wide wings they have”: the adjective moves before the noun instead of being separated from it by the verb.
- *pennuto il gran ventre* ‘feathered the large belly’ → “their large belly feathered”: the adjective moves after the noun phrase, and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 15 Fanno lamenti in su gli alberi strani.  

They make rueful cries on the strange trees.

- *lamenti* ‘laments’ → “rueful cries”: the noun is expanded into an adjective and noun.
- *alberi strani* ‘trees strange’ → “strange trees”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 16 E il buon Maestro: Prima che più entre,  
> 17 Sappi che sei nel secondo girone,  
> 18 Mi cominciò a dire; e sarai, mentre  
> 19 Che tu verrai all’ orribil sabbione.  

And the kind Master began to say to me: “Before thou goest farther, know that thou art in the second round; and shalt be, until thou comest to the horrid sand.

- *buon* ‘good’ → “kind”: the adjective is rendered specifically as kindness.
- *Mi cominciò a dire* ‘to me began to say’ → “began to say to me”: the reporting clause moves from within the speech to its beginning, and the indirect object moves after the infinitive.
- *Prima che più entre* ‘before farther thou enter’ → “Before thou goest farther”: entering becomes going; the adverb moves after the verb, and the Italian present subjunctive becomes an English present indicative.
- *mentre Che tu verrai* ‘while thou wilt come’ → “until thou comest”: the conjunction is interpreted as marking an endpoint rather than simultaneous duration; the future indicative becomes the present customary in English temporal clauses.
- *sabbione* ‘expanse of sand’ → “sand”: the augmentative force of the Italian noun is not separately expressed.

> 20 Però riguarda bene, e sì vedrai  
> 21 Cose, che daran fede al mio sermone.  

Therefore look well, and thou shalt see things that will confirm my speech.”

- *e sì vedrai* ‘and thus thou wilt see’ → “and thou shalt see”: *sì*, linking the seeing to the preceding instruction, is omitted.
- *daran fede al mio sermone* ‘will give credence to my speech’ → “will confirm my speech”: the idiom with a noun and an indirect object becomes a single transitive verb with a direct object.

> 22 Io sentia già d’ ogni parte trar guai,  
> 23 E non vedea persona che il facesse;  

Already I heard wailings uttered on every side, and saw no one to make them;

- *Io sentia già* ‘I heard already’ → “Already I heard”: the adverb moves to the beginning of the clause.
- *d’ ogni parte* ‘from every side’ → “on every side”: a phrase indicating source becomes one indicating location, and it moves after the expression for uttering cries.
- *trar guai* ‘utter laments’ → “wailings uttered”: the active infinitive and its object become a noun followed by a passive participle.
- *E non vedea persona* ‘and I did not see a person’ → “and saw no one”: negation moves from the verb to the pronoun replacing *persona*.
- *che il facesse* ‘who did it’ → “to make them”: the finite relative clause, with an imperfect subjunctive, becomes an infinitive; the singular pronoun referring to the action becomes a plural pronoun referring to the wailings.

> 24 Per ch’ io tutto smarrito m’ arrestai.  

wherefore I, all bewildered, stood still.

- *m’ arrestai* ‘I stopped myself’ → “stood still”: the reflexive verb of stopping becomes a non-reflexive phrase expressing stillness.

## Lines 25–39 — The Thorn Cries Out

> 25 I’ credo ch’ ei credette ch’ io credesse,  
> 26 Che tante voci uscisser tra que’ bronchi  
> 27 Da gente che per noi si nascondesse.  

I think he thought that I was thinking so many voices came, amongst those stumps, from people who hid themselves on our account.

- *ch’ ei credette* ‘that he thought’ → “he thought”: the conjunction introducing the reported thought is omitted.
- *ch’ io credesse* ‘that I thought’ → “that I was thinking”: the imperfect subjunctive becomes an English past progressive indicative.
- *Che tante voci uscisser* ‘that so many voices issued’ → “so many voices came”: the introductory conjunction is omitted; ‘issued’ becomes “came,” without explicitly expressing outward movement; and the imperfect subjunctive becomes a simple past indicative.
- *bronchi* ‘gnarled trunks’ → “stumps”: the English suggests the remaining portions of broken or felled trees rather than rough, tangled woody growth.
- *che per noi si nascondesse* ‘who on our account were hiding themselves’ → “who hid themselves on our account”: the causal phrase moves after the verb and its object, and the imperfect subjunctive becomes a simple past indicative.

> 28 Però disse il Maestro: Se tu tronchi  
> 29 Qualche fraschetta d’ una d’ este piante,  
> 30 Li pensier ch’ hai si faran tutti monchi.  

Therefore the Master said: “If thou breakest off any little shoot from one of these plants, the thoughts, which thou hast, will all become defective.”

- *disse il Maestro* ‘said the Master’ → “the Master said”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *tronchi* ‘cuttest off’ → “breakest off”: breaking replaces cutting or severing.
- *Qualche fraschetta* ‘some little branch’ → “any little shoot”: the indefinite becomes “any,” natural in this conditional construction, and “shoot” replaces ‘branch’; the diminutive is retained through “little.”
- *d’ una* ‘of one’ → “from one”: the preposition explicitly marks separation.
- *si faran tutti monchi* ‘will become all maimed’ → “will all become defective”: “all” moves before “become,” and the bodily image of being maimed or mutilated becomes the more abstract “defective.”

> 31 Allor porsi la mano un poco avante,  
> 32 E colsi un ramuscel da un gran pruno,  

Then I stretched my hand a little forward, and plucked a branchlet from a great thorn;

- *porsi la mano* ‘I extended the hand’ → “I stretched my hand”: the definite article accompanying the body part becomes a possessive, and extending or holding out the hand is expressed as stretching it.

> 33 E il tronco suo gridò: Perchè mi schiante?  

and the trunk of it cried: “Why dost thou rend me?”

- *il tronco suo* ‘its trunk’ → “the trunk of it”: the postposed possessive adjective becomes an “of” phrase, with the definite article retained.
- *mi schiante* ‘me thou breakest’ → “dost thou rend me”: the object moves after the verb, the English question uses auxiliary “dost,” and breaking or snapping is expressed as rending.

> 34 Da che fatto fu poi di sangue bruno,  
> 35 Ricominciò a gridar: Perchè mi scerpi?  

And when it had grown dark with blood, it again began to cry: “Why dost thou tear me?

- *Da che fatto fu poi di sangue bruno* ‘after it was then made dark with blood’ → “And when it had grown dark with blood”: “And” is supplied, the temporal conjunction becomes “when,” and *poi* has no separate equivalent. The passive simple past becomes an inchoative past perfect, “had grown.” “Dark” renders *bruno* as a description of the trunk’s resulting condition, moving it before “with blood” rather than leaving it after that phrase.
- *Ricominciò a gridar* ‘recommenced to cry’ → “it again began to cry”: the repetition expressed by the prefix becomes the separate adverb “again,” placed before “began.”
- *mi scerpi* ‘me thou pluckest’ → “dost thou tear me”: the object moves after the verb, auxiliary “dost” forms the English question, and plucking or stripping is expressed as tearing.

> 36 Non hai tu spirto di pietate alcuno?  

Hast thou no breath of pity?

- *Non hai tu spirto di pietate alcuno* ‘hast thou not any spirit of pity’ → “Hast thou no breath of pity”: the negated verb and postposed *alcuno* become “no” before the noun. *Spirto* is rendered through its concrete sense ‘breath’ rather than ‘spirit’ or ‘feeling.’

> 37 Uomini fummo, ed or siam fatti sterpi:  

Men we were, and now are turned to trees.

- *siam fatti sterpi* ‘we are made shrubs’ → “are turned to trees”: being ‘made’ something becomes being “turned to” it, and the more specific, rough woody growth denoted by *sterpi* is generalized to “trees.”

> 38 Ben dovrebb’ esser la tua man più pia,  
> 39 Se state fossimo anime di serpi.  

Truly thy hand should be more merciful, had we been souls of serpents.”

- *Ben* ‘well’ → “Truly”: the adverb’s emphatic force is rendered as an explicit affirmation.
- *dovrebb’ esser la tua man* ‘ought to be thy hand’ → “thy hand should be”: the subject moves before the verb, and conditional obligation is expressed with “should.”
- *Se state fossimo* ‘if been we had’ → “had we been”: the Italian places the participle before its auxiliary; English instead places the auxiliary before the subject and the participle after it. This conditional inversion also replaces the explicit conjunction *Se*.

## Lines 40–54 — Words and Blood From the Splinter

> 40 Come d’ un stizzo verde, che arso sia  
> 41 Dall’ un de’ capi, che dall’ altro geme,  
> 42 E cigola per vento che va via;  

As a green brand, that is burning at one end, at the other drops, and hisses with the wind which is escaping;

- *Come d’ un stizzo verde* ‘as from a brand green’ → “As a green brand”: ‘from’ is omitted, making the brand the subject of the comparison’s verbs; the adjective moves before the noun.
- *che arso sia / Dall’ un de’ capi* ‘that be burnt at one of the ends’ → “that is burning at one end”: the passive subjunctive becomes an active indicative progressive; the partitive ‘one of the ends’ is shortened to “one end”.
- *che dall’ altro geme* ‘which from the other weeps’ → “at the other drops”: the repeated relative pronoun is omitted, ‘from’ becomes “at”, and ‘weeps’ becomes “drops”.
- *cigola* ‘squeaks’ → “hisses”: a different sound-word is used.
- *per vento che va via* ‘because of wind that goes away’ → “with the wind which is escaping”: the causal preposition becomes “with”, a definite article is supplied, and ‘goes away’ becomes the progressive “is escaping”.

> 43 Sì della scheggia rotta usciva insieme  
> 44 Parole e sangue: ond’ io lasciai la cima  
> 45 Cadere, e stetti come l’ uom che teme.  

so from that broken splint, words and blood came forth together: whereat I let fall the top, and stood like one who is afraid.

- *della scheggia rotta* ‘from the splint broken’ → “from that broken splint”: the definite article becomes a demonstrative, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *usciva insieme / Parole e sangue* ‘came out together words and blood’ → “words and blood came forth together”: the compound subject moves before the verb, and the adverb follows it. The Italian singular imperfect is rendered by an English simple past with a plural subject.
- *ond’* ‘whence’ → “whereat”: the connective presents the event as the occasion for the response rather than its source.
- *io lasciai la cima / Cadere* ‘I let the top fall’ → “I let fall the top”: the infinitive moves before its understood subject, “the top”.
- *come l’ uom che teme* ‘like the man who fears’ → “like one who is afraid”: the definite noun becomes an indefinite pronoun, and the verb of fearing becomes an adjective with “is”.

> 46 S’ egli avesse potuto creder prima,  
> 47 Rispose il Savio mio, anima lesa,  
> 48 Ciò che ha veduto pur con la mia rima,  
> 49 Non averebbe in te la man distesa;  

“If he, O wounded spirit!” my Sage replied, “could have believed before, what he has seen only in my verse, he would not have stretched forth his hand against thee;

- *avesse potuto creder* ‘had been able to believe’ → “could have believed”: the pluperfect subjunctive construction becomes a modal with a perfect infinitive.
- *Rispose il Savio mio* ‘replied my Sage’ → “my Sage replied”: the subject moves before the verb, and the possessive precedes its noun.
- *anima lesa* ‘soul injured’ → “O wounded spirit”: “O” is supplied, ‘soul’ becomes “spirit”, and the adjective moves before the noun. The address also moves before the reporting clause.
- *Ciò che ha veduto pur con la mia rima* ‘what he has just seen, through my rhyme’ → “what he has seen only in my verse”: the translation takes *pur* as “only” with the following phrase, rather than ‘just’ with ‘has seen’. It renders *con* as “in”, attaching the verse phrase to seeing rather than to the earlier believing; ‘rhyme’ is generalized to “verse”.
- *Non averebbe in te la man distesa* ‘would not have against you the hand extended’ → “he would not have stretched forth his hand against thee”: the participle moves directly after the auxiliary, and the phrase ‘against you’ moves after the object. The definite article becomes “his”, and ‘extended’ becomes “stretched forth”.

> 50 Ma la cosa incredibile mi fece  
> 51 Indurlo ad ovra, ch’ a me stesso pesa.  

but the incredibility of the thing made me prompt him to do what grieves myself.

- *Ma la cosa incredibile* ‘but the thing incredible’ → “but the incredibility of the thing”: the adjective becomes the abstract head noun “incredibility”, while ‘the thing’ becomes a dependent phrase.
- *mi fece* ‘me made’ → “made me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Indurlo ad ovra* ‘to induce him to an act’ → “prompt him to do”: ‘induce’ becomes “prompt”, with a bare infinitive after “made”; the noun ‘act’ becomes the verb “do”.
- *ch’ a me stesso pesa* ‘which weighs upon me myself’ → “what grieves myself”: the relative referring to ‘an act’ becomes “what”, serving as the object of “do”. The metaphor of weighing becomes the direct verb “grieves”, and the prepositional complement becomes its object.

> 52 Ma dilli chi tu fosti, sì che, in vece  
> 53 D’ alcuna ammenda, tua fama rinfreschi  
> 54 Nel mondo su, dove tornar gli lece.  

But tell him who thou wast; so that, to make thee some amends, he may refresh thy fame up in the world, to which he is permitted to return.”

- *in vece / D’ alcuna ammenda* ‘in place of some amends’ → “to make thee some amends”: the phrase expressing substitution becomes an infinitive expressing purpose; “make” and “thee” are supplied.
- *tua fama rinfreschi* ‘your fame he may refresh’ → “he may refresh thy fame”: the object moves after the verb, and the subjunctive is expressed with “may”.
- *Nel mondo su* ‘in the world above’ → “up in the world”: the adverb moves from after ‘world’ to before the prepositional phrase.
- *dove tornar gli lece* ‘where to return is permitted to him’ → “to which he is permitted to return”: ‘where’ becomes the directional relative phrase “to which”; the impersonal construction becomes a personal passive, and the infinitive moves to the end.

## Lines 55–63 — The Keeper of Frederick’s Heart

> 55 E il tronco: Sì col dolce dir m’ adeschi,  
> 56 Ch’ io non posso tacere; e voi non gravi  
> 57 Perch’ io un poco a ragionar m’ inveschi.  

And the trunk: “Thou so allurest me with thy sweet words, that I cannot keep silent; and let it not seem burdensome to you, if I enlarge a little in discourse.

- *Sì … m’ adeschi* ‘so … me thou baitest’ → “Thou so allurest me”: the image of attracting with bait becomes the more general “allurest”; the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *col dolce dir* ‘with the sweet speaking’ → “with thy sweet words”: the substantivized infinitive becomes the plural noun “words”, the definite article becomes the possessive “thy”, and the phrase moves after the verb and its object.
- *tacere* ‘be silent’ → “keep silent”: the Italian verb becomes a verb–adjective phrase expressing continued silence.
- *e voi non gravi* ‘and may it not weigh upon you’ → “and let it not seem burdensome to you”: the subjunctive becomes a construction with “let”; the verb meaning ‘weigh upon’ becomes “seem burdensome”, adding the notion of seeming. The person affected moves from before the verb to the final phrase “to you”.
- *Perch’* ‘because’ → “if”: Carlyle renders the subordinate clause as a condition rather than as a cause.
- *io un poco a ragionar m’ inveschi* ‘I a little in discoursing entangle myself’ → “I enlarge a little in discourse”: the reflexive image of becoming entangled becomes the nonreflexive “enlarge”. The subjunctive becomes indicative, the infinitive becomes the noun “discourse”, and “a little” moves after the main verb.

> 58 Io son colui, che tenni ambo le chiavi  
> 59 Del cor di Federigo, e che le volsi  
> 60 Serrando e disserrando sì soavi,  
> 61 Che dal segreto suo quasi ogni uom tolsi:  

I am he, who held both keys of Frederick’s heart, and turned them, locking and unlocking so softly, that from his secrets I excluded almost every other man.

- *colui* ‘the one’ → “he”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *Del cor di Federigo* ‘of the heart of Frederick’ → “of Frederick’s heart”: the name’s following prepositional phrase becomes a possessive placed before “heart”.
- *e che le volsi* ‘and who them turned’ → “and turned them”: the repeated relative pronoun is omitted, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Serrando e disserrando* ‘closing and opening’ → “locking and unlocking”: the translation makes the actions specifically those of operating locks.
- *sì soavi* ‘so gentle’ → “so softly”: the plural adjective, formally agreeing with *chiavi*, is rendered as an adverb describing the turning.
- *dal segreto suo* ‘from the secret his’ → “from his secrets”: the singular becomes plural, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *quasi ogni uom* ‘almost every man’ → “almost every other man”: “other” is supplied.
- *tolsi* ‘I took away’ → “I excluded”: removal is rendered as exclusion, and the verb moves before its object.

> 62 Fede portai al glorioso ufizio,  
> 63 Tanto ch’ io ne perdei lo sonno e i polsi.  

So great fidelity I bore to the glorious office, that I lost thereby both sleep and life.

- *Fede … Tanto* ‘fidelity … so much’ → “So great fidelity”: the degree expressed after the statement by *Tanto* is transferred to an adjective phrase modifying “fidelity” at the beginning.
- *ch’ io ne perdei* ‘that I from it lost’ → “that I lost thereby”: *ne* is interpreted causally as “thereby”, and its English equivalent follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *lo sonno e i polsi* ‘the sleep and the pulses’ → “both sleep and life”: “both” is supplied, the definite articles are omitted, and the plural ‘pulses’ is interpreted as the singular “life”.

## Lines 64–80 — Envy, Suicide, and Protested Loyalty

> 64 La meretrice, che mai dall’ ospizio  
> 65 Di Cesare non torse gli occhi putti,  
> 66 Morte comune, e delle corti vizio,  
> 67 Infiammò contra me gli animi tutti,  

The harlot, that never from Cæsar’s dwelling turned her adulterous eyes, common bane, and vice of courts, inflamed all minds against me;

- *mai … non* ‘never … not’ → “never”: Italian negative concord is expressed by a single English negative.
- *dall’ ospizio Di Cesare* ‘from the dwelling of Cæsar’ → “from Cæsar’s dwelling”: the possessive precedes the noun instead of following it in a prepositional phrase.
- *gli occhi putti* ‘the wanton eyes’ → “her adulterous eyes”: the definite article becomes a possessive; the adjective precedes the noun, and “adulterous” gives a more specific sexual sense than ‘wanton’.
- *Morte comune* ‘death common’ → “common bane”: ‘death’ becomes the figurative “bane”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *delle corti vizio* ‘of the courts vice’ → “vice of courts”: the modifying phrase follows the noun, and its definite article is omitted.
- *Infiammò contra me gli animi tutti* ‘inflamed against me the minds all’ → “inflamed all minds against me”: the object moves before “against me”; “all” precedes “minds”, and the definite article is omitted.

> 68 E gl’ infiammati infiammâr sì Augusto,  
> 69 Che i lieti onor tornaro in tristi lutti.  

and these so inflamed Augustus, that my joyous honours were changed to dismal sorrows.

- *gl’ infiammati* ‘the inflamed ones’ → “these”: a demonstrative replaces the substantivized participle, removing the repetition of ‘inflamed’.
- *infiammâr sì* ‘inflamed so’ → “so inflamed”: the intensifier moves before the verb.
- *i lieti onor* ‘the joyous honours’ → “my joyous honours”: the definite article becomes a possessive not explicitly present in the Italian.
- *tornaro in* ‘turned into’ → “were changed to”: an active intransitive verb becomes a passive construction.
- *tristi lutti* ‘sad mournings’ → “dismal sorrows”: “sorrows” generalizes the sense of mourning, while “dismal” renders ‘sad’ more strongly.

> 70 L’ animo mio, per disdegnoso gusto,  
> 71 Credendo col morir fuggir disdegno,  
> 72 Ingiusto fece me contra me giusto.  

My soul, in its disdainful mood, thinking to escape disdain by death, made me, though just, unjust against myself.

- *L’ animo mio* ‘the soul my’ → “My soul”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *per disdegnoso gusto* ‘through disdainful taste’ → “in its disdainful mood”: the causal phrase becomes a description of the soul’s state; ‘taste’ or ‘inclination’ becomes “mood”, and “its” is supplied.
- *Credendo col morir fuggir disdegno* ‘believing by dying to flee disdain’ → “thinking to escape disdain by death”: ‘believing’ becomes “thinking”, and ‘flee’ becomes “escape”; the substantivized infinitive ‘dying’ becomes the noun “death”, and the phrase expressing means moves to the end.
- *Ingiusto fece me contra me giusto* ‘unjust made me against me just’ → “made me, though just, unjust against myself”: the initial predicate adjective moves after the object; the final ‘just’ becomes an earlier concessive phrase with “though” supplied, and the second *me* takes the English reflexive form “myself”.

> 73 Per le nuove radici d’ esto legno  
> 74 Vi giuro, che giammai non ruppi fede  
> 75 Al mio signor, che fu d’ onor sì degno.  

By the new roots of this tree, I swear to you, never did I break faith to my lord, who was so worthy of honour.

- *legno* ‘wood’ → “tree”: the material designation is rendered as the thing it denotes here.
- *Vi giuro* ‘to you I swear’ → “I swear to you”: the indirect object follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *che giammai non ruppi fede* ‘that never not I broke faith’ → “never did I break faith”: the conjunction is omitted, Italian negative concord becomes one negative, and fronted “never” prompts English auxiliary–subject inversion with “did”.
- *d’ onor sì degno* ‘of honour so worthy’ → “so worthy of honour”: the adjective’s complement moves after the adjective.

> 76 E se di voi alcun nel mondo riede,  
> 77 Conforti la memoria mia, che giace  
> 78 Ancor del colpo che invidia la diede.  

And if any of you return to the world, strengthen the memory of me, which still lies prostrate from the blow that envy gave it.”

- *di voi alcun* ‘of you anyone’ → “any of you”: the indefinite pronoun precedes its modifying phrase and takes the form “any”.
- *nel mondo riede* ‘to the world returns’ → “return to the world”: the verb moves before the phrase expressing destination.
- *Conforti la memoria mia* ‘let him strengthen the memory my’ → “strengthen the memory of me”: the third-person exhortative subjunctive becomes an English imperative; the possessive adjective becomes the phrase “of me”.
- *che giace Ancor* ‘which lies still’ → “which still lies prostrate”: “still” moves before the verb, and “prostrate” is supplied to make explicit the condition conveyed by *giace*.
- *che invidia la diede* ‘that envy to it gave’ → “that envy gave it”: *la* is understood as an indirect object, and that object follows the verb in English.

> 79 Un poco attese, e poi: Da ch’ ei si tace,  
> 80 Disse il Poeta a me, non perder l’ ora;  

The Poet listened a while, and then said to me: “Since he is silent, lose not the hour;

- *Un poco attese* ‘a little he waited’ → “listened a while”: waiting is interpreted as listening, and the expression of duration follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *Da ch’ ei si tace* ‘since he keeps silent’ → “Since he is silent”: the pronominal verb becomes a construction with “is” and an adjective.
- *Disse il Poeta a me* ‘said the Poet to me’ → “The Poet” … “said to me”: the subject moves before the earlier verb “listened”, and the reporting verb moves before the quoted speech instead of interrupting it.
- *non perder* ‘not to lose’ → “lose not”: the Italian infinitive used for a negative command becomes an English imperative, with the negative following the verb.

## Lines 81–93 — Questions to the Imprisoned Spirit

> 81 Ma parla, e chiedi a lui, se più ti piace.  

but speak, and ask him, if thou wouldst know more.”

- *chiedi a lui* ‘ask of him’ → “ask him”: the prepositional complement becomes a direct object.
- *se più ti piace* ‘if it pleases thee more’ → “if thou wouldst know more”: an impersonal expression of preference becomes a personal expression of desire; knowing is supplied, and ‘more’ now qualifies what is known.

> 82 Ond’ io a lui: Dimandal tu ancora  
> 83 Di quel che credi ch’ a me soddisfaccia;  

Whereat I to him: “Do thou ask him farther, respecting what thou thinkest will satisfy me;

- *Dimandal tu ancora* ‘ask him thou again’ → “Do thou ask him farther”: emphatic “Do” is supplied, the subject precedes “ask,” and “farther” presents the asking as a continuation rather than a repetition.
- *Di quel che credi ch’ a me soddisfaccia* ‘of that which thou thinkest should satisfy me’ → “respecting what thou thinkest will satisfy me”: ‘of’ becomes “respecting,” and ‘that which’ is compressed to “what.” The conjunction after *credi* is omitted; the subjunctive *soddisfaccia* becomes the future “will satisfy,” and the preceding prepositional complement *a me* becomes the following direct object “me.”

> 84 Ch’ io non potrei: tanta pietà m’ accora.  

for I could not, such pity is upon my heart.”

- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *tanta pietà m’ accora* ‘so much pity pains my heart’ → “such pity is upon my heart”: the expression of quantity becomes “such pity,” and the active verb describing pain becomes a construction with “is upon.” The heart implicit in *accora* is expressed as a noun.

> 85 Però ricominciò: Se l’ uom ti faccia  
> 86 Liberamente ciò che il tuo dir prega,  

He therefore resumed: “So may the man do freely for thee what thy words entreat him,

- *Però ricominciò* ‘therefore he began again’ → “He therefore resumed”: “resumed” compresses ‘began again,’ and “therefore” follows the subject.
- *Se l’ uom ti faccia Liberamente* ‘if the man should do freely for thee’ → “So may the man do freely for thee”: the conditional form is rendered as a wish with “So may,” bringing out its optative force. The dative pronoun preceding the Italian verb becomes “for thee” after the verb and adverb.
- *ciò che il tuo dir prega* ‘that which thy speaking entreats’ → “what thy words entreat him”: ‘that which’ becomes “what”; the substantivized infinitive *dir* becomes the plural noun “words”; and “him,” the person entreated, is supplied.

> 87 Spirito incarcerato, ancor ti piaccia  
> 88 Di dirne come l’ anima si lega  
> 89 In questi nocchi; e dinne, se tu puoi,  

O imprisoned spirit, please thee tell us farther, how the soul gets bound up in these knots. And tell us, if thou mayest,

- *Spirito incarcerato* ‘spirit imprisoned’ → “O imprisoned spirit”: the vocative marker “O” is supplied, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *ancor ti piaccia Di dirne* ‘may it please thee again to tell us’ → “please thee tell us farther”: the impersonal subjunctive construction is shortened to “please thee,” and the infinitive is rendered without ‘to.’ *Ancor* becomes “farther” rather than ‘again’ and moves after “tell us.”
- *come l’ anima si lega* ‘how the soul binds itself’ → “how the soul gets bound up”: the reflexive construction becomes a passive construction with “gets,” and “up” is supplied.
- *se tu puoi* ‘if thou canst’ → “if thou mayest”: the verb of ability is rendered with ‘may,’ allowing a sense of permission or possibility.

> 90 S’ alcuna mai da tai membra si spiega.  

whether any ever frees itself from such members.”

- *S’ alcuna mai da tai membra si spiega* ‘whether any ever from such members unfolds itself’ → “whether any ever frees itself from such members”: *si spiega* is interpreted as freeing itself rather than literally unfolding itself, and the phrase expressing separation moves after the verb.

> 91 Allor soffiò lo tronco forte, e poi  
> 92 Si convertì quel vento in cotal voce:  
> 93 Brevemente sarà risposto a voi.  

Then the trunk blew strongly, and soon that wind was changed into these words: “Briefly shall you be answered.

- *soffiò lo tronco forte* ‘blew the trunk strongly’ → “the trunk blew strongly”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *e poi* ‘and then’ → “and soon”: sequence is rendered as a short interval.
- *Si convertì quel vento* ‘that wind transformed itself’ → “that wind was changed”: the reflexive or middle construction becomes an English passive, and the Italian postverbal subject moves before the verb.
- *in cotal voce* ‘into such a voice’ → “into these words”: singular ‘voice’ becomes plural “words,” while ‘such’ becomes the demonstrative “these.”
- *Brevemente sarà risposto a voi* ‘briefly an answer will be given to you’ → “Briefly shall you be answered”: the impersonal passive becomes a personal passive, making the recipients, *a voi*, the grammatical subject “you.”

## Lines 94–108 — The Souls Become Trees

> 94 Quando si parte l’ anima feroce  
> 95 Dal corpo, ond’ ella stessa s’ è disvelta,  
> 96 Minos la manda alla settima foce.  

When the fierce spirit quits the body, from which it has torn itself, Minos sends it to the seventh gulf.

- *si parte … Dal corpo* ‘departs … from the body’ → “quits the body”: the Italian verb with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object.
- *l’ anima feroce* ‘the soul fierce’ → “the fierce spirit”: “spirit” replaces ‘soul’, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *ond’ ella stessa s’ è disvelta* ‘from which she herself has uprooted herself’ → “from which it has torn itself”: ‘uprooted’ becomes “torn”; the emphasis of *stessa* is omitted; the feminine pronouns become neuter, and the reflexive pronoun follows the English verb.
- *alla settima foce* ‘to the seventh mouth’ → “to the seventh gulf”: “gulf” replaces the literal ‘mouth’ or ‘opening’.

> 97 Cade in la selva, e non l’ è parte scelta;  

It falls into the wood, and no place is chosen for it;

- *e non l’ è parte scelta* ‘and not for it is a place chosen’ → “and no place is chosen for it”: the negative moves into “no place”, the subject precedes the verb, and the indirect object moves to the end.

> 98 Ma là dove fortuna la balestra,  
> 99 Quivi germoglia come gran di spelta;  

but wherever fortune flings it, there it sprouts, like grain of spelt;

- *là dove* ‘there where’ → “wherever”: the two-word expression becomes a generalizing relative adverb.
- *la balestra* ‘it catapults’ → “flings it”: the verb’s more specific sense of shooting or catapulting becomes the broader “flings”, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 100 Surge in vermena ed in pianta silvestra:  

shoots up to a sapling, and to a savage plant.

- *Surge* ‘Rises’ → “shoots up”: the simple verb becomes a phrasal verb particularly suited to growth.
- *in vermena* ‘into shoot’ → “to a sapling”: “to” replaces ‘into’, “sapling” replaces ‘shoot’ or ‘twig’, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *in pianta silvestra* ‘into plant woodland’ → “to a savage plant”: “to” replaces ‘into’, an indefinite article is supplied, and the adjective moves before the noun. “Savage” gives *silvestra* a stronger sense than ‘woodland’ or ‘wild-growing’.

> 101 Le Arpie, pascendo poi delle sue foglie,  
> 102 Fanno dolore, ed al dolor finestra.  

The Harpies, feeding then upon its leaves, give pain, and to the pain an outlet.

- *delle sue foglie* ‘of its leaves’ → “upon its leaves”: the Italian preposition introducing the food consumed becomes the English preposition used with “feeding”.
- *Fanno dolore* ‘Make pain’ → “give pain”: “give” replaces ‘make’.
- *finestra* ‘window’ → “an outlet”: the concrete ‘window’ becomes the more general “outlet”, and an indefinite article is supplied.

> 103 Come l’ altre, verrem per nostre spoglie,  

Like the others, we shall go for our spoils;

- *verrem* ‘we shall come’ → “we shall go”: the verb of motion changes its directional viewpoint.

> 104 Ma non però ch’ alcuna sen rivesta:  

yet none shall thereby clothe himself with them again:

- *Ma non però ch’ alcuna sen rivesta* ‘But not for this, that any should clothe herself in them again’ → “yet none shall thereby clothe himself with them again”: the compressed negative purpose-or-result construction becomes an independent prediction. *Però* is represented by “thereby”; ‘not … any’ becomes “none”; and the subjunctive becomes “shall … clothe”. The reflexive and complement pronouns move after the verb, ‘in them’ becomes “with them”, and the feminine reference becomes masculine “himself”.

> 105 Chè non è giusto aver ciò ch’ uom si toglie.  

for it is not just that a man have what he takes from himself.

- *Chè* ‘Because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *non è giusto aver ciò ch’ uom si toglie* ‘it is not just to have that which one takes away from oneself’ → “it is not just that a man have what he takes from himself”: the infinitive becomes a finite clause with subjunctive “have”. Generic *uom* becomes “a man”, made the subject of that clause and represented again by “he” in the relative clause. ‘That which’ contracts to “what”; the reflexive moves after the verb as “from himself”; and ‘away’ is not separately expressed.

> 106 Qui le strascineremo, e per la mesta  
> 107 Selva saranno i nostri corpi appesi,  
> 108 Ciascuno al prun dell’ ombra sua molesta.  

Hither shall we drag them, and through the mournful wood our bodies shall be suspended, each on the thorny tree of its tormented shade.”

- *Qui le strascineremo* ‘Here them we shall drag’ → “Hither shall we drag them”: “Hither” makes direction explicit where *Qui* means ‘here’; the object moves after the verb, and the English subject follows the auxiliary.
- *saranno i nostri corpi appesi* ‘shall be our bodies suspended’ → “our bodies shall be suspended”: the subject moves before the auxiliary instead of standing between auxiliary and participle.
- *al prun* ‘to the thornbush’ → “on the thorny tree”: the preposition becomes “on”, and the single noun for a thornbush or thorn-tree becomes a noun qualified by an adjective.
- *dell’ ombra sua molesta* ‘of the shade its troublesome’ → “of its tormented shade”: the possessive and adjective move before the noun. “Tormented” reads *molesta* as suffering distress rather than causing trouble.

## Lines 109–118 — A Noise and Two Fugitives

> 109 Noi eravamo ancora al tronco attesi,  
> 110 Credendo ch’ altro ne volesse dire;  

We still were listening to the trunk, thinking it would tell us more,

- *eravamo ancora al tronco attesi* ‘were still intent on the trunk’ → “still were listening to the trunk”: a state of attentiveness becomes an ongoing act of listening; “still” precedes “were,” and the phrase referring to the trunk follows rather than precedes the expression of attention.
- *Credendo* ‘believing’ → “thinking”: belief is rendered as a less definite supposition.
- *ch’ altro ne volesse dire* ‘that it wished to say something else to us’ → “it would tell us more”: the conjunction is omitted, and the subjunctive expressing willingness becomes “would”; the fronted object becomes the final “more,” while saying something to someone becomes telling someone something.

> 111 Quando noi fummo d’ un romor sorpresi,  

when by a noise we were surprised;

- *noi fummo d’ un romor sorpresi* ‘we were by a noise surprised’ → “by a noise we were surprised”: the phrase naming the cause moves before the subject and verb.

> 112 Similemente a colui, che venire  
> 113 Sente il porco e la caccia alla sua posta,  
> 114 Ch’ ode le bestie e le frasche stormire.  

like one who feels the boar and chase approaching to his stand; who hears the beasts and the branches crashing.

- *Similemente a colui* ‘similarly to him’ → “like one”: the adverbial expression becomes a prepositional comparison, and the demonstrative becomes indefinite.
- *venire* ‘come’ → “approaching”: the infinitive becomes a present participle and moves from before the verb of perception to after its objects.
- *Sente* ‘hears’ → “feels”: auditory perception is rendered by a broader expression of sensing.
- *la caccia* ‘the chase’ → “chase”: the separate article is omitted, with the preceding “the” serving both coordinated nouns.
- *le frasche* ‘the leafy branches’ → “the branches”: the reference to foliage is not made explicit.
- *stormire* ‘rustle’ → “crashing”: the infinitive becomes a present participle, and the sound is rendered as louder and more violent.

> 115 Ed ecco duo dalla sinistra costa,  
> 116 Nudi e graffiati fuggendo sì forte,  
> 117 Che della selva rompièno ogni rosta.  

And, lo! on the left hand, two spirits, naked and torn, fleeing so violently that they broke every fan of the wood.

- *duo* ‘two’ → “two spirits”: the noun is supplied.
- *dalla sinistra costa* ‘from the left side’ → “on the left hand”: origin becomes position, ‘side’ becomes “hand,” and the phrase moves before “two spirits.”
- *graffiati* ‘scratched’ → “torn”: the specific kind of injury becomes a stronger, less specific description.
- *sì forte* ‘so fast’ → “so violently”: the rendering emphasizes the force of the flight rather than its speed.
- *della selva rompièno ogni rosta* ‘of the wood they were breaking every barrier’ → “they broke every fan of the wood”: the phrase referring to the wood moves from before the verb to after the noun it qualifies; the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past; and the translation chooses the fan-like sense of *rosta* rather than its contextual sense of a screen or obstruction formed by branches.

> 118 Quel dinanzi: Ora accorri, accorri, morte.  

The foremost: “Come now, come, O death!”

- *Quel dinanzi* ‘the one in front’ → “The foremost”: a demonstrative qualified by an adverb becomes an adjective used substantivally.
- *Ora accorri, accorri* ‘now run to help, run to help’ → “Come now, come”: the verb’s urgency and implication of assistance are reduced to simple coming, and “now” moves after the first imperative.
- *morte* ‘death’ → “O death”: the vocative marker “O” is supplied.

## Lines 119–134 — The Hounds and the Bleeding Bush

> 119 E l’ altro, a cui pareva tardar troppo,  
> 120 Gridava: Lano, sì non furo accorte  
> 121 Le gambe tue alle giostre del Toppo.  

And the other, who thought himself too slow, cried: “Lano, thy legs were not so ready at the jousts of Toppo.”

- *a cui pareva tardar troppo* ‘to whom it seemed that he was delaying too much’ → “who thought himself too slow”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal judgment; the infinitive expressing delay becomes an adjective expressing slowness.
- *Gridava* ‘was crying’ → “cried”: the imperfect’s continuing action is rendered by the simple past.
- *sì non furo accorte* ‘so were not wary’ → “were not so ready”: the adverb moves after the negative, and wariness or alertness becomes readiness.
- *Le gambe tue* ‘the legs thy’ → “thy legs”: the possessive precedes the noun, and the subject moves before its verb.
- *del Toppo* ‘of the Toppo’ → “of Toppo”: the article before the place name is omitted.

> 122 E poi che forse gli fallia la lena,  
> 123 Di sè e d’ un cespuglio fe’ un groppo.  

And then, his breath perhaps failing him, of himself and of a bush he made one group.

- *E poi che* ‘And after that’ → “And then”: a conjunction introducing a temporal subordinate clause becomes a coordinating conjunction and temporal adverb.
- *forse gli fallia la lena* ‘perhaps to him was failing the breath’ → “his breath perhaps failing him”: the finite imperfect becomes a participle in an absolute construction; the noun moves before the verb, the indirect-object pronoun follows it, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *un groppo* ‘a knot’ → “one group”: the indefinite article is rendered numerically, and the image of a knot becomes that of a group.

> 124 Dirietro a loro era la selva piena  
> 125 Di nere cagne bramose e correnti,  
> 126 Come veltri che uscisser di catena.  

Behind them, the wood was filled with black braches, eager and fleet, as greyhounds that have escaped the leash.

- *era la selva piena* ‘was the wood full’ → “the wood was filled”: the subject moves before the verb, and an adjective describing a state becomes a passive participle.
- *Di* ‘of’ → “with”: the preposition changes to suit “filled”.
- *cagne* ‘female dogs’ → “braches”: the English uses a more specific term for female hunting hounds.
- *correnti* ‘running’ → “fleet”: an active participle becomes an adjective describing speed.
- *uscisser di catena* ‘should come out of a chain’ → “have escaped the leash”: the imperfect subjunctive of the comparison becomes a present perfect indicative; coming out becomes escaping, and a chain becomes a definite leash. The prepositional complement becomes the direct object of “escaped”.

> 127 In quel, che s’ appiattò, miser li denti,  
> 128 E quel dilaceraro a brano a brano;  
> 129 Poi sen portâr quelle membra dolenti.  

Into him, who squatted, they thrust their teeth, and rent him piece by piece; then carried off his miserable limbs.

- *quel* ‘that one’ → “him”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *s’ appiattò* ‘hid himself’ → “squatted”: concealment is rendered as a bodily posture, and the reflexive construction disappears.
- *miser li denti* ‘put the teeth’ → “they thrust their teeth”: “thrust” gives the verb greater force, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *E quel dilaceraro* ‘And that one they rent’ → “and rent him”: the demonstrative object becomes a personal pronoun and moves after the verb.
- *sen portâr* ‘carried away with them’ → “carried off”: “off” conveys removal, while the reflexive element expressing taking something with oneself is not separately rendered.
- *quelle membra dolenti* ‘those suffering limbs’ → “his miserable limbs”: the demonstrative becomes a possessive, “miserable” replaces the more directly pain-bearing adjective, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 130 Presemi allor la mia scorta per mano,  
> 131 E menommi al cespuglio che piangea  
> 132 Per le rotture sanguinenti, invano.  

My Guide now took me by the hand, and led me to the bush, which was lamenting through its bleeding fractures, in vain.

- *Presemi allor la mia scorta* ‘Took me then my escort’ → “My Guide now took me”: the subject moves from after the verb to the beginning, and the temporal adverb moves before the verb; “now” replaces ‘then’, and “Guide” replaces ‘escort’.
- *per mano* ‘by hand’ → “by the hand”: English supplies the definite article.
- *piangea* ‘was weeping’ → “was lamenting”: the translation chooses lamentation rather than the more literal expression of weeping.
- *le rotture sanguinenti* ‘the fractures bleeding’ → “its bleeding fractures”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 133 O Jacopo, dicea, da Sant’ Andrea,  
> 134 Ché t’ è giovato di me fare schermo?  

“O Jacopo da Sant’ Andrea!” it cried, “what hast thou gained by making me thy screen?

- *dicea* ‘was saying’ → “it cried”: neutral speech becomes a cry, the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the reporting clause moves from within the name to after the complete address.
- *Ché t’ è giovato* ‘What has it availed you’ → “what hast thou gained”: the construction with an indirect object becomes a personal construction in which the person benefited is the subject of “gained”.
- *di me fare schermo* ‘of me to make a screen’ → “by making me thy screen”: the infinitive becomes a gerund introduced by “by”; the preceding prepositional phrase becomes the direct object after the verb, and the possessive “thy” is supplied.

## Lines 135–142 — A Plea to Gather the Leaves

> 135 Che colpa ho io della tua vita rea?  

What blame have I of thy sinful life?”

- *vita rea* ‘life sinful’ → “sinful life”: the adjective is placed before the noun.

> 136 Quando il Maestro fu sovr’ esso fermo,  
> 137 Disse: Chi fusti, che per tante punte  
> 138 Soffi col sangue doloroso sermo?  

When the Master had stopped beside it, he said: “Who wast thou, who, through so many wounds, blowest forth with blood thy dolorous speech?”

- *fu ... fermo* ‘was ... stationary’ → “had stopped”: a past state becomes a completed action, expressed in the pluperfect.
- *sovr’ esso* ‘over it’ → “beside it”: the spatial relation is interpreted as proximity rather than position above; the phrase follows the completed verbal expression.
- *per tante punte* ‘through so many punctures’ → “through so many wounds”: the specific punctures are rendered by the broader “wounds”.
- *Soffi* ‘blowest’ → “blowest forth”: “forth” makes the outward direction explicit.
- *col sangue* ‘with the blood’ → “with blood”: the definite article is omitted.
- *doloroso sermo* ‘dolorous speech’ → “thy dolorous speech”: the possessive “thy” is supplied.

> 139 E quegli a noi: O anime, che giunte  
> 140 Siete a veder lo strazio disonesto,  
> 141 Che le mie frondi ha sì da me disgiunte,  

And he to us: “Ye spirits, who are come to see the ignominious mangling which has thus disjoined my leaves from me,

- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *O anime* ‘O souls’ → “Ye spirits”: ‘souls’ becomes “spirits”, and the address supplies “Ye”; the vocative particle is deferred to the ensuing imperative.
- *giunte Siete* ‘come are’ → “are come”: the auxiliary is placed before the participle.
- *lo strazio disonesto* ‘the mangling shameful’ → “the ignominious mangling”: the adjective precedes the noun, and ‘shameful’ is expressed as “ignominious”.
- *le mie frondi ha sì da me disgiunte* ‘my leaves has thus from me disjoined’ → “has thus disjoined my leaves from me”: the object moves after the verb, while the participle moves before both the object and “from me”.

> 142 Raccoglietele al piè del tristo cesto:  

O gather them to the foot of the dismal shrub!

- *Raccoglietele* ‘Gather them’ → “O gather them”: the vocative particle from the earlier address is attached to the imperative.
- *al piè* ‘at the foot’ → “to the foot”: the gathering place is expressed as a destination.
- *del tristo cesto* ‘of the sad clump’ → “of the dismal shrub”: “dismal” renders the sadness as gloom, while “shrub” identifies the clump more specifically as a bush.

## Lines 143–151 — A Florentine’s Self-Made Gibbet

> 143 Io fui della città, che nel Battista  
> 144 Cangiò ’l primo padrone: ond’ ei per questo  
> 145 Sempre con l’ arte sua la farà trista.  

I was of the city that changed its first patron for the Baptist, on which account he with his art will always make it sorrowful.

- *che nel Battista Cangiò ’l primo padrone* ‘that in the Baptist changed the first master’ → “that changed its first patron for the Baptist”: the phrase naming the replacement moves after the object, and “for” expresses the exchange; “patron” replaces ‘master’, and the definite article becomes the possessive “its”.
- *ond’ ei per questo* ‘whence he for this’ → “on which account he”: the two expressions of consequence, *ond’* and *per questo*, are combined into one causal phrase.
- *Sempre con l’ arte sua la farà trista* ‘always with the art his her will make sorrowful’ → “with his art will always make it sorrowful”: “always” moves inside the future verb phrase; the possessive precedes its noun; and the object pronoun follows “make”, with feminine *la* rendered by neuter “it”.

> 146 E se non fosse che in sul passo d’ Arno  
> 147 Rimane ancor di lui alcuna vista;  

And were it not that at the passage of the Arno there yet remains some semblance of him,

- *E se non fosse* ‘And if it were not’ → “And were it not”: conditional inversion replaces the construction with *se*, so ‘if’ is omitted.
- *in sul passo d’ Arno* ‘on the passage of Arno’ → “at the passage of the Arno”: “at” replaces ‘on’, and English supplies an article before the river’s name.
- *Rimane ancor di lui alcuna vista* ‘remains still of him some sight’ → “there yet remains some semblance of him”: existential “there” is supplied, the adverb moves before the verb, and “of him” moves after the noun it qualifies. “Semblance” interprets *vista* as a visible likeness rather than simply a sight.

> 148 Quei cittadin, che poi la rifondarno  
> 149 Sovra il cener che d’ Attila rimase,  
> 150 Avrebber fatto lavorare indarno.  

those citizens, who afterwards rebuilt it on the ashes left by Attila, would have laboured in vain.

- *la rifondarno* ‘her refounded’ → “rebuilt it”: ‘refounded’ becomes “rebuilt”; the object pronoun moves after the verb, and its feminine gender becomes neuter.
- *il cener che d’ Attila rimase* ‘the ash that from Attila remained’ → “the ashes left by Attila”: singular ‘ash’ becomes plural “ashes”; the finite relative clause becomes a passive participial phrase, and ‘from Attila’ becomes “by Attila”, naming the agent of “left”.
- *Avrebber fatto lavorare indarno* ‘would have caused others to work in vain’ → “would have laboured in vain”: the causative construction with *fatto* is replaced by a simple verb of working, making the citizens themselves the labourers rather than those who caused the work to be done.

> 151 Io fei giubbetto a me delle mie case.  

“I made a gibbet for myself of my own dwelling.”

- *fei giubbetto a me* ‘made a gibbet for me’ → “made a gibbet for myself”: “myself” explicitly marks the reference back to the speaker.
- *delle mie case* ‘of my houses’ → “of my own dwelling”: plural ‘houses’ becomes singular “dwelling”, and “own” adds emphasis to the possessive.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Reshaping

Carlyle stays close to the Italian’s sequence of statements, repetitions, and concrete images, but frequently rearranges their parts. Subjects and objects move into more usual English positions, adjectives generally precede their nouns, and intervening phrases move outside the main verbal construction. Reporting clauses sometimes move ahead of the speech they introduce. Yet the translation also preserves marked inversions, as in “Not green the foliage” and “Hither shall we drag them,” retaining something of the Italian’s emphatic ordering rather than consistently smoothing it into ordinary prose.

Word choice ranges from close equivalents to interpretations that alter specificity or emphasis. *Pomi*, ‘fruits’, becomes “apples,” while *sterpi*, ‘shrubs’ or ‘rough woody growth’, becomes the broader “trees.” *Involti*, ‘entwined’, becomes “warped,” and *graffiati*, ‘scratched’, becomes the stronger “torn.” Some figurative expressions remain conspicuous, including the keys of the heart and the refreshing of fame; others are reduced or redirected. Thus *monchi*, ‘maimed’, becomes the abstract “defective,” *m’ inveschi*, ‘I entangle myself’, becomes “I enlarge,” and *finestra*, ‘window’, becomes the more functional “outlet.”

Changes of construction often make the English more direct. *In odio hanno*, ‘have in hatred’, becomes “hate,” and *daran fede*, ‘will give credence’, becomes “will confirm.” Elsewhere Carlyle expands or recasts the grammar: *la cosa incredibile*, ‘the incredible thing’, becomes “the incredibility of the thing,” and the impersonal *sarà risposto a voi* becomes the personal passive “shall you be answered.” Reflexive or middle verbs may become English passives, finite clauses may become infinitives or participial phrases, and Italian subjunctives are variously represented by indicatives, modal verbs, or wishes. Such shifts generally preserve the relation between the statements, though particular choices can change the emphasis or attachment of a phrase, notably “only in my verse.”

Omissions and additions also recur. Repeated conjunctions and relative pronouns sometimes disappear, as do individual adverbs such as *sì* in *e sì vedrai*. Conversely, “withered” supplements *stecchi*, “prostrate” supplements *giace*, and “spirits” supplies the noun understood in *duo*. Possessives often replace definite articles, especially with body parts. Beyond the individual changes recorded in the comparison, English regularly supplies subject pronouns where Italian identifies the subject through the verb ending, and omits the Italian definite article before a possessive. Together, these routine adjustments and more selective interpretive choices produce a translation closely tied to the Italian’s wording and rhetorical shape, but not bound to its grammar word for word.
