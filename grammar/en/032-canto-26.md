# Inferno 26

## Lines 1–9 — Florence’s Shame and Coming Woe

> 1 Godi, Firenze, poi che sei sì grande,  
> 2 Che per mare e per terra batti l’ ali,  
> 3 E per lo Inferno il tuo nome si spande.  

Joy, Florence, since thou art so great that over land and sea thou beatest thy wings, and thy name through Hell expands itself!

- *per mare e per terra* ‘by sea and by land’ → “over land and sea”: the order of sea and land is reversed, and one “over” renders the repeated preposition.
- *batti l’ ali* ‘beatest the wings’ → “thou beatest thy wings”: the definite article becomes a possessive, identifying the wings as Florence’s.
- *per lo Inferno* ‘through the Hell’ → “through Hell”: the article is omitted, and the phrase moves after the subject.
- *si spande* ‘spreads’ → “expands itself”: the pronominal verb becomes an explicitly reflexive construction, with “expands” replacing ‘spreads’.

> 4 Tra li ladron trovai cinque cotali  
> 5 Tuoi cittadini, onde mi vien vergogna,  
> 6 E tu in grande onranza non ne sali.  

Among the thieves I found five such, thy citizens; whereat shame comes on me, and thou to great honour mountest not thereby.

- *cinque cotali Tuoi cittadini* ‘five such citizens of thine’ → “five such, thy citizens”: the integrated noun phrase becomes “five such” followed by the appositional identification “thy citizens”.
- *onde* ‘whence’ → “whereat”: the connection is expressed as an occasion for shame rather than its source.
- *mi vien vergogna* ‘to me comes shame’ → “shame comes on me”: the subject moves before the verb, and the preceding dative pronoun becomes the following prepositional phrase “on me”.
- *in grande onranza* ‘in great honour’ → “to great honour”: “to” explicitly marks the destination of the figurative ascent.
- *non ne sali* ‘not from it mountest’ → “mountest not thereby”: the negative follows the verb, while *ne* becomes “thereby”, placed last and expressing the means or cause of the ascent.

> 7 Ma se presso al mattin del ver si sogna,  
> 8 Tu sentirai di qua da picciol tempo  
> 9 Di quel che Prato, non ch’ altri, t’ agogna.  

But if the truth is dreamed of near the morning, thou shalt feel ere long what Prato, not to speak of others, craves for thee.

- *presso al mattin del ver si sogna* ‘near the morning of the truth one dreams’ → “the truth is dreamed of near the morning”: the time phrase moves from before the dreaming construction to after it; the impersonal construction with *si* becomes passive, making “the truth” the grammatical subject and leaving “of” after the verb.
- *di qua da picciol tempo* ‘on this side of a little time’ → “ere long”: the spatially phrased expression of a short interval becomes an English temporal idiom.
- *di quel che* ‘of that which’ → “what”: the partitive *di* is omitted, and the demonstrative and relative pronoun are combined into “what”.
- *non ch’ altri* ‘not that others’ → “not to speak of others”: the elliptical Italian expression is expanded with “to speak of”.
- *t’ agogna* ‘for thee craves’ → “craves for thee”: the preceding pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase following the verb.

## Lines 10–18 — A Wished-For Fate and the Climb

> 10 E se già fosse, non saria per tempo.  

And if it were already come, it would not be too early.

- *già fosse* ‘already it were’ → “it were already come”: “already” follows the verb, and “come” is supplied, making the bare subjunctive of ‘be’ express arrival.
- *per tempo* ‘early’ → “too early”: “too” is supplied, turning simple earliness into excessive earliness.

> 11 Così foss’ ei, da che pure esser dee!  

So were it! since indeed it must be;

- *pure* ‘after all’ → “indeed”: the particle is rendered as an emphatic affirmation.
- *esser dee* ‘be it must’ → “it must be”: the infinitive is moved after the modal verb.

> 12 Chè più mi graverà com’ più m’ attempo.  

for it will weigh the heavier on me as I grow older.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes coordinating.
- *più mi graverà* ‘more on me it will weigh’ → “it will weigh the heavier on me”: the comparative and the personal complement move after the verb; ‘more’ becomes the comparative expression “the heavier”.
- *com’ più m’ attempo* ‘as more I age’ → “as I grow older”: the pronominal verb becomes “grow older”, with *più* absorbed into the comparative “older”.

> 13 Noi ci partimmo; e su per le scalee,  
> 14 Che n’ avean fatte i borni a scender pria,  
> 15 Rimontò il Duca mio, e trasse mee.  

We departed thence; and, by the stairs which the bourns had given us to descend before, my Guide remounted and drew me up.

- *Noi ci partimmo* ‘we took ourselves away’ → “We departed thence”: the pronominal construction becomes an intransitive verb with an explicit adverb of departure, “thence”.
- *su per le scalee* ‘up along the stairs’ → “by the stairs”: the directional phrase becomes an expression of route; *su* has no separate equivalent here, although upward movement is conveyed by “remounted”.
- *Che n’ avean fatte i borni* ‘which the projecting stones had made for us’ → “which the bourns had given us”: ‘made’ becomes “given”, and the subject moves before the verb. The unusual “bourns” represents *borni* as boundary or marking stones rather than simply projecting rocks.
- *Rimontò il Duca mio* ‘remounted the guide my’ → “my Guide remounted”: the subject moves before the verb, and the possessive moves before its noun.
- *e trasse mee* ‘and drew me’ → “and drew me up”: “up” is supplied to specify the direction.

> 16 E proseguendo la solinga via  
> 17 Tra le schegge e tra’ rocchi dello scoglio,  
> 18 Lo piè senza la man non si spedia.  

And pursuing our solitary way among the jaggs and branches of the cliff, the foot without the hand sped not.

- *proseguendo* ‘continuing’ → “pursuing”: continuing along a route is expressed as pursuing it.
- *la solinga via* ‘the solitary way’ → “our solitary way”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *schegge* ‘splinters’ → “jaggs”: the rendering emphasizes jagged projections rather than splinter-like pieces.
- *tra’ rocchi* ‘among the blocks’ → “branches”: the repeated preposition and article are omitted, and rocky blocks or masses are represented as “branches”.
- *scoglio* ‘rock’ → “cliff”: the English specifies a particular kind of rock formation.
- *non si spedia* ‘did not extricate itself’ → “sped not”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, expressing progress rather than explicitly freeing or disentangling itself.

## Lines 19–30 — Sorrow, Restraint, and Fireflies

> 19 Allor mi dolsi, e ora mi ridoglio,  
> 20 Quando drizzo la mente a ciò ch’ io vidi;  

I sorrowed then, and sorrow now again when I direct my memory to what I saw;

- *Allor mi dolsi, e ora mi ridoglio* ‘Then I sorrowed, and now I sorrow again’ → “I sorrowed then, and sorrow now again”: the temporal adverbs move after their verbs. The Italian pronominal verbs become non-reflexive English verbs, and the prefix *ri-* is expressed separately by “again”.
- *la mente* ‘the mind’ → “my memory”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and ‘mind’ is interpreted more specifically as memory.
- *ciò ch’* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun become a single fused relative.

> 21 E più lo ingegno affreno ch’ io non soglio,  
> 22 Perchè non corra, che virtù non guidi;  

and curb my genius more than I am wont, lest it run where Virtue guides it not;

- *E più lo ingegno affreno* ‘And more the intellect I curb’ → “and curb my genius more”: the object moves after the verb, and “more” follows the object. The definite article becomes “my”, while *ingegno* is rendered as “genius” rather than ‘intellect’.
- *ch’ io non soglio* ‘than I am not accustomed’ → “than I am wont”: the Italian comparative construction’s pleonastic *non* is omitted; it does not negate the comparison.
- *Perchè non corra* ‘So that it may not run’ → “lest it run”: the negative purpose clause is recast with “lest”, which itself carries the negative force.
- *che virtù non guidi* ‘that virtue does not guide’ → “where Virtue guides it not”: *che* is interpreted locatively as “where”, and “it” is supplied as the object of “guides”.

> 23 Sì che, se stella buona o miglior cosa  
> 24 M’ ha dato il ben, ch’ io stesso nol m’ invidi.  

so that, if kindly star or something better have given to me the good, I may not grudge myself that gift.

- *stella buona* ‘star good’ → “kindly star”: the adjective moves before the noun, and ‘good’ becomes “kindly”.
- *miglior cosa* ‘better thing’ → “something better”: the noun becomes an indefinite pronoun, with the adjective following it.
- *M’ ha dato il ben* ‘To me has given the good’ → “have given to me the good”: the preceding indirect-object pronoun becomes a prepositional phrase after the verb. The Italian singular indicative perfect becomes the English perfect subjunctive “have given” in the conditional clause.
- *ch’ io stesso nol m’ invidi* ‘that I myself may not begrudge it to myself’ → “I may not grudge myself that gift”: the resumptive *ch’*, which repeats the construction begun by *Sì che*, is omitted, as is the emphasis of *io stesso*. The subjunctive is expressed with “may”; the indirect object follows the verb as “myself”; and the object pronoun contained in *nol* is expanded into “that gift”, interpreting the preceding ‘good’ as a gift.

> 25 Quante il villan ch’ al poggio si riposa,  

As many fireflies as the peasant who is resting on the hill—

- *Quante … lucciole* ‘as many … fireflies’ → “As many fireflies as”: “fireflies”, translating *lucciole* in line 29, is brought forward beside the opening quantifier; the additional “as” introduces the clause specifying their number.
- *ch’ al poggio si riposa* ‘who on the hill rests’ → “who is resting on the hill”: the location moves after the verb, and the simple present becomes a progressive. The pronominal *si riposa* is represented by non-reflexive “is resting”.

> 26 Nel tempo che colui, che il mondo schiara,  
> 27 La faccia sua a noi tien meno ascosa,  

at the time that he who lights the world least hides his face from us,

- *Nel tempo che* ‘In the time that’ → “at the time that”: the temporal preposition changes from ‘in’ to “at”.
- *colui, che il mondo schiara* ‘he who the world lights’ → “he who lights the world”: the object moves after the verb.
- *La faccia sua a noi tien meno ascosa* ‘the face his to us keeps less hidden’ → “least hides his face from us”: ‘keeps hidden’ becomes the single verb “hides”, and the comparative *meno* becomes the superlative “least”. The possessive moves before its noun; the object follows the verb; and ‘to us’ becomes “from us” after the object.

> 28 Come la mosca cede alla zanzara,  

when as the fly yields to the gnat—

- *Come* ‘As’ → “when as”: the conjunction is expanded into an explicitly temporal expression.
- *zanzara* ‘mosquito’ → “gnat”: a broader insect name replaces the more specific Italian term.

> 29 Vede lucciole giù per la vallea,  
> 30 Forse colà dove vendemmia ed ara:  

sees down along the valley, there perchance where he gathers grapes and tills:

- *Forse colà* ‘Perchance there’ → “there perchance”: the two adverbs exchange positions.
- *vendemmia ed ara* ‘harvests grapes and ploughs’ → “gathers grapes and tills”: the single verb *vendemmia* is unpacked into a verb and its object, while the specific action ‘ploughs’ becomes the broader “tills”.

## Lines 31–45 — Flames Concealing Sinners

> 31 Di tante fiamme tutta risplendea  
> 32 L’ ottava bolgia, sì com’ io m’ accorsi,  
> 33 Tosto che fui là ’ve il fondo parea.  

with flames thus numerous the eighth chasm was all gleaming, as I perceived, so soon as I came to where the bottom shewed itself.

- *Di tante fiamme* ‘with so many flames’ → “with flames thus numerous”: the expression of number follows the noun rather than preceding it; *tante* becomes “thus numerous”.
- *tutta risplendea L’ ottava bolgia* ‘all shone the eighth ditch’ → “the eighth chasm was all gleaming”: the subject moves before the verb, the imperfect becomes an English past progressive, and “all” follows the auxiliary. *Bolgia* is rendered more broadly as “chasm”.
- *sì com’ io m’ accorsi* ‘just as I became aware’ → “as I perceived”: the emphatic *sì* is omitted, and the pronominal expression *accorgersi* becomes the simple verb “perceived”.
- *fui* ‘I was’ → “I came”: being at a place is rendered as arriving there.
- *là ’ve* ‘there where’ → “to where”: “to” replaces the indication of position with one of destination.
- *parea* ‘appeared’ → “shewed itself”: the simple verb becomes a verb with a reflexive object.

> 34 E qual colui, che si vengiò con gli orsi,  
> 35 Vide il carro d’ Elia al dipartire,  
> 36 Quand i cavalli al Cielo erti levorsi;  

And as he, who was avenged by the bears, saw Elijah’s chariot at its departure, when the horses rose erect to heaven;

- *colui* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *che si vengiò* ‘who avenged himself’ → “who was avenged”: the active reflexive construction becomes passive.
- *con gli orsi* ‘with the bears’ → “by the bears”: an expression of means becomes an expression of agency.
- *il carro d’ Elia* ‘the chariot of Elijah’ → “Elijah’s chariot”: the possessive precedes the noun instead of following it in a prepositional phrase.
- *al dipartire* ‘at the departing’ → “at its departure”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun, and “its” is supplied.
- *al Cielo erti levorsi* ‘to heaven erect lifted themselves’ → “rose erect to heaven”: the verb moves before the adjective and the destination phrase; the reflexive verb becomes the intransitive “rose”.

> 37 Chè nol potea sì con gli occhi seguire,  
> 38 Che vedesse altro che la fiamma sola,  
> 39 Sì come nuvoletta, in su salire:  

for he could not so follow it with his eyes as to see other than the flame alone, like a little cloud, ascending up:

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *nol potea sì con gli occhi seguire* ‘he could not so with the eyes follow it’ → “he could not so follow it with his eyes”: the object pronoun, attached to the negative in *nol*, follows the infinitive in English; the phrase about the eyes also moves after the infinitive, and the definite article becomes “his”.
- *Che vedesse* ‘that he should see’ → “as to see”: the finite subjunctive result clause becomes an infinitival construction dependent on “so”.
- *Sì come nuvoletta* ‘just like a little cloud’ → “like a little cloud”: the emphatic *sì* is omitted; the diminutive ending is expressed by “little”, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *in su salire* ‘upward to ascend’ → “ascending up”: the infinitive becomes a present participle, and the expression of direction follows the verb.

> 40 Tal si movea ciascuna per la gola  
> 41 Del fosso, chè nessuna mostra il furto,  
> 42 Ed ogni fiamma un peccatore invola.  

thus moved each of those flames along the gullet of the foss, for none of them shews the theft, and every flame steals a sinner.

- *si movea ciascuna* ‘moved itself each one’ → “moved each of those flames”: the pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive, and “of those flames” makes the reference of *ciascuna* explicit.
- *per* ‘through’ → “along”: the preposition presents movement along the channel rather than through it.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *nessuna* ‘none’ → “none of them”: “of them” is supplied to make the pronoun’s reference explicit.
- *un peccatore invola* ‘a sinner steals’ → “steals a sinner”: the object moves from before the verb to after it.

> 43 Io stava sovra il ponte a veder surto,  
> 44 Sì che s’ io non avessi un ronchion preso,  
> 45 Caduto sarei giù senza esser urto.  

I stood upon the bridge, having risen so to look, that, if I had not caught a rock, I should have fallen down without being pushed.

- *a veder surto, Sì che* ‘to see, risen, so that’ → “having risen so to look, that”: the postposed participle becomes a perfect participial phrase preceding the purpose infinitive; “look” replaces ‘see’. The “so” belonging to *Sì che* is moved into the preceding phrase.
- *s’ io non avessi un ronchion preso* ‘if I had not a rocky spur grasped’ → “if I had not caught a rock”: the object moves after the participle; the Italian pluperfect subjunctive is expressed by the English past perfect. “Rock” generalizes *ronchion*, and “caught” renders the act of taking hold.
- *Caduto sarei giù* ‘fallen I would be down’ → “I should have fallen down”: the participle moves after the subject and auxiliary; the Italian past conditional, formed with ‘be’, becomes the English conditional perfect with “have”.
- *senza esser urto* ‘without being struck’ → “without being pushed”: “pushed” specifies the kind of impact expressed by *urto*.

## Lines 46–57 — The Spirits Within the Divided Flame

> 46 E il Duca, che mi vide tanto atteso,  
> 47 Disse: Dentro da’ fuochi son gli spirti:  

And the Guide, who saw me thus attent, said: “Within those fires are the spirits:

- *Duca* ‘leader’ → “Guide”: the designation emphasizes guidance rather than leadership.
- *mi vide* ‘me saw’ → “saw me”: the object follows the verb.
- *tanto atteso* ‘so intent’ → “thus attent”: the expression of degree becomes an expression of manner.
- *Dentro da’ fuochi* ‘within the fires’ → “Within those fires”: the definite article becomes the demonstrative “those”.

> 48 Ciascun si fascia di quel ch’ egli è inceso.  

each swathes himself with that which burns him.”

- *Ciascun si fascia* ‘each himself swathes’ → “each swathes himself”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.
- *di quel ch’ egli è inceso* ‘with that by which he is burned’ → “with that which burns him”: the passive relative clause becomes active; the agent of burning becomes its subject, and the person burned becomes its object.

> 49 Maestro mio, risposi, per udirti  
> 50 Son io più certo; ma già m’ era avviso  
> 51 Che così fusse, e già voleva dirti:  

“Master,” I replied, “from hearing thee I feel more certain; but had already discerned it to be so, and already wished to say to thee:

- *Maestro mio* ‘my master’ → “Master”: the possessive is omitted.
- *per udirti* ‘through hearing thee’ → “from hearing thee”: the infinitive becomes a gerund, and “from” presents the hearing as the source of increased certainty.
- *Son io più certo* ‘am I more certain’ → “I feel more certain”: being certain becomes feeling certain, and the subject moves before the verb.
- *ma già m’ era avviso / Che così fusse* ‘but already it seemed to me that it was so’ → “but had already discerned it to be so”: an impersonal expression of an impression becomes a personal act of discernment. The imperfect becomes a pluperfect, “already” moves between auxiliary and participle, and the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object with an infinitive.

> 52 Chi è in quel fuoco, che vien sì diviso  
> 53 Di sopra, che par surger della pira,  
> 54 Ov’ Eteòcle col fratel fu miso?  

Who is in that fire, which comes so parted at the top, as if it rose from the pyre where Eteocles with his brother was placed?”

- *Di sopra* ‘above’ → “at the top”: the general indication of upper position becomes a phrase identifying the fire’s top.
- *che par surger della pira* ‘that it seems to rise from the pyre’ → “as if it rose from the pyre”: the result clause dependent on *sì* becomes a hypothetical comparison. The present-tense verb of seeming and its infinitive are replaced by “as if” with “rose”.
- *col fratel* ‘with the brother’ → “with his brother”: a possessive replaces the definite article.

> 55 Risposemi: Là entro si martira  
> 56 Ulisse e Diomede, e così insieme  
> 57 Alla vendetta corron, com’ all’ ira:  

He answered me: “Within it there, Ulysses is tortured, and Diomed; and thus they run together in punishment, as erst in wrath.

- *Là entro* ‘there within’ → “Within it there”: the locative expressions are reordered, and “it” is supplied as the object of “Within”.
- *si martira / Ulisse e Diomede* ‘is tormented Ulysses and Diomed’ → “Ulysses is tortured, and Diomed”: the first member of the compound subject moves before the verb, while the second remains after it; *martira* is rendered as “is tortured”.
- *e così insieme / Alla vendetta corron* ‘and thus together to vengeance they run’ → “and thus they run together in punishment”: the verb moves before the adverb and prepositional phrase. Direction towards vengeance becomes participation “in punishment”, interpreting *vendetta* as the punishment that exacts retribution.
- *com’ all’ ira* ‘as to wrath’ → “as erst in wrath”: “erst” supplies an explicit reference to an earlier time, and “in” replaces the directional preposition, following the preceding construction.

## Lines 58–72 — The Flame and Dante’s Request

> 58 E dentro dalla lor fiamma si geme  
> 59 L’ aguato del caval, che fe’ la porta  
> 60 Ond’ uscì de’ Romani il gentil seme.  

And in their flame they groan for the ambush of the horse, that made the door by which the noble seed of the Romans came forth.

- *si geme L’ aguato del caval* ‘the ambush of the horse is groaned over’ → “they groan for the ambush of the horse”: the passive construction becomes active, with an unspecified plural subject; the thing lamented becomes the complement of “for”.
- *Ond’* ‘whence’ → “by which”: the expression of departure from a place becomes one of passage through it.
- *uscì de’ Romani il gentil seme* ‘came forth of the Romans the noble seed’ → “the noble seed of the Romans came forth”: the subject moves before the verb, and the possessive phrase follows its noun instead of preceding it.

> 61 Piangevisi entro l’ arte, per che morta  
> 62 Deidamia ancor sì duol d’ Achille;  

Within it they lament the artifice, whereby Deidamia in death still sorrows for Achilles;

- *Piangevisi entro l’ arte* ‘the art is lamented within it’ → “Within it they lament the artifice”: the passive construction becomes active, with an unspecified plural subject, and the location moves to the beginning. *Arte* is rendered more specifically as “artifice”.
- *morta Deidamia* ‘dead Deidamia’ → “Deidamia in death”: the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase and follows the name.
- *d’ Achille* ‘of Achilles’ → “for Achilles”: the preposition changes to suit the English expression of sorrow.

> 63 E del Palladio pena vi si porta.  

and there for the Palladium they suffer punishment.”

- *del Palladio pena vi si porta* ‘of the Palladium punishment there is borne’ → “there for the Palladium they suffer punishment”: *del* is interpreted causally as “for”; the locative moves to the beginning; and the passive construction becomes active, with an unspecified plural subject. Bearing punishment is expressed as suffering it.

> 64 S’ ei posson dentro da quelle faville  
> 65 Parlar, diss’ io, Maestro, assai ten priego,  
> 66 E ripriego che il priego vaglia mille,  

“If they within those sparks can speak,” said I, “Master! I pray thee much, and repray that my prayer may equal a thousand,

- *posson dentro da quelle faville Parlar* ‘can within those sparks speak’ → “within those sparks can speak”: the locative moves before the modal verb, bringing “can speak” together.
- *assai ten priego* ‘much thee of it I pray’ → “I pray thee much”: the object pronoun and the adverb move after the verb; the *ne* contained in *ten*, referring to what is requested, is not separately rendered.
- *che il priego vaglia mille* ‘that the prayer be worth a thousand’ → “that my prayer may equal a thousand”: the definite article becomes a possessive; being worth becomes equalling; and the subjunctive is expressed with “may”.

> 67 Che non mi facci dell’ attender niego,  
> 68 Finchè la fiamma cornuta qua vegna:  
> 69 Vedi che del disio vêr lei mi piego.  

deny me not to wait until the horned flame comes hither. Thou seest how with desire I bend me towards it.”

- *Che non mi facci dell’ attender niego* ‘that you not make me a refusal of waiting’ → “deny me not to wait”: the subordinate clause introduced by *che* becomes a direct imperative. The construction with *facci* and the noun *niego* becomes the verb “deny”; the nominal infinitive *dell’ attender* becomes “to wait”; and the object pronoun and negative follow the verb.
- *la fiamma cornuta* ‘the flame horned’ → “the horned flame”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *qua vegna* ‘hither come’ → “comes hither”: the directional adverb moves after the verb, and the Italian subjunctive becomes an English indicative in the temporal clause.
- *Vedi che* ‘you see that’ → “Thou seest how”: the conjunction introducing the object clause is rendered as “how” rather than ‘that’.
- *del disio* ‘from the desire’ → “with desire”: the causal expression becomes a phrase with “with”, and the definite article is omitted.
- *vêr lei mi piego* ‘towards her myself I bend’ → “I bend me towards it”: the directional phrase and reflexive pronoun move after the verb; the feminine pronoun referring to the flame becomes neuter “it”.

> 70 Ed egli a me: La tua preghiera è degna  
> 71 Di molta lode; ed io però l’ accetto:  

And he to me: “Thy request is worthy of much praise, and therefore I accept it.

- *preghiera* ‘prayer’ → “request”: the translation uses a less supplicatory term.
- *ed io però l’ accetto* ‘and I therefore it accept’ → “and therefore I accept it”: “therefore” moves before the subject, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 72 Ma fa che la tua lingua si sostegna.  

But do thou refrain thy tongue.

- *fa che la tua lingua si sostegna* ‘make that your tongue restrain itself’ → “do thou refrain thy tongue”: the imperative governing a subordinate reflexive clause becomes a single transitive imperative. The tongue changes from the subordinate clause’s subject to the verb’s object, and “do thou” gives the command an explicit, emphatic form.

## Lines 73–87 — Virgil Addresses the Flame

> 73 Lascia parlare a me, ch’ io ho concetto  
> 74 Ciò che tu vuoi; ch’ ei sarebbero schivi,  
> 75 Perch’ ei fur Greci, forse del tuo detto.  

Let me speak, for I have conceived what thou wishest; and they, perhaps, because they were Greeks, might disdain thy words.”

- *Lascia parlare a me* ‘let speak to me’ → “Let me speak”: the emphatic *a me* becomes the object of “Let” and precedes the infinitive.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *Ciò che* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun are combined.
- *ch’* ‘for’ → “and”: an explanatory connection becomes a coordinating addition.
- *sarebbero schivi … del* ‘would be averse … to’ → “might disdain”: the conditional construction with an adjective becomes a modal verb followed by a transitive verb.
- *Perch’ ei fur Greci, forse* ‘because they were Greeks, perhaps’ → “perhaps, because they were Greeks”: the adverb moves before the clause giving the reason, rather than following it.
- *tuo detto* ‘thy utterance’ → “thy words”: the singular noun becomes plural.

> 76 Poichè la fiamma fu venuta quivi,  
> 77 Ove parve al mio Duca tempo e loco,  
> 78 In questa forma lui parlare audivi:  

After the flame had come where time and place seemed fitting to my Guide, I heard him speak in this manner:

- *fu venuta* ‘was come’ → “had come”: Italian’s past anterior, formed here with *essere*, becomes an English past perfect formed with “had”.
- *quivi, Ove* ‘there, where’ → “where”: the separate adverb of place is omitted.
- *parve … tempo e loco* ‘seemed … time and place’ → “time and place seemed fitting”: the nouns move before the verb, and “fitting” is supplied to express the Italian’s compressed construction.
- *In questa forma lui parlare audivi* ‘in this form him speak I heard’ → “I heard him speak in this manner”: the main verb moves before its object and dependent infinitive, the opening phrase moves to the end, and “manner” replaces ‘form’.

> 79 O voi, che siete duo dentro ad un fuoco,  

“O ye, two in one fire!

- *O voi, che siete duo dentro ad un fuoco* ‘O you, who are two within one fire’ → “O ye, two in one fire!”: the relative clause’s pronoun and verb are omitted, leaving a phrase describing the persons addressed.

> 80 S’ io meritai di voi mentre ch’ io vissi,  
> 81 S’ io meritai di voi assai o poco,  
> 82 Quando nel mondo gli alti versi scrissi,  

If I merited of you whilst I lived, if I merited of you much or little, when on earth I wrote the High Verses,

- *nel mondo* ‘in the world’ → “on earth”: both the noun and the preposition change.
- *gli alti versi scrissi* ‘the high verses I wrote’ → “I wrote the High Verses”: the object moves after the verb.

> 83 Non vi movete; ma l’ un di voi dica  
> 84 Dove per lui perduto a morir gissi.  

move ye not; but let the one of you tell where he wandering went to die.”

- *Non vi movete* ‘do not move yourselves’ → “move ye not”: the reflexive pronoun is omitted, the subject pronoun is expressed, and the negative follows the imperative.
- *l’ un di voi dica* ‘the one of you say’ → “let the one of you tell”: the third-person subjunctive expressing a command becomes a construction with “let”; “tell” replaces ‘say’.
- *per lui perduto* ‘by him, lost’ → “he wandering”: the oblique expression becomes the subject, and ‘lost’ is interpreted as “wandering”.
- *a morir gissi* ‘to die one went’ → “went to die”: the impersonal construction becomes a personal verb, and the infinitive of purpose moves after it.

> 85 Lo maggior corno della fiamma antica  
> 86 Cominciò a crollarsi, mormorando,  
> 87 Pur come quella cui vento affatica.  

The greater horn of the ancient flame began to shake itself, murmuring, just like a flame that struggles with the wind.

- *fiamma antica* ‘flame ancient’ → “ancient flame”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *quella* ‘that one’ → “a flame”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes an explicit noun with an indefinite article.
- *cui vento affatica* ‘which wind wearies’ → “that struggles with the wind”: the flame becomes the subject rather than the object, while the wind becomes the complement of “with”; the transitive verb becomes an intransitive expression of struggle, and an article is supplied before “wind”.

## Lines 88–102 — The Ardour for Experience

> 88 Indi la cima qua e là menando,  
> 89 Come fosse la lingua che parlasse,  
> 90 Gittò voce di fuori, e disse: Quando  

Then carrying to and fro the top, as if it were the tongue that spake, threw forth a voice, and said: “When

- *la cima qua e là menando* ‘the top here and there moving’ → “carrying to and fro the top”: the verb comes before its object; ‘here and there’ becomes the directional phrase “to and fro”.
- *Come fosse* ‘as it were’ → “as if it were”: “if” explicitly marks the hypothetical comparison conveyed by the Italian subjunctive.
- *che parlasse* ‘that might speak’ → “that spake”: the imperfect subjunctive becomes an ordinary English past tense, with the hypothetical force already expressed by “as if”.
- *Gittò voce di fuori* ‘threw voice out’ → “threw forth a voice”: “forth” precedes the object, and an indefinite article is supplied before “voice”.

> 91 Mi diparti’ da Circe, che sottrasse  
> 92 Me più d’ un anno là presso a Gaeta,  
> 93 Prima che sì Enea la nominasse;  

I departed from Circe, who beyond a year detained me there near Gaeta, ere Æneas thus had named it,

- *Mi diparti’* ‘I parted myself’ → “I departed”: the Italian pronominal verb becomes an English intransitive verb without a reflexive pronoun.
- *che sottrasse Me più d’ un anno* ‘who withheld me more than a year’ → “who beyond a year detained me”: *sottrasse* is rendered as detention; “beyond” replaces ‘more than’, and the expression of duration moves before the verb.
- *Prima che sì Enea la nominasse* ‘before thus Æneas it should name’ → “ere Æneas thus had named it”: “thus” moves after the subject, and the object pronoun moves after the verb; the imperfect subjunctive becomes a past perfect indicative.

> 94 Nè dolcezza di figlio, nè la piéta  
> 95 Del vecchio padre, nè il debito amore,  
> 96 Lo qual dovea Penelope far lieta,  

neither fondness for my son, nor reverence for my aged father, nor the due love that should have cheered Penelope,

- *dolcezza di figlio* ‘sweetness of a son’ → “fondness for my son”: “fondness” interprets ‘sweetness’ as affection, *di* becomes “for”, and “my” makes the relationship explicitly possessive.
- *la piéta Del vecchio padre* ‘the piety of the old father’ → “reverence for my aged father”: *piéta* is interpreted as filial reverence, *del* becomes “for my”, and “aged” replaces ‘old’.
- *Lo qual dovea Penelope far lieta* ‘which ought to make Penelope glad’ → “that should have cheered Penelope”: the imperfect expression of obligation becomes “should have” with a perfect infinitive; ‘make glad’ becomes the single verb “cheered”, and “Penelope” moves from before the infinitive to after it.

> 97 Vincer potero dentro a me l’ ardore  
> 98 Ch’ io ebbi a divenir del mondo esperto,  
> 99 E degli vizj umani, e del valore:  

could conquer in me the ardour that I had to gain experience of the world, and of human vice and worth:

- *Vincer potero dentro a me* ‘conquer could within me’ → “could conquer in me”: the finite modal precedes the infinitive, and ‘within’ becomes “in”.
- *a divenir del mondo esperto* ‘to become of the world experienced’ → “to gain experience of the world”: ‘become experienced’ becomes a verb with a noun object, “gain experience”; the phrase “of the world” follows that noun rather than preceding the adjective.
- *E degli vizj umani, e del valore* ‘and of the vices human, and of the worth’ → “and of human vice and worth”: plural *vizj* becomes singular “vice”; “human” moves before its noun; the definite articles are omitted, and the second preposition is not repeated.

> 100 Ma misi me per l’ alto mare aperto  
> 101 Sol con un legno, e con quella compagna  
> 102 Picciola, dalla qual non fui deserto.  

I put forth on the deep open sea, with but one ship, and with that small company, which had not deserted me.

- *Ma misi me* ‘But I put myself’ → “I put forth”: *Ma* is omitted, and the reflexive construction becomes the intransitive phrase “put forth”.
- *per l’ alto mare aperto* ‘through the high sea open’ → “on the deep open sea”: *per* becomes “on”; *alto* is understood as ‘deep’ rather than ‘high’, and “open” moves before “sea”.
- *Sol con un legno* ‘only with a wood’ → “with but one ship”: “but” expresses the restriction after “with”; *un* becomes explicitly numerical “one”, and the material named by *legno* is rendered as the vessel it denotes.
- *quella compagna Picciola* ‘that company small’ → “that small company”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *dalla qual non fui deserto* ‘by which I was not deserted’ → “which had not deserted me”: the passive becomes active, making the relative pronoun the subject and the speaker the object; the simple past becomes a past perfect.

## Lines 103–117 — The Voyage Beyond the Bounds

> 103 L’ un lito e l’ altro vidi infin la Spagna,  
> 104 Fin nel Marrocco, e l’ isola de’ Sardi,  
> 105 E l’ altre che quel mare intorno bagna.  

Both the shores I saw as far as Spain, far as Morocco; and saw Sardinia and the other isles which that sea bathes round.

- *L’ un lito e l’ altro* ‘The one shore and the other’ → “Both the shores”: the two singular expressions become one plural expression.
- *Fin nel Marrocco* ‘as far as in Morocco’ → “far as Morocco”: the first ‘as’ and the preposition contained in *nel* are omitted.
- *e l’ isola de’ Sardi* ‘and the island of the Sardinians’ → “and saw Sardinia”: the verb is repeated, and the description of the island becomes its name.
- *l’ altre* ‘the others’ → “the other isles”: the noun understood in Italian is supplied.
- *intorno bagna* ‘round bathes’ → “bathes round”: the adverb follows the verb instead of preceding it.

> 106 Io e i compagni eravam vecchi e tardi,  
> 107 Quando venimmo a quella foce stretta,  
> 108 Ov’ Ercole segnò li suoi riguardi,  
> 109 Acciocchè l’ uom più oltre non si metta:  

“I and my companions were old and tardy, when we came to that narrow pass, where Hercules assigned his landmarks to hinder man from venturing farther.

- *foce stretta* ‘mouth narrow’ → “narrow pass”: “pass” replaces ‘mouth’, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *segnò li suoi riguardi* ‘marked his bounds’ → “assigned his landmarks”: marking becomes assigning, and the bounds are rendered as the landmarks that indicate them.
- *Acciocchè l’ uom più oltre non si metta* ‘so that man may not put himself farther beyond’ → “to hinder man from venturing farther”: the finite purpose clause becomes an infinitive phrase; its negative becomes the preventing action of “hinder”, and the reflexive expression becomes “venturing”. *Più oltre* is compressed into “farther”.

> 110 Dalla man destra mi lasciai Sibilia,  
> 111 Dall’ altra già m’ avea lasciata Setta.  

On the right hand, I left Seville; on the other, had already left Ceuta.

- *Dalla man destra* ‘from the right hand’ → “On the right hand”: the Italian preposition is rendered by the English preposition expressing position.
- *mi lasciai* ‘I left for myself’ → “I left”: the reflexive pronoun is not separately represented.
- *Dall’ altra* ‘from the other’ → “on the other”: again, the preposition becomes one expressing position.
- *già m’ avea lasciata* ‘already I had left for myself’ → “had already left”: the reflexive pronoun is not separately represented, and “already” moves between the auxiliary and the participle.

> 112 O frati, dissi, che per cento milia  
> 113 Perigli siete giunti all’ Occidente,  

‘O brothers!’ I said, ‘who through a hundred thousand dangers have reached the West,

- *cento milia Perigli* ‘hundred thousand dangers’ → “a hundred thousand dangers”: English supplies the indefinite article before the numerical expression.
- *siete giunti all’ Occidente* ‘have arrived at the West’ → “have reached the West”: the intransitive verb and its preposition become a transitive verb with a direct object.

> 114 A questa tanto picciola vigilia  
> 115 De’ vostri sensi, ch’ è del rimanente,  
> 116 Non vogliate negar l’ esperïenza,  
> 117 Dietro al Sol, del mondo senza gente.  

deny not, to this the brief vigil of your senses that remains, experience of the unpeopled world behind the Sun.

- *A questa tanto picciola vigilia* ‘to this so small vigil’ → “to this the brief vigil”: the phrase moves after the imperative; *tanto* is omitted, and “brief” renders smallness as duration. The supplied “the” makes “the brief vigil” appositional to “this”.
- *ch’ è del rimanente* ‘which is of the remainder’ → “that remains”: the copular expression with a noun becomes a simple verb.
- *Non vogliate negar* ‘Do not wish to deny’ → “deny not”: the expression of willing is omitted, leaving a direct negative imperative.
- *l’ esperïenza* ‘the experience’ → “experience”: the definite article is omitted.
- *Dietro al Sol, del mondo senza gente* ‘behind the Sun, of the world without people’ → “of the unpeopled world behind the Sun”: the two prepositional phrases exchange places, and ‘without people’ becomes the adjective “unpeopled”, placed before its noun.

## Lines 118–129 — An Exhortation and a Voyage

> 118 Considerate la vostra semenza:  

Consider your origin:

- *semenza* ‘seed’ → “origin”: the concrete noun is rendered by its figurative sense.

> 119 Fatti non foste a viver come bruti,  
> 120 Ma per seguir virtute e conoscenza.  

ye were not formed to live like brutes, but to follow virtue and knowledge.’

- *Fatti non foste* ‘made not were ye’ → “ye were not formed”: the participle moves after the auxiliary and negation, and ‘made’ becomes “formed”.

> 121 Li miei compagni fec’ io sì acuti,  
> 122 Con questa orazion picciola, al cammino,  
> 123 Che appena poscia gli avrei ritenuti.  

With this brief speech I made my companions so eager for the voyage, that I could hardly then have checked them.

- *Li miei compagni fec’ io sì acuti* ‘my companions made I so sharp’ → “I made my companions so eager”: the object–verb–subject order becomes subject–verb–object, and the figurative ‘sharp’ becomes “eager”.
- *Con questa orazion picciola* ‘with this speech little’ → “With this brief speech”: the phrase moves to the beginning of the sentence; the adjective moves before the noun, and ‘little’ becomes “brief”, specifying duration.
- *al cammino* ‘for the journey’ → “for the voyage”: the general term for a journey becomes one specifically suited to travel by sea.
- *Che appena poscia gli avrei ritenuti* ‘that scarcely afterwards them I would have held back’ → “that I could hardly then have checked them”: the conditional perfect is rendered with “could”, making ability explicit; ‘afterwards’ becomes “then”, ‘held back’ becomes “checked”, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 124 E, volta nostra poppa nel mattino,  
> 125 De’ remi facemmo ale al folle volo,  
> 126 Sempre acquistando del lato mancino.  

And, turning the poop towards morning, we of our oars made wings for the foolish flight, always gaining on the left.

- *volta nostra poppa nel mattino* ‘our poop turned towards the morning’ → “turning the poop towards morning”: the absolute construction, with a past participle agreeing with *poppa*, becomes an active participial phrase whose understood subject is “we”; “the” replaces ‘our’, and the article before ‘morning’ disappears.
- *De’ remi facemmo ale* ‘of the oars we made wings’ → “we of our oars made wings”: the subject precedes the prepositional phrase, and “our” replaces the definite article, making possession explicit.
- *del lato mancino* ‘on the left-hand side’ → “on the left”: ‘side’ is omitted, and the adjective is rendered substantivally as “the left”.

> 127 Tutte le stelle già dell’ altro polo  
> 128 Vedea la notte, e il nostro tanto basso,  
> 129 Che non surgeva fuor del marin suolo.  

Night already saw the other pole, with all its stars; and ours so low, that it rose not from the ocean floor.

- *Tutte le stelle già dell’ altro polo / Vedea la notte* ‘all the stars already of the other pole I saw at night’ → “Night already saw the other pole, with all its stars”: the translation takes *la notte* as the subject, ‘night’, rather than as an adverbial, ‘at night’, with an implied first-person subject. It also makes “the other pole” the direct object: ‘all the stars of the other pole’ becomes “the other pole, with all its stars”, supplying “with” and “its” and rearranging the clause into subject–verb–object order.
- *non surgeva* ‘did not rise’ → “rose not”: the negative follows the main verb rather than preceding it, and English uses no auxiliary.
- *fuor del marin suolo* ‘out of the marine ground’ → “from the ocean floor”: ‘out of’ becomes “from”, the adjective ‘marine’ becomes the attributive noun “ocean”, and ‘ground’ becomes “floor”.

## Lines 130–142 — The Mountain and the Shipwreck

> 130 Cinque volte racceso, e tante casso  
> 131 Lo lume era di sotto dalla Luna,  
> 132 Poi ch’ entrati eravam nell’ alto passo,  

Five times the light beneath the Moon had been rekindled and quenched as oft, since we had entered on the arduous passage,

- *racceso … Lo lume era* ‘rekindled … the light was’ → “the light beneath the Moon had been rekindled”: the subject and auxiliary precede the participle rather than follow it; the imperfect passive becomes a past-perfect passive.
- *e tante casso* ‘and as many times extinguished’ → “and quenched as oft”: the expression of frequency follows the participle rather than precedes it, and ‘extinguished’ becomes “quenched”.
- *Poi ch’ entrati eravam* ‘after we had entered’ → “since we had entered”: “since” presents the entry as the starting point of the elapsed time; the auxiliary precedes the participle rather than follows it.
- *nell’ alto passo* ‘into the high passage’ → “on the arduous passage”: “entered on” replaces the construction with ‘into’; *alto* is interpreted as expressing difficulty rather than height or depth.

> 133 Quando n’ apparve una montagna, bruna  
> 134 Per la distanza, e parvemi alta tanto,  
> 135 Quanto veduta non n’ aveva alcuna.  

when there appeared to us a Mountain, dim with distance; and to me it seemed the highest I had ever seen.

- *n’ apparve una montagna* ‘to us appeared a mountain’ → “there appeared to us a Mountain”: introductory “there” is supplied, and the indirect object follows the verb rather than precedes it.
- *bruna / Per la distanza* ‘dark because of the distance’ → “dim with distance”: darkness becomes diminished visibility; “with” replaces the explicit causal phrase, and the article before ‘distance’ is omitted.
- *e parvemi* ‘and it seemed to me’ → “and to me it seemed”: the indirect object is placed before the subject and verb.
- *alta tanto, / Quanto veduta non n’ aveva alcuna* ‘so high as I had not seen any [of them]’ → “the highest I had ever seen”: the correlative comparison becomes a definite superlative. The negative construction with ‘any’ becomes an affirmative construction with “ever”; the partitive *n’* is not separately expressed, and the auxiliary precedes the participle rather than follows it.

> 136 Noi ci allegrammo, e tosto tornò in pianto;  

We joyed, and soon our joy was turned to grief;

- *Noi ci allegrammo* ‘we made ourselves glad’ → “We joyed”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb.
- *e tosto tornò in pianto* ‘and soon it turned into weeping’ → “and soon our joy was turned to grief”: “our joy” supplies an explicit subject for the Italian’s unstated one; the intransitive ‘turned’ becomes passive “was turned”, and the outward expression ‘weeping’ becomes the emotion “grief”.

> 137 Chè dalla nuova terra un turbo nacque,  
> 138 E percosse del legno il primo canto.  

for a tempest rose from the new land, and struck the forepart of our ship.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *dalla nuova terra un turbo nacque* ‘from the new land a whirlwind was born’ → “a tempest rose from the new land”: the phrase indicating origin moves after the subject and verb; the specifically whirling wind becomes the broader “tempest”, and ‘was born’ becomes “rose”.
- *del legno il primo canto* ‘of the wood the first corner’ → “the forepart of our ship”: the material designation *legno* is rendered as the vessel it denotes, and “our” is supplied. ‘The first corner’ is interpreted as “the forepart”; the dependent ‘of’ phrase follows its noun rather than precedes it.

> 139 Tre volte il fe’ girar con tutte l’ acque,  

Three times it made her whirl round with all the waters;

- *il fe’ girar* ‘it made it turn’ → “it made her whirl round”: the object pronoun follows the causative verb rather than precedes it; Italian masculine *il* becomes the English nautical feminine “her”. “Whirl round” makes the turning more forceful and explicitly circular.

> 140 Alla quarta levar la poppa in suso,  
> 141 E la prora ire in giù, com’ altrui piacque,  

at the fourth, made the poop rise up and prow go down, as pleased Another,

- *levar la poppa in suso* ‘rise the poop upward’ → “made the poop rise up”: “made” repeats the causative verb understood from the preceding line; the subject of the infinitive, ‘the poop’, precedes “rise” rather than follows it.
- *E la prora ire in giù* ‘and the prow go down’ → “and prow go down”: the article before ‘prow’ is omitted.
- *com’ altrui piacque* ‘as it pleased another’ → “as pleased Another”: the impersonal construction has no expressed ‘it’ in English; *altrui*, the person pleased, follows the verb rather than precedes it.

> 142 Infin che il mar fu sopra noi richiuso.  

till the sea was closed above us.”

- *il mar fu sopra noi richiuso* ‘the sea was closed again above us’ → “the sea was closed above us”: the repeated closing suggested by *ri-* is not separately expressed, and the phrase ‘above us’ follows the participle rather than precedes it.

## Summary — Close Wording, Reshaped Syntax

Carlyle stays close to much of the Italian vocabulary and imagery, while repeatedly rearranging its syntax. Objects placed before verbs generally move after them, postposed subjects move forward, and adjectives and possessives take their usual English positions. Larger rearrangements sometimes clarify a suspended construction: *lucciole* is brought forward into “As many fireflies as”, while *alta tanto, Quanto …* becomes “the highest I had ever seen”. Yet the translation also preserves conspicuously Italian turns, including “the foot without the hand sped not”, “every flame steals a sinner”, and “we of our oars made wings”.

Changes of construction often make the English more direct or explicit. Pronominal verbs become ordinary intransitives, as *mi diparti’* becomes “I departed”; passive expressions become active, as *dalla qual non fui deserto* becomes “which had not deserted me”. Conversely, *si vengiò* becomes the passive “was avenged”. Finite clauses may become infinitival phrases, and commands expressed indirectly may become direct imperatives: *Non vogliate negar* is compressed into “deny not”. Tense and aspect also shift, with simple forms sometimes becoming progressive or perfect forms. More unusually, Carlyle’s “Night already saw” takes *la notte* as the subject rather than as ‘at night’.

Lexical choices range from close equivalents to interpretive substitutions. *Mente* becomes “memory”, *semenza* becomes “origin”, and *pianto* becomes “grief”, moving respectively towards a particular mental faculty, a figurative meaning, and the emotion behind an outward expression. *Legno* is rendered as “ship”, replacing the material with the object it denotes; *nell’ alto passo* becomes “on the arduous passage”, interpreting *alto* through difficulty. Elsewhere, distinctive choices such as “bourns”, “jaggs”, and “foss” retain an unusual verbal texture rather than smoothing everything into ordinary English.

Omissions and additions are usually small but recurrent. Repeated prepositions, emphatic particles, and some reflexive or partitive pronouns disappear; understood references become explicit in “each of those flames”, “that gift”, and “our joy”. Definite articles sometimes become possessives, as *la faccia sua* becomes “his face” and *la solinga via* becomes “our solitary way”. Beyond the changes individually noted in the comparison, English regularly supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings identify the subject, and omits the Italian articles before possessives. The resulting prose remains closely tied to Dante’s phrasing, but achieves English intelligibility through reordered clauses, expanded references, compressed idioms, and occasional interpretive choices.
