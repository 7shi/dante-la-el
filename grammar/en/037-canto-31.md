# Inferno 31

## Lines 1–13 — Healing Words and a Thunderous Horn

> 1 Una medesma lingua pria mi morse,  
> 2 Sì che mi tinse l’ una e l’ altra guancia,  
> 3 E poi la medicina mi riporse.  

One and the same tongue first wounded me so that it tinged with blushes both my cheeks, and then held forth the medicine to me.

- *Una medesma* ‘one same’ → “One and the same”: English expands the expression with “and the”.
- *pria mi morse* ‘first me bit’ → “first wounded me”: “wounded” generalizes the more specific ‘bit’, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *mi tinse l’una e l’altra guancia* ‘tinged for me the one and the other cheek’ → “it tinged with blushes both my cheeks”: “with blushes” is supplied; the personal pronoun becomes possessive “my”; and the singular distributive expression becomes “both” with a plural noun.
- *la medicina mi riporse* ‘the medicine to me proffered in return’ → “held forth the medicine to me”: the verb moves before its objects, and the indirect object follows the direct object. “Held forth” does not explicitly retain the sense of offering something in return.

> 4 Così od’ io che soleva la lancia  
> 5 D’ Achille, e del suo padre, esser cagione  
> 6 Prima di trista e poi di buona mancia.  

Thus I have heard that the lance of Achilles, and of his father, used to be occasion first of sad and then of healing gift.

- *od’ io* ‘hear I’ → “I have heard”: the present becomes the present perfect, and subject–verb order replaces inversion.
- *soleva ... esser cagione* ‘was wont ... to be cause’ → “used to be occasion”: the habitual construction becomes “used to”; its parts are brought together after the subject, which intervenes in Italian; and ‘cause’ becomes “occasion”.
- *buona* ‘good’ → “healing”: the translation specifies the kind of benefit rather than retaining the general adjective.

> 7 Noi demmo il dosso al misero vallone,  
> 8 Su per la ripa che il cinge dintorno,  
> 9 Attraversando senza alcun sermone.  

We turned our back to the wretched valley, up by the bank that girds it round, crossing without any speech.

- *Noi demmo il dosso* ‘We gave the back’ → “We turned our back”: the Italian idiom becomes the English idiom with “turned”, and the definite article becomes possessive “our”.
- *vallone* ‘large valley’ → “valley”: the noun’s augmentative force is not separately expressed.
- *Su per la ripa* ‘Up along the bank’ → “up by the bank”: “by” replaces the indication of movement along or over the bank.
- *che il cinge dintorno* ‘that it girds around’ → “that girds it round”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 10 Quivi era men che notte e men che giorno,  
> 11 Sì che il viso m’ andava innanzi poco:  

Here was less than night and less than day, so that my sight went little way before me;

- *Quivi* ‘There’ → “Here”: the spatial reference changes from ‘there’ to “Here”.
- *il viso m’andava innanzi poco* ‘the sight for me went ahead little’ → “my sight went little way before me”: the personal relation expressed by *m’* appears both in possessive “my” and in “before me”; *poco* becomes “little way”, supplying a noun of distance, and moves before the expression of direction.

> 12 Ma io senti’ sonare un alto corno  
> 13 Tanto, ch’ avrebbe ogni tuon fatto fioco;  

but I heard a high horn sound so loudly, that it would have made any thunder weak:

- *senti’ sonare un alto corno* ‘heard sound a high horn’ → “heard a high horn sound”: the noun phrase moves before the infinitive.
- *Tanto* ‘so much’ → “so loudly”: the degree is expressed specifically as loudness.
- *ch’avrebbe ogni tuon fatto fioco* ‘that would have every thunder made faint’ → “that it would have made any thunder weak”: the object moves from between the auxiliary and participle to after the complete verb; ‘every’ becomes “any”, and ‘faint’ becomes “weak”.

## Lines 14–24 — The Sound and Seeming Towers

> 14 Che, contra sè la sua via seguitando,  
> 15 Dirizzò gli occhi miei tutti ad un loco.  

which, towards it following its way, directed my eyes all to one place.

- *contra sè* ‘against itself’ → “towards it”: the reflexive expression is replaced by a direction towards the sound, conveying the tracing of its course backwards.
- *la sua via seguitando* ‘its way following’ → “following its way”: the object is moved after the participle.
- *gli occhi miei* ‘the eyes my’ → “my eyes”: the possessive moves before the noun.

> 16 Dopo la dolorosa rotta, quando  
> 17 Carlo Magno perdè la santa gesta,  
> 18 Non sonò sì terribilmente Orlando.  

After the dolorous rout, when Charlemain had lost the holy emprise, Orlando did not sound with his so terribly.

- *perdè* ‘lost’ → “had lost”: the simple past becomes a past perfect.
- *la santa gesta* ‘the holy host’ → “the holy emprise”: the translation takes *gesta* as an undertaking or enterprise rather than a company of warriors.
- *Non sonò sì terribilmente Orlando* ‘Orlando sounded not so terribly’ → “Orlando did not sound with his so terribly”: the subject moves from the end to the beginning, English auxiliary negation replaces the simple negative verb, and “with his” supplies an instrument left unexpressed in the Italian.

> 19 Poco portai in là alta la testa,  
> 20 Che mi parve veder molte alte torri;  

Short while had I kept looking up in that direction, when I seemed to see many lofty towers;

- *Poco* ‘a little’ → “Short while”: the adverbial expression is expanded into an explicit duration.
- *portai in là alta la testa* ‘I carried my head high that way’ → “had I kept looking up in that direction”: holding the head high becomes sustained upward looking, with no explicit equivalent for *la testa*. The simple past becomes a past perfect, and the auxiliary precedes the subject after the initial expression of duration.
- *Che* ‘that’ → “when”: the connective following *Poco* becomes an explicitly temporal conjunction.
- *mi parve veder* ‘it seemed to me to see’ → “I seemed to see”: the impersonal construction with an experiencer expressed by *mi* becomes a personal construction with that experiencer as subject.

> 21 Ond’ io; Maestro, di’, che terra è questa?  

whereat I: “Master! say, what town is this?”

- *Ond’* ‘whence’ → “whereat”: a connective expressing origin or consequence becomes one expressing a response to what has just been perceived.

> 22 Ed egli a me: Però che tu trascorri  
> 23 Per le tenebre troppo dalla lungi,  
> 24 Avvien che poi nel maginare aborri.  

And he to me: “Because thou traversest the darkness too far off, it follows that thou errest in thy imagining.

- *trascorri Per le tenebre* ‘passest through the darkness’ → “traversest the darkness”: the verb and prepositional phrase become a transitive verb with a direct object.
- *troppo dalla lungi* ‘too much from afar’ → “too far off”: excess is attached directly to distance, while the standpoint expressed by *dalla* is no longer explicitly marked by ‘from’.
- *Avvien che poi* ‘it happens that then’ → “it follows that”: occurrence and sequence become an explicit consequential relationship; *poi* has no separate equivalent.
- *nel maginare* ‘in the imagining’ → “in thy imagining”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves from before the verb to after it.

## Lines 25–39 — Giants Instead of Towers

> 25 Tu vedrai ben, se tu là ti congiungi,  
> 26 Quanto il senso s’ inganna di lontano:  

Thou shalt see right well, when thou arrivest there, how much the sense at distance is deceived:

- *ben* ‘well’ → “right well”: “right” supplies an intensifier.
- *se tu là ti congiungi* ‘if thou joinest thyself there’ → “when thou arrivest there”: the conditional becomes temporal; the reflexive expression becomes an intransitive verb of arrival, and “there” moves after the verb.
- *s’inganna* ‘deceives itself’ → “is deceived”: the reflexive construction becomes passive.
- *di lontano* ‘from afar’ → “at distance”: the prepositional expression changes and moves before the verb.

> 27 Però alquanto più te stesso pungi.  

therefore spur thee somewhat more.”

- *alquanto più te stesso pungi* ‘somewhat more thyself prick’ → “spur thee somewhat more”: “spur” interprets the figurative pricking as an incitement; the emphatic reflexive becomes “thee,” and the expression of degree moves from before the object and verb to after them.

> 28 Poi caramente mi prese per mano,  
> 29 E disse: Pria che noi siam più avanti,  
> 30 Acciocchè il fatto men ti paia strano,  

Then lovingly he took me by the hand, and said: “Ere we go farther, that the reality may seem less strange to thee,

- *caramente* ‘dearly’ → “lovingly”: the adverb is rendered as an explicit expression of affection.
- *mi prese per mano* ‘me he took by hand’ → “he took me by the hand”: the object follows the verb, and English supplies the article before “hand.”
- *Pria che noi siam più avanti* ‘before we are farther forward’ → “Ere we go farther”: being farther forward becomes going farther; the Italian subjunctive is rendered by an English present-tense verb.
- *il fatto* ‘the fact’ → “the reality”: the noun is rendered as what is actually present rather than simply as a fact.
- *men ti paia strano* ‘less to thee seem strange’ → “may seem less strange to thee”: the subjunctive is expressed with “may”; “less” moves beside “strange,” and “to thee” moves to the end.

> 31 Sappi che non son torri, ma giganti,  
> 32 E son nel pozzo, intorno dalla ripa,  
> 33 Dall’ ombelico in giuso tutti quanti.  

know, they are not towers, but Giants; and are in the well, around its bank, from the navel downwards all of them.”

- *che non son torri* ‘that they are not towers’ → “they are not towers”: the subordinating ‘that’ is omitted, leaving the statement without an explicit grammatical link to “know.”
- *intorno dalla ripa* ‘around by the bank’ → “around its bank”: the locative force of *dalla* is absorbed into “around,” and the definite article becomes the possessive “its.”
- *tutti quanti* ‘all, as many as there are’ → “all of them”: the emphatic combination is recast as an English partitive expression.

> 34 Come quando la nebbia si dissipa,  
> 35 Lo sguardo a poco a poco raffigura  
> 36 Ciò che cela il vapor, che l’ aere stipa;  

As when a mist is vanishing, the eye by little and little reshapes that which the air-crowding vapour hides;

- *la nebbia* ‘the mist’ → “a mist”: the definite article becomes indefinite.
- *si dissipa* ‘dissipates itself’ → “is vanishing”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, and the simple present becomes progressive.
- *Lo sguardo* ‘the gaze’ → “the eye”: the act or faculty of looking is replaced by its bodily organ.
- *a poco a poco* ‘little by little’ → “by little and little”: the repeated expression is recast with a conjunction.
- *raffigura* ‘recognizes’ → “reshapes”: Carlyle chooses a rendering that suggests forming the shapes anew rather than recognizing them.
- *Ciò che cela il vapor, che l’aere stipa* ‘that which the vapour hides, which crowds the air’ → “that which the air-crowding vapour hides”: the subject *il vapor*, which follows *cela*, is placed before “hides”; the relative clause describing the vapour becomes the compound adjective “air-crowding,” placed before its noun.

> 37 Così forando l’ aura grossa e scura,  
> 38 Più e più appressando in vêr la sponda,  
> 39 Fuggemi errore, e giungemi paura:  

so whilst piercing through that gross and darksome air, more and more approaching towards the brink, error flees from me, and fear comes over me:

- *forando* ‘piercing’ → “whilst piercing through”: “whilst” makes the temporal relation explicit, and the directly transitive Italian verb becomes an English verb followed by “through.”
- *l’aura grossa e scura* ‘the air thick and dark’ → “that gross and darksome air”: the definite article becomes demonstrative; “gross” and “darksome” render the adjectives, which move before the noun.
- *Fuggemi errore* ‘flees from me error’ → “error flees from me”: the subject moves before the verb, and the attached pronoun is expressed by the separate phrase “from me.”
- *giungemi paura* ‘reaches me fear’ → “fear comes over me”: the subject moves before the verb, and reaching someone becomes coming over someone.

## Lines 40–54 — The Giants Around the Pit

> 40 Perocchè come in su la cerchia tonda  
> 41 Montereggion di torri si corona;  

for as on its round wall Montereggione crowns itself with towers;

- *Perocchè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *la cerchia tonda* ‘the circular enclosure’ → “its round wall”: the definite article becomes a possessive, the enclosure is rendered specifically as a wall, and the adjective precedes the noun.
- *di torri* ‘of towers’ → “with towers”: English uses “with” for what constitutes the crown; the phrase moves from before the verb to after it.
- *si corona* ‘itself crowns’ → “crowns itself”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb.

> 42 Così la proda, che il pozzo circonda,  
> 43 Torreggiavan di mezza la persona  
> 44 Gli orribili giganti, cui minaccia  
> 45 Giove del cielo ancora, quando tuona:  

so with half their bodies the horrible giants, whom Jove from heaven still threatens when he thunders, turreted the brow which compasses the pit:

- *la proda* ‘the bank’ → “the brow”: a different term for the edge is used, and the object moves from before the verb to after it.
- *che il pozzo circonda* ‘which surrounds the pit’ → “which compasses the pit”: the Italian places the object *il pozzo* before its verb; English places it after.
- *Torreggiavan … Gli orribili giganti* ‘turreted … the horrible giants’ → “the horrible giants” … “turreted”: the subject precedes rather than follows the verb; the Italian imperfect is expressed by the English simple past.
- *di mezza la persona* ‘with half the person’ → “with half their bodies”: the distributive singular becomes plural, “bodies” replaces ‘person’, and a possessive replaces the definite article. The phrase moves from after the verb to before the subject.
- *cui minaccia / Giove del cielo ancora* ‘whom threatens Jove from the heaven still’ → “whom Jove from heaven still threatens”: the subject moves before the verb, while the prepositional phrase and adverb also precede rather than follow it. The article before ‘heaven’ is omitted.

> 46 Ed io scorgeva già d’ alcun la faccia,  
> 47 Le spalle, e il petto, e del ventre gran parte,  
> 48 E per le coste giù ambo le braccia.  

and already I discerned the face of one, the shoulders and the breast, and great part of the belly, and down along his sides both arms.

- *Ed io scorgeva già* ‘and I discerned already’ → “and already I discerned”: the adverb moves before the subject; the Italian imperfect is expressed by the English simple past.
- *d’ alcun la faccia* ‘of one the face’ → “the face of one”: the possessive phrase follows rather than precedes the noun.
- *del ventre gran parte* ‘of the belly great part’ → “great part of the belly”: the partitive phrase follows rather than precedes the quantity.
- *E per le coste giù ambo le braccia* ‘and along the sides down both the arms’ → “and down along his sides both arms”: “down” moves before the prepositional phrase, the article before ‘sides’ becomes “his”, and the article before ‘arms’ is omitted.

> 49 Natura certo, quando lasciò l’ arte  
> 50 Di sì fatti animali, assai fe’ bene,  
> 51 Per tor cotali esecutori a Marte.  

Nature certainly, when she left off the art of making animals like these, did very well, in taking away such executioners from Mars.

- *lasciò* ‘left’ → “left off”: the phrasal verb explicitly expresses cessation.
- *l’ arte / Di sì fatti animali* ‘the art of such animals’ → “the art of making animals like these”: “making” is supplied to explain the relationship between the art and the animals; the preceding modifier *sì fatti* becomes the following phrase “like these”.
- *assai fe’ bene* ‘much did well’ → “did very well”: the degree modifier moves from before the verb to before “well” and becomes “very”.
- *Per tor* ‘to take away’ → “in taking away”: the infinitive introduced by *per* becomes an “in” phrase with a gerund, presenting the removal as the action in which Nature did well.
- *cotali esecutori* ‘such executors’ → “such executioners”: the general term for agents who carry something out is rendered by the more specific “executioners”.
- *a Marte* ‘to Mars’ → “from Mars”: Italian marks the person deprived with *a*; English uses “from”.

> 52 E s’ ella d’ elefanti e di balene  
> 53 Non si pente, chi guarda sottilmente,  
> 54 Più giusta e più discreta la ne tiene;  

And if she repents her not of Elephants and Whales, whoso subtly looks, therein regards her as more just and prudent;

- *s’ ella d’ elefanti e di balene / Non si pente* ‘if she of elephants and of whales does not repent herself’ → “if she repents her not of Elephants and Whales”: the prepositional complements move after the verb, and the second *di* is not separately rendered. The reflexive becomes the archaic “her” rather than ‘herself’; the negative follows the verb and pronoun instead of preceding them.
- *chi guarda sottilmente* ‘whoever looks subtly’ → “whoso subtly looks”: the adverb precedes rather than follows the verb.
- *Più giusta e più discreta la ne tiene* ‘more just and more discerning her in this holds’ → “therein regards her as more just and prudent”: the predicate adjectives move after the verb and object, with “as” supplied to introduce them. The object pronoun follows the verb, while *ne*, rendered “therein”, moves to the beginning of the clause. ‘Holds’ becomes “regards”, *discreta* is rendered “prudent”, and the second *più* is not separately expressed.

## Lines 55–66 — Malice and a Giant’s Stature

> 55 Chè dove l’ argomento della mente  
> 56 S’ aggiunge al mal volere ed alla possa,  
> 57 Nessun riparo vi può far la gente.  

for where argument of mind is joined to evil will and potency, men can make no defence against it.

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *l’argomento della mente* ‘the argument of the mind’ → “argument of mind”: both definite articles are omitted.
- *S’aggiunge* ‘adds itself’ → “is joined”: the reflexive construction becomes an English passive, and adding becomes joining.
- *al mal volere ed alla possa* ‘to the evil will and to the potency’ → “to evil will and potency”: the definite articles and the repeated preposition are omitted.
- *Nessun riparo vi può far la gente* ‘no defence there can make the people’ → “men can make no defence against it”: the subject moves before the verb, and the object follows it; the singular collective *la gente* becomes plural “men”; *vi* is interpreted as “against it” rather than rendered as a locative adverb.

> 58 La faccia sua mi parea lunga e grossa,  
> 59 Come la pina di San Pietro a Roma;  
> 60 E a sua proporzione eran le altr’ ossa;  

His face seemed to me as long and large as the pine of St. Peter’s at Rome, and his other bones were in proportion to it;

- *La faccia sua* ‘the face his’ → “His face”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *mi parea* ‘to me seemed’ → “seemed to me”: the indirect object follows the verb.
- *lunga e grossa, Come* ‘long and thick, like’ → “as long and large as”: the comparison becomes the correlative construction “as … as”; *grossa* is rendered by the less dimensionally specific “large”.
- *la pina* ‘the pine-cone’ → “the pine”: the cone is called a “pine”.
- *di San Pietro* ‘of St. Peter’ → “of St. Peter’s”: English adds a possessive ending to the name within the phrase introduced by “of”.
- *a sua proporzione* ‘to its proportion’ → “in proportion to it”: the possessive becomes a pronoun after “to”, and the relation is expressed through the English construction “in proportion to”.
- *eran le altr’ ossa* ‘were the other bones’ → “his other bones were”: the subject moves before the verb, and the definite article becomes the possessive “his”.

> 61 Sì che la ripa, ch’ era perizoma  
> 62 Dal mezzo in giù, ne mostrava ben tanto  
> 63 Di sopra, che di giungere alla chioma  
> 64 Tre Frison s’ averian dato mal vanto;  

so that the bank, which was an apron from his middle downwards, shewed us certainly so much of him above, that three Friezelanders had vainly boasted to have reached his hair:

- *perizoma* ‘loincloth’ → “an apron”: a different garment term is used, and an indefinite article is supplied.
- *Dal mezzo in giù* ‘from the middle downwards’ → “from his middle downwards”: the definite article becomes the possessive “his”.
- *ne mostrava ben tanto Di sopra* ‘of him showed indeed so much above’ → “shewed us certainly so much of him above”: “us” is supplied; the pronominal *ne* becomes “of him” after “so much”; emphatic *ben* is rendered as “certainly”.
- *di giungere alla chioma* ‘of reaching to the hair’ → “to have reached his hair”: the phrase moves from before the subject to after “boasted”; the simple infinitive becomes a perfect infinitive, and *di* becomes the infinitive marker “to”; reaching takes a direct object rather than a phrase with *a*, and the definite article becomes “his”.
- *s’averian dato mal vanto* ‘would have given themselves a bad boast’ → “had vainly boasted”: the conditional perfect becomes an English past-perfect construction with counterfactual force; the noun *vanto* becomes the verb “boasted”, *mal* becomes the adverb “vainly”, and the reflexive expression of giving oneself a boast disappears.

> 65 Perocch’ io ne vedea trenta gran palmi  
> 66 Dal luogo in giù, dov’ uom s’ affibbia il manto.  

for downwards from the place where a man buckles on his mantle, I saw thirty large spans of him.

- *Perocch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *io ne vedea trenta gran palmi* ‘I of him was seeing thirty large spans’ → “I saw thirty large spans of him”: *ne* becomes “of him” after the measured quantity rather than before the verb, and the Italian imperfect is rendered by the English simple past.
- *Dal luogo in giù* ‘from the place downwards’ → “downwards from the place”: “downwards” moves before the prepositional phrase; the whole locative phrase, including its relative clause, moves before “I saw” rather than following the measured quantity.
- *dov’uom s’affibbia il manto* ‘where man buckles to himself the mantle’ → “where a man buckles on his mantle”: an indefinite article is supplied before “man”; the reflexive dative becomes the possessive “his” in place of the definite article, and “on” is supplied with “buckles”.

## Lines 67–78 — Nimrod’s Cry and Virgil’s Rebuke

> 67 Rafel maì amech zabì almi,  
> 68 Cominciò a gridar la fiera bocca,  
> 69 Cui non si convenien più dolci salmi.  

“RAAFEL MAAEE AAMECH ZAABEE ALMEE,” began to shout the savage mouth, for which no sweeter psalmody is fit.

- *Rafel maì amech zabì almi* ‘Rafel maì amech zabì almi’ → “RAAFEL MAAEE AAMECH ZAABEE ALMEE”: the words are phonetically respelled for English readers rather than translated.
- *Cui non si convenien più dolci salmi* ‘to which sweeter psalms are not fitting’ → “for which no sweeter psalmody is fit”: the negative is transferred from the verb to the noun phrase; plural ‘psalms’ becomes the singular collective “psalmody”; and the subject precedes rather than follows the verb.

> 70 E il Duca mio vêr lui: Anima sciocca,  
> 71 Tienti col corno, e con quel ti disfoga,  
> 72 Quand’ ira o altra passïon ti tocca.  

And towards him my Guide: “Stupid soul! keep to thy horn; and vent thyself with that, when rage or other passion touches thee.

- *E il Duca mio vêr lui* ‘and my leader towards him’ → “And towards him my Guide”: “Guide” replaces ‘leader’, and the directional phrase is moved before the subject.
- *Anima sciocca* ‘soul stupid’ → “Stupid soul”: the adjective is moved before the noun.
- *Tienti col corno* ‘keep thyself with the horn’ → “keep to thy horn”: the reflexive construction becomes the English idiom “keep to”, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *e con quel ti disfoga* ‘and with that thyself vent’ → “and vent thyself with that”: the reflexive pronoun follows the verb, and the prepositional phrase moves to the end.
- *ti tocca* ‘thee touches’ → “touches thee”: the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 73 Cercati al collo, e troverai la soga  
> 74 Che il tien legato, o anima confusa,  
> 75 E vedi lui che il gran petto ti doga.  

Search on thy neck, and thou wilt find the belt that holds it tied, O soul confused, and see itself that girdles thy huge breast.”

- *Cercati al collo* ‘search for thyself at the neck’ → “Search on thy neck”: the dative pronoun is represented by the possessive “thy”, and ‘at’ becomes “on”.
- *soga* ‘rope’ → “belt”: Carlyle interprets the fastening as a belt rather than a rope.
- *Che il tien legato* ‘that it holds tied’ → “that holds it tied”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *lui* ‘it’ → “itself”: the independent object pronoun is rendered with an emphatic English form.
- *che il gran petto ti doga* ‘that the great breast for thee bands’ → “that girdles thy huge breast”: the verb moves before its object; the dative *ti* becomes the possessive “thy”; ‘great’ becomes “huge”; and the image of banding is rendered as “girdles”.

> 76 Poi disse a me: Egli stesso s’ accusa.  

Then he said to me: “He accuses himself.

- *Egli stesso s’ accusa* ‘he himself accuses himself’ → “He accuses himself”: the separate emphasis on the subject supplied by *stesso* is omitted, and the reflexive object follows the verb.

> 77 Questi è Nembrotto, per lo cui mal coto  
> 78 Pure un linguaggio nel mondo non s’ usa.  

This is Nimrod, through whose ill device one language is not still used in the world.

- *mal coto* ‘evil thought’ → “ill device”: ‘thought’ or ‘intention’ is interpreted as a devised scheme.
- *Pure un linguaggio nel mondo non s’ usa* ‘only one language in the world is not used’ → “one language is not still used in the world”: Carlyle gives *Pure* the temporal sense ‘still’ rather than the restrictive sense ‘only’, placing it within the negative predicate; “in the world” moves from before the verb to the end.

## Lines 79–96 — Ephialtes Bound in Chains

> 79 Lasciamlo stare, e non parliamo a voto;  

Let us leave him standing, and not speak in vain;

- *Lasciamlo stare* ‘let us leave him be’ → “Let us leave him standing”: *stare* is given the specific physical sense of standing rather than the general sense of being left alone.
- *non parliamo* ‘let us not speak’ → “not speak”: the second exhortation shares the opening “Let us” rather than repeating that construction.

> 80 Chè così è a lui ciascun linguaggio,  
> 81 Come il suo ad altrui, ch’ a nullo è noto.  

for every language is to him, as to others his which no one understands.”

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *così … Come* ‘so … as’ → “as”: the two-part comparison is reduced to a single conjunction.
- *è a lui ciascun linguaggio* ‘is to him every language’ → “every language is to him”: the subject is moved before the verb.
- *il suo ad altrui* ‘his to others’ → “to others his”: the possessive pronoun and the phrase referring to others exchange places.
- *ch’ a nullo è noto* ‘which to no one is known’ → “which no one understands”: the construction with ‘is known’ becomes an active verb, and the person to whom it is unknown becomes the subject.

> 82 Facemmo adunque più lungo vïaggio  
> 83 Volti a sinistra; ed al trar d’ un balestro  
> 84 Trovammo l’ altro assai più fiero e maggio.  

We therefore journeyed on, turning to the left; and, a crossbow-shot off, we found another far more fierce and large.

- *Facemmo … più lungo vïaggio* ‘we made a longer journey’ → “We” … “journeyed on”: the verb and noun become a single verb, while the comparative description of the journey becomes the adverb “on”.
- *Volti a sinistra* ‘turned to the left’ → “turning to the left”: the past participle becomes a present participle.
- *al trar d’ un balestro* ‘at the shooting of a crossbow’ → “a crossbow-shot off”: the act of shooting is rendered as a compound noun expressing distance, with “off” supplied.
- *l’ altro* ‘the other’ → “another”: the definite expression becomes indefinite.
- *assai* ‘much’ → “far”: the degree of comparison is expressed with a different intensifying adverb.
- *maggio* ‘larger’ → “large”: the Italian comparative adjective becomes a positive adjective sharing the preceding “more”.

> 85 A cinger lui, qual che fosse il maestro,  
> 86 Non so io dir; ma ei tenea succinto  
> 87 Dinanzi l’ altro, e dietro il braccio destro,  

Who and what the master could be that girt him thus, I cannot tell; but he had his right arm pinioned down behind, and the other before,

- *qual che fosse il maestro* ‘whoever the master was’ → “Who and what the master could be”: the indefinite expression becomes an indirect question; *qual* is expanded into “Who and what”, and the imperfect subjunctive is rendered with “could be”.
- *A cinger lui* ‘to gird him’ → “that girt him thus”: the introductory infinitive becomes a finite relative clause following “the master”; “thus” is supplied.
- *Non so io dir* ‘I do not know how to say’ → “I cannot tell”: lack of knowledge is expressed as inability, and “tell” replaces ‘say’.
- *ei tenea succinto Dinanzi l’ altro, e dietro il braccio destro* ‘he kept bound in front the other, and behind the right arm’ → “he had his right arm pinioned down behind, and the other before”: the right arm is mentioned first instead of second; the positions follow the arms rather than preceding them. ‘Kept bound’ becomes “had … pinioned down”, with “down” making the restraint more explicit, and the definite article before ‘right arm’ becomes “his”.

> 88 D’ una catena, che il teneva avvinto  
> 89 Dal collo in giù, sì che in su lo scoperto  
> 90 Si ravvolgeva infino al giro quinto.  

with a chain which held him clasped from the neck downwards, and on the uncovered part went round to the fifth turn.

- *D’ una catena* ‘by a chain’ → “with a chain”: the preposition marking the means of restraint changes.
- *avvinto* ‘bound’ → “clasped”: binding is expressed as clasping.
- *sì che* ‘so that’ → “and”: the explicit relation of consequence becomes simple coordination.
- *in su lo scoperto* ‘on the uncovered’ → “on the uncovered part”: “part” is supplied to make explicit the noun implied by the substantivized adjective.
- *Si ravvolgeva* ‘wound itself around’ → “went round”: the reflexive verb becomes an intransitive phrase, without an expressed reflexive pronoun.
- *infino al giro quinto* ‘as far as the turn fifth’ → “to the fifth turn”: “to” compresses the expression of extent, and the ordinal adjective is moved before its noun.

> 91 Questo superbo voll’ essere esperto  
> 92 Di sua potenza contra il sommo Giove,  
> 93 Disse il mio Duca, ond’ egli ha cotal merto.  

“This proud spirit willed to try his power against high Jove,” said my Guide; “whence he has such reward.

- *Questo superbo* ‘this proud one’ → “This proud spirit”: “spirit” supplies a noun for the substantivized adjective.
- *essere esperto Di sua potenza* ‘to be experienced in his power’ → “to try his power”: a construction with ‘be’, a predicate adjective, and a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object.
- *sommo* ‘highest’ → “high”: the superlative force is not retained.
- *Duca* ‘leader’ → “Guide”: leadership is expressed specifically as guidance.
- *merto* ‘desert’ → “reward”: what is deserved is rendered as the recompense received.

> 94 Fialte ha nome; e fece le gran prove,  
> 95 Quando i giganti fer paura ai Dei:  

Ephialtes is his name; and he made the great endeavours, when the giants made the gods afraid:

- *Fialte ha nome* ‘Ephialtes he has for name’ → “Ephialtes is his name”: the construction with ‘has’ becomes one with “is”, and the relation of possession is expressed by “his”.
- *prove* ‘feats’ → “endeavours”: accomplished or demonstrated feats are expressed as efforts.
- *i giganti fer paura ai Dei* ‘the giants made fear to the gods’ → “the giants made the gods afraid”: the noun ‘fear’ becomes the adjective “afraid”, and the indirect object becomes the direct object of “made”.

> 96 Le braccia, ch’ ei menò, giammai non muove.  

the arms he agitated then, he never moves.”

- *ch’ ei menò* ‘which he wielded’ → “he agitated then”: the relative pronoun is omitted, ‘wielded’ becomes “agitated”, and “then” is supplied.
- *giammai non* ‘never … not’ → “never”: the Italian negative concord is expressed by a single English negative.

## Lines 97–108 — Briareus, Antæus, and Ephialtes

> 97 Ed io a lui: S’ esser puote, io vorrei  
> 98 Che dello smisurato Briareo  
> 99 Esperïenza avesser gli occhi miei.  

And I to him: “If it were possible, I should wish my eyes might have experience of the immense Briareus.”

- *S’ esser puote* ‘if it can be’ → “If it were possible”: the present indicative becomes a hypothetical past subjunctive, and the verb expressing possibility becomes the adjective “possible”.
- *Che dello smisurato Briareo Esperïenza avesser gli occhi miei* ‘that of the immense Briareus experience might have the eyes mine’ → “my eyes might have experience of the immense Briareus”: ‘that’ is omitted; the subject moves before the verb, “experience” follows it, and the phrase identifying whose experience is sought moves after “experience”. The possessive moves before “eyes”, and “might have” expresses the Italian imperfect subjunctive.

> 100 Ond’ ei rispose: Tu vedrai Anteo  
> 101 Presso di qui, che parla, ed è disciolto,  
> 102 Che ne porrà nel fondo d’ ogni reo.  

Whereat he answered: “Thou shalt see Antæus near at hand, who speaks, and is unfettered, who will put us into the bottom of all guilt.

- *Ond’* ‘whence’ → “Whereat”: the connective is expressed as a response to the preceding words rather than as something proceeding from them.
- *Presso di qui* ‘near here’ → “near at hand”: the explicit reference to ‘here’ becomes an idiom expressing proximity.
- *ne porrà* ‘us will put’ → “will put us”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *nel fondo* ‘in the bottom’ → “into the bottom”: English explicitly marks movement to the destination.
- *d’ogni reo* ‘of every evil’ → “of all guilt”: the substantival *reo* is interpreted as moral guilt; distributive ‘every’ becomes “all” with an uncountable noun.

> 103 Quel che tu vuoi veder, più là è molto,  
> 104 Ed è legato, e fatto come questo,  
> 105 Salvo che più feroce par nel volto.  

He whom thou desirest to see is far beyond; and is tied and shaped like this one, save that he seems in aspect more ferocious.”

- *Quel che* ‘the one whom’ → “He whom”: the demonstrative expression becomes a personal pronoun.
- *più là è molto* ‘farther on is much’ → “is far beyond”: the verb moves before the expression of place; the comparative expression and its following intensifier become “far beyond”, with the intensifier first.
- *fatto* ‘made’ → “shaped”: the general participle is rendered specifically in terms of bodily form.
- *più feroce par nel volto* ‘more ferocious he seems in the face’ → “he seems in aspect more ferocious”: the verb precedes its adjectival complement, and the phrase describing appearance intervenes between them. The concrete ‘face’ becomes the more general “aspect”.

> 106 Non fu tremuoto già tanto rubesto,  
> 107 Che scotesse una torre così forte,  
> 108 Come Fialte a scuotersi fu presto.  

No mighty earthquake ever shook a tower so violently, as Ephialtes forthwith shook himself.

- *Non fu tremuoto già tanto rubesto, Che scotesse* ‘never was there an earthquake so mighty that it should shake’ → “No mighty earthquake ever shook”: the existential construction becomes a direct statement with “earthquake” as subject. Negation moves onto that subject, *già* is represented by “ever”, and the degree-and-result construction *tanto … Che* disappears. The subordinate imperfect subjunctive becomes the main verb in the simple past indicative.
- *così forte* ‘so strongly’ → “so violently”: strength of action is expressed as violence.
- *Fialte a scuotersi fu presto* ‘Ephialtes to shake himself was quick’ → “Ephialtes forthwith shook himself”: the predicate expressing readiness or quickness becomes an adverb of immediacy, while the reflexive infinitive becomes the finite past-tense verb with its reflexive object following it.

## Lines 109–123 — Fear of Death and an Appeal to Antæus

> 109 Allor temetti più che mai la morte,  

Then more than ever I dreaded death;

- *temetti più che mai la morte* ‘I feared more than ever the death’ → “more than ever I dreaded death”: the adverbial phrase moves before the subject and verb; *temetti* becomes “dreaded,” and the article before the abstract noun is omitted.

> 110 E non v’ era mestier più che la dotta,  
> 111 S’ io non avessi viste le ritorte.  

and nothing else was wanted for it but the fear, had I not seen his bands.

- *non v’ era mestier più che la dotta* ‘there was no need of more than the fear’ → “nothing else was wanted for it but the fear”: the construction expressing need becomes a passive construction with “nothing else” as subject; *più che* is recast as “else … but,” and *v’* is represented by “for it,” referring back to death.
- *S’ io non avessi viste* ‘if I had not seen’ → “had I not seen”: inversion replaces the conditional conjunction.
- *le ritorte* ‘the twisted cords’ → “his bands”: the specific designation of twisted bonds becomes the broader “bands,” and the definite article becomes a possessive.

> 112 Noi procedemmo più avanti allotta,  
> 113 E venimmo ad Anteo, che ben cinqu’ alle,  
> 114 Senza la testa, uscia fuor della grotta.  

We then proceeded farther on, and reached Antæus, who full five ells, besides the head, forth issued from the cavern.

- *Noi procedemmo più avanti allotta* ‘we proceeded farther on then’ → “We then proceeded farther on”: the final adverb of time moves between subject and verb.
- *venimmo ad Anteo* ‘we came to Antæus’ → “reached Antæus”: a verb with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb.
- *Senza la testa* ‘without the head’ → “besides the head”: exclusion from the measurement is expressed instead as an addition beyond it.
- *uscia fuor* ‘was coming out’ → “forth issued”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, “issued” replaces ‘was coming,’ and the directional adverb moves before the verb.

> 115 O tu, che nella fortunata valle,  
> 116 Che fece Scipïon di gloria ereda,  
> 117 Quando Annibal co’ suoi diede le spalle,  

“O thou! who in the fateful valley, which made Scipio heir of glory when Hannibal retreated with his hosts,

- *fortunata* ‘fortunate’ → “fateful”: the adjective is interpreted in terms of destiny rather than good fortune.
- *di gloria ereda* ‘of glory heir’ → “heir of glory”: the noun precedes its dependent phrase in English.
- *co’ suoi* ‘with his own’ → “with his hosts”: “hosts” supplies a noun where the Italian uses a possessive substantivally; the phrase also moves from before the verb to after it.
- *diede le spalle* ‘gave his shoulders’ → “retreated”: the Italian idiom for turning one’s back is replaced by a single verb expressing withdrawal.

> 118 Recasti già mille lion per preda,  

didst take of old a thousand lions for thy prey;

- *Recasti* ‘you brought’ → “didst take”: bringing becomes taking, and the simple Italian past is rendered with the auxiliary “didst.”
- *già* ‘once’ → “of old”: the adverb becomes a phrase explicitly placing the action in the distant past.
- *per preda* ‘as prey’ → “for thy prey”: the relation is expressed with “for,” and a possessive absent from the Italian is supplied.

> 119 E che se fossi stato all’ alta guerra  
> 120 De’ tuoi fratelli, ancor par ch’ ei si creda,  
> 121 Che avrebber vinto i figli della terra;  

and through whom, hadst thou been at the high war of thy brethren, it seems yet to be believed that the sons of earth had conquered:

- *E che* ‘and who’ → “and through whom”: the relative pronoun is expanded into a prepositional phrase explicitly expressing agency or causation.
- *se fossi stato* ‘if you had been’ → “hadst thou been”: inversion replaces the conditional conjunction.
- *ancor par ch’ ei si creda* ‘still it seems that it is believed’ → “it seems yet to be believed”: the finite impersonal clause becomes a passive infinitive, and “yet” follows “seems” rather than preceding it.
- *avrebber vinto i figli della terra* ‘would have conquered the sons of the earth’ → “the sons of earth had conquered”: the postposed subject moves before the verb; the conditional perfect becomes a counterfactual past perfect, and the article before ‘earth’ is omitted.

> 122 Mettine giuso, e non ten venga schifo,  
> 123 Dove Cocito la freddura serra.  

set us down—and be not shy to do it—where the cold locks up Cocytus.

- *e non ten venga schifo* ‘and let no disgust at it come to you’ → “and be not shy to do it”: the construction in which disgust comes to someone becomes a direct negative imperative with an adjective; aversion is rendered as shyness, and “to do it” makes explicit the action referred to by *ne*.
- *Dove Cocito la freddura serra* ‘where Cocytus the cold locks up’ → “where the cold locks up Cocytus”: the object, *Cocito*, moves from before the subject and verb to its usual English position after the verb.

## Lines 124–135 — Virgil and Dante Gathered Together

> 124 Non ci far ire a Tizio, nè a Tifo:  
> 125 Questi può dar di quel che qui si brama:  

Do not make us go to Tityos nor Typhon: this man can give of that which here is longed for.

- *Non ci far ire* ‘not us make go’ → “Do not make us go”: English uses “Do” to form the negative imperative and places “us” after “make”.
- *nè a Tifo* ‘nor to Typhon’ → “nor Typhon”: the repeated preposition is omitted.
- *Questi* ‘this one’ → “this man”: the demonstrative pronoun is expanded into a demonstrative and noun.
- *si brama* ‘one desires’ → “is longed for”: the impersonal construction becomes an English passive, and ‘desires’ becomes “longed for”.

> 126 Però ti china, e non torcer lo grifo.  

Therefore bend thee, and curl not thy lip in scorn:

- *ti china* ‘thyself bend’ → “bend thee”: the reflexive pronoun follows the imperative instead of preceding it.
- *non torcer lo grifo* ‘do not twist the snout’ → “curl not thy lip in scorn”: “curl” replaces ‘twist’, “lip” replaces ‘snout’, and the definite article becomes a possessive. “in scorn” makes the gesture’s implication explicit; the negative follows the verb.

> 127 Ancor ti può nel mondo render fama;  
> 128 Ch’ ei vive, e lunga vita ancor aspetta,  
> 129 Se innanzi tempo grazia a sè nol chiama.  

he can restore thy fame on earth; for he lives, and still awaits long life, so Grace before the time call him not unto herself.”

- *Ancor ti può nel mondo render fama* ‘also to thee he can in the world give fame’ → “he can restore thy fame on earth”: ‘also’ is omitted; the recipient ‘to thee’ becomes the possessive “thy”; and “restore” gives ‘give’ the sense of giving back. ‘In the world’ becomes “on earth” and moves after “fame”.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *lunga vita ancor aspetta* ‘long life still awaits’ → “still awaits long life”: the object moves from before the adverb and verb to after them.
- *Se innanzi tempo grazia a sè nol chiama* ‘if before time grace to herself not him calls’ → “so Grace before the time call him not unto herself”: “so” serves as a conditional conjunction in place of ‘if’. The subject moves before the time phrase, “the” is supplied before “time”, and the indicative ‘calls’ becomes the subjunctive “call”. The object and negative follow the verb, while ‘to herself’ moves to the end.

> 130 Così disse il Maestro: e quegli in fretta  
> 131 Le man distese, e prese il Duca mio,  
> 132 Ond’ Ercole sentì già grande stretta.  

Thus spake the Master; and he in haste stretched forth the hands, whence Hercules of old did feel great stress, and took my Guide.

- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *Le man distese* ‘the hands extended’ → “stretched forth the hands”: ‘extended’ becomes the verbal phrase “stretched forth”, and the verb moves before its object.
- *e prese il Duca mio* ‘and took the leader my’ → “and took my Guide”: “Guide” replaces ‘leader’, and the possessive precedes its noun. The whole clause moves after the relative clause describing the hands.
- *sentì già* ‘felt once’ → “of old did feel”: the temporal adverb becomes “of old” and moves before the verb; the simple past is expressed with “did” and an infinitive.
- *grande stretta* ‘great squeeze’ → “great stress”: the concrete squeezing is rendered as the pressure or strain it produces.

> 133 Virgilio, quando prender si sentio,  
> 134 Disse a me: Fatti in qua, sì ch’ io ti prenda.  

Virgil, when he felt their grasp, said to me: “Come here, that I may take thee.”

- *quando prender si sentio* ‘when he felt himself being taken’ → “when he felt their grasp”: the infinitival construction becomes a noun phrase, “their grasp”. The reflexive pronoun is no longer expressed, and “their” is supplied.
- *Fatti in qua* ‘make thyself hither’ → “Come here”: the reflexive expression of movement becomes a simple imperative.
- *sì ch’ io ti prenda* ‘so that I thee take’ → “that I may take thee”: ‘so’ is omitted, the subjunctive is expressed with “may”, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 135 Poi fece sì, che un fascio er’ egli ed io.  

Then of himself and me he made one bundle.

- *Poi fece sì, che un fascio er’ egli ed io* ‘then he did so that a bundle were he and I’ → “Then of himself and me he made one bundle”: the main verb and result clause are recast as a single construction with “made”. ‘He and I’, the subjects of ‘were’, become “of himself and me”, placed before the main subject and verb; ‘a bundle’ becomes the direct object “one bundle”.

## Lines 136–145 — Antæus Lowers the Poets

> 136 Qual pare a riguardar la Carisenda  
> 137 Sotto il chinato, quando un nuvol vada  
> 138 Sovr’ essa sì, ch’ ella in contrario penda;  

Such as the Carisenda seems to one’s view, beneath the leaning side, when a cloud is going over it so, that it hangs opposed;

- *Qual pare a riguardar la Carisenda* ‘As seems, on looking, the Carisenda’ → “Such as the Carisenda seems to one’s view”: the subject moves before the verb; the infinitive of looking becomes the noun “view”, with the indefinite observer “one’s” supplied. The article before the tower’s name is omitted.
- *Sotto il chinato* ‘Under the leaning [part]’ → “beneath the leaning side”: the substantivized adjective is expanded by supplying “side”.
- *vada* ‘go’ → “is going”: the present subjunctive becomes an indicative progressive.
- *ch’ ella in contrario penda* ‘that it lean in the opposite direction’ → “that it hangs opposed”: “hangs” replaces ‘lean’, the subjunctive becomes indicative, and the directional phrase becomes the adjective “opposed”.

> 139 Tal parve Anteo a me che stava a bada  
> 140 Di vederlo chinare, e fu tal ora  
> 141 Ch’ io avrei voluto ir per altra strada:  

such Antæus seemed to me who stood watching to see him bend: and at the time I should have wished to go by other road.

- *Tal parve Anteo a me* ‘Such seemed Antæus to me’ → “such Antæus seemed to me”: the subject moves before the verb.
- *che stava a bada / Di vederlo chinare* ‘who was intent on seeing him bend’ → “who stood watching to see him bend”: the idiom *stava a bada* becomes “stood watching”, giving *stava* the more concrete sense of standing; the dependent phrase becomes an English infinitive introduced by “to”.
- *e fu tal ora / Ch’* ‘and it was such a time that’ → “and at the time”: the finite clause becomes an adverbial phrase. The correlative construction ‘such … that’ is dropped, making the following wish an independent assertion.

> 142 Ma lievemente al fondo, che divora  
> 143 Lucifero con Giuda, ci posò:  

But gently on the deep, which swallows Lucifer with Judas, he placed us;

- *lievemente* ‘lightly’ → “gently”: lightness is expressed as gentleness.
- *al fondo* ‘at the bottom’ → “on the deep”: “on” replaces ‘at’, and the noun meaning ‘bottom’ becomes the substantivized adjective “deep”.
- *divora* ‘devours’ → “swallows”: the verb of devouring is rendered by a verb of swallowing.
- *ci posò* ‘us he placed’ → “he placed us”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 144 Nè sì chinato lì fece dimora,  
> 145 E come albero in nave si levò.  

nor lingered there thus bent, but raised himself as in a ship the mast.

- *Nè sì chinato lì fece dimora* ‘Nor thus bent there did he make a stay’ → “nor lingered there thus bent”: the verb–noun expression *fece dimora* becomes the single verb “lingered”; “thus bent” moves after both the verb and “there”.
- *E* ‘And’ → “but”: the conjunction is made explicitly adversative.
- *come albero in nave* ‘like mast in ship’ → “as in a ship the mast”: the phrase locating the mast moves before it, and the articles “a” and “the” are supplied. The whole comparison also moves from before the verb to after it.
- *si levò* ‘rose’ → “raised himself”: the Italian pronominal verb is rendered by a transitive verb with an explicit reflexive object.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Syntax

Carlyle keeps much of the Italian’s vocabulary, imagery, and sequence of expression, but regularly rearranges its syntax for English. Subjects placed after verbs move before them, objects follow rather than precede their verbs, and dependent phrases move after their nouns: *d’alcun la faccia* becomes “the face of one”, and *del ventre gran parte* becomes “great part of the belly”. Yet the translation often preserves marked arrangements, including “the arms he agitated then, he never moves”, as well as close verbal correspondences such as *Fuggemi errore, e giungemi paura*: “error flees from me, and fear comes over me”. More generally, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject, and omits the articles that Italian commonly places before possessives.

Changes of construction frequently go beyond rearrangement. Reflexive or impersonal expressions become passives, as *s’inganna* becomes “is deceived” and *si brama* becomes “is longed for”; elsewhere, *ch’a nullo è noto* becomes the active “which no one understands”. Noun-based expressions become verbs: *fece dimora* is compressed into “lingered”, while *fer paura ai Dei* becomes “made the gods afraid”. Conversely, *quando prender si sentio* becomes “when he felt their grasp”, replacing an infinitival construction with a noun phrase. Tenses and moods also shift: *od’io* becomes “I have heard”, and subjunctives are sometimes represented by modal auxiliaries, as in “may seem” and “might have”. Conditional clauses can lose their conjunction through inversion, as in “had I not seen his bands”.

The vocabulary ranges from close correspondence to specification or reinterpretation. *Buona*, ‘good’, becomes “healing”; *mi morse*, ‘bit me’, becomes the broader “wounded me”; and *lo sguardo*, ‘the gaze’, becomes “the eye”. Some choices alter the sense more substantially: *la santa gesta*, ‘the holy host’, is rendered “the holy emprise”, while *Pure* is taken as “still” rather than ‘only’. Italian idioms are often replaced by English ones or by their implied action: *demmo il dosso* becomes “turned our back”, and *diede le spalle* becomes “retreated”. Nevertheless, Carlyle retains many concrete terms and unusual formulations, including “argument of mind”, “air-crowding vapour”, and “the bottom of all guilt”.

Additions commonly make an implication explicit: “with blushes”, “of making”, and “in scorn” explain what the Italian leaves within its wording or gesture. Personal relations expressed by Italian pronouns often become possessives, as *il gran petto ti doga* becomes “girdles thy huge breast”. Omissions tend to reduce grammatical repetition or emphasis: the separate force of *stesso* disappears from “He accuses himself”, and repeated prepositions or comparative markers are sometimes shared in English. Connectives also reshape relationships: *sì che* becomes “and”, whereas the final *E* becomes “but”. Overall, the translation remains closely attached to the Italian’s verbal texture while repeatedly clarifying, compressing, or reorganizing its grammar.
