# Inferno 21

## Lines 1–13 — The Dark Cleft and Boiling Pitch

> 1 Così di ponte in ponte, altro parlando  
> 2 Che la mia Commedìa cantar non cura,  
> 3 Venimmo; e tenevamo il colmo, quando  

Thus from bridge to bridge we came, with other talk which my Comedy cares not to recite; and held the summit, when

- *Così di ponte in ponte, altro parlando Che la mia Commedìa cantar non cura, Venimmo* ‘Thus from bridge to bridge, speaking of other things which my Comedy to sing cares not, we came’ → “Thus from bridge to bridge we came, with other talk which my Comedy cares not to recite”: the main verb moves before the phrase about speaking and its accompanying relative clause; the participial construction becomes a prepositional phrase, and speaking becomes the noun “talk”; the infinitive moves after the governing verb, and “recite” replaces ‘sing’.
- *tenevamo* ‘we were holding’ → “held”: the Italian imperfect, expressing an ongoing action, becomes an English simple past.

> 4 Ristemmo per veder l’ altra fessura  
> 5 Di Malebolge, e gli altri pianti vani:  

we stood still to see the other cleft of Malebolge and other vain lamentings:

- *Ristemmo* ‘we stopped’ → “we stood still”: the verb of stopping becomes a phrase describing remaining motionless.
- *gli altri pianti vani* ‘the other weepings vain’ → “other vain lamentings”: the article is omitted, “lamentings” replaces ‘weepings’, and the adjective follows English order before the noun.

> 6 E vidila mirabilmente oscura.  

and I found it marvellously dark.

- *vidila* ‘I saw it’ → “I found it”: seeing becomes finding something to have a particular quality.

> 7 Quale nell’ Arzanà de’ Viniziani  
> 8 Bolle l’ inverno la tenace pece  
> 9 A rimpalmar li legni lor non sani,  
> 10 Che navicar non ponno; e in quella vece  

As in the arsenal of the Venetians boils the clammy pitch, to caulk their damaged ships, in winter when they cannot navigate; and, instead thereof,

- *l’inverno* ‘the winter’ → “in winter”: the noun phrase used adverbially becomes a prepositional phrase without an article; it moves from immediately after the verb to after the purpose clause.
- *tenace* ‘tenacious’ → “clammy”: the adjective of adhesion becomes one describing a sticky consistency.
- *A rimpalmar* ‘to coat again’ → “to caulk”: coating the hull again is rendered as sealing its seams; the sense of repetition is not separately expressed.
- *li legni lor non sani* ‘the timbers their not sound’ → “their damaged ships”: “ships” gives the referent of the metonymic ‘timbers’; the possessive moves before the noun, and the negative description ‘not sound’ becomes the positive adjective “damaged”, also placed before the noun.
- *Che navicar non ponno* ‘which navigate cannot’ → “when they cannot navigate”: the relative clause referring to the ships becomes a temporal clause linked to “winter”; the relative pronoun becomes “when they”, and the infinitive moves after the negative modal.
- *in quella vece* ‘in that stead’ → “instead thereof”: the demonstrative and noun become an adverbial expression with “thereof” referring back to the activity being replaced.

> 11 Chi fa suo legno nuovo, e chi ristoppa  
> 12 Le coste a quel che più viaggi fece;  

one builds his ship anew, one plugs the ribs of that which hath made many voyages;

- *Chi … e chi* ‘who … and who’ → “one” … “one”: the distributive pronouns become indefinite “one”, and the coordinating conjunction is omitted.
- *fa suo legno nuovo* ‘makes his timber new’ → “builds his ship anew”: “builds” specifies the kind of making, and “ship” renders the metonymic ‘timber’; the adjective ‘new’, describing the vessel, becomes the adverb “anew”, modifying the building.
- *ristoppa* ‘caulks again’ → “plugs”: the specific operation becomes a more general action, without an explicit indication of repetition.
- *Le coste a quel* ‘the ribs to that one’ → “the ribs of that”: the Italian construction with *a*, marking whose ribs they are, becomes an English possessive construction with “of”.
- *che più viaggi fece* ‘which more voyages made’ → “which hath made many voyages”: *più* is interpreted as ‘many’ rather than as an explicit comparative; the simple past becomes a present perfect, and the object moves after the verb.

> 13 Chi ribatte da proda, e chi da poppa;  

some hammer at the prow, some at the stern;

- *Chi … e chi* ‘who … and who’ → “some” … “some”: the singular distributive pronouns become plural indefinite pronouns, and the conjunction is omitted.
- *ribatte* ‘beats again’ → “hammer”: the action is specified as hammering, without separately expressing repetition.
- *da proda … da poppa* ‘at prow … at stern’ → “at the prow” … “at the stern”: English supplies definite articles before both nouns.

## Lines 14–28 — Boiling Pitch and Sudden Alarm

> 14 Altri fa remi, ed altri volge sarte;  
> 15 Chi terzeruolo, ed artimon rintoppa:  

some make oars, and some twist ropes; one mends the jib, and one the mainsail:

- *Altri fa remi* ‘another makes oars’ → “some make oars”: the indefinite singular becomes plural.
- *ed altri volge sarte* ‘and another turns rigging’ → “and some twist ropes”: the indefinite singular becomes plural; turning is specified as twisting, and the nautical ‘rigging’ becomes the more general “ropes”.
- *Chi terzeruolo, ed artimon rintoppa* ‘one patches the small sail and the mizzen’ → “one mends the jib, and one the mainsail”: the verb moves before its objects; articles and a second “one” are supplied. Patching becomes the broader “mends”, and the sail names are interpreted as “jib” and “mainsail”.

> 16 Tal, non per fuoco, ma per divina arte  
> 17 Bollia laggiuso una pegola spessa,  
> 18 Che inviscava la ripa d’ ogni parte.  

So, not by fire but by art Divine, a dense pitch boiled down there, and overglued the banks on every side.

- *divina arte* ‘divine art’ → “art Divine”: the adjective follows rather than precedes the noun.
- *Bollia laggiuso una pegola spessa* ‘boiled down there a pitch dense’ → “a dense pitch boiled down there”: the subject moves before the verb, and its adjective moves before the noun.
- *Che* ‘which’ → “and”: the relative clause becomes a coordinated clause.
- *inviscava* ‘made sticky’ → “overglued”: the English uses a compound verb suggesting a coating of glue.
- *la ripa* ‘the bank’ → “the banks”: singular becomes plural.
- *d’ ogni parte* ‘from every side’ → “on every side”: the Italian expression of distribution is rendered with “on” rather than ‘from’.

> 19 Io vedea lei, ma non vedeva in essa  
> 20 Ma’ che le bolle che il bollor levava,  
> 21 E gonfiar tutta, e riseder compressa.  

It I saw; but saw nought therein, except the bubbles which the boiling raised, and the heaving and compressed subsiding of the whole.

- *Io vedea lei* ‘I saw it’ → “It I saw”: the object moves before the subject and verb.
- *non vedeva* ‘did not see’ → “saw nought”: verbal negation becomes a negative object.
- *in essa* ‘in it’ → “therein”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *Ma’ che* ‘other than’ → “except”: the restrictive expression becomes a single preposition.
- *E gonfiar tutta, e riseder compressa* ‘and swell all over, and subside compressed’ → “and the heaving and compressed subsiding of the whole”: the infinitives describing what the speaker saw become nouns with a supplied article. Swelling becomes “heaving”; *tutta*, describing the pitch as a whole, becomes the noun phrase “of the whole”; and *compressa*, describing the pitch as it subsides, becomes an adjective modifying “subsiding”.

> 22 Mentr’ io laggiù fisamente mirava,  
> 23 Lo Duca mio dicendo: Guarda, guarda!  
> 24 Mi trasse a sè del luogo dov’ io stava.  

Whilst I was gazing fixtly down on it, my Guide, saying, “Take care, take care!” drew me to him from the place where I was standing.

- *laggiù fisamente mirava* ‘down there fixedly gazed’ → “was gazing fixtly down on it”: the verb moves before the adverbs and takes the past progressive. “down on it” replaces the indication of location, supplying an explicit object of the gaze.
- *Lo Duca mio* ‘the leader my’ → “my Guide”: ‘leader’ is interpreted as “Guide”, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *Guarda, guarda!* ‘Look, look!’ → “Take care, take care!”: the commands to look are interpreted as warnings.
- *Mi trasse a sè* ‘me drew to himself’ → “drew me to him”: the object follows the verb, and the reflexive pronoun becomes the non-reflexive “him”.
- *stava* ‘stood’ → “was standing”: the Italian imperfect is rendered by the past progressive.

> 25 Allor mi volsi come l’ uom, cui tarda  
> 26 Di veder quel che gli convien fuggire,  
> 27 E cui paura subita sgagliarda,  
> 28 Che, per veder, non indugia il partire:  

Then I turned round, like one who longs to see what he must shun, and who is dashed with sudden fear, so that he puts not off his flight to look;

- *mi volsi* ‘I turned myself’ → “I turned round”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb with “round”.
- *come l’ uom* ‘like the man’ → “like one”: the definite noun becomes an indefinite pronoun.
- *cui tarda Di veder* ‘to whom it seems long to see’ → “who longs to see”: the Italian construction expressing impatient delay becomes a personal verb expressing desire; the person represented by the dative relative becomes the subject.
- *quel che* ‘that which’ → “what”: demonstrative and relative pronoun become a single fused relative.
- *gli convien* ‘it behoves him’ → “he must”: the impersonal construction with a dative pronoun becomes a personal construction with a modal verb.
- *fuggire* ‘flee’ → “shun”: flight is rendered as avoidance.
- *E cui paura subita sgagliarda* ‘and whom fear sudden weakens’ → “and who is dashed with sudden fear”: the active clause becomes passive, making the affected person its subject. ‘Weakens’ becomes the figurative “is dashed”, and the adjective moves before “fear”.
- *Che* ‘that’ → “so that”: “so” explicitly marks the clause as expressing a consequence.
- *per veder* ‘in order to see’ → “to look”: the purpose phrase moves from before the main verb to the end of the clause, and seeing becomes looking.
- *non indugia il partire* ‘does not delay the departing’ → “he puts not off his flight”: ‘delay’ becomes the phrasal verb “puts … off”, with the negative placed within it. The substantivized infinitive becomes “flight”, and the definite article becomes the possessive “his”.

## Lines 29–40 — A Demon Brings a Sinner

> 29 E vidi dietro a noi un Diavol nero  
> 30 Correndo su per lo scoglio venire.  

and behind us I saw a black Demon come running up the cliff.

- *vidi dietro a noi* ‘I saw behind us’ → “behind us I saw”: the phrase of place moves before the subject and verb.
- *un Diavol nero* ‘a Devil black’ → “a black Demon”: “Demon” replaces ‘Devil’, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Correndo su per lo scoglio venire* ‘running up along the rock to come’ → “come running up the cliff”: the infinitive moves before the participle and its accompanying phrase; English uses a bare infinitive after “saw”. *Su per* is compressed into “up”, and *scoglio* is rendered as “cliff”.

> 31 Ahi quanto egli era nell’ aspetto fiero!  

Ah, how ferocious was his aspect!

- *quanto egli era nell’ aspetto fiero* ‘how he was in aspect fierce’ → “how ferocious was his aspect”: the Italian makes ‘he’ the subject and qualifies him as fierce ‘in aspect’; the translation makes “his aspect” the subject. “Ferocious” replaces ‘fierce’, and the adjective moves to the front of the exclamation.

> 32 E quanto mi parea nell’ atto acerbo,  
> 33 Con l’ ale aperte, e sovra i piè leggiero!  

And how bitter he seemed to me in gesture, with his wings outspread, and light of foot!

- *quanto mi parea nell’ atto acerbo* ‘how to me he seemed in his action harsh’ → “how bitter he seemed to me in gesture”: the final adjective moves immediately after “how”, and ‘to me’ moves after the verb. *Acerbo* becomes “bitter” rather than ‘harsh’; *atto* becomes “gesture” rather than ‘action’, and its definite article is omitted.
- *Con l’ ale aperte* ‘with the wings open’ → “with his wings outspread”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and “outspread” gives a more specific rendering of ‘open’.
- *sovra i piè leggiero* ‘upon the feet light’ → “light of foot”: the adjective moves before the prepositional phrase. The English idiom replaces ‘upon’ with “of” and the definite plural ‘the feet’ with the articleless singular “foot”.

> 34 L’ omero suo, ch’ era acuto e superbo,  
> 35 Carcava un peccator con ambo l’ anche,  
> 36 Ed ei tenea de’ piè ghermito il nerbo.  

His shoulders that were sharp and high, a sinner with both haunches laded; and of each foot he held the sinew grasped.

- *L’ omero suo, ch’ era* ‘the shoulder his, which was’ → “His shoulders that were”: the singular shoulder becomes plural, with corresponding plural agreement in “were”; the possessive moves before the noun.
- *superbo* ‘proud’ → “high”: the translation expresses physical elevation rather than retaining the adjective’s suggestion of pride.
- *Carcava un peccator con ambo l’ anche* ‘loaded a sinner with both the haunches’ → “a sinner with both haunches laded”: *un peccator*, the subject of *carcava*, moves before the verb, which is placed after the accompanying phrase. “Laded” renders ‘loaded’, and the article before ‘haunches’ is omitted. The shoulders remain the fronted object.
- *de’ piè* ‘of the feet’ → “of each foot”: a definite plural becomes a distributive singular, and the phrase moves from after the verb to before the subject.
- *ghermito il nerbo* ‘grasped the sinew’ → “the sinew grasped”: the participle moves after the noun it qualifies.

> 37 Del nostro ponte, disse, o Malebranche,  

“Ye Malebranche of our bridge!” he said,

- *Del nostro ponte, disse, o Malebranche* ‘Of our bridge, he said, O Malebranche’ → ““Ye Malebranche of our bridge!” he said,”: the phrase ‘of our bridge’ moves from the beginning of the address to after “Malebranche”, and the reporting clause moves from within the address to after it. The vocative particle becomes an explicit second-person plural pronoun.

> 38 Ecco un degli Anzian di Santa Zita:  

“lo! one of Santa Zita’s Elders.

- *un degli Anzian di Santa Zita* ‘one of the Elders of Santa Zita’ → “one of Santa Zita’s Elders”: the postposed phrase with ‘of’ becomes a possessive before “Elders”, replacing the Italian definite-article construction.

> 39 Mettetel sotto, ch’ io torno per anche  
> 40 A quella terra che n’ è ben fornita.  

Thrust him under, while I return for others to that city which is well provided with them.

- *Mettetel sotto* ‘Put him underneath’ → “Thrust him under”: “Thrust” gives the command greater force than ‘put’.
- *ch’* ‘for’ → “while”: the explanatory or causal connection becomes a temporal one.
- *per anche* ‘for more’ → “for others”: “others” explicitly presents the implied additional objects as other individuals.
- *terra* ‘land; town’ → “city”: the translation selects the word’s urban sense rather than its territorial one.
- *che n’ è ben fornita* ‘which of them is well supplied’ → “which is well provided with them”: the preverbal pronoun *ne* becomes the phrase “with them” after the participle, using the preposition required by “provided”.

## Lines 41–48 — A Sinner Thrown Down and Mocked

> 41 Ogni uom v’ è barattier, fuor che Bonturo:  

Every man there is a barrator, except Bonturo:

- Literal.

> 42 Del no per li denar vi si fa ita.  

there they make ‘Ay’ of ‘No’ for money.”

- *Del no … vi si fa ita* ‘Of no … there yes is made’ → “there they make ‘Ay’ of ‘No’”: the passive or impersonal construction becomes an active construction with indefinite “they”; the place adverb moves to the beginning, and the material from which something is made follows the verb and its object.
- *per li denar* ‘for the moneys’ → “for money”: the article is omitted, and the plural noun becomes an English mass noun.

> 43 Laggiù il buttò, e per lo scoglio duro  
> 44 Si volse; e mai non fu mastino sciolto  
> 45 Con tanta fretta a seguitar lo furo.  

Down he threw him, then wheeled along the flinty cliff; and never was mastiff loosed with such a haste to follow thief.

- *Laggiù* ‘Down there’ → “Down”: the explicit indication of distance, ‘there’, is omitted.
- *il buttò* ‘him he threw’ → “he threw him”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *e* ‘and’ → “then”: coordination becomes an explicit indication of temporal sequence.
- *lo scoglio duro* ‘the cliff hard’ → “the flinty cliff”: the adjective precedes the noun, and general hardness becomes the more specific “flinty”.
- *Si volse* ‘he turned himself’ → “wheeled”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, with a more forceful verb; the verb also moves before the phrase describing his course.
- *Con tanta fretta* ‘with such haste’ → “with such a haste”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *a seguitar lo furo* ‘to follow the thief’ → “to follow thief”: the definite article is omitted.

> 46 Quei s’ attuffò, e tornò su convolto;  

The sinner plunged in, and came up again writhing convolved.

- *Quei* ‘That one’ → “The sinner”: a demonstrative pronoun is replaced by a noun phrase identifying its referent.
- *s’ attuffò* ‘plunged himself’ → “plunged in”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive, with “in” expressing the direction of the plunge.
- *tornò su* ‘returned up’ → “came up again”: the sense of returning is distributed between “came” and “again”.
- *convolto* ‘contorted’ → “writhing convolved”: the single participle is expanded into two words; “writhing” makes the contortion an explicit ongoing movement.

> 47 Ma i Demon, che del ponte avean coverchio,  
> 48 Gridar: Qui non ha luogo il Santo Volto;  

But the Demons, who were under cover of the bridge, cried: “Here the Sacred Face besteads not; here swim ye otherwise than in the Serchio.

- *che del ponte avean coverchio* ‘who of the bridge had cover’ → “who were under cover of the bridge”: possession of cover becomes a construction describing position beneath it, and the phrase identifying the source of cover moves after “cover”.
- *Qui non ha luogo il Santo Volto* ‘Here has no place the Holy Face’ → “Here the Sacred Face besteads not”: the subject moves before the verb; ‘has no place’ becomes “besteads not”, meaning affords no help; and ‘Holy’ is rendered “Sacred”. The continuation “here swim ye otherwise than in the Serchio.” has no counterpart in the supplied Italian: the English extends beyond line 48.

## Lines 49–63 — Threats and the Master’s Counsel

> 49 Qui si nuota altrimenti che nel Serchio:  
> 50 Però se tu non vuoi de’ nostri graffi,  
> 51 Non far sovra la pegola soverchio.  

Therefore, unless thou wishest to make trial of our drags, come not out above the pitch.”

- *Qui si nuota altrimenti che nel Serchio* ‘here one swims otherwise than in the Serchio’ → not rendered: the supplied English omits line 49 entirely.
- *se tu non* ‘if thou not’ → “unless thou”: the conditional conjunction and negation become a single conjunction.
- *vuoi de’ nostri graffi* ‘wantest some of our scratches’ → “wishest to make trial of our drags”: the partitive object becomes an infinitival phrase; “make trial of” is supplied, and the scratches are rendered as the instruments that inflict them.
- *Non far … soverchio* ‘do not make excess’ → “come not out”: the Italian expression is interpreted as a prohibition against emerging; the negative follows rather than precedes the main verb.

> 52 Poi l’ addentâr con più di cento raffi,  
> 53 Disser: Coverto convien che qui balli,  
> 54 Sì che, se puoi, nascosamente accaffi.  

Then they struck him with more than a hundred prongs, and said: “Covered thou must dance thee here; so that, if thou canst, thou mayest pilfer privately.”

- *l’ addentâr* ‘him they bit’ → “they struck him”: the figurative biting becomes striking, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *Disser* ‘they said’ → “and said”: a coordinating conjunction is supplied.
- *Coverto convien che qui balli* ‘covered it is necessary that here thou dance’ → “Covered thou must dance thee here”: the impersonal construction with a subordinate subjunctive becomes a personal construction with “must”; “here” moves after the verb, and the reflexive “thee” is supplied.
- *nascosamente accaffi* ‘secretly thou snatch’ → “thou mayest pilfer privately”: the subjunctive is expressed with “mayest,” the verb is interpreted specifically as stealing, and the adverb moves after the verb.

> 55 Non altrimenti i cuochi a’ lor vassalli  
> 56 Fanno attuffare in mezzo la caldaia  
> 57 La carne cogli uncin, perchè non galli.  

Not otherwise do the cooks make their vassals dip the flesh into the middle of the boiler with their hooks, to hinder it from floating.

- *Non altrimenti i cuochi a’ lor vassalli / Fanno attuffare* ‘not otherwise the cooks to their vassals make dip’ → “Not otherwise do the cooks make their vassals dip”: English supplies auxiliary “do” and inverts it with the subject after the initial negative phrase; the Italian causative’s prepositional complement becomes the direct object of “make” and moves after it.
- *in mezzo la caldaia / La carne* ‘into the middle of the boiler the flesh’ → “the flesh into the middle of the boiler”: the object moves before the phrase expressing direction.
- *cogli uncin* ‘with the hooks’ → “with their hooks”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *perchè non galli* ‘so that it may not float’ → “to hinder it from floating”: the negative purpose clause with a subjunctive becomes an infinitival phrase; its negation is expressed through “hinder,” and floating becomes a gerund after “from.”

> 58 Lo buon Maestro: Acciocchè non si paia  
> 59 Che tu ci sii, mi disse, giù t’ acquatta  
> 60 Dopo uno scheggio, che alcun schermo t’ haia;  

The kind Master said to me: “That it may not be seen that thou art here, cower down behind a jagg which has some screen for thee;

- *buon* ‘good’ → “kind”: the adjective is interpreted specifically as kindness.
- *Acciocchè non si paia / Che tu ci sii* ‘in order that it not appear that thou here be’ → “That it may not be seen that thou art here”: the impersonal construction becomes a passive of seeing; the first subjunctive is expressed with “may,” while *sii* becomes indicative “art,” and the locative moves after the verb.
- *mi disse* ‘to me he said’ → “said to me”: the reporting clause moves before the speech, with the Master as its explicit subject, and the pronoun complement follows the verb.
- *giù t’ acquatta* ‘down thyself crouch’ → “cower down”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive verb, and the directional adverb follows it.
- *che alcun schermo t’ haia* ‘which some screen for thee may have’ → “which has some screen for thee”: the subjunctive becomes indicative, the object follows the verb, and the pronoun complement moves to the end.

> 61 E per nulla offension, che a me sia fatta,  
> 62 Non temer tu, ch’ io ho le cose conte,  
> 63 Perch’ altra volta fui a tal baratta.  

and whatever outrage may be done to me, fear not thou; for I know these matters, having once before been in the like affray.”

- *E per nulla offension, che a me sia fatta* ‘and for no outrage that to me may be done’ → “and whatever outrage may be done to me”: the prepositional phrase and relative clause become a concessive clause introduced by “whatever”; the negative qualification is absorbed into that construction, and “to me” moves after the verb.
- *Non temer tu* ‘not fear thou’ → “fear not thou”: the negative moves after the imperative.
- *ch’ io ho le cose conte* ‘for I have the things known’ → “for I know these matters”: the construction with ‘have’ and an object complement becomes the simple verb “know”; the definite article becomes demonstrative “these.”
- *Perch’ altra volta fui a tal baratta* ‘because another time I was at such an affray’ → “having once before been in the like affray”: the finite causal clause becomes a perfect participial phrase, leaving the causal connection implicit; ‘another time’ becomes explicitly retrospective “once before,” and ‘at such an affray’ becomes “in the like affray.”

## Lines 64–76 — Virgil Confronts the Demons

> 64 Poscia passò di là dal co del ponte,  

Then he passed beyond the head of the bridge;

- *di là dal* ‘on the far side of the’ → “beyond the”: the Italian phrase is condensed into a single preposition and article.

> 65 E com’ ei giunse in su la ripa sesta,  
> 66 Mestier gli fu d’ aver sicura fronte.  

and when he arrived on the sixth bank, it was needful for him to have a stedfast front.

- *com’* ‘as’ → “when”: the temporal conjunction is rendered by a more explicit indication of time.
- *in su* ‘up on’ → “on”: the compound preposition is simplified.
- *la ripa sesta* ‘the bank sixth’ → “the sixth bank”: the ordinal moves before the noun.
- *Mestier gli fu d’ aver* ‘need for him was to have’ → “it was needful for him to have”: the noun expressing necessity becomes an adjective, with anticipatory “it”; the equivalent of *gli* follows rather than precedes the verb.
- *sicura fronte* ‘secure brow’ → “a stedfast front”: an indefinite article is supplied, and the physical expression is rendered as a phrase suggesting firmness.

> 67 Con quel furore, e con quella tempesta,  
> 68 Ch’ escono i cani addosso al poverello,  
> 69 Che di subito chiede ove s’ arresta;  

With that fury and that storm, wherewith the dogs rush forth upon the poor man who where he stops suddenly seeks alms,

- *e con quella tempesta* ‘and with that tempest’ → “and that storm”: the repeated preposition is omitted, and *tempesta* becomes “storm”.
- *Ch’ escono i cani* ‘with which come out the dogs’ → “wherewith the dogs rush forth”: the subject moves before the verb, and “rush forth” adds force and speed to ‘come out’.
- *poverello* ‘poor little man’ → “poor man”: the diminutive, with its suggestion of pity, is not separately represented.
- *chiede* ‘asks’ → “seeks alms”: the unspecified asking is interpreted as begging, and its object is supplied.
- *ove s’ arresta* ‘where he stops himself’ → “where he stops”: the reflexive becomes an English intransitive verb; the clause moves from after the main verb to before its adverb and verb.

> 70 Usciron quei di sotto al ponticello,  
> 71 E volser contra lui tutti i roncigli;  

rushed those Demons from beneath the bridge, and turned against him all their crooks.

- *Usciron* ‘came out’ → “rushed”: the neutral verb of emergence becomes a verb expressing rapid, forceful movement.
- *quei* ‘those’ → “those Demons”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a determiner, and its referent is supplied as a noun.
- *ponticello* ‘little bridge’ → “bridge”: the diminutive is not represented.
- *i roncigli* ‘the hooks’ → “their crooks”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the hooked implements are called “crooks”.

> 72 Ma ei gridò: Nessun di voi sia fello.  

But he cried: “Be none of ye outrageous.

- *Nessun di voi sia fello* ‘none of you be wicked’ → “Be none of ye outrageous”: the verb moves before the subject, and the third-person hortatory subjunctive becomes an English imperative construction; “outrageous” renders *fello* in terms of violent or offensive conduct rather than wickedness generally.

> 73 Innanzi che l’ uncin vostro mi pigli,  
> 74 Traggasi avanti l’ un di voi che m’ oda,  
> 75 E poi di roncigliarmi si consigli.  

Before ye touch me with your forks, let one of you come forth to hear me, and then take counsel about hooking me.”

- *Innanzi che l’ uncin vostro mi pigli* ‘before your hook seize me’ → “Before ye touch me with your forks”: the hook ceases to be the subject and becomes an instrument introduced by “with”, while its owners become the subject. The singular becomes plural, ‘hook’ becomes “forks”, and ‘seize’ becomes “touch”. The possessive moves before its noun, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Traggasi avanti l’ un di voi* ‘let the one of you draw himself forward’ → “let one of you come forth”: the reflexive movement becomes intransitive “come forth”, and the hortatory subjunctive is expressed through “let”. The subject, which follows the Italian verb, precedes “come”; the definite article before *un* is omitted.
- *che m’ oda* ‘who may hear me’ → “to hear me”: the relative clause with a subjunctive expressing purpose becomes an infinitive of purpose, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *di roncigliarmi si consigli* ‘about hooking me let him deliberate’ → “take counsel about hooking me”: the complement moves after the verb, and reflexive *consigliarsi* becomes “take counsel”. The hortatory force is carried by the earlier “let”, with “take” coordinated with “come”.

> 76 Tutti gridaron: Vada Malacoda;  

All cried: “Let Malacoda go.”

- *Vada Malacoda* ‘may Malacoda go’ → “Let Malacoda go”: the hortatory subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”, and the subject moves from after the Italian verb to before “go”.

## Lines 77–91 — Virgil Secures Safe Passage

> 77 Perch’ un si mosse, e gli altri stetter fermi;  
> 78 E venne a lui dicendo: Che gli approda?  

Thereat one moved himself, the others standing firm, and came to him, saying: “What will this avail him?”

- *Perch’* ‘Wherefore’ → “Thereat”: a connective expressing consequence becomes one expressing occasion.
- *e gli altri stetter fermi* ‘and the others stood firm’ → “the others standing firm”: the coordinating conjunction is omitted, and the finite past-tense clause becomes a participial absolute construction.
- *Che gli approda?* ‘What avails him?’ → “What will this avail him?”: the present becomes future, and “this” is supplied as the subject.

> 79 Credi tu, Malacoda, qui vedermi  
> 80 Esser venuto, disse il mio Maestro,  
> 81 Securo già da tutti i vostri schermi,  
> 82 Senza voler divino e fato destro?  

“Dost thou expect, Malacoda,” said my Master, “to find I have come here, secure already against all your weapons, without will Divine and fate propitious?

- *Credi tu* ‘Believe thou’ → “Dost thou expect”: believing becomes expecting, and English uses an auxiliary to form the question.
- *qui vedermi Esser venuto* ‘here to see me to have come’ → “to find I have come here”: seeing becomes finding; the dependent infinitive becomes a finite clause, with *mi* becoming its subject “I”; and “here” moves after “come”.
- *da tutti i vostri schermi* ‘from all your defenses’ → “against all your weapons”: “against” replaces ‘from’, and *schermi* is interpreted as weapons rather than defensive measures.

> 83 Lasciami andar, chè nel Cielo è voluto  
> 84 Ch’ io mostri altrui questo cammin silvestro.  

Let me pass on; for it is willed in Heaven that I shew another this savage way.”

- *Lasciami andar* ‘Let me go’ → “Let me pass on”: ‘go’ is expanded into “pass on”, and the attached pronoun becomes a separate word between the verbs.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *nel Cielo è voluto* ‘in Heaven it is willed’ → “it is willed in Heaven”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb.
- *altrui* ‘to another’ → “another”: the Italian dative pronoun becomes an English indirect object without a preposition.
- *questo cammin silvestro* ‘this way wooded’ → “this savage way”: *silvestro* is rendered as “savage” rather than ‘wooded’ or ‘sylvan’, and the adjective moves before its noun.

> 85 Allor gli fu l’ orgoglio sì caduto,  
> 86 Che si lasciò cascar l’ uncino ai piedi,  
> 87 E disse agli altri: Omai non sia feruto.  

Then was his pride so fallen, that he let the hook drop at his feet, and said to the others: “Now strike him not!”

- *gli … l’ orgoglio* ‘to him … the pride’ → “his pride”: the dative expressing the person affected becomes a possessive modifying the noun.
- *si … ai piedi* ‘to himself … at the feet’ → “at his feet”: the reflexive dative is absorbed into the possessive “his”.
- *lasciò cascar l’ uncino* ‘let fall the hook’ → “let the hook drop”: the noun moves before the infinitive, and ‘fall’ becomes “drop”.
- *Omai non sia feruto* ‘Now let him not be wounded’ → “Now strike him not!”: the passive third-person subjunctive becomes an active imperative addressed to the others; the person protected becomes the object “him”, and ‘wound’ becomes “strike”.

> 88 E il Duca mio a me: O tu, che siedi  
> 89 Tra gli scheggion del ponte quatto quatto,  
> 90 Sicuramente omai a me ti riedi.  

And my Guide to me: “O thou that sittest cowering, cowering amongst the great splinters of the bridge, securely now return to me!”

- *il Duca mio* ‘the Leader my’ → “my Guide”: “Guide” replaces ‘Leader’, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *quatto quatto* ‘crouched, crouched’ → “cowering, cowering”: the adjectives become participles suggesting fear, and the phrase moves before the words identifying the place.
- *a me ti riedi* ‘to me return yourself’ → “return to me”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb, and the reflexive *ti* is not separately represented.

> 91 Perch’ io mi mossi, ed a lui venni ratto;  

Whereat I moved, and quickly came to him;

- *Perch’* ‘Wherefore’ → “Whereat”: a connective expressing consequence becomes one expressing occasion.
- *io mi mossi* ‘I moved myself’ → “I moved”: the reflexive pronoun is omitted.
- *a lui venni ratto* ‘to him I came quickly’ → “quickly came to him”: the prepositional phrase moves after the verb, while the adverb moves before it.

## Lines 92–102 — The Devils Threaten Dante

> 92 E i Diavoli si fecer tutti avanti,  
> 93 Sì ch’ io temetti non tenesser patto.  

and the Devils all pressed forward, so that I feared they might not hold the compact.

- *si fecer tutti avanti* ‘made themselves all forward’ → “all pressed forward”: the reflexive expression becomes an intransitive verb of movement; “pressed” gives the movement greater force, and “all” moves before the verb.
- *non tenesser patto* ‘they should not keep pact’ → “they might not hold the compact”: the imperfect subjunctive is represented by “might”; keeping an agreement becomes holding it, and the definite article is supplied.

> 94 E così vid’ io già temer li fanti  
> 95 Ch’ uscivan patteggiati di Caprona,  
> 96 Veggendo sè tra nemici cotanti.  

And thus once I saw the footmen, who marched out under treaty from Caprona, fear at seeing themselves among so many enemies.

- *vid’ io già* ‘saw I formerly’ → “once I saw”: *già* is rendered as “once” and moved to the beginning of the clause; the subject moves before the verb.
- *temer li fanti* ‘fear the footmen’ → “the footmen” … “fear”: the subject of the infinitive moves before it; the relative clause consequently intervenes between “the footmen” and “fear”.
- *uscivan* ‘were going out’ → “marched out”: the imperfect becomes a simple past, and the general verb of departure becomes the more specific “marched”.
- *patteggiati* ‘having come to terms’ → “under treaty”: a participial description of the footmen becomes a prepositional phrase describing the conditions of their departure.
- *Veggendo* ‘seeing’ → “at seeing”: “at” is supplied to connect the act of seeing explicitly with their fear.
- *nemici cotanti* ‘enemies so many’ → “so many enemies”: the quantity expression moves before the noun.

> 97 Io m’ accostai con tutta la persona  
> 98 Lungo il mio Duca, e non torceva gli occhi  
> 99 Dalla sembianza lor, ch’ era non buona.  

I drew near my Guide with my whole body, and turned not away my eyes from the look of them, which was not good.

- *Io m’accostai* ‘I brought myself near’ → “I drew near”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive one.
- *con tutta la persona* ‘with the whole person’ → “with my whole body”: *persona* is rendered in its bodily sense, and the definite article becomes the possessive “my”.
- *Lungo il mio Duca* ‘alongside my Leader’ → “my Guide”: the spatial relation expressed by *Lungo* is absorbed into “drew near”; *Duca* becomes “Guide” rather than ‘Leader’. The phrase moves before “with my whole body”.
- *non torceva gli occhi* ‘was not turning the eyes’ → “turned not away my eyes”: the imperfect becomes a simple past; the negative moves after the verb, “away” makes the direction explicit, and the definite article becomes “my”.
- *sembianza lor* ‘look their’ → “look of them”: the postposed possessive becomes an “of” phrase.

> 100 Ei chinavan gli raffi, e: Vuoi ch’ io ’l tocchi,  
> 101 Diceva l’ un con l’ altro, in sul groppone?  
> 102 E rispondean: Sì, fa che gliele accocchi.  

They lowered their drag-hooks, and kept saying to one another: “Shall I touch him on the rump?” and answering: “Yes, see thou nick him.”

- *chinavan* ‘were lowering’ → “lowered”: the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *gli raffi* ‘the drag-hooks’ → “their drag-hooks”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Vuoi ch’ io ’l tocchi* ‘do you wish that I touch him’ → “Shall I touch him”: a question about the other speaker’s wishes, followed by a subjunctive clause, becomes a question with “Shall” proposing an action. The object pronoun moves from before the verb to after it.
- *Diceva l’un con l’altro* ‘said the one with the other’ → “kept saying to one another”: the imperfect is interpreted as repeated speech through “kept saying”. The reciprocal subject expression becomes “to one another”, with the speakers carried over as the subject from “They”; *con* becomes “to”. The reporting clause moves ahead of the question instead of interrupting it.
- *in sul* ‘in upon the’ → “on the”: the combined Italian prepositions become a single English preposition.
- *E rispondean* ‘and they were answering’ → “and answering”: the finite imperfect becomes a participle coordinated with “saying” and governed by “kept”.
- *fa che gliele accocchi* ‘make that you land it on him’ → “see thou nick him”: the imperative construction becomes the idiomatic “see”, without an expressed conjunction. *Accocchi*, meaning to land or deliver a blow, is interpreted more specifically as “nick”; the combined object pronoun, expressing both what is delivered and its recipient, is reduced to “him”, now the direct object.

## Lines 103–114 — The Broken Arch and Another Path

> 103 Ma quel Demonio, che tenea sermone  
> 104 Col Duca mio, si volse tutto presto  
> 105 E disse: Posa, posa, Scarmiglione.  

But that Demon, who had spoken with my Guide, turned instant round, and said: “Quiet, quiet, Scaramiglione!”

- *tenea sermone* ‘was holding discourse’ → “had spoken”: the phrase becomes a single verb, and the imperfect becomes a past perfect, presenting the speaking as anterior to the turning.
- *Duca mio* ‘duke my’ → “my Guide”: the title is rendered by the guiding function, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *si volse tutto presto* ‘turned himself very quickly’ → “turned instant round”: the reflexive construction becomes intransitive; “round” makes the direction explicit, while “instant” renders the speed as immediacy.
- *Posa, posa* ‘Rest, rest’ → “Quiet, quiet”: the verbal imperatives become adjectival commands.

> 106 Poi disse a noi: Più oltre andar per questo  
> 107 Scoglio non si potrà; perocchè giace  
> 108 Tutto spezzato al fondo l’ arco sesto:  

Then he said to us: “To go farther by this cliff will not be possible; for the sixth arch lies all in fragments at the bottom.

- *Più oltre andar* ‘farther to go’ → “To go farther”: the adverbial phrase moves after the infinitive.
- *non si potrà* ‘one will not be able’ → “will not be possible”: the impersonal construction with *potere* becomes a construction with “possible”, taking the preceding infinitive phrase as its subject.
- *perocchè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *giace Tutto spezzato al fondo l’ arco sesto* ‘lies all broken at the bottom the arch sixth’ → “the sixth arch lies all in fragments at the bottom”: the delayed subject moves before the verb, and the ordinal moves before its noun; the participle ‘broken’ becomes the prepositional phrase “in fragments”.

> 109 E se l’ andare avanti pur vi piace,  
> 110 Andatevene su per questa grotta:  
> 111 Presso è un altro scoglio che via face.  

And if it please you still to go onward, go along this cavern: near at hand is another cliff which forms a path.

- *E se l’ andare avanti pur vi piace* ‘And if the going onward still pleases you’ → “And if it please you still to go onward”: the nominal infinitive becomes a following infinitive clause, with anticipatory “it” supplied; the indicative becomes the English subjunctive “please”, and “still” moves after the verb and its object.
- *Andatevene su per* ‘Go yourselves away up along’ → “go along”: the pronominal verb becomes a plain imperative, leaving the departure expressed by *ne* and the upward direction expressed by *su* unstated.
- *grotta* ‘rocky bank’ → “cavern”: the translation takes the word in its cave sense rather than its contextual sense of a rocky bank or slope.
- *Presso* ‘near’ → “near at hand”: the single adverb is expanded into an adverbial phrase.
- *che via face* ‘which a way makes’ → “which forms a path”: the verb moves before its object; ‘makes a way’ becomes “forms a path”, with an indefinite article supplied before the noun.

> 112 Ier, più oltre cinqu’ ore che quest’ otta,  
> 113 Mille dugento con sessanta sei  
> 114 Anni compièr, che qui la via fu rotta.  

Yesterday, five hours later than this hour, a thousand two hundred and sixty-six years were fulfilled since the way here was broken.

- *più oltre cinqu’ ore* ‘farther on five hours’ → “five hours later”: the measure of time moves before the comparative, and ‘farther on’ is rendered by the explicitly temporal “later”.
- *con* ‘with’ → “and”: the preposition linking the parts of the number becomes a conjunction.
- *compièr* ‘completed’ → “were fulfilled”: the active, intransitive past tense becomes an English passive.
- *che* ‘that’ → “since”: the general connective becomes an explicitly temporal conjunction.
- *qui la via* ‘here the way’ → “the way here”: the adverb moves after the subject.

## Lines 115–123 — Choosing the Escort

> 115 Io mando verso là di questi miei  
> 116 A riguardar s’ alcun se ne sciorina:  

Thitherward I send some of these my men, to look if any one be out airing himself.

- *verso là* ‘towards there’ → “Thitherward”: the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb and moves before the subject and verb.
- *di questi miei* ‘of these mine’ → “some of these my men”: “some” makes the partitive construction explicit, and “men” supplies a noun for the substantival possessive.
- *se ne sciorina* ‘airs himself out of it’ → “be out airing himself”: the simple present indicative becomes a subjunctive construction with “be” and a participle; the source expressed by *ne* is left implicit in “out”.

> 117 Gite con lor, ch’ ei non saranno rei.  

Go with them; for they will not be treacherous.

- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *rei* ‘wicked’ → “treacherous”: the general description of wickedness is narrowed to untrustworthiness.

> 118 Tratti avanti, Alichino e Calcabrina,  
> 119 Cominciò egli a dire, e tu, Cagnazzo,  
> 120 E Barbariccia guidi la decina.  

“Draw forward, Alichino and Calcabrina,” he then began to say, “and thou, Cagnazzo; and let Barbariccia lead the ten.

- *Tratti avanti* ‘draw yourself forward’ → “Draw forward”: the reflexive construction becomes an intransitive English imperative, without a reflexive pronoun.
- *Cominciò egli a dire* ‘began he to say’ → “he then began to say”: the subject moves before the verb, and “then” is supplied.
- *E Barbariccia guidi la decina* ‘and Barbariccia lead the group of ten’ → “and let Barbariccia lead the ten”: the exhortative subjunctive is expressed with “let”, and the collective noun becomes the substantival numeral “ten”.

> 121 Libicocco vegna oltre, e Draghignazzo,  
> 122 Ciriatto sannuto, e Graffiacane,  
> 123 E Farfarello, e Rubicante pazzo.  

Let Libicocco come besides, and Draghignazzo, tusked Ciriatto, and Graffiacane, and Farfarello, and furious Rubicante. Search around the boiling glue.

- *Libicocco vegna oltre* ‘Libicocco come forward’ → “Let Libicocco come besides”: the exhortative subjunctive is expressed with “Let”; “besides” interprets *oltre* as addition rather than forward movement.
- *Ciriatto sannuto* ‘Ciriatto tusked’ → “tusked Ciriatto”: the adjective moves before the name.
- *Rubicante pazzo* ‘Rubicante mad’ → “furious Rubicante”: “furious” substitutes rage or frenzy for madness, and the adjective moves before the name.
- *∅* ‘no corresponding Italian words’ → “Search around the boiling glue.”: this entire sentence is additional to the supplied Italian; it has no counterpart in lines 115–123.

## Lines 124–135 — Fear of the Escort

> 124 Cercate intorno le bollenti pane;  
> 125 Costor sien salvi insino all’ altro scheggio,  
> 126 Che tutto intero va sovra le tane.  

Be these two guided safe to the other crag, which all unbroken goes across the dens.”

- *Cercate intorno le bollenti pane; Costor sien salvi* ‘Search around the boiling pitches; let these be safe’ → “Be these two guided safe”: the command in line 124 is omitted entirely from the supplied English. In line 125, “two” and “guided” are supplied; the wish expressed by *sien salvi* becomes a passive command, with the subject following “Be”.
- *insino all’ altro scheggio* ‘as far as the other crag’ → “to the other crag”: the explicit limit expressed by *insino* is reduced to the destination marked by “to”.
- *tutto intero* ‘all whole’ → “all unbroken”: *intero* is rendered specifically as physically unbroken.
- *sovra* ‘over’ → “across”: the preposition expresses spanning rather than position above.

> 127 O me! Maestro, che è quel che io veggio?  
> 128 Diss’ io: deh senza scorta andiamci soli,  
> 129 Se tu sa’ ir, ch’ io per me non la cheggio.  

“Oh me! Master, what is this that I see?” said I: “Ah, without escort let us go alone, if thou knowest the way; for as to me, I seek it not!

- *quel* ‘that’ → “this”: the demonstrative changes from distant to near.
- *deh* ‘pray’ → “Ah”: an expression of entreaty becomes an exclamation.
- *andiamci* ‘let us go from here’ → “let us go”: the locative force of the enclitic *ci* is not explicitly rendered.
- *Se tu sa’ ir* ‘if thou knowest how to go’ → “if thou knowest the way”: the infinitive *ir*, dependent on *sa’*, is replaced by the noun phrase “the way”.
- *ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *io per me* ‘I, for myself’ → “as to me, I”: the qualifying phrase is moved before the subject and recast as a statement of personal standpoint.
- *non la cheggio* ‘do not ask for it’ → “seek it not”: asking becomes seeking, and the negative follows the verb and object rather than preceding them.

> 130 Se tu sei sì accorto come suoli,  
> 131 Non vedi tu ch’ ei digrignan li denti,  
> 132 E con le ciglia ne minaccian duoli?  

If thou beest so wary, as thou art wont, dost thou not see how they grin their teeth, and with their brows threaten mischief to us?”

- *suoli* ‘thou customarily dost’ → “thou art wont”: the finite verb expressing habitual action becomes a construction with “art” and the adjective “wont”.
- *Non vedi tu* ‘seest thou not’ → “dost thou not see”: English introduces the auxiliary “dost” to form the question, leaving “see” in the infinitive.
- *ch’* ‘that’ → “how”: the conjunction introducing what is seen instead introduces its manner.
- *digrignan* ‘gnash’ → “grin”: the action is rendered as grinning rather than gnashing.
- *li denti* ‘the teeth’ → “their teeth”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *le ciglia* ‘the eyebrows’ → “their brows”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *ne minaccian duoli* ‘to us they threaten pains’ → “threaten mischief to us”: the preceding pronoun *ne* becomes the following prepositional phrase “to us”; plural ‘pains’ becomes the mass noun “mischief”.

> 133 Ed egli a me: Non vo’ che tu paventi:  
> 134 Lasciali digrignar pure a lor senno,  
> 135 Ch’ ei fanno ciò per li lessi dolenti.  

And he to me: “I would not have thee be afraid. Let them grin on at their will; for they do it at the boiled wretches.”

- *Non vo’ che tu paventi* ‘I do not wish that thou fear’ → “I would not have thee be afraid”: the present-tense expression of wishing becomes “would not have”; the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object followed by a bare infinitive, and the verb of fearing becomes “be afraid”.
- *digrignar pure* ‘just gnash’ → “grin on”: gnashing becomes grinning, while the permissive emphasis of *pure* is conveyed through the suggestion of continuation in “on”.
- *a lor senno* ‘according to their judgment’ → “at their will”: judgment or discretion is rendered as personal will.
- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *ciò* ‘this’ → “it”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *per* ‘for’ → “at”: the preposition explicitly marks the targets of the action.
- *li lessi dolenti* ‘the suffering boiled ones’ → “the boiled wretches”: the substantivized *lessi* becomes the adjective “boiled”, while *dolenti* is represented by the noun “wretches” rather than an adjective expressing suffering.

## Lines 136–139 — Signals and a Trumpet

> 136 Per l’ argine sinistro volta dienno;  

By the sinister bank they turned;

- *Per l’ argine sinistro* ‘along the bank left’ → “By the sinister bank”: “By” replaces ‘along’; “sinister” preserves the Italian adjective’s form and its sense ‘left’, and precedes rather than follows the noun.
- *volta dienno* ‘a turn they gave’ → “they turned”: the noun-and-verb expression becomes a single verb.

> 137 Ma prima avea ciascun la lingua stretta  
> 138 Co’ denti verso lor duca per cenno:  

but first, each of them had pressed his tongue between the teeth, toward their Captain as a signal;

- *avea ciascun la lingua stretta* ‘had each the tongue pressed’ → “each of them had pressed his tongue”: the subject moves before the auxiliary, and the participle moves before the object; “of them” is supplied, and the definite article becomes the possessive “his”.
- *Co’ denti* ‘with the teeth’ → “between the teeth”: an instrumental expression becomes one of position.
- *duca* ‘leader’ → “Captain”: a more specifically military title replaces the general term.
- *per cenno* ‘for a sign’ → “as a signal”: the expression of purpose becomes an expression of function.

> 139 Ed egli avea del cul fatto trombetta.  

and he of his —— had made a trumpet.

- *del cul* ‘of the arse’ → “of his ——”: the explicit noun is suppressed and represented by dashes; the definite article becomes “his”, and the phrase moves before the auxiliary.
- *trombetta* ‘little trumpet’ → “a trumpet”: the diminutive force is omitted, and an indefinite article is supplied.

## Summary — Close Echoes and English Recasting

Carlyle often follows the Italian closely in vocabulary, repetition, and emphasis, but freely rearranges its syntax. Delayed subjects and objects move into more usual English positions, adjectives generally precede their nouns, and reporting clauses sometimes move ahead of the speech they introduce. Yet the translation also preserves conspicuous inversions and repetitions: “It I saw”, “art Divine”, and “cowering, cowering” retain something of the Italian’s emphatic or unusual arrangement rather than consistently smoothing it into ordinary prose.

Changes of construction recur throughout. Participles and infinitives become noun phrases: *altro parlando* becomes “with other talk”, while *gonfiar … e riseder* becomes “the heaving and compressed subsiding”. Impersonal expressions become personal constructions, as *convien che … balli* becomes “thou must dance thee”; reflexive verbs frequently become English intransitives. Active and passive constructions also exchange places: *non sia feruto*, ‘let him not be wounded’, becomes “strike him not!” Italian imperfects receive different treatments according to context, including simple pasts, progressives, and the explicitly iterative “kept saying”. Connectives occasionally shift the relation between clauses, notably when explanatory *ch’* becomes temporal “while”.

Vocabulary ranges from close equivalents to interpretive substitutions. “ships” identifies the referent of *legni*, ‘timbers’, while “Take care” interprets *Guarda* as a warning rather than simply ‘look’. Other choices alter emphasis: *superbo*, ‘proud’, becomes “high”, and *rei*, ‘wicked’, becomes the more specific “treacherous”. Carlyle supplies clarifying nouns and complements, including “Demons” for *quei* and “alms” with *chiede*. Conversely, repetition implied by some Italian verbs and diminutive force in words such as *ponticello* and *trombetta* are not separately expressed. The strongest suppression is *cul*, replaced by “——”. The apparent omissions and additions at lines 49 and 124 are instead shifts across the supplied block boundaries: both commands are translated in the preceding English blocks.

More routinely, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian verb endings alone identify the subject. Italian articles before possessives disappear in constructions such as *il mio Duca*, rendered “my Guide”; articles attached to body parts also often become possessives. Such regular grammatical adjustments accompany a translation that remains recognizably close to the Italian’s sequence and phrasing, even when it expands, interprets, or reconstructs individual expressions.
