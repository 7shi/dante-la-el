# Inferno 7

## Lines 1–10 — Virgil Silences Plutus

> 1 Pape Satan, pape Satan aleppe,  
> 2 Cominciò Pluto colla voce chioccia:  

“Papè Satan! papè Satan, aleppè!” began Plutus, with clucking voice.

- *colla voce chioccia* ‘with the hoarse voice’ → “with clucking voice”: the definite article is omitted, the adjective precedes the noun, and “clucking” gives an animal-like quality to the Italian description of a harsh, cracked voice.

> 3 E quel Savio gentil, che tutto seppe,  
> 4 Disse per confortarmi: Non ti noccia  
> 5 La tua paura, chè, poder ch’ egli abbia,  
> 6 Non ci terrà lo scender questa roccia.  

And that gentle Sage, who knew all, said, comforting me: “Let not thy fear hurt thee; for, whatever power he have, he shall not hinder us from descending this rock.”

- *Savio gentil* ‘Sage gentle’ → “gentle Sage”: the adjective is moved before the noun.
- *che tutto seppe* ‘who all knew’ → “who knew all”: the object follows the verb rather than preceding it.
- *per confortarmi* ‘to comfort me’ → “comforting me”: a purpose construction becomes a participial phrase describing the action accompanying his speech.
- *Non ti noccia La tua paura* ‘may thy fear not hurt thee’ → “Let not thy fear hurt thee”: the independent subjunctive becomes a construction with “Let”; the subject moves before the verb expressing harm, and the object pronoun follows it.
- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *poder ch’ egli abbia* ‘power that he may have’ → “whatever power he have”: the Italian concessive construction is recast with “whatever”; the subjunctive is retained in “have”.
- *Non ci terrà lo scender questa roccia* ‘will not withhold from us the descending this rock’ → “he shall not hinder us from descending this rock”: the construction with the substantivized infinitive *lo scender* becomes “hinder us from descending”; what is withheld becomes the action from which someone is hindered.

> 7 Poi si rivolse a quella enfiata labbia,  
> 8 E disse: Taci, maledetto lupo:  
> 9 Consuma dentro te con la tua rabbia.  

Then he turned himself to that inflated visage, and said: “Peace, cursed Wolf! Consume thyself internally with thy greedy rage.

- *Taci* ‘be silent’ → “Peace”: a verbal imperative becomes a noun used as a command.
- *Consuma dentro te* ‘waste away within thyself’ → “Consume thyself internally”: the intransitive imperative becomes a transitive verb with a reflexive object, while the prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *rabbia* ‘rage’ → “greedy rage”: “greedy” is supplied, narrowing the meaning of the Italian noun.

> 10 Non è senza cagion l’ andare al cupo:  

Not without cause is our journey to the deep.

- *Non è senza cagion* ‘is not without cause’ → “Not without cause is”: the negated prepositional phrase is moved before the verb.
- *l’ andare* ‘the going’ → “our journey”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun, and the definite article is replaced by a possessive identifying whose journey it is.

## Lines 11–23 — The Monster Falls and the Descent Continues

> 11 Vuolsi così nell’ alto, ove Michele  
> 12 Fe’ la vendetta del superbo strupo.  

Thus it is willed on high, where Michael took vengeance of the proud adultery.”

- *Vuolsi così* ‘it is willed thus’ → “Thus it is willed”: the adverb moves before the passive construction.
- *nell’ alto* ‘in the height’ → “on high”: the substantivized adjective becomes an English adverbial phrase.
- *Fe’ la vendetta* ‘made the vengeance’ → “took vengeance”: English substitutes its idiomatic verb and omits the definite article.
- *strupo* ‘violation’ → “adultery”: Carlyle chooses a specifically sexual interpretation of the noun.

> 13 Quali dal vento le gonfiate vele  
> 14 Caggiono avvolte, poichè l’ alber fiacca;  
> 15 Tal cadde a terra la fiera crudele.  

As sails, swelled by the wind, fall entangled when the mast breaks; so fell that cruel monster to the ground.

- *Quali … Tal* ‘such as … such’ → “As” … “so”: the Italian adjectival correlatives become English adverbial correlatives.
- *dal vento le gonfiate vele* ‘by the wind the swollen sails’ → “sails, swelled by the wind”: the definite article disappears, and the participle and its agent follow the noun rather than precede it.
- *Caggiono avvolte* ‘fall wrapped’ → “fall entangled”: the participle is interpreted as describing entanglement rather than simply wrapping.
- *poichè* ‘after’ → “when”: the temporal conjunction becomes a less explicitly sequential one.
- *cadde a terra la fiera crudele* ‘fell to earth the beast cruel’ → “fell that cruel monster to the ground”: the directional phrase moves after the subject, the adjective moves before its noun, “that” replaces the definite article, and “monster” gives a stronger characterization than ‘beast’.

> 16 Così scendemmo nella quarta lacca,  
> 17 Prendendo più della dolente ripa,  
> 18 Che il mal dell’ universo tutto insacca.  

Then we descended into the fourth concavity, taking in more of the dismal bank, which shuts up all the evil of the universe.

- *Così* ‘thus’ → “Then”: a connection of manner or consequence becomes one of temporal sequence.
- *Prendendo* ‘taking’ → “taking in”: the added particle makes an English phrasal verb.
- *dolente ripa* ‘sorrowful bank’ → “dismal bank”: the adjective precedes its noun, and an adjective suggesting sorrow becomes one describing a gloomy quality.
- *Che il mal dell’ universo tutto insacca* ‘which the evil of the universe all packs into a sack’ → “which shuts up all the evil of the universe”: the verb moves before its object, “all” moves before the noun phrase it quantifies, and the specific image of packing into a sack becomes the more general “shuts up”.

> 19 Ahi giustizia di Dio! tante chi stipa  
> 20 Nuove travaglie e pene, quante io viddi?  

Ah, Justice Divine! Who shall tell in few the many fresh pains and travails that I saw?

- *di Dio* ‘of God’ → “Divine”: a prepositional phrase containing a noun becomes an adjective.
- *chi stipa* ‘who packs’ → “Who shall tell in few”: Carlyle interprets packing as compression into words, rather than physical accumulation; the present tense becomes a construction with “shall”.
- *tante … quante* ‘as many … as’ → “the many” … “that”: the quantitative correlation becomes a definite noun phrase followed by a relative clause. The opening quantitative element also moves after the interrogative subject and verb.
- *Nuove travaglie e pene* ‘new travails and pains’ → “fresh pains and travails”: “fresh” renders ‘new’, and the two coordinated nouns exchange places.

> 21 E perchè nostra colpa sì ne scipa?  

And why does guilt of ours thus waste us?

- *nostra colpa* ‘our guilt’ → “guilt of ours”: the possessive adjective becomes a postposed phrase with an independent possessive pronoun.
- *perchè … ne scipa* ‘why … us ruins’ → “why does” … “waste us”: English introduces interrogative “does” and uses the main verb in its uninflected form; the object pronoun follows that verb instead of preceding it. “Waste” renders the destructive sense of *scipa*.

> 22 Come fa l’ onda là sovra Cariddi,  
> 23 Che si frange con quella in cui s’ intoppa;  

As does the surge, there above Charybdis, that breaks itself against the surge wherewith it meets;

- *con* ‘with’ → “against”: the preposition is interpreted as expressing collision.
- *quella* ‘that one’ → “the surge”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a definite noun phrase repeating the antecedent.
- *in cui s’ intoppa* ‘into which it bumps’ → “wherewith it meets”: “wherewith” expresses ‘with which’ rather than ‘into which’, and the Italian pronominal verb becomes the nonpronominal “meets”.

## Lines 24–35 — Rolling Weights Around the Circle

> 24 Così convien che qui la gente riddi.  

so have the people here to counter-dance.

- *Così convien che qui la gente riddi* ‘thus it is necessary that here the people dance in a circle’ → “so have the people here to counter-dance.”: the impersonal construction with a subordinate clause becomes “the people” with “have … to”; the singular collective *gente* becomes plural, and “here” follows rather than precedes it. “counter-dance” gives the dancing an explicitly opposed movement.

> 25 Qui vidi gente più che altrove troppa,  
> 26 E d’ una parte e d’ altra, con grandi urli,  
> 27 Voltando pesi per forza di poppa:  

Here saw I too many more than elsewhere, both on the one side and on the other, with loud howlings, rolling weights by force of chest.

- *gente più che altrove troppa* ‘people, more than elsewhere, too numerous’ → “too many more than elsewhere”: the noun *gente* is absorbed into the substantival “many,” and the expression of excess moves before the comparison.
- *E d’ una parte e d’ altra* ‘and on one side and on the other’ → “both on the one side and on the other”: the paired conjunctions become “both … and,” and “the” is supplied before “one side.”
- *con grandi urli* ‘with great howls’ → “with loud howlings”: ‘great’ is interpreted specifically as loudness, and “howlings” replaces the count noun ‘howls.’
- *Voltando pesi* ‘turning weights’ → “rolling weights”: “rolling” specifies the manner of turning.
- *per forza di poppa* ‘by force of stern’ → “by force of chest”: the nautical word *poppa* is rendered as the bodily part it figuratively designates.

> 28 Percotevansi incontro, e poscia pur lì  
> 29 Si rivolgea ciascun, voltando a retro,  
> 30 Gridando: Perchè tieni? e perchè burli?  

They smote against each other, and then each wheeled round just there, rolling aback, shouting, “Why holdest thou?” and “Why throwest thou away?”

- *Percotevansi incontro* ‘they struck one another head-on’ → “They smote against each other”: the reciprocal pronoun becomes the complement of “against,” which renders *incontro*, rather than the direct object of the verb.
- *e poscia pur lì Si rivolgea ciascun* ‘and then just there turned back each’ → “and then each wheeled round just there”: “each” moves before the verb, and “just there” moves after it; the reflexive verb becomes the intransitive phrase “wheeled round.”
- *voltando a retro* ‘turning backward’ → “rolling aback”: “rolling” again specifies the manner of turning.
- *e perchè burli* ‘and why do you squander?’ → “and “Why throwest thou away?””: the single verb *burli* becomes the phrasal verb “throwest … away.”

> 31 Così tornavan per lo cerchio tetro,  
> 32 Da ogni mano all’ opposito punto,  
> 33 Gridando sempre in loro ontoso metro.  

Thus they returned along the gloomy circle, on either hand, to the opposite point, shouting always in their reproachful measure.

- *per lo cerchio tetro* ‘through the circle gloomy’ → “along the gloomy circle”: “along” presents the circle as the course followed, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Da ogni mano* ‘from every hand’ → “on either hand”: ‘from’ becomes “on,” and ‘every’ becomes “either,” expressing the two-sided distribution.

> 34 Poi si volgea ciascun, quand’ era giunto,  
> 35 Per lo suo mezzo cerchio, all’ altra giostra.  

Then every one, when he had reached it, turned through his half-circle towards the other joust.

- *si volgea ciascun* ‘turned each’ → “every one” … “turned”: ‘each’ is expanded to “every one” and moved before the verb; the temporal clause consequently separates subject and verb. The reflexive construction becomes intransitive “turned.”
- *quand’ era giunto* ‘when he had arrived’ → “when he had reached it”: the intransitive verb of arrival becomes transitive “reached,” with “it” supplied as its object.
- *all’ altra giostra* ‘to the other joust’ → “towards the other joust”: “towards” replaces ‘to,’ emphasizing direction rather than destination.

## Lines 36–48 — The Avaricious Clergy

> 36 Ed io che avea lo cor quasi compunto,  
> 37 Dissi: Maestro mio, or mi dimostra  
> 38 Che gente è questa; e se tutti fur cherci  
> 39 Questi chercuti alla sinistra nostra.  

And I, who felt my heart as it were stung, said: “My Master, now shew me what people these are; and whether all those tonsured on our left were of the clergy.”

- *avea lo cor quasi compunto* ‘had the heart almost pricked’ → “felt my heart as it were stung”: having the heart in a particular condition becomes feeling it so; the definite article becomes a possessive; “as it were” expresses qualification rather than the degree conveyed by ‘almost’; ‘pricked’ becomes “stung”.
- *Maestro mio* ‘Master my’ → “My Master”: the possessive precedes the noun.
- *or mi dimostra* ‘now to me show’ → “now shew me”: the object pronoun follows the imperative.
- *Che gente è questa* ‘what people is this’ → “what people these are”: the singular construction with collective *gente* becomes plural, and the subject precedes the verb in the English indirect question.
- *tutti fur cherci Questi chercuti alla sinistra nostra* ‘all were clerics, these tonsured ones at the left of ours’ → “all those tonsured on our left were of the clergy”: the postposed subject moves before the verb and joins “all”; ‘these’ becomes “those”; ‘clerics’ becomes the phrase “of the clergy”; and the expression of position becomes “on our left”, with the possessive placed before the noun.

> 40 Ed egli a me: Tutti quanti fur guerci  
> 41 Sì della mente in la vita primaia,  
> 42 Che con misura nullo spendio ferci.  

And he to me: “In their first life, all were so squinteyed in mind, that they made no expenditure in it with moderation.

- *Tutti quanti* ‘all of them’ → “all”: the emphatic combination is reduced to a single word.
- *guerci Sì della mente* ‘squinteyed so of the mind’ → “so squinteyed in mind”: the degree word moves before the adjective, and ‘of the mind’ becomes “in mind”.
- *in la vita primaia* ‘in the first life’ → “In their first life”: the phrase moves to the beginning of the speech, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *con misura* ‘with measure’ → “with moderation”: the noun is rendered by a word specifying restraint, and the phrase moves from before the object to the end of the clause.
- *nullo spendio ferci* ‘no expenditure they made there’ → “they made no expenditure in it”: the object moves after the verb; the enclitic *ci*, ‘there’, becomes “in it”, referring back to life.

> 43 Assai la voce lor chiaro l’ abbaia,  
> 44 Quando vengono a’ duo punti del cerchio,  
> 45 Ove colpa contraria li dispaia.  

Most clearly do their voices bark out this, when they come to the two points of the circle, where contrary guilt divides them.

- *Assai la voce lor chiaro l’ abbaia* ‘very much their voice clearly barks it’ → “Most clearly do their voices bark out this”: the separated expressions *Assai* and *chiaro* are combined and placed first as “Most clearly”; the adverbially used adjective *chiaro* becomes an English adverb. The singular ‘voice’ becomes plural, and the postposed possessive moves before its noun. English supplies the emphatic auxiliary “do” before the subject; ‘barks’ becomes “bark out”; and the object pronoun moves after the verb and becomes the demonstrative “this”.
- *colpa contraria* ‘guilt contrary’ → “contrary guilt”: the adjective precedes the noun.
- *li dispaia* ‘them unpairs’ → “divides them”: the verb’s specific sense of separating a pair becomes the more general “divides”, and the object pronoun follows the verb.

> 46 Questi fur Cherci, che non han coperchio  
> 47 Piloso al capo, e Papi e Cardinali,  
> 48 In cui usa avarizia il suo soperchio.  

These were Priests, that have not hairy covering on their heads, and Popes and Cardinals, in whom avarice does its utmost.”

- *Cherci* ‘clerics’ → “Priests”: the translation uses a narrower designation.
- *coperchio Piloso al capo* ‘covering hairy on the head’ → “hairy covering on their heads”: the adjective moves before the noun; the singular ‘head’ becomes plural, and its definite article becomes a possessive.
- *usa avarizia il suo soperchio* ‘exercises avarice its excess’ → “avarice does its utmost”: the subject moves before the verb. The construction ‘exercises its excess’ becomes the idiom “does its utmost”, interpreting *soperchio* as avarice’s fullest exertion rather than directly retaining the word’s sense of excess.

## Lines 49–63 — The Unrecognisable Souls and Fortune’s Goods

> 49 Ed io: Maestro, tra questi cotali  
> 50 Dovrei io ben riconoscere alcuni,  
> 51 Che furo immondi di cotesti mali.  

And I: “Master, among this set, I surely ought to recognise some that were defiled by these evils.”

- *tra questi cotali* ‘among such ones as these’ → “among this set”: the plural demonstrative and qualifying word become a singular collective noun.
- *Dovrei io ben riconoscere alcuni* ‘ought I indeed to recognise some’ → “I surely ought to recognise some”: the subject moves before the modal verb, and the emphatic adverb moves from after it to before it.
- *Che furo immondi* ‘who were unclean’ → “that were defiled”: an adjective describing a condition becomes a passive participle suggesting the action that produced it.
- *di cotesti mali* ‘with those evils’ → “by these evils”: the preposition changes to suit the passive construction, and the demonstrative associated with the person addressed becomes “these”.

> 52 Ed egli a me: Vani pensieri aduni:  

And he to me: “Vain thoughts combinest thou:

- *Vani pensieri aduni* ‘vain thoughts you gather’ → “Vain thoughts combinest thou”: gathering becomes combining; the supplied subject follows the verb, while the object retains its initial position.

> 53 La sconoscente vita, che i fe’ sozzi,  
> 54 Ad ogni conoscenza or li fa bruni.  

their undiscerning life, which made them sordid, now makes them too obscure for any recognition.

- *La sconoscente vita* ‘the undiscerning life’ → “their undiscerning life”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *che i fe’ sozzi* ‘which them made sordid’ → “which made them sordid”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Ad ogni conoscenza or li fa bruni* ‘to every recognition now them makes dark’ → “now makes them too obscure for any recognition”: the initial prepositional phrase moves to the end, and the object pronoun moves after the verb. Darkness becomes obscurity; “too” is supplied to express recognition’s impossibility, and ‘every’ becomes “any” within that construction.

> 55 In eterno verranno agli due cozzi:  

To all eternity they shall continue butting one another.

- *In eterno* ‘for eternity’ → “To all eternity”: “all” is added for emphasis.
- *verranno agli due cozzi* ‘they will come to the two collisions’ → “they shall continue butting one another”: coming to collisions becomes continuing an action. The noun becomes a participle, ‘two’ is omitted, and the reciprocal object “one another” is supplied.

> 56 Questi risurgeranno del sepulcro  
> 57 Col pugno chiuso, e questi co’ crin mozzi.  

These shall arise from their graves with closed fists; and these with wasted hair.

- *Questi risurgeranno* ‘these will rise again’ → “These shall arise”: the sense of rising again is left implicit in “arise”.
- *del sepulcro* ‘from the tomb’ → “from their graves”: the singular becomes plural, and the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *Col pugno chiuso* ‘with the fist closed’ → “with closed fists”: the singular becomes plural, the article disappears, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *co’ crin mozzi* ‘with the hairs shorn’ → “with wasted hair”: the plural noun becomes an English mass noun, without the article; the adjective moves before it. “Wasted” renders the hair as depleted rather than explicitly cut short.

> 58 Mal dare, e mal tener lo mondo pulcro  
> 59 Ha tolto loro, e posti a questa zuffa:  
> 60 Qual ella sia, parole non ci appulcro.  

Ill-giving, and ill-keeping, has deprived them of the bright world, and put them to this conflict: what a conflict it is, I adorn no words to tell.

- *Mal dare, e mal tener* ‘badly to give, and badly to keep’ → “Ill-giving, and ill-keeping”: substantival infinitives qualified by adverbs become compound verbal nouns.
- *lo mondo pulcro Ha tolto loro* ‘the beautiful world has taken from them’ → “has deprived them of the bright world”: the verb precedes its complements rather than following its direct object. Taking something from someone becomes depriving someone of something, changing both complements’ grammatical roles. The adjective moves before the noun, and beauty becomes brightness.
- *e posti a questa zuffa* ‘and placed at this brawl’ → “and put them to this conflict”: the object “them”, understood in the Italian, is expressed; “conflict” is a more general term than ‘brawl’.
- *Qual ella sia* ‘of what kind it may be’ → “what a conflict it is”: the pronoun is replaced by the repeated noun “conflict”, and the subjunctive becomes indicative. The expression of kind or character becomes an exclamatory construction.
- *parole non ci appulcro* ‘words I do not embellish about it’ → “I adorn no words to tell”: the object moves after the verb, and verbal negation becomes the determiner “no”. The pronominal adverb referring to the matter becomes the explanatory infinitive “to tell”.

> 61 Or puoi, figliuol, veder la corta buffa  
> 62 De’ ben, che son commessi alla Fortuna,  
> 63 Per che l’ umana gente si rabbuffa.  

But thou, my Son, mayest see the brief mockery of the goods that are committed unto Fortune, for which the human kind contend with one another.

- *Or* ‘now’ → “But”: a temporal or transitional marker becomes an adversative conjunction.
- *puoi, figliuol, veder* ‘you can, son, see’ → “thou, my Son, mayest see”: the modal follows rather than precedes the address, “my” is supplied, and ability is expressed through “mayest” rather than ‘can’.
- *l’ umana gente* ‘the human people’ → “the human kind”: the collective noun for people becomes a designation of humankind.
- *si rabbuffa* ‘scuffles’ → “contend with one another”: physical scuffling becomes the more general action of contending. The pronominal construction is explicitly reciprocal, and the singular verb becomes plural in agreement with the collective sense of its English subject.

## Lines 64–74 — The Question of Fortune

> 64 Chè tutto l’ oro, ch’ è sotto la luna,  
> 65 E che già fu, di queste anime stanche  
> 66 Non poterebbe farne posar una.  

For all the gold that is beneath the moon, or ever was, could not give rest to a single one of these weary souls.”

- *Chè* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *E che già fu* ‘and which already was’ → “or ever was”: “or” replaces ‘and’, *già* becomes “ever” rather than ‘already’, and the repeated relative pronoun is omitted.
- *di queste anime stanche Non poterebbe farne posar una* ‘of these souls weary it could not make one of them rest’ → “could not give rest to a single one of these weary souls”: the initial partitive phrase moves after “one”, and its resumptive pronoun *ne* is not separately represented. The causative construction ‘make … rest’ becomes “give rest to”; “single” is supplied, and the adjective moves before “souls”.

> 67 Maestro, dissi lui, or mi di’ anche:  
> 68 Questa Fortuna, di che tu mi tocche,  
> 69 Che è, che i ben del mondo ha sì tra branche?  

“Master,” I said to him, “now tell me also: this Fortune, of which thou hintest to me; what is she, that has the good things of the world thus within her clutches?”

- *or mi di’ anche* ‘now to me tell also’ → “now tell me also”: the object pronoun moves after the imperative.
- *di che tu mi tocche* ‘of which thou touchest me’ → “of which thou hintest to me”: *tocche* is interpreted figuratively as touching upon a subject, rendered by “hintest”; *mi* is expressed as “to me”.
- *i ben del mondo ha* ‘the goods of the world has’ → “has the good things of the world”: the verb moves before its object, and the substantival *ben* becomes the adjective-and-noun phrase “good things”.
- *tra branche* ‘between paws’ → “within her clutches”: “within” replaces ‘between’, “clutches” renders *branche* idiomatically, and the possessive “her” is supplied.

> 70 E quegli a me: O creature sciocche,  
> 71 Quanta ignoranza è quella che vi offende?  

And he to me: “O foolish creatures, how great is this ignorance that falls upon ye!

- *quegli* ‘that one’ → “he”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *creature sciocche* ‘creatures foolish’ → “foolish creatures”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Quanta ignoranza è quella* ‘how much ignorance is that’ → “how great is this ignorance”: the noun moves into the subject phrase, while *quanta* becomes the predicative “how great”; the distal demonstrative ‘that’ becomes “this”.
- *che vi offende* ‘that harms you’ → “that falls upon ye”: the transitive verb ‘harms’ becomes “falls upon”, with the object pronoun expressed as the complement of a preposition.

> 72 Or vo’ che tu mia sentenza ne imbocche.  

Now I wish thee to receive my judgment of her.

- *vo’ che tu mia sentenza ne imbocche* ‘I wish that thou my judgment of it take into thy mouth’ → “I wish thee to receive my judgment of her”: the finite subjunctive clause becomes an object-and-infinitive construction, “thee to receive”. The bodily metaphor in *imbocche* is replaced by “receive”; the object moves after the verb, and *ne* becomes the following phrase “of her”, giving its reference a personal form.

> 73 Colui, lo cui saver tutto trascende,  
> 74 Fece li cieli, e diè lor chi conduce,  

He whose wisdom is transcendent over all, made the heavens and gave them guides;

- *Colui* ‘that one’ → “He”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *saver tutto trascende* ‘wisdom everything transcends’ → “wisdom is transcendent over all”: the transitive verb becomes a copula and adjective, “is transcendent”; its preceding direct object becomes the following prepositional phrase “over all”.
- *chi conduce* ‘one who leads’ → “guides”: a singular relative construction becomes a plural noun.

## Lines 75–87 — Fortune’s Rule Over Worldly Goods

> 75 Sì ch’ ogni parte ad ogni parte splende,  
> 76 Distribuendo ugualmente la luce:  

so that every part may shine to every part, equally distributing the light.

- *ogni parte ad ogni parte splende* ‘every part to every part shines’ → “every part may shine to every part”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase, and the present indicative becomes “may shine”.
- *Distribuendo ugualmente* ‘distributing equally’ → “equally distributing”: the adverb moves before the participle.

> 77 Similemente agli splendor mondani  
> 78 Ordinò general ministra e duce,  

In like manner, for worldly splendours, he ordained a general minister and guide;

- *Similemente* ‘similarly’ → “In like manner”: a single adverb becomes an adverbial phrase.
- *agli splendor mondani* ‘to the splendours worldly’ → “for worldly splendours”: “for” replaces ‘to’, the definite article is omitted, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *general ministra e duce* ‘general minister and guide’ → “a general minister and guide”: the indefinite article is supplied; the feminine form *ministra* is represented by the ungendered “minister”.

> 79 Che permutasse a tempo li ben vani,  
> 80 Di gente in gente, e d’ uno in altro sangue,  
> 81 Oltre la difension de’ senni umani:  

to change betimes the vain possessions, from people to people, and from one kindred to another, beyond the hindrance of human wisdom.

- *Che permutasse* ‘who should exchange’ → “to change”: a relative clause with an imperfect subjunctive becomes an infinitive expressing purpose; ‘exchange’ is rendered by the broader “change”.
- *a tempo* ‘at the proper time’ → “betimes”: a prepositional phrase becomes an adverb.
- *li ben vani* ‘the goods vain’ → “the vain possessions”: ‘goods’ becomes “possessions”, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *d’ uno in altro sangue* ‘from one to another blood’ → “from one kindred to another”: “kindred” renders the figurative sense of ‘blood’; the noun moves after “one” and is understood after “another”.
- *la difension* ‘the defence’ → “the hindrance”: the translation takes ‘defence’ in the sense of resistance or obstruction.
- *de’ senni umani* ‘of the understandings human’ → “of human wisdom”: the plural becomes the singular abstract “wisdom”, the definite article is omitted, and the adjective moves before the noun.

> 82 Per ch’ una gente impera, e l’ altra langue,  
> 83 Seguendo lo giudicio di costei,  
> 84 Che è occulto, come in erba l’ angue.  

Hence one people commands, another languishes; obeying her sentence, which is hidden like the serpent in the grass.

- *Per ch’* ‘for which reason’ → “Hence”: the connective phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *e l’ altra* ‘and the other’ → “another”: the conjunction is omitted, and the definite ‘the other’ becomes indefinite “another”.
- *Seguendo* ‘following’ → “obeying”: the translation specifies obedience rather than following more generally.
- *lo giudicio di costei* ‘the judgment of this woman’ → “her sentence”: ‘judgment’ becomes “sentence”, and the following demonstrative phrase becomes a possessive before the noun.
- *come in erba l’ angue* ‘like in grass the serpent’ → “like the serpent in the grass”: the noun precedes the phrase locating it, and an article is supplied before “grass”.

> 85 Vostro saver non ha contrasto a lei:  

Your knowledge cannot withstand her.

- *Vostro saver* ‘your knowing’ → “Your knowledge”: the substantivized infinitive becomes an ordinary abstract noun.
- *non ha contrasto a lei* ‘has no opposition to her’ → “cannot withstand her”: the construction with ‘have’ and a noun becomes a modal verb expressing inability followed by “withstand”; the prepositional complement becomes a direct object.

> 86 Ella provvede, giudica, e persegue  
> 87 Suo regno, come il loro gli altri Dei.  

She provides, judges, and maintains her kingdom, as the other gods do theirs.

- *persegue* ‘pursues’ → “maintains”: the translation interprets continued pursuit as maintenance.
- *come il loro gli altri Dei* ‘as theirs the other gods’ → “as the other gods do theirs”: the subject moves before the possessive pronoun, and “do” supplies the verb left understood in Italian.

## Lines 88–99 — Fortune’s Changes and the Descent

> 88 Le sue permutazion non hanno triegue:  

Her permutations have no truce.

- *triegue* ‘truces’ → “truce”: the plural becomes singular.

> 89 Necessità la fa esser veloce;  

Necessity makes her be swift;

- *la fa esser veloce* ‘her makes be swift’ → “makes her be swift”: the object pronoun moves after the causative verb.

> 90 Sì spesso vien chi vicenda consegue.  

so oft come things requiring change.

- *vien chi vicenda consegue* ‘comes whoever obtains a turn’ → “come things requiring change”: the indefinite personal relative becomes the impersonal plural “things”, and the verb consequently becomes plural. Obtaining a turn is interpreted as requiring change; the relative clause becomes a participial phrase, with its object following rather than preceding the participle.

> 91 Quest’ è colei, ch’ è tanto posta in croce  
> 92 Pur da color, che le dovrian dar lode,  
> 93 Dandole biasmo a torto e mala voce.  

This is she, who is so much reviled, even by those who ought to praise her, when blaming her wrongfully, and with evil words.

- *posta in croce* ‘put on the cross’ → “reviled”: the figurative expression is rendered by its sense rather than its image.
- *che le dovrian dar lode* ‘who to her ought to give praise’ → “who ought to praise her”: the noun-and-verb expression becomes the verb “praise”; the indirect object becomes a direct object and moves after that verb.
- *Dandole biasmo* ‘giving her blame’ → “when blaming her”: “when” makes the circumstance explicit; the noun ‘blame’ becomes a verb, and the indirect object becomes its direct object.
- *e mala voce* ‘and bad repute’ → “and with evil words”: bad reputation or ill report is interpreted as abusive words. “with” is supplied, making this a phrase describing how the blaming is done rather than another object of giving.

> 94 Ma ella s’ è beata, e ciò non ode:  

But she is in bliss, and hears it not.

- *s’ è beata* ‘is herself blessed’ → “is in bliss”: the adjective becomes a prepositional phrase, and the reinforcing reflexive has no separate equivalent.
- *ciò non ode* ‘this not hears’ → “hears it not”: ‘this’ becomes “it”; the object moves after the verb, and the negative follows the object instead of preceding the verb.

> 95 Con l’ altre prime creature lieta  
> 96 Volve sua spera, e beata si gode.  

With the other Primal Creatures joyful, she wheels her sphere, and tastes her blessedness.

- *prime* ‘first’ → “Primal”: being first is expressed as being primal.
- *Volve* ‘turns’ → “wheels”: the translation uses a verb specifically suggesting circular movement.
- *beata si gode* ‘blessed, enjoys herself’ → “tastes her blessedness”: the adjective describing the subject becomes a possessive noun phrase serving as the object. Reflexive enjoyment becomes the transitive metaphor “tastes”.

> 97 Or discendiamo omai a maggior pieta.  

“But let us now descend to greater misery.

- *Or* ‘now’ → “But”: the transitional adverb becomes an adversative conjunction.
- *discendiamo omai* ‘let us descend now’ → “let us now descend”: the adverb moves before the main verb.
- *pieta* ‘pity’ → “misery”: the word is understood as the suffering that evokes pity, rather than the feeling itself.

> 98 Già ogni stella cade, che saliva  
> 99 Quando mi mossi, e il troppo star si vieta.  

Already every star is falling, that was ascending when we entered; and to stay too long is not permitted.”

- *cade* ‘falls’ → “is falling”: the simple present becomes the present progressive.
- *mi mossi* ‘I set out’ → “we entered”: the singular becomes plural, and setting out or moving is interpreted specifically as entering.
- *il troppo star si vieta* ‘the excessive staying is forbidden’ → “to stay too long is not permitted”: the article-marked infinitive becomes an English infinitive subject, and excess is explicitly expressed as duration. The passive prohibition becomes a negated expression of permission.

## Lines 100–114 — Descent Into the Styx

> 100 Noi ricidemmo il cerchio all’ altra riva  
> 101 Sovr’ una fonte, che bolle, e riversa  
> 102 Per un fossato, che da lei diriva.  

We crossed the circle, to the other bank; near a fount, that boils and pours down through a cleft, which it has formed.

- *ricidemmo* ‘cut across’ → “crossed”: the image of cutting is replaced by a straightforward verb of passage.
- *Sovr’* ‘above’ → “near”: a relation of height becomes one of proximity.
- *riversa* ‘pours out’ → “pours down”: the translation specifies downward movement rather than outflow.
- *fossato* ‘ditch’ → “cleft”: an excavated or channel-like feature is rendered as a fissure.
- *che da lei diriva* ‘which derives from it’ → “which it has formed”: the source becomes the grammatical agent, and present derivation becomes completed formation in the present perfect.

> 103 L’ acqua era buia molto più che persa:  

The water was darker far than perse.

- *buia molto più che persa* ‘dark much more than perse’ → “darker far than perse”: the comparison is incorporated into “darker”; *molto* becomes “far” and follows rather than precedes the comparative.

> 104 E noi, in compagnia dell’ onde bige,  
> 105 Entrammo giù per una via diversa.  

And we, accompanying the dusky waves, entered down by a strange path.

- *in compagnia dell’ onde bige* ‘in the company of the grey waves’ → “accompanying the dusky waves”: a prepositional phrase becomes a participial construction; *bige* becomes “dusky” rather than ‘grey’, and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *per* ‘through’ → “by”: the preposition of passage becomes an expression of route.
- *via diversa* ‘path different’ → “strange path”: *diversa* is interpreted as unfamiliar or unusual rather than merely different, and the adjective precedes the noun.

> 106 Una palude fa, che ha nome Stige,  
> 107 Questo tristo ruscel, quando è disceso  
> 108 Al piè delle maligne piagge grige.  

This dreary streamlet makes a Marsh, that is named Styx, when it has descended to the foot of the grey malignant shores.

- *Una palude fa* ‘a marsh makes’ → “makes a Marsh”: the object, placed before the verb in Italian, follows it in English.
- *che ha nome Stige* ‘which has the name Styx’ → “that is named Styx”: possession of a name becomes a passive naming construction.
- *Questo tristo ruscel* ‘this sad streamlet’ → “This dreary streamlet”: the delayed subject moves to the beginning of the sentence, before the verb and the relative clause; *tristo* becomes “dreary”.
- *delle maligne piagge grige* ‘of the malignant slopes grey’ → “of the grey malignant shores”: *piagge* is interpreted as shores rather than slopes, and “grey” moves before both the noun and “malignant”.

> 109 Ed io, che a rimirar mi stava inteso,  
> 110 Vidi genti fangose in quel pantano,  
> 111 Ignude tutte, e con sembiante offeso.  

And I, who stood intent on looking, saw muddy people in that bog, all naked and with a look of anger.

- *a rimirar mi stava inteso* ‘to gazing I was remaining intent’ → “stood intent on looking”: the fronted infinitival phrase follows “intent” as an *on*-phrase with a gerund; the pronominal *mi stava* becomes “stood”, specifying posture, and the imperfect becomes a simple past.
- *genti fangose* ‘people muddy’ → “muddy people”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Ignude tutte* ‘naked all’ → “all naked”: the order is reversed.
- *con sembiante offeso* ‘with countenance offended’ → “with a look of anger”: an indefinite article is supplied, ‘countenance’ becomes “look”, and the adjective *offeso* is interpreted as anger and expressed by a noun phrase.

> 112 Questi si percotean non pur con mano,  
> 113 Ma con la testa, e col petto, e co’ piedi,  
> 114 Troncandosi coi denti a brano a brano.  

They were smiting each other, not with hands only, but with head, and with chest, and with feet; maiming one another with their teeth, piece by piece.

- *Questi* ‘these’ → “They”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *non pur con mano* ‘not only with hand’ → “not with hands only”: “only” moves after the prepositional phrase, and singular *mano* becomes plural “hands”.
- *con la testa, e col petto, e co’ piedi* ‘with the head, and with the chest, and with the feet’ → “with head, and with chest, and with feet”: the definite articles are omitted.
- *Troncandosi* ‘cutting one another apart’ → “maiming one another”: the specific action of cutting or severing becomes the broader action of mutilation.
- *coi denti* ‘with the teeth’ → “with their teeth”: the definite article becomes a possessive.
- *a brano a brano* ‘shred by shred’ → “piece by piece”: the more specific ‘shred’ becomes the general “piece”.

## Lines 115–126 — Anger and Sullenness in the Mire

> 115 Lo buon Maestro disse: Figlio, or vedi  
> 116 L’ anime di color cui vinse l’ ira:  

The kind Master said: “Son, now see the souls of those whom anger overcame.

- *buon* ‘good’ → “kind”: the adjective is rendered specifically as kindness.
- *cui vinse l’ira* ‘whom overcame anger’ → “whom anger overcame”: the subject moves before the verb.

> 117 Ed anche vo’ che tu per certo credi,  
> 118 Che sotto l’ acqua ha gente che sospira,  
> 119 E fanno pullular quest’ acqua al summo,  

And also I would have thee to believe for certain, that there are people underneath the water, who sob, and make it bubble at the surface;

- *vo’ che tu per certo credi* ‘I want that thou for certain believe’ → “I would have thee to believe for certain”: the construction with *che* and a subjunctive becomes “would have” with an object and an infinitive; the expression of certainty moves after “believe”.
- *Che sotto l’acqua ha gente* ‘that underneath the water is a body of people’ → “that there are people underneath the water”: the impersonal singular *ha* and collective *gente* become a plural existential construction; the phrase locating the people moves after them.
- *che sospira* ‘who sighs’ → “who sob”: sighing becomes sobbing, and the singular verb agreeing with *gente* becomes plural.
- *pullular quest’acqua* ‘bubble this water’ → “it bubble”: the subject of the infinitive moves before it, as English requires after “make”; “it” replaces ‘this water’.
- *al summo* ‘at the top’ → “at the surface”: the general expression for the uppermost part is rendered specifically as the water’s surface.

> 120 Come l’ occhio ti dice u’ che s’ aggira.  

as thy eye may tell thee, whichever way it turns.

- *l’occhio ti dice* ‘the eye tells thee’ → “thy eye may tell thee”: the definite article becomes a possessive, “may” is supplied, and the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *u’ che s’aggira* ‘wherever it turns itself’ → “whichever way it turns”: the expression of place becomes one of direction, and the reflexive construction becomes an English intransitive verb.

> 121 Fitti nel limo dicon: Tristi fummo  
> 122 Nell’ aer dolce che dal Sol s’ allegra,  
> 123 Portando dentro accidioso fummo:  

Fixed in the slime, they say: ‘Sullen were we in the sweet air, that is gladdened by the Sun, carrying lazy smoke within our hearts:

- *Tristi fummo* ‘sad we were’ → “Sullen were we”: sadness is interpreted as sullenness, and the expressed English subject follows the verb.
- *Nell’aer dolce* ‘in the air sweet’ → “in the sweet air”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *che dal Sol s’allegra* ‘that from the Sun takes joy’ → “that is gladdened by the Sun”: the reflexive verb expressing rejoicing becomes a passive construction; the phrase naming the source of joy moves after the verb and becomes its agent.
- *dentro* ‘within’ → “within our hearts”: “our hearts” is supplied, and the expression moves after the object of “carrying”.
- *accidioso* ‘slothful’ → “lazy”: the adjective is rendered in the more everyday vocabulary of laziness.

> 124 Or ci attristiam nella belletta negra.  

now lie we sullen here in the black mire.’

- *ci attristiam* ‘we sadden ourselves’ → “lie we sullen here”: the reflexive verb becomes “lie” with an adjective describing a state; “here” is supplied, and the subject follows the verb.
- *nella belletta negra* ‘in the mire black’ → “in the black mire”: the adjective moves before the noun.

> 125 Quest’ inno si gorgoglian nella strozza,  
> 126 Che dir nol posson con parola integra.  

This hymn they gurgle in their throats, for they cannot speak it in full words.”

- *si gorgoglian* ‘they gurgle to themselves’ → “they gurgle”: the reflexive pronoun is not separately rendered.
- *nella strozza* ‘in the throat’ → “in their throats”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the distributive singular becomes plural.
- *Che* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *dir nol posson* ‘say it they cannot’ → “they cannot speak it”: the modal and negative move before the infinitive, the object pronoun follows it, and “speak” replaces ‘say’.
- *con parola integra* ‘with a word whole’ → “in full words”: “in” replaces ‘with’, the singular becomes plural, and the adjective moves before the noun.

## Lines 127–130 — Circling the Filthy Pool

> 127 Così girammo della lorda pozza  
> 128 Grand’ arco, tra la ripa secca e il mezzo,  
> 129 Con gli occhi volti a chi del fango ingozza.  

Thus, between the dry bank and the putrid fen, we compassed a large arc of that loathly slough, with eyes turned towards those that swallow of its filth.

- *girammo della lorda pozza Grand’ arco* ‘we went round a great arc of the filthy pool’ → “we compassed a large arc of that loathly slough”: “compassed” expresses going round; the phrase describing the pool moves from before *Grand’ arco* to after “a large arc”. The definite article becomes the demonstrative “that”; “loathly” emphasizes repulsiveness rather than dirtiness, and “slough” makes the pool specifically muddy or swampy.
- *tra la ripa secca e il mezzo* ‘between the bank dry and the middle’ → “between the dry bank and the putrid fen”: the phrase moves before the verb and its object, and “dry” moves before its noun. “The putrid fen” interprets ‘the middle’ as the swamp’s interior, replacing the spatial expression with a description and supplying “putrid”.
- *Con gli occhi* ‘with the eyes’ → “with eyes”: the definite article is omitted.
- *chi del fango ingozza* ‘whoever gulps down some mud’ → “those that swallow of its filth”: the singular indefinite relative becomes a plural demonstrative with a relative clause. The verb moves before what is swallowed; “swallow” is less forceful than ‘gulp down’. The partitive *del* is represented by “of”, but “its” supplies a possessive reference to the slough, while “filth” replaces the more concrete ‘mud’.

## Summary — Close Following with Selective Recasting

Carlyle generally follows the Italian’s sequence of clauses and preserves much of its vocabulary and imagery, while adjusting the wording within clauses to English usage. Objects and delayed subjects frequently move before or after the verb into more usual English positions; adjectives and possessives commonly move before their nouns. Thus *che tutto seppe* becomes “who knew all”, and *colpa contraria* becomes “contrary guilt”. Yet the prose also retains marked inversions, such as “Here saw I” and “Vain thoughts combinest thou”, rather than consistently adopting ordinary English order.

Changes of construction often replace a literal grammatical correspondence with an English equivalent. Substantivized infinitives become nouns, as *l’ andare* becomes “our journey”; purpose clauses and phrases become participles or infinitives, as *per confortarmi* becomes “comforting me” and *Che permutasse* becomes “to change”. Noun-and-verb expressions are sometimes compressed into verbs: *dar lode* becomes “praise”, while *non ha contrasto a lei* becomes “cannot withstand her”. Elsewhere Carlyle expands or redirects the construction: *farne posar una* becomes “give rest to a single one”, and *beata si gode* becomes “tastes her blessedness”. Reflexive forms likewise receive varied treatment, appearing as English reflexives, reciprocals, intransitive verbs, or passive constructions according to his reading.

Word choice ranges from close correspondence to explanatory interpretation. “clucking voice” gives *voce chioccia* a distinctly animal quality; “greedy rage” adds a qualification to *rabbia*; and “within our hearts” supplies a location for *dentro*. Some choices replace an image with its understood meaning: *posta in croce* becomes “reviled”, and *poppa* becomes “chest”. Others make a more substantial interpretive departure, notably *chi stipa*, rendered as “Who shall tell in few”, and *chi vicenda consegue*, rendered as “things requiring change”. Small shifts in conjunctions, prepositions, and demonstratives also recur, changing emphasis or connection without extensively reshaping the sentence.

Additions and omissions frequently clarify reference or accommodate English idiom. Carlyle supplies possessives in “our journey”, “their graves”, and “their throats”; replaces pronouns with explicit nouns, as *quella* becomes “the surge”; and leaves out elements such as *due* in “they shall continue butting one another”. Singular collectives and distributive singulars often become plurals. Beyond the changes individually noted in the comparison, English regularly supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending, and omits the Italian definite article before possessives.

These adjustments coexist with considerable literal closeness. Carlyle often preserves clause order, repeated terms, concrete images, and even unusual phrasing, as in “by force of chest” and “Her permutations have no truce”. His translation therefore remains closely traceable to the Italian, but not mechanically word for word: grammatical accommodation, explicit reference, and selective interpretation continually qualify that closeness.
