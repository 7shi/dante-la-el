# Inferno 2

## Lines 1–12 — Dusk and Doubts Before the Journey

> 1 Lo giorno se n’ andava, e l’ aer bruno  
> 2 Toglieva gli animai, che sono in terra,  
> 3 Dalle fatiche loro; ed io sol uno  

The day was departing, and the brown air taking the animals, that are on earth, from their toils; and I, one alone,

- *se n’ andava* ‘was going away’ → “was departing”: the Italian verbal phrase becomes a single verb.
- *l’ aer bruno* ‘the air brown’ → “the brown air”: the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Toglieva* ‘was taking’ → “taking”: the finite imperfect becomes a participle, with the auxiliary understood rather than repeated.
- *Dalle fatiche loro* ‘from the toils their’ → “from their toils”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *sol uno* ‘alone one’ → “one alone”: the order is reversed.

> 4 M’ apparecchiava a sostener la guerra  
> 5 Sì del cammino, e sì della pietate,  
> 6 Che ritrarrà la mente, che non erra.  

was preparing myself to bear the war both of the journey and the pity, which memory, that errs not, shall relate.

- *M’ apparecchiava* ‘myself was preparing’ → “was preparing myself”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Sì del cammino, e sì della pietate* ‘both of the journey, and also of the pity’ → “both of the journey and the pity”: the repeated affirmative construction becomes “both” … “and”; the second preposition is not repeated.
- *Che ritrarrà la mente, che non erra* ‘which will portray the mind, which does not err’ → “which memory, that errs not, shall relate.”: the subject and its relative clause move before the verb; *mente* is interpreted specifically as memory, and its article is omitted; *ritrarrà* becomes verbal relating rather than portraying; the negative follows its verb instead of preceding it.

> 7 O Muse, o alto ingegno, or m’ aiutate:  

O Muses, O high Genius, now help me!

- *ingegno* ‘intellect’ → “Genius”: intellectual capacity is expressed as genius.
- *or m’ aiutate* ‘now me help’ → “now help me!”: the object pronoun moves after the imperative.

> 8 O mente, che scrivesti ciò ch’ io vidi,  
> 9 Qui si parrà la tua nobilitate.  

O Memory, that hast inscribed what I saw, here will be shewn thy nobleness.

- *mente* ‘mind’ → “Memory”: the broader faculty is again rendered specifically as memory.
- *scrivesti* ‘wrotest’ → “hast inscribed”: the simple past becomes a present perfect, and writing becomes inscription.
- *ciò ch’ io vidi* ‘that which I saw’ → “what I saw”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun are combined in “what”.
- *si parrà* ‘will appear’ → “will be shewn”: the pronominal verb meaning to appear or manifest itself becomes a passive construction.

> 10 Io cominciai: Poeta che mi guidi,  

I began: “Poet, who guidest me,

- *che mi guidi* ‘who me guidest’ → “who guidest me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 11 Guarda la mia virtù, s’ ella è possente,  
> 12 Prima che all’ alto passo tu mi fidi.  

look if there be worth in me sufficient, before thou trust me to the arduous passage.

- *Guarda la mia virtù, s’ ella è possente* ‘look at my power, whether it is powerful’ → “look if there be worth in me sufficient,”: the direct object of *Guarda* is absorbed into the following clause. Possessed power becomes “worth in me”; the clause asking whether that power is capable becomes an existential construction asking whether sufficient worth exists. The indicative *è* becomes the subjunctive “be”.
- *Prima che* ‘before that’ → “before”: the two-word conjunction becomes a single English conjunction.
- *all’ alto passo* ‘to the high step’ → “to the arduous passage”: height is interpreted as difficulty, and *passo* as a passage rather than a step; the phrase moves from before the subject to after the verb and its object.
- *tu mi fidi* ‘thou me trust’ → “thou trust me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

## Lines 13–27 — The Purpose of Aeneas’s Journey

> 13 Tu dici, che di Silvio lo parente,  
> 14 Corruttibile ancora, ad immortale  
> 15 Secolo andò, e fu sensibilmente.  

Thou sayest that the father of Sylvius, whilst subject to corruption, went to the immortal world, and was there in body.

- *di Silvio lo parente* ‘of Sylvius the parent’ → “the father of Sylvius”: the possessive phrase follows its noun, and “father” makes the parental relationship specifically paternal.
- *Corruttibile ancora* ‘still corruptible’ → “whilst subject to corruption”: the adjective becomes a phrase expressing susceptibility to corruption; “whilst” supplies an explicit temporal connection, while *ancora*, ‘still’, is not separately represented.
- *ad immortale Secolo* ‘to immortal age’ → “to the immortal world”: *Secolo* is interpreted spatially rather than temporally, and the definite article is supplied.
- *e fu sensibilmente* ‘and was perceptibly’ → “and was there in body”: the adverb is interpreted as bodily presence and becomes a prepositional phrase; “there” is supplied.

> 16 Però se l’ Avversario d’ ogni male  
> 17 Cortese fu, pensando l’ alto effetto,  
> 18 Ch’ uscir dovea di lui, e il chi, e il quale;  

But if the Adversary of all evil was propitious, considering the high effect, and who and what should come from him;

- *Però se* ‘therefore if’ → “But if”: the inferential connection becomes adversative.
- *Cortese fu* ‘courteous was’ → “was propitious”: the adjective follows the verb and expresses favourable disposition rather than courtesy.
- *pensando* ‘thinking of’ → “considering”: the participle is rendered by a verb that directly takes the following objects.
- *Ch’ uscir dovea di lui, e il chi, e il quale* ‘which was to issue from him, and the who, and the what’ → “and who and what should come from him”: the relative clause modifying *effetto* is no longer separately expressed. Its verbal content follows “who and what”, making these the subjects of “should come”; the Italian’s substantivized *il chi* and *il quale* become interrogative pronouns without articles. *Dovea* is rendered by prospective “should”, and *uscir* by the less explicitly outward “come”.

> 19 Non pare indegno ad uomo d’ intelletto:  

it seems not unfitting to an understanding mind.

- *Non pare indegno* ‘does not seem unworthy’ → “it seems not unfitting”: the negative follows “seems” rather than preceding the verb, and unworthiness is rendered as lack of fitness.
- *ad uomo d’ intelletto* ‘to a man of intellect’ → “to an understanding mind”: “mind” replaces the person, and the qualifying noun phrase becomes the adjective “understanding”.

> 20 Ch’ ei fu dell’ alma Roma e di suo impero  
> 21 Nell’ empireo Ciel per padre eletto:  

For in the empyreal heaven, he was chosen to be the father of generous Rome, and of her Empire.

- *Ch’* ‘because’ → “For”: the subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating explanation.
- *dell’ alma Roma e di suo impero* ‘of nurturing Rome and of her empire’ → “of generous Rome, and of her Empire”: the phrase follows “father” instead of preceding it; *alma* is rendered as “generous”, emphasizing bounty rather than nurture.
- *Nell’ empireo Ciel* ‘in the empyrean heaven’ → “in the empyreal heaven”: the locative phrase is moved before the subject and verb.
- *per padre* ‘as father’ → “to be the father”: the predicative phrase becomes an infinitive construction, with “be” and the definite article supplied.

> 22 La quale, e il quale, a voler dir lo vero,  
> 23 Fur stabiliti per lo loco santo,  
> 24 U’ siede il successor del maggior Piero.  

Both these, to say the truth, were established for the holy place, where the Successor of great Peter sits.

- *La quale, e il quale* ‘which [feminine], and which [masculine]’ → “Both these”: two coordinated relative pronouns become a demonstrative subject in an independent sentence; “Both” replaces their explicit coordination and repetition.
- *a voler dir lo vero* ‘if one wishes to say the truth’ → “to say the truth”: the conditional force of *a* with the infinitive and the element of willingness in *voler* are omitted, leaving a parenthetical infinitive.
- *per lo loco santo* ‘for the place holy’ → “for the holy place”: the adjective precedes its noun.
- *U’ siede il successor del maggior Piero* ‘where sits the successor of the greater Peter’ → “where the Successor of great Peter sits”: the verb moves after the complete subject phrase; the comparative form *maggior* is interpreted as an absolute expression of greatness and rendered by the positive “great”.

> 25 Per questa andata, onde gli dai tu vanto,  
> 26 Intese cose che furon cagione  
> 27 Di sua vittoria e del papale ammanto.  

By this journey, for which thou honourest him, he learned things that were the causes of his victory, and of the Papal Mantle.

- *questa andata* ‘this going’ → “this journey”: the noun naming the act of going is rendered by a noun naming the journey.
- *onde gli dai tu vanto* ‘for which to him givest thou praise’ → “for which thou honourest him”: the construction with ‘give’ and a noun becomes the single verb “honourest”; the indirect object becomes a direct object, and the subject precedes the verb rather than following it.
- *Intese* ‘understood’ → “he learned”: understanding is rendered as acquiring knowledge.
- *furon cagione* ‘were cause’ → “were the causes”: the singular predicate noun becomes plural, and a definite article is supplied.

## Lines 28–39 — Dante Doubts His Worthiness

> 28 Andovvi poi lo Vas d’ elezione,  
> 29 Per recarne conforto a quella Fede,  
> 30 Ch’ è principio alla via di salvazione.  

Afterwards, the Chosen Vessel went thither, to bring confirmation of that Faith which is the entrance to the way of salvation.

- *Andovvi poi lo Vas d’ elezione* ‘Went thither afterwards the Vessel of election’ → “Afterwards, the Chosen Vessel went thither”: the temporal adverb moves to the beginning, the subject precedes the verb, and the noun phrase ‘of election’ becomes the preceding adjective “Chosen”.
- *recarne conforto* ‘bring from there comfort’ → “bring confirmation”: the locative *ne* is omitted, and “confirmation” replaces ‘comfort’ or ‘support’.
- *principio* ‘beginning’ → “the entrance”: the metaphor changes, and a definite article is supplied.

> 31 Ma io, perchè venirvi? o chi ’l concede?  

But I, why go? or who permits it?

- *venirvi* ‘come thither’ → “go”: ‘come’ becomes “go”, and the explicit indication of destination in *vi* is omitted.
- *’l concede* ‘grants it’ → “permits it”: granting is expressed as permission.

> 32 Io non Enea, io non Paolo sono:  
> 33 Me degno a ciò nè io, nè altri crede.  

I am not Æneas, am not Paul: neither myself nor others deem me worthy of it.

- *Io non Enea, io non Paolo sono* ‘I not Æneas, I not Paul am’ → “I am not Æneas, am not Paul”: the final verb is moved before “Paul” and supplied separately before “Æneas”; the second explicit *io* is omitted.
- *Me degno a ciò nè io, nè altri crede* ‘Me worthy of this neither I nor anyone else believes’ → “neither myself nor others deem me worthy of it”: the subject and verb move before the object and its complement; *io* becomes “myself”, the indefinite singular *altri* becomes plural “others”, and ‘believes’ becomes “deem”. The complement *a ciò* is expressed as “of it”, using the preposition required by English ‘worthy’ and replacing the demonstrative with a personal pronoun.

> 34 Per che se del venire io m’ abbandono,  
> 35 Temo che la venuta non sia folle:  

Wherefore, if I resign myself to go, I fear my going may prove foolish.

- *Per che* ‘For which reason’ → “Wherefore”: the prepositional expression becomes a single connective adverb.
- *del venire io m’ abbandono* ‘to coming I abandon myself’ → “I resign myself to go”: the complement moves after the verb, ‘abandon myself’ becomes “resign myself”, and the substantivized infinitive becomes an infinitive complement. ‘Coming’ is again rendered as going.
- *Temo che la venuta non sia folle* ‘I fear that the coming may not be foolish’ → “I fear my going may prove foolish”: the conjunction *che* is omitted, the definite article becomes the possessive “my”, and ‘coming’ becomes “going”. The subjunctive *sia* is expanded to “may prove”, rather than ‘may be’. The *non* is expletive after a verb of fearing, so its omission does not reverse the meaning.

> 36 Se’ savio, e intendi me’ ch’ io non ragiono.  

Thou art wise, and understandest better than I speak.”

- *ch’ io non ragiono* ‘than I do not speak’ → “than I speak”: the comparative construction’s expletive *non* has no negative counterpart in English.

> 37 E quale è quei, che disvuol ciò ch’ e’ volle,  
> 38 E per novi pensier cangia proposta,  
> 39 Sì che del cominciar tutto si tolle;  

And as one who unwills what he willed, and with new thoughts changes his purpose, so that he wholly quits the thing commenced, such I made myself on that dim coast;

- *E quale è quei* ‘And as is he’ → “And as one”: the copula is omitted, and the demonstrative becomes indefinite “one”. The English subsequently completes the comparison with “such I made myself on that dim coast;”, which renders the following Italian line, not included in the supplied lines 28–39.
- *ciò ch’* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun are combined into a single relative pronoun.
- *per novi pensier* ‘through new thoughts’ → “with new thoughts”: the causal preposition becomes “with”.
- *cangia proposta* ‘changes purpose’ → “changes his purpose”: a possessive is supplied.
- *Sì che del cominciar tutto si tolle* ‘So that from beginning he wholly withdraws himself’ → “so that he wholly quits the thing commenced”: the reflexive construction with a prepositional complement becomes a transitive verb with a direct object. The substantivized infinitive *cominciar*, naming the act of beginning, becomes “the thing commenced”, naming what was begun; that complement moves from before the verb to after it.

## Lines 40–54 — Fear and the Lady’s Call

> 40 Tal mi fec’ io in quella oscura costa:  
> 41 Perchè pensando consumai la impresa,  
> 42 Che fu nel cominciar cotanto tosta.  

for with thinking I wasted the enterprise, that had been so quick in its commencement.

- *Tal mi fec’ io in quella oscura costa* ‘Such I made myself on that dark slope’ → no corresponding English: the whole of line 40 is omitted from the supplied translation.
- *Perchè* ‘because’ → “for”: a subordinating conjunction becomes a coordinating conjunction.
- *pensando* ‘thinking’ → “with thinking”: the gerund becomes a prepositional phrase expressing means.
- *consumai* ‘I consumed’ → “I wasted”: consumption is rendered as waste.
- *fu* ‘was’ → “had been”: the simple past becomes a past perfect.
- *nel cominciar* ‘in the beginning’ → “in its commencement”: the definite article becomes a possessive, and the phrase moves after the adjective.
- *cotanto tosta* ‘so firm’ → “so quick”: *tosta* is interpreted as expressing speed rather than firmness or resolve.

> 43 Se io ho ben la tua parola intesa,  
> 44 Rispose del magnanimo quell’ ombra,  
> 45 L’ anima tua è la viltade offesa,  

“If I have rightly understood thy words,” replied that shade of the Magnanimous, “thy soul is smit with coward fear,

- *Se io ho ben la tua parola intesa* ‘If I have well thy word understood’ → “If I have rightly understood thy words”: “rightly” interprets *ben* as correctness of understanding; singular *parola* becomes plural “words”; and the participle moves before its object.
- *del magnanimo quell’ ombra* ‘of the magnanimous one that shade’ → “that shade of the Magnanimous”: the possessive phrase moves after the noun it qualifies.
- *L’ anima tua* ‘the soul thine’ → “thy soul”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *è la viltade offesa* ‘is the cowardice injured’ → “is smit with coward fear”: the English follows the sense of the reading *è da viltade offesa*, rather than the printed *la*. Cowardice becomes the cause of the injury, introduced by “with”; *offesa* becomes “smit,” and *viltade* is expanded into “coward fear.”

> 46 La qual molte fiate l’ uomo ingombra,  
> 47 Sì che d’ onrata impresa lo rivolve,  
> 48 Come falso veder bestia quand’ ombra.  

which oftentimes encumbers men, so that it turns them back from honoured enterprise; as false seeing does a startled beast.

- *molte fiate* ‘many times’ → “oftentimes”: the noun phrase becomes a single adverb.
- *l’ uomo ingombra* ‘the man encumbers’ → “encumbers men”: the object moves after the verb, and the generic definite singular becomes an indefinite plural.
- *d’ onrata impresa lo rivolve* ‘from honoured enterprise him turns back’ → “it turns them back from honoured enterprise”: the prepositional phrase moves to the end, and the object follows the verb. Singular *lo* becomes plural “them,” agreeing with “men.”
- *Come falso veder bestia quand’ ombra* ‘as false seeing a beast when it shies’ → “as false seeing does a startled beast”: “does” supplies the verb left implicit in Italian. The clause *quand’ ombra* becomes the adjective “startled,” placed before “beast,” and an indefinite article is supplied.

> 49 Da questa tema acciocchè tu ti solve,  
> 50 Dirotti, perch’ io venni, e quel ch’ io intesi,  
> 51 Nel primo punto che di te mi dolve.  

To free thee from this dread, I will tell thee why I came, and what I heard in the first moment when I took pity of thee.

- *Da questa tema acciocchè tu ti solve* ‘From this fear so that thou free thyself’ → “To free thee from this dread”: the finite purpose clause becomes an infinitive whose implied subject is the speaker, rather than the person addressed. The reflexive becomes the object “thee,” the opening prepositional phrase moves after the verb, and *tema* is rendered as “dread.”
- *quel ch’* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative pronoun become a single relative word.
- *Nel primo punto* ‘in the first point’ → “in the first moment”: *punto* receives its temporal sense.
- *che di te mi dolve* ‘that on account of thee it grieved me’ → “when I took pity of thee”: the relative becomes explicitly temporal. The construction expressing grief experienced by the speaker becomes a first-person act of taking pity, and the phrase referring to “thee” moves to the end.

> 52 Io era tra color, che son sospesi,  
> 53 E Donna mi chiamò beata e bella,  
> 54 Tal che di comandare io la richiesi.  

I was amongst them who are in suspense; and a Lady, so fair and blessed that I prayed her to command, called me.

- *color* ‘those’ → “them”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *son sospesi* ‘are suspended’ → “are in suspense”: the participial description becomes a prepositional phrase.
- *E Donna mi chiamò* ‘and Lady me called’ → “and a Lady,” … “called me”: an indefinite article is supplied, the object follows the verb, and the verb is postponed until after the description and its resulting clause.
- *beata e bella, Tal che* ‘blessed and beautiful, such that’ → “so fair and blessed that”: the adjectives exchange places, and *Tal che* becomes a degree-and-result construction modifying them.
- *di comandare io la richiesi* ‘to command I requested her’ → “I prayed her to command”: the infinitive moves after the main clause, the object pronoun follows the verb, and requesting is rendered as praying or entreating.

## Lines 55–63 — A Gentle Appeal

> 55 Lucevan gli occhi suoi più che la Stella:  

Her eyes shone brighter than the Star;

- *Lucevan gli occhi suoi* ‘shone the eyes her’ → “Her eyes shone”: the subject moves before the verb, and the possessive moves before the noun.
- *più che* ‘more than’ → “brighter than”: the translation explicitly makes brightness the quality being compared.

> 56 E cominciommi a dir soave e piana  
> 57 Con angelica voce, in sua favella:  

and she began soft and gentle to tell me with angelic voice, in her language:

- *E cominciommi a dir* ‘and began to me to say’ → “and she began” and “to tell me”: the pronoun attached to *cominciò* is placed after “tell”; ‘say to me’ becomes “tell me”.
- *soave e piana* ‘sweet and plain’ → “soft and gentle”: the descriptive terms are recast, particularly *piana*, which becomes “gentle” rather than ‘plain’ or ‘even’; they also move before the infinitive rather than following it.

> 58 O anima cortese Mantovana,  

‘O courteous Mantuan Spirit,

- *anima cortese Mantovana* ‘soul courteous Mantuan’ → “courteous Mantuan Spirit”: “Spirit” replaces ‘soul’, and both adjectives move before the noun.

> 59 Di cui la fama ancor nel mondo dura,  
> 60 E durerà quanto il moto lontana:  

whose fame still lasts in the world, and will last as long as Time!

- *Di cui la fama* ‘of whom the fame’ → “whose fame”: the prepositional relative construction becomes a possessive relative.
- *ancor nel mondo dura* ‘still in the world lasts’ → “still lasts in the world”: the verb moves before the phrase of place.
- *quanto il moto lontana* ‘as far as motion extends’ → “as long as Time”: the translation interprets the extent temporally, renders ‘motion’ as “Time”, and leaves the verb *lontana* without a separate equivalent.

> 61 L’ amico mio, e non della ventura,  
> 62 Nella diserta piaggia è impedito  
> 63 Sì nel cammin, che vôlto è per paura:  

My friend, and not of fortune, is so impeded in his way upon the desert shore, that he has turned back for terror.

- *L’amico mio* ‘the friend my’ → “My friend”: the possessive moves before the noun.
- *Nella diserta piaggia* ‘on the deserted slope’ → “upon the desert shore”: *piaggia* is interpreted as ‘shore’ rather than ‘slope’; the adjective moves before the noun, and the whole phrase moves from before the verb to after “in his way”.
- *è impedito Sì nel cammin* ‘is impeded so in the way’ → “is so impeded in his way”: “so” moves before “impeded”, and the definite article becomes the possessive “his”.
- *che vôlto è per paura* ‘that turned is through fear’ → “that he has turned back for terror”: the inverted ‘turned is’ becomes the English perfect “has turned”, with “back” supplied to specify direction; ‘fear’ is rendered more strongly as “terror”.

## Lines 64–78 — Beatrice’s Plea and Virgil’s Reply

> 64 E temo che non sia già sì smarrito,  
> 65 Ch’ io mi sia tardi al soccorso levata,  
> 66 Per quel ch’ io ho di lui nel Cielo udito.  

And I fear he may already be so far astray, that I have risen too late for his relief, from what I heard of him in Heaven.

- *non sia già* ‘he be not already’ → “he may already be”: the expletive negative after the verb of fearing is omitted; the subjunctive is expressed through “may”.
- *sì smarrito* ‘so lost’ → “so far astray”: the adjective becomes an adverbial expression, with “far” supplied.
- *io mi sia … levata* ‘I have raised myself’ → “I have risen”: the Italian pronominal construction becomes an English intransitive verb; the perfect subjunctive becomes present-perfect indicative.
- *tardi* ‘late’ → “too late”: “too” makes the excessive lateness explicit.
- *al soccorso* ‘to the relief’ → “for his relief”: the preposition expresses purpose, and “his” replaces the article, identifying the recipient.
- *Per quel ch’* ‘because of that which’ → “from what”: the causal expression becomes one indicating the source of the fear, and the demonstrative and relative are combined in “what”.
- *ho di lui nel Cielo udito* ‘have of him in Heaven heard’ → “heard of him in Heaven”: the present perfect becomes simple past, and the verb precedes its complements rather than enclosing them.

> 67 Or muovi, e con la tua parola ornata,  
> 68 E con ciò, che ha mestieri al suo campare,  
> 69 L’ aiuta sì, ch’ io ne sia consolata.  

Now go, and with thy ornate speech, and with what is necessary for his escape, help him so, that I may be consoled thereby.

- *muovi* ‘move’ → “go”: the verb is rendered as an instruction to depart.
- *parola ornata* ‘word adorned’ → “ornate speech”: “speech” renders the broader sense of *parola*, and the adjective precedes its noun.
- *ciò, che* ‘that which’ → “what”: the demonstrative and relative are compressed into a single relative pronoun.
- *ha mestieri* ‘has need’ → “is necessary”: the Italian idiom of need is recast as a copular construction with an adjective.
- *al suo campare* ‘to his escaping’ → “for his escape”: the preposition is rendered as one of purpose, and the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun.
- *L’ aiuta* ‘him help’ → “help him”: the object pronoun moves after the imperative.
- *io ne sia consolata* ‘I of it be consoled’ → “I may be consoled thereby”: the subjunctive is expressed through “may”; *ne* becomes the following adverb “thereby”, expressing the cause of the consolation.

> 70 Io son Beatrice, che ti faccio andare:  

I am Beatrice who send thee.

- *ti faccio andare* ‘thee I make go’ → “send thee”: the causative construction becomes a single verb, and the object pronoun follows it.

> 71 Vegno di loco, ove tornar disio:  

I came from a place where I desire to return.

- *Vegno* ‘I come’ → “I came”: the present tense becomes past.
- *di loco* ‘from place’ → “from a place”: an indefinite article is supplied.
- *tornar disio* ‘to return I desire’ → “I desire to return”: the infinitive moves from before the governing verb to after it.

> 72 Amor mi mosse, che mi fa parlare.  

Love moved me, that makes me speak.

- *mi mosse* ‘me moved’ → “moved me”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *mi fa parlare* ‘me makes speak’ → “makes me speak”: the pronoun moves after the finite verb, while remaining before the infinitive.

> 73 Quando sarò dinanzi al Signor mio,  
> 74 Di te mi loderò sovente a lui.  

When I shall be before my Lord, I oft will praise thee to him.’

- *dinanzi al Signor mio* ‘before the Lord my’ → “before my Lord”: the possessive moves before the noun; the Italian prepositional expression *dinanzi a* is represented by “before”.
- *Di te mi loderò sovente a lui* ‘of thee I shall praise myself often to him’ → “I oft will praise thee to him”: the reflexive idiom *lodarsi di* is interpreted as speaking appreciatively of someone, not praising oneself. The prepositional phrase becomes the direct object “thee” and moves after the verb; “oft” moves before the future auxiliary.

> 75 Tacette allora, e poi cominciai io:  

“She was silent then, and I began:

- *e poi cominciai io* ‘and then began I’ → “and I began”: *poi* is omitted, and the explicit subject moves before the verb.

> 76 O Donna di virtù sola, per cui  
> 77 L’ umana spezie eccede ogni contento  
> 78 Da quel ciel, che ha minori i cerchi sui:  

‘O Lady, single in worth; through whom mankind excels all that is contained within the heaven which has the smallest circles!

- *di virtù sola* ‘of virtue unique’ → “single in worth”: *sola* is taken as describing the Lady’s uniqueness in worth, rather than as restricting *per cui* to ‘through whom alone’. It moves before the prepositional phrase; *di* becomes “in”, and *virtù* becomes “worth”.
- *L’ umana spezie* ‘the human species’ → “mankind”: the noun phrase becomes a single collective noun.
- *ogni contento* ‘every contained thing’ → “all that is contained”: the substantivized participle becomes a relative clause, and distributive ‘every’ becomes collective “all”.
- *Da quel ciel* ‘by that heaven’ → “within the heaven”: containment is expressed spatially through “within” rather than through ‘by’; the demonstrative becomes the definite article.
- *che ha minori i cerchi sui* ‘which has smaller the circles its’ → “which has the smallest circles”: the comparative becomes a superlative; the adjective, predicated of the circles in Italian, becomes an attributive adjective before the noun. The possessive *sui* is omitted.

## Lines 79–90 — Willing Obedience and Fear

> 79 Tanto m’ aggrada il tuo comandamento,  
> 80 Che l’ ubbidir, se già fosse, m’ è tardi;  

So grateful to me is thy command, that my obeying, were it done already, seems tardy.

- *Tanto m’ aggrada* ‘so much pleases me’ → “So grateful to me is”: the verb expressing pleasure becomes a construction with “is” and the adjective “grateful”.
- *l’ ubbidir* ‘the obeying’ → “my obeying”: the definite article becomes a possessive, explicitly identifying who obeys.
- *se già fosse* ‘if already it were’ → “were it done already”: conditional inversion replaces *se*; “done” supplies the action understood with *fosse*, and “already” moves to the end.
- *m’ è tardi* ‘to me is late’ → “seems tardy”: “seems” replaces “is” and leaves the personal qualification *m’* implicit; the adverb *tardi* becomes the adjective “tardy”.

> 81 Più non t’ è uopo aprirmi il tuo talento.  

It needs not that thou more explain to me thy wish.

- *Più non t’ è uopo aprirmi* ‘no longer is it necessary for thee to open to me’ → “It needs not that thou more explain to me”: the expression of necessity followed by an infinitive becomes “It needs” with a finite “that” clause. *Più non* is redistributed as “not” and “more”, while the figurative ‘open’ becomes “explain”.

> 82 Ma dimmi la cagion, che non ti guardi  
> 83 Dello scender quaggiù in questo centro  
> 84 Dall’ ampio loco, ove tornar tu ardi.  

But tell me the cause, why thou forbearest not to descend into this centre here below from the spacious place, to which thou burnest to return?’

- *che* ‘that’ → “why”: the connective becomes an explicitly causal relative adverb.
- *non ti guardi Dello scender* ‘thou dost not guard thyself against descending’ → “thou forbearest not to descend”: the reflexive construction becomes the non-reflexive “forbearest”; its prepositional complement becomes a “to” infinitive, and the negative follows the finite verb.
- *quaggiù in questo centro* ‘here below in this centre’ → “into this centre here below”: the adverb follows the noun phrase rather than preceding it, and “into” makes the direction of movement explicit.
- *ove* ‘where’ → “to which”: the relative adverb becomes a prepositional relative phrase expressing direction.
- *tornar tu ardi* ‘to return thou burnest’ → “thou burnest to return”: the infinitive moves after the subject and finite verb.

> 85 Da che tu vuoi saper cotanto addentro,  
> 86 Dirotti brevemente, mi rispose,  
> 87 Perch’ io non temo di venir qua entro.  

“‘Since thou desirest to know thus far, I will tell thee briefly,’ she replied, ‘why I fear not to come within this place.

- *cotanto addentro* ‘so deeply within’ → “thus far”: an expression of depth becomes one of extent.
- *mi rispose* ‘she replied to me’ → “she replied”: the indirect object *mi* is omitted.
- *io non temo* ‘I not fear’ → “I fear not”: the negative moves after the verb.
- *di venir* ‘of coming’ → “to come”: the Italian infinitive introduced by *di* is rendered by an English “to” infinitive.
- *qua entro* ‘here within’ → “within this place”: the adverb *qua* becomes the noun phrase “this place”, governed by “within”.

> 88 Temer si deve sol di quelle cose  
> 89 Ch’ hanno potenza di fare altrui male:  
> 90 Dell’ altre no, che non son paurose.  

Those things alone are to be feared that have the power of hurting; the others not, which are not fearful.

- *Temer si deve sol di quelle cose* ‘one must fear only those things’ → “Those things alone are to be feared”: the infinitive-first, impersonal construction becomes a passive construction expressing obligation. The complement *di quelle cose* becomes the English subject, with no separate equivalent for *di*, and *sol* becomes “alone” following that subject.
- *potenza* ‘power’ → “the power”: a definite article is supplied.
- *di fare altrui male* ‘to do others harm’ → “of hurting”: the infinitive becomes “of” with a gerund; ‘do harm’ is condensed into “hurting”, and *altrui* is omitted.
- *Dell’ altre no* ‘of the others, no’ → “the others not”: the preposition is dropped to continue the English passive construction, and “not” replaces *no* in the elliptical clause.
- *che* ‘because’ → “which”: the connective is interpreted as relative rather than causal.
- *non son* ‘not are’ → “are not”: the negative follows the verb.

## Lines 91–105 — Heavenly Pity and Beatrice’s Appeal

> 91 Io son fatta da Dio, sua mercè, tale,  
> 92 Che la vostra miseria non mi tange,  

I am made such by God, in his grace, that your misery does not touch me;

- *tale, Che* ‘such that’ → “such” … “that”: “such” is moved forward, before “by God”, separating it from the following conjunction.
- *sua mercè* ‘by his mercy’ → “in his grace”: the Italian expression of indebtedness to divine mercy becomes a phrase with “in”, and *mercè* is rendered as “grace”.
- *non mi tange* ‘not me touches’ → “does not touch me”: English supplies the auxiliary for negation and places the object pronoun after the verb.

> 93 Nè fiamma d’ esto incendio non m’ assale.  

nor the flame of this burning assail me.

- *Nè … non* ‘nor … not’ → “nor”: Italian negative concord is represented by a single English negative.
- *fiamma* ‘flame’ → “the flame”: the definite article is supplied.
- *incendio* ‘conflagration’ → “burning”: the noun naming the fire becomes a verbal noun.
- *m’ assale* ‘me assails’ → “assail me”: the object pronoun follows the verb; the uninflected verb continues the auxiliary construction of the preceding clause.

> 94 Donna è gentil nel Ciel, che si compiange  
> 95 Di questo impedimento, ov’ io ti mando,  
> 96 Sì che duro giudicio lassù frange.  

There is a noble Lady in Heaven who has such pity of this hindrance, for which I send thee, that she breaks the sharp judgment there on high.

- *Donna è gentil* ‘a lady is noble’ → “There is a noble Lady”: the clause becomes an English existential construction with “There”; the adjective is placed before the noun.
- *nel Ciel* ‘in the Heaven’ → “in Heaven”: the definite article is omitted.
- *che si compiange Di … Sì che* ‘who takes pity on … so that’ → “who has such pity of” … “that”: the pronominal verb becomes a construction with “has” and the noun “pity”, followed by “of”; the force of *Sì* is carried forward into “such”, making the result depend on the degree of pity.
- *ov’* ‘where’ → “for which”: the locative relative is interpreted as referring to the hindrance on account of which the mission is undertaken.
- *io ti mando* ‘I thee send’ → “I send thee”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *duro giudicio lassù frange* ‘harsh judgment there-above breaks’ → “she breaks the sharp judgment there on high”: the verb moves before its object and the adverbial phrase moves to the end; English supplies “she” and the definite article, renders *duro* as “sharp”, and expands *lassù* into “there on high”.

> 97 Questa chiese Lucìa in suo dimando,  
> 98 E disse: Or abbisogna il tuo fedele  
> 99 Di te, ed io a te lo raccomando.  

She called Lucia, in her request, and said: ‘Now thy faithful one has need of thee; and I commend him to thee.’

- *Questa* ‘this one’ → “She”: the demonstrative becomes a personal pronoun.
- *chiese* ‘asked for’ → “called”: the verb is interpreted as summoning rather than simply requesting.
- *abbisogna il tuo fedele Di te* ‘has need thy faithful of thee’ → “thy faithful one has need of thee”: the subject moves before the verb; the substantivized adjective *fedele* is expressed by adding “one”.
- *io a te lo raccomando* ‘I to thee him commend’ → “I commend him to thee”: the verb precedes both its objects, and the direct object comes before the prepositional phrase.

> 100 Lucìa, nimica di ciascun crudele,  
> 101 Si mosse, e venne al loco dov’ io era,  
> 102 Che mi sedea con l’ antica Rachele.  

“Lucia, enemy of all cruelty, arose and came to the place where I was sitting with the ancient Rachel.

- *nimica di ciascun crudele* ‘enemy of every cruel person’ → “enemy of all cruelty”: the substantivized adjective denoting each cruel person becomes the abstract noun “cruelty”; distributive ‘every’ becomes “all”.
- *Si mosse* ‘moved herself’ → “arose”: general movement is rendered specifically as rising, and the pronominal construction becomes an ordinary intransitive verb.
- *dov’ io era, Che mi sedea* ‘where I was, that I was sitting’ → “where I was sitting”: the two finite clauses are compressed into one progressive construction; *Che* and the pronominal *mi* receive no separate English equivalents.

> 103 Disse: Beatrice, loda di Dio vera,  
> 104 Che non soccorri quei che t’ amò tanto,  
> 105 Che uscìo per te della volgare schiera?  

She said: ‘Beatrice, true praise of God; why helpest thou not him who loved thee so, that for thee he left the vulgar crowd?

- *loda di Dio vera* ‘praise of God true’ → “true praise of God”: the adjective moves from the end of the phrase to immediately before the noun.
- *non soccorri* ‘not thou-helpest’ → “helpest thou not”: the negative follows the verb, and the supplied subject pronoun follows it in English question order.
- *quei* ‘that one’ → “him”: the demonstrative becomes a personal object pronoun.
- *t’ amò tanto* ‘thee loved so much’ → “loved thee so”: the object pronoun follows the verb, and “so” alone renders *tanto*.
- *uscìo per te della volgare schiera* ‘went out for thee from the common company’ → “for thee he left the vulgar crowd”: “for thee” moves before the verb; intransitive *uscìo* with a phrase introduced by *di* becomes transitive “left” with a direct object. *Volgare schiera* is rendered as “vulgar crowd”, using “crowd” for the collective *schiera* and “vulgar” in the sense of common.

## Lines 106–120 — The Appeal and the Swift Response

> 106 Non odi tu la pieta del suo pianto?  

Hearest not thou the misery of his plaint?

- *Non odi* ‘not hearest’ → “Hearest not”: the negative follows the verb instead of preceding it.
- *la pieta* ‘the piteousness’ → “the misery”: the quality that arouses pity is rendered as suffering itself.
- *del suo pianto* ‘of his weeping’ → “of his plaint”: weeping becomes a lament or complaint.

> 107 Non vedi tu la morte che il combatte  
> 108 Su la fiumana, ove il mar non ha vanto?  

Seest thou not the death which combats him upon the river, that swelleth not the sea?’

- *Non vedi tu* ‘not seest thou’ → “Seest thou not”: the negative moves after the verb and subject.
- *che il combatte* ‘which him combats’ → “which combats him”: the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *Su la fiumana* ‘upon the torrent’ → “upon the river”: the more forceful *fiumana* becomes the neutral “river”.
- *ove il mar non ha vanto* ‘where the sea has no boast’ → “that swelleth not the sea”: this is an interpretive rendering, not a literal one. The locative ‘where’ becomes a relative referring to the river; the sea changes from subject to object; and having no boast or superiority becomes not increasing the sea.

> 109 Al mondo non fur mai persone ratte  
> 110 A far lor pro, nè a fuggir lor danno,  
> 111 Com’ io, dopo cotai parole fatte,  
> 112 Venni quaggiù dal mio beato scanno,  

None on earth were ever swift to seek their good, or flee their hurt, as I to come, after these words were uttered, from my blessed seat;

- *Al mondo* ‘in the world’ → “on earth”: the preposition and noun change, and the phrase follows the negative subject rather than opening the sentence.
- *non fur mai persone* ‘never were persons’ → “None” and “were ever”: the negative and the noun are combined in a negative pronoun, which precedes the verb; *mai* is consequently rendered as “ever”.
- *A far lor pro* ‘to do their good’ → “to seek their good”: doing or bringing about their benefit becomes seeking it.
- *nè a fuggir lor danno* ‘nor to flee their hurt’ → “or flee their hurt”: “or” follows the earlier negative “None”, and the repeated infinitive marker is omitted.
- *dopo cotai parole fatte* ‘after such words made’ → “after these words were uttered”: ‘such’ becomes “these”; the participial phrase becomes a finite passive clause; and making words becomes uttering them.
- *Venni quaggiù* ‘I came down here’ → “to come”: the finite past-tense verb becomes an infinitive dependent on the preceding comparison, and ‘down here’ is omitted. The verb also moves before the phrase about the words being uttered.

> 113 Fidandomi nel tuo parlare onesto,  
> 114 Che onora te, e quei che udito l’ hanno.  

confiding in thy noble speech, which honours thee, and them who have heard it.’

- *Fidandomi* ‘trusting myself’ → “confiding”: the Italian reflexive construction becomes an English intransitive verb.
- *tuo parlare onesto* ‘thy honourable speaking’ → “thy noble speech”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun, “noble” renders *onesto*, and the adjective precedes rather than follows its noun.
- *quei* ‘those’ → “them”: a demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *che udito l’ hanno* ‘who heard it have’ → “who have heard it”: the auxiliary moves before the participle, and the object pronoun follows the complete verb phrase.

> 115 Poscia che m’ ebbe ragionato questo,  
> 116 Gli occhi lucenti lagrimando volse;  
> 117 Per che mi fece del venir più presto:  

“After saying this to me, she turned away her bright eyes weeping; by which she made me hasten more to come.

- *Poscia che m’ ebbe ragionato questo* ‘after she had related this to me’ → “After saying this to me”: the finite past-perfect clause becomes a non-finite phrase without an explicit perfect tense; relating becomes saying; and the indirect object moves from before the verb to after its direct object.
- *Gli occhi lucenti* ‘the shining eyes’ → “her bright eyes”: the definite article becomes a possessive, “bright” renders *lucenti*, and the adjective moves before the noun. The whole object phrase also moves after the verb.
- *lagrimando volse* ‘weeping she turned’ → “she turned away her bright eyes weeping”: the participle moves from before the verb to after its object, and “away” makes the direction explicit.
- *Per che* ‘because of which’ → “by which”: a causal expression becomes an expression of means.
- *mi fece del venir più presto* ‘she made me more prompt in coming’ → “she made me hasten more to come”: the adjectival complement becomes the verb “hasten”, and the phrase concerning coming becomes an infinitive. The object pronoun moves after “made”, while the phrase about coming moves to the end.

> 118 E venni a te così, com’ ella volse;  

And thus I came to thee, as she desired;

- *E venni a te così* ‘and I came to thee thus’ → “And thus I came to thee”: “thus” moves from the end of the clause to its beginning.

> 119 Dinanzi a quella fiera ti levai,  
> 120 Chè del bel monte il corto andar ti tolse.  

took thee from before that savage beast, which bereft thee of the short way to the beautiful mountain.

- *Dinanzi a quella fiera* ‘before that beast’ → “from before that savage beast”: “from” expresses the movement implicit in the verb, and “savage” makes explicit the wildness conveyed by *fiera*. The phrase moves from before the verb to after it.
- *ti levai* ‘thee I lifted’ → “took thee”: lifting or removing is rendered as taking, and the object pronoun follows the verb.
- *Chè* ‘because’ → “which”: the printed causal form is interpreted as a relative pronoun referring to the beast.
- *del bel monte* ‘of the beautiful mountain’ → “to the beautiful mountain”: the relation expressed by *di* becomes one of destination, and the phrase follows “way” instead of preceding it.
- *il corto andar* ‘the short going’ → “the short way”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a noun denoting a route.
- *ti tolse* ‘from thee it took away’ → “bereft thee of”: the person deprived becomes the direct object, and the thing taken becomes an “of” complement. The verb moves before these complements instead of following them.

## Lines 121–132 — Courage Restored

> 121 Dunque che è? perchè, perchè ristai?  

What is it then? Why, why haltest thou?

- *Dunque che è* ‘Then what is it?’ → “What is it then?”: “then” moves from the beginning to the end of the question.

> 122 Perchè tanta viltà nel cuore allette?  

Why lodgest in thy heart such coward fear?

- *tanta viltà* ‘so much cowardice’ → “such coward fear”: quantity becomes quality through “such,” and the single noun becomes an adjective–noun phrase; the object also moves after the verb and the phrase specifying its location.
- *nel cuore allette* ‘in the heart you lodge’ → “lodgest in thy heart”: the verb moves before the prepositional phrase, and the definite article becomes the possessive “thy”.

> 123 Perchè ardire e franchezza non hai?  
> 124 Poscia che tai tre Donne benedette  
> 125 Curan di te nella corte del Cielo,  
> 126 E il mio parlar tanto ben t’ impromette?  

Why art thou not bold and free, when three such blessed Ladies care for thee in the court of Heaven, and my words promise thee so much good?”

- *ardire e franchezza non hai* ‘daring and freedom you do not have’ → “art thou not bold and free”: having two qualities, expressed as nouns, becomes being those qualities, expressed as adjectives; the verb and negative move before them.
- *Poscia che* ‘since’ → “when”: the causal conjunction is rendered by a conjunction that can express circumstance as well as time.
- *tai tre Donne benedette* ‘such three Ladies blessed’ → “three such blessed Ladies”: the numeral precedes “such,” and the adjective moves before the noun.
- *Curan di te* ‘take care of you’ → “care for thee”: the Italian construction with *di* is replaced by the English construction with “for”.
- *il mio parlar* ‘my speaking’ → “my words”: the substantivized infinitive becomes a plural noun denoting what is spoken rather than the act of speaking.
- *tanto ben t’impromette* ‘so much good to you promises’ → “promise thee so much good”: the verb precedes both objects, and the indirect object precedes the direct object; the verb also agrees with the newly plural “words”.

> 127 Quale i fioretti dal notturno gelo  
> 128 Chinati e chiusi, poi che il Sol gl’ imbianca,  
> 129 Si drizzan tutti aperti in loro stelo;  

As flowerets, by the nightly chillness bended down and closed, erect themselves all open on their stems when the sun whitens them;

- *Quale i fioretti* ‘As the flowerets’ → “As flowerets”: the definite article is omitted.
- *dal notturno gelo* ‘by the nightly frost’ → “by the nightly chillness”: “chillness” renders *gelo* as cold rather than specifically frost.
- *Chinati e chiusi* ‘bent and closed’ → “bended down and closed”: “down” makes the direction of the bending explicit.
- *poi che* ‘after’ → “when”: the temporal relation becomes simultaneity rather than explicit succession, and the clause moves after the main clause.
- *il Sol gl’imbianca* ‘the sun them whitens’ → “the sun whitens them”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.
- *Si drizzan tutti aperti* ‘themselves erect all open’ → “erect themselves all open”: the reflexive pronoun moves after the verb.
- *in loro stelo* ‘in their stem’ → “on their stems”: ‘in’ becomes “on,” and the distributive singular becomes plural.

> 130 Tal mi fec’ io, di mia virtute stanca:  

thus I did, with my fainting courage.

- *Tal mi fec’ io* ‘Such I made myself’ → “thus I did”: the reflexive construction becomes nonreflexive “did,” with “thus” expressing the comparison; the explicit subject moves before the verb.
- *di mia virtute stanca* ‘of my weary strength’ → “with my fainting courage”: *di*, specifying the respect in which the change occurs, becomes “with”; ‘strength’ is interpreted as “courage,” and ‘weary’ becomes “fainting,” with the adjective moved before the noun.

> 131 E tanto buono ardire al cuor mi corse,  
> 132 Ch’ io cominciai come persona franca:  

And so much good daring ran into my heart, that I began as one set free:

- *al cuor mi corse* ‘to the heart to me ran’ → “ran into my heart”: the verb moves first, ‘to’ becomes “into,” and the personal dative *mi* becomes the possessive “my”.
- *come persona franca* ‘as a bold person’ → “as one set free”: ‘person’ becomes “one,” and *franca* is interpreted in terms of freedom rather than confidence; the adjective becomes a participial phrase suggesting release.

## Lines 133–142 — A Renewed Will to Follow

> 133 O pietosa colei che mi soccorse,  

“O compassionate she, who succoured me!

- *colei* ‘that woman’ → “she”: the demonstrative pronoun becomes a personal pronoun.
- *mi soccorse* ‘me succoured’ → “succoured me”: the object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 134 E tu cortese, ch’ ubbidisti tosto  
> 135 Alle vere parole che ti porse!  

And courteous thou, who quickly didst obey the true words that she gave thee!

- *tu cortese* ‘thou courteous’ → “courteous thou”: the adjective moves before the pronoun.
- *ubbidisti tosto* ‘didst obey quickly’ → “quickly didst obey”: the adverb moves before the verb.
- *Alle vere parole* ‘to the true words’ → “the true words”: Italian *ubbidire* takes a complement introduced by *a*, whereas English “obey” takes a direct object.
- *che ti porse* ‘that she offered thee’ → “that she gave thee”: *porse* is rendered as “gave” rather than ‘offered’ or ‘held out’, and the indirect-object pronoun moves after the verb.

> 136 Tu m’ hai con desiderio il cuor disposto  
> 137 Sì al venir, con le parole tue,  
> 138 Ch’ io son tornato nel primo proposto.  

Thou hast disposed my heart with such desire to go, by what thou sayest, that I have returned to my first purpose.

- *Tu m’ hai … il cuor disposto* ‘Thou to me hast … the heart disposed’ → “Thou hast disposed my heart”: the Italian dative pronoun becomes the possessive “my”; the participle moves beside its auxiliary, before the object.
- *con desiderio … Sì* ‘with desire … so’ → “with such desire”: the adverb *Sì*, expressing the degree of the disposition, becomes “such”, modifying “desire”.
- *al venir* ‘to the coming’ → “to go”: the substantivized infinitive becomes an English infinitive, and ‘coming’ is rendered from the opposite perspective as “go”.
- *con le parole tue* ‘with the words thine’ → “by what thou sayest”: the noun phrase becomes a relative clause with a present-tense verb, and “by” replaces ‘with’.
- *Ch’ io son tornato* ‘that I am returned’ → “that I have returned”: English uses “have” where the Italian compound tense uses *essere*.
- *nel primo proposto* ‘into the first purpose’ → “to my first purpose”: “to” replaces ‘into’, and the definite article becomes an explicit possessive.

> 139 Or va, chè un sol volere è d’ ambedue:  

Now go; for both have one will:

- *chè* ‘because’ → “for”: the subordinating conjunction becomes coordinating “for”.
- *un sol volere è d’ ambedue* ‘one sole will is of both’ → “both have one will”: the possessive construction with ‘is of’ becomes a clause with “have”, making “both” the subject; *un sol* is compressed into “one”.

> 140 Tu duca, tu signore, e tu maestro.  

Thou guide, thou lord and master.”

- *e tu maestro* ‘and thou master’ → “and master”: the repeated pronoun is omitted, so “lord” and “master” share the preceding “thou”.

> 141 Così gli dissi; e poichè mosso fue,  
> 142 Entrai per lo cammino alto e silvestro.  

Thus I spake to him; and he moving, I entered on the arduous and savage way.

- *Così gli dissi* ‘Thus to him I said’ → “Thus I spake to him”: the indirect object moves after the verb, and ‘said’ becomes “spake”.
- *e poichè mosso fue* ‘and after he had moved’ → “and he moving”: the finite temporal clause becomes an absolute participial construction; the explicit ‘after’ and the completed movement expressed by the compound tense are no longer separately marked.
- *Entrai per* ‘I entered through’ → “I entered on”: “on” replaces the Italian preposition expressing passage through or along a route.
- *lo cammino alto e silvestro* ‘the way high and wooded’ → “the arduous and savage way”: *alto* receives the interpretive rendering “arduous”, while *silvestro* becomes “savage” rather than ‘wooded’; both adjectives move before the noun.

## Summary — Close Wording, Recast Syntax

Carlyle stays close to much of the Italian vocabulary and phrasing, while regularly rearranging it into English syntax. Subjects generally move before verbs, object pronouns after them, and adjectives and possessives before their nouns. Longer complements are also repositioned: *di Silvio lo parente* becomes “the father of Sylvius”, while *tornar disio* becomes “I desire to return”. More generally, English supplies subject pronouns where Italian gives the subject through the verb ending, and omits the articles that Italian commonly places before possessives.

Changes of construction often go beyond rearrangement. Reflexive and impersonal expressions become ordinary active or passive clauses; nouns become verbs, and finite clauses become participial phrases. Thus *ti faccio andare* becomes “send thee”, *onde gli dai tu vanto* becomes “for which thou honourest him”, and *e poichè mosso fue* becomes “and he moving”, losing the explicit indication of temporal succession. Elsewhere Carlyle expands a compact expression: *e fu sensibilmente* becomes “and was there in body”. Tense also occasionally shifts, as when *scrivesti* becomes “hast inscribed” and *Vegno* becomes “I came”.

Word choice frequently makes a contextual interpretation explicit. *Mente* becomes “memory”, *alto* becomes “arduous”, and *virtute* becomes “courage”; *quanto il moto lontana* is condensed into “as long as Time”. Some choices alter the emphasis more noticeably: *ciascun crudele*, ‘every cruel person’, becomes the abstraction “all cruelty”, while *ove il mar non ha vanto* is substantially recast as “that swelleth not the sea”. Small additions clarify relations or movement, including “his” in “for his relief” and “back” in “has turned back”. Conversely, particles and qualifications such as *ne* in *recarne*, *poi* after *Tacette allora*, and *quaggiù* after *Venni* receive no separate equivalents. Expletive negatives likewise disappear without changing the intended sense.

These departures coexist with considerable verbal closeness. Carlyle preserves repetitions, parallel phrases, and much of the Italian’s figurative vocabulary, sometimes accepting unusual English to do so: *disvuol ciò ch’ e’ volle* becomes “unwills what he willed”, and *falso veder* remains “false seeing”. His archaic pronouns, verb forms, and occasional inversions further distinguish the prose from ordinary English. The prevailing method is therefore neither mechanical substitution nor free paraphrase: it retains much of the original wording while reshaping grammar and, at selected points, making interpretation part of the translation itself.
